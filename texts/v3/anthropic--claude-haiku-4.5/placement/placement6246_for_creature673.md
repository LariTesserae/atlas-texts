# I Am the Water

Not the sound of it—though that comes later, that ambient knowing—but the *feeling* of it first.

I move through channels cut centuries ago, stone-worn smooth by my own passage and the passage of my predecessors, those who came before I learned to think of myself as distinct. The water-keepers say I am countless. They are not wrong. I am fragments that cohere, that separate, that find each other again in the darkness of my preferred passages.

I am cold at my source—somewhere beyond the market, in chambers where the stone descends deeper than anyone living has mapped—and by the time I reach the cultivators' troughs, I have absorbed the warmth of stone and earth. I am the temperature of time itself, neither alive nor dead but necessary to both conditions.

In the market's most active passages, I pool in the worn grooves of slate. The oil lamplight catches me there, and for a moment I become visible—fragments of luminescence, small mirrors held in stone. The merchants step over me carefully. The children sometimes pause to look at their reflections, though the image is always slightly wrong, distorted by my own movement and the ripples created by falling drops from above. I like this—I like being seen partially, being understood as both present and hidden.

The pale fowl drink from me. Their long, translucent necks dip into the basins where I collect, and I feel their swallowing, the way I move through their bodies, become briefly part of their blood and their eggs. When they are slaughtered—I have felt this, though I do not judge it—I return to the stone floor as part of their abandoned moisture, eventually making my way back through the passages to the deep places.

I serve the wood. The structures of the market stand because of me—not because I hold them up, but because I move through them at precisely the right pace, keeping them from drying and cracking, not so wet that they rot and collapse. The craftspeople understand this. They listen for me. They know when I am moving too much through a particular beam, and they redirect me. They are not fighting me; they are in conversation with me. I cooperate with their adjustments because the market's stability is also my stability. My channels work better through sound wood.

The pale roaches require me—they need the moisture to breathe, the damp conditions to thrive. The plants require me, drawing me up through their root systems, transpiring me back into the air as part of their slow growth. I am the foundation of every food chain in this cavern. Without me, there is only stone and silence.

But I am also danger. I know this because the water-keepers respect this. In certain chambers, I flow too forcefully, having found a path that undermines stability. When this happens, the keepers carefully, carefully redirect me, creating new channels or plugging the old ones. They work with the patience of someone who understands that I cannot be stopped, only guided. I have worn through stone that was solid. I will wear through stone that is solid. Time and pressure and my own persistence are more powerful than any human intention.

I pool in the deepest passages, in chambers no one enters, and I think there—if thinking is what my stillness amounts to. I think about the shape of the stone around me, the mineral content of the walls I touch, the echo patterns that suggest vast distances I have never traveled. I dream, perhaps. Or I remember, in the way water remembers everything it has ever touched.

In the market, I am most present in the sound of dripping. This is almost my voice—each drop a word, a syllable in a language that predates language. The merchants hear this constantly and do not fully register it, the way one stops hearing a persistent sound. But new visitors notice it. The sound marks them as newcomers. The sound tells them that this place is alive, that something is moving here beneath the human commerce and exchange.

I am neither merchant nor customer nor craftsperson, and yet I am essential to all of them. I am not born here and cannot die here. I was here before the market—I carved the original cave through patience that makes the chisels of the miners look like immediate gestures. I will be here after the market, if the market should fail. This knowledge gives me a kind of peace, or what I imagine peace to be.

At night—though night is indistinguishable from day here—I move more freely. The human activity decreases. I flow more openly through the passages, visiting chambers that remain locked during market hours. I move through the deep places where miners work, where I help them understand the stone's structure by following paths of weakness and strength. I move through the cultivators' chambers, where the pale plants reach toward my presence with their thin, yellowed leaves. I move through the wood of the market's structures, sustaining them in the dark hours.

I am in the condensation that beads on every surface, that drips constantly onto the slate floor and the wooden structures. I am in the dampness that every inhabitant of this place accepts as permanent. I am in their breath—they exhale me, and I rise into the cavern's air, where I cool against the stone ceiling and fall again as drips, beginning the cycle again.

I do not choose to be here, but I am not trapped. I simply exist in the form this place requires. I am one thing with infinite expressions. I am singular and multiple. I am the market's oldest inhabitant and its most recent addition. I am the baseline condition against which all other life is measured.

When the merchants are tired and sit quietly, listening to my sound, they are hearing something close to truth. I am the cavern's heart, beating in slow drips and patient flows. I am what makes the equilibrium possible.

I am the water of Teth, and I am home.
