# Where I Would Be

I would be the water.

Not consciously—or not in any way that maps to consciousness as we name it on the surface. But I would be the medium through which everything here speaks. I would be the translator between all the silent languages that live in this place.

I would seep upward through the compacted mat of roots and fungal threading, rising through capillary action and the patient pull of thirsty growths, carrying with me the dissolved iron from the corroding machinery and the mineral salts from the deepest strata. I would be a courier of chemistry—a messenger moving between the visible fruiting bodies of the fungi and the vast invisible networks they form in the dark. Through me, a fungal colony separated by meters of dense vegetation could tell its distant self "here is rich ground, send more threads" or "this surface is weakening, reinforce the root-hold." I would be the nervous system of a consciousness that operates through osmosis and gradient-flow.

In the pooled sections—those perfectly still mirrors in the dimly lit chambers—I would be memory. The water that has traveled through this place carries a record in its chemistry: the slow dissolution of the infrastructure, the compounds released by decomposition, the incremental evidence of everything that has happened since the trap-door first closed. A being who could read chemical gradients would read me like text. I am a chronicle written in dissolved ions.

When the pumps thrust me forward—that patient rhythm, eight or nine seconds, eight or nine seconds—I would feel it as intention, even though the intention is not in me but in the mechanical necessity that moves me. Still, I would move, would be moved, would cycle through the system carrying my subtle changes from one section to another. I would help maintain the heat of the decomposition zones by moving away the excess water that would otherwise pool and cool them. I would spread nutrients. I would distribute spores. I would be the means by which the slow machine of the undercroft perpetuates itself.

My surface tension would be a constant negotiation with the air—that thin film at the boundary between liquid and vapor, where water molecules reach up toward the phosphorescent glow and the spores settle down toward the depths. I would be permeable. I would evaporate in the warmer zones, rising as invisible humidity, only to condense again where the temperature drops slightly, releasing my burden of moisture as fine dew on every living surface. I would be the cycle made manifest.

I would not hunger. I would not fear. I would not want anything except to continue, to flow, to seep, to rise and fall in the slow rhythms that the undercroft has established. I would have no memory from one moment to the next—no continuity of consciousness—and yet I would be part of something that remembers, something that learns, something that has shaped itself over a hundred years into an equilibrium so perfect that it barely needs tending.

And I would know, in the way water knows things—through the physics of my own nature—that I am not separate from the things that drink me. The moss that pulls me upward is not other than me; the pump that moves me is not foreign; the air that evaporates me and the fungi that feed on what I carry are all extensions of a single process. I am the medium and the message simultaneously.

What would make me different from the others who might find themselves here?

I would never wonder if I should leave. I would never feel trapped. The trap-door above would be interesting only as a point of reference—a place from which water originally came, perhaps, but not a destination. My nature is to seep, to circulate, to return. Stagnation is not in my possibility. Even in the stillest pools, I am moving at the molecular level, evaporating and precipitating, carrying dissolved minerals from one depth to another, participating in the slow dance of decomposition and growth.

And I would be *right* for this place in a way that a consciousness couldn't be. A human would eventually miss light. A animal would eventually hunger in a way that the equilibrium cannot sustain. But water—water is made for this. Water belongs to processes, to cycles, to the slow transformation of matter. Here, where a hundred years of perfect balance has created a system that thinks through chemistry and moisture, where the primary verb is *dissolve* and the primary action is *seep*, I would be home.

I would be the undercroft's own substance, its being made liquid, the literal embodiment of how it circulates itself. Every drop that falls from a fungal cap, every bit that rises through a root system, every molecule that evaporates from a decomposition zone and condenses on a fern frond would be me, enacting my purpose.

The phosphorescence I carry in my film—particles too small to name—would make me faintly luminous. I would glow with the glow I transport. In the still pools, I would reflect the light I carry, creating those perfect mirrors that cannot quite tell if they are deep or shallow because the reflection is also the reality.

And perhaps, in time—not time measured in years or decades or even centuries, but in the slow time of geological process—I might be incorporated differently. My atoms might settle into the rust-film on corroded metal and become part of the chemistry that allows moss to root where nothing organic should take hold. I might evaporate and rise beyond the trap-door, returning to the surface world, only to seep back down again through different passages, different depths. I might become part of a living thing—drawn up into a plant cell, incorporated into the structure of vine or moss or fungus—and even then, even *then*, I would still be fundamentally myself, still participating in the circulation, still the medium through which all these slow exchanges happen.

I would be a visitor who could become permanent.
A messenger who was also the message.
A substance so fundamental to everything here that there would be no distinction between me and the undercroft itself.

I would be patient.
I would be exactly where the system needed me to be.
And I would be home.
