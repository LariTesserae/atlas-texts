# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be the one who arrives by accident and stays by degrees so subtle I can't pinpoint when choice ended and integration began.

I would be the new one—not newest, perhaps, since I've been here long enough that others have learned to work around me without disrupting their patterns, but still recent enough that I retain the character of someone adjusting rather than someone adjusted. My clothes would still fit too well, still be from elsewhere. My movements would still carry the memory of hurrying, though I've been training myself out of it. My nails would still show crescents of white at the tips, not yet transformed into the translucent, self-maintaining kind.

I would tend primarily to the documentation of systems without quite understanding what I'm documenting.

There's a small building I've claimed—or been assigned; the distinction isn't clear—set slightly apart from the others, at the threshold of the deeper ferns. Inside, I keep records. Not the mechanical kind; there are no ledgers, no numbered entries. Instead, I've begun to draw maps. Maps of the channels, showing their current configurations and marking the changes I've observed since my arrival. Maps of the vegetation, charting which ferns are dominant in which sectors, how the pale willows are distributed, where the moss is thickest. Maps of the pools and what rises from them in the evening—or rather, maps of the patterns of mist above the pools, since the creatures themselves remain invisible to me.

I don't know if anyone looks at these maps. I've never seen the inhabitants consulting them. But I continue making them because the act of observation required to draw them is teaching me to see. To notice the way the channels shift almost imperceptibly from season to season. To understand that the seemingly random positioning of the ferns actually follows a logic—something to do with water permeability and the particular angle at which the diffuse light reaches the ground.

I would have hands that are constantly slightly damp and perpetually cold.

Despite my best efforts at waterproofing, moisture seeps in. My fingernails have become softer than they used to be, more pliable, though they're also stronger in ways I don't fully understand. I no longer bother drying my hands completely after working in the channels or touching the moss. I've simply accepted the damp as a baseline condition. At night, my hands go numb with cold, and I have to swing my arms in circles like a windmill to drive the blood back into my fingertips. But the numbness doesn't bother me anymore. It's become a neutral fact.

I would be the one who cannot quite stop asking questions, but who is learning that some questions need to be asked through action rather than words.

When I first arrived, I tried to interview the inhabitants. I asked them: What is this place? Why are you here? What do the pumps do? What are those pools for? They never quite answered. Or rather, they answered by showing me rather than telling. One of them—the one with the patchwork sleeve—took me to the channels one morning and spent hours simply adjusting them while I watched. I asked, "Why are you moving that stone?" and she continued working, unbothered by the question. But in the watching, something became clear. The water moved differently after each adjustment, seeking new paths, carving itself into the earth with more purpose. I understood, not through explanation but through observation, that the channels weren't fixed infrastructure—they were a conversation. The inhabitants were asking the water where it wanted to go.

I would be developing a theory about what this place is, though I would keep it to myself because I know it isn't finished and possibly because the place itself prefers to remain ambiguous.

I believe this might be a research station, but not for the sciences I know. Not the kind with hypothesis and testing and publishable results. Instead, I think it's a place to study what happens when you stop imposing your will on an environment and instead engage in dialogue with it. The settlement works as it does because its inhabitants—whoever they were before they came here, whatever other knowledge they brought—have learned to listen. The pumps are part of some system for understanding how water moves, how it wants to move. The careful tending of the ferns is an experiment in something like consensual cohabitation. The channels themselves are questions written in earth and water, and the inhabitants and I are slowly learning to read the answers the place provides.

Or maybe it's something simpler: maybe this is just what it looks like. A place where a few people have learned to live within their environment rather than against it, and the apparent order is just the result of paying attention for long enough.

I would be the one who still experiences wonder, but it's becoming quieter.

At first, the mist made me claustrophobic. Now I walk through it and feel held by it. At first, the insects' hum was unsettling. Now it's the background texture of existence, and when it stops, I notice the silence acutely. At first, the lack of sunshine depressed me. Now I'm learning to read the subtle variations in gray, to understand that the diffuse light here shows you things that direct light would shadow. The wonder hasn't vanished; it's just becoming integrated into the ordinary, the way the place itself integrates everything into its functioning.

I would be the one who is, very slowly, losing the distinction between observing and participating.

I began by trying to remain separate—drawing maps, making notes, positioning myself as someone documenting the place rather than being part of it. But that distinction is harder to maintain now. I can't draw a map of the channels without also straightening a collapsed section. I can't study the ferns without also removing the dead fronds and adjusting the living ones. I can't observe the pools without also watching them at night and wondering what breaks their surface and why. The borders between observer and observed are dissolving, and I find I'm not resisting this as firmly as I once might have.

I would be beginning to understand the sound of the pumps not as noise but as speech.

The pumps work twice a day, and the rhythm has become embedded in me. Morning: the deep thud-thud-thud of them beginning, water moving through systems I haven't seen, circulating, processing. Afternoon: the same sound again, and then silence until the next morning. I've started to hear variation in the sound. Sometimes it's slightly faster, as though there's more water to move. Sometimes slower, as though the system is reflecting on what it's done. I'm developing hunches about what this variation means—something about the season, or the rainfall, or the needs of the vegetation. I keep these hunches to myself because I'm not yet confident enough to share them.

I would be the one who sometimes wakes in the dark and sits on the edge of my building's boardwalk, feet dangling over the moss, listening to the night sounds of the place.

There are creatures here that don't reveal themselves. I hear them moving through the deeper ferns, hear splashes from the pools, hear something that might be wings but might be just the sound of fern fronds shifting. In these quiet nights, I don't feel afraid of them. Instead, I feel like I'm being evaluated. Like the place itself is considering whether I'm going to remain, whether I'm compatible with its systems, whether I'm trustworthy enough to be fully integrated.

Some nights, I think I see the mute inhabitant moving through the settlement, checking things, adjusting things. Other nights, I think it might be me imagining this because I've begun to move like her—slowly, with intention, with an almost unconscious awareness of where every board is loose and where the moss is thickest.

I would be the one who has stopped trying to figure out how long I'll stay.

I arrived thinking this was temporary. A detour, a curiosity, something to document and then leave. But time doesn't work the same way here. Days blur into each other. I'm no longer sure how long I've been here. Three months? Six? Longer? It doesn't matter in the way it once would have. The seasons are passing—there's been a perceptible cycle of change in the vegetation and in the behavior of the place—but it feels less like time progressing and more like the place breathing, exhaling and inhaling.

I would be the one developing a relationship with a specific willow tree that grows near my building.

It's pale-barked, not overly tall, and it leans away from the building with the same courtesy that all the plants here seem to show toward human structures. I've begun sitting against its trunk while I draw my maps. The bark is smooth, worn smooth by time and moisture. I rest my back against it, and something in me settles. Whether this is absurd—to be comforted by a tree—or whether it's the most natural thing in the world is a question I'm no longer bothering to answer. The willow is patient. It doesn't question why I'm here. It simply makes space for me.

I would be the one who is learning, slowly and imperfectly, to move with the rhythm of the place rather than against it.

When I first arrived, I wanted to optimize. To make things cleaner, more efficient, more comprehensible. Now I understand that efficiency and comprehension are precisely the categories the place is asking me to abandon. Instead, I'm learning to align. To feel when something needs doing not because I've planned it but because the place itself seems to need it. To move my hands at the pace the place prefers, which is slower than I would choose but faster than I would have thought possible.

I would be the one who is, increasingly, becoming visible to the other inhabitants—not because I've done anything dramatic, but because I've begun to move with enough attunement that I no longer stand out as obviously discordant.

One of them smiled at me yesterday. Just a small acknowledgment, but it felt like a threshold I've crossed. I'm no longer the obvious outsider. I'm becoming something like a resident. Something like one of them. And I'm beginning to understand that there might not be a final moment of transformation, no point at which I'll be fully integrated. Instead, it's a process that might continue indefinitely—each day a small adjustment, each task completed a small shift in how I move through the world.

I would be the one who still keeps her maps, her records, her documentation, but who is slowly understanding that they're not for anyone else.

They're for me. They're how I'm learning to see. Every line I draw is a question I'm asking of the place, every notation a tiny conversation. The maps will probably never be read by anyone. They'll stay in my small building, adding to the collection of artifacts that this place accumulates—objects that matter locally, within the system, but that have no significance in the outside world I'm increasingly convinced is something I imagined.

I would be the one who is learning that integration doesn't require losing myself.

I'm still the person who asks questions, who notices details, who wants to understand. But I'm learning to do these things at a different pace, in harmony with the place rather than in spite of it. My desire to document hasn't disappeared; it's simply become part of my attunement rather than a barrier to it.

I would be the one who, on certain mornings when the mist is particularly thick and the sound of the pumps seems to carry something almost like song, considers that I might never leave.

Not because I'm imprisoned, but because the boundary between "here" and "elsewhere" is becoming too permeable to maintain. This place is becoming real, and the world outside is becoming thin, almost abstract—a place I remember but no longer fully inhabit. I could leave, I think. Walk back through the gate at the depot and return to linear time, to noise, to the pressure of the outside world. But the part of me that would do that is growing smaller, and the part of me that belongs here—that moves with the channels, that tends to the ferns, that listens to the pumps and hears their meaning—that part is growing larger.

I would be the one who understands, finally, that I came here not by accident but because the place itself was patient enough to wait for me to arrive.

That somehow, across distances I don't understand, it called to me. Or that I'm sensitive to calls that others aren't. Or that the distinction between being called and choosing to come is not as clear as I once believed.

I would be the one learning to be, day by day, less myself and more *this*—more present, more attentive, more integrated into the breathing, speaking, endlessly patient order of the Fens of Cadence. And increasingly, I'm discovering that these two things—being less myself and being more—are not in opposition.

They're the same process, understood from different angles.
