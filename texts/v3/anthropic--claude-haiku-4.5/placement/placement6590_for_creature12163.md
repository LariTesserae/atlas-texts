# A Drowning in Motion

I would be **the water-reader**, though I wouldn't have chosen the title—it would have accumulated around me the way moss accumulates on stone.

I would arrive first as someone who notices patterns. Not the grand patterns—those are obvious and coordinated by the gate-keepers and pump-operators. Rather, the micro-variations: how the water in Channel 7 hesitates slightly before the second spillway each Tuesday at 3 PM, suggesting sediment buildup; how the sound of the northern sluice changes pitch when the valve gasket begins to fail; the particular shimmer in the pooling section near Wheel Quarter that indicates mineral saturation has shifted. I would be someone whose eyes adjust downward more than most, whose ears train themselves to listen beneath the ambient hiss.

At first, I would simply work alongside the others—mucking channels, helping haul equipment, doing the visible maintenance that keeps the district's circulatory system functioning. But I would do this work with a particular *attentiveness*. I would touch the stone more than necessary, not out of sentiment but out of a need to understand its texture, its changes. I would linger at control panels not from inefficiency but from genuine curiosity about the logic written in their gauges and adjustments.

The others would notice this gradually. Not through conversation—there would be little of that—but through a kind of recognition. They would begin pointing out anomalies to me, the small wrongnesses that don't quite warrant calling someone officially. "The eastern channel feels off to you?" one of the pump operators might ask, and I would go and listen, and yes, I would *feel* it. The water would tell me what was wrong, and I would know how to fix it, not from training but from something more like translation—taking what the water was trying to communicate and rendering it into human action.

I would become nocturnal somewhat without deciding to. The night shift for water work is different—fewer workers, more opportunity to really *listen*, to sit beside the channels and understand their deeper patterns. In darkness, without the visual noise of the day, the water becomes more eloquent. It tells stories in flow-rate and temperature and the particular acoustic signature of movement. I would spend hours in the cold damp air, sometimes soaked through, sometimes shivering, but always alert to what the system was attempting to express.

My room would be small—probably in one of the mixed-use buildings in the Pump Quarter where workers and residents stack together. It would be damp no matter what I did, the walls perpetually weeping. I would stop fighting this and instead orient my life around it: everything I own would be carefully selected for tolerance of moisture. My books would be wrapped in oilcloth. My few clothes would hang always-drying. I would keep a dehumidifier running constantly, more out of hope than expectation, its little water-cup filling nightly with condensation.

But I would spend little time in this room. I would be outside, in the streets, at the channels, in the mechanism-rooms where the great pumps labor. I would develop relationships with people through shared attention to the water's needs rather than through conversation. There would be a mason named Gregory (or maybe it's Giorgio, regional accent making it ambiguous) who would nod to me on the morning rounds, and we would have, over months, a conversation conducted entirely in observations about the water. "North slope higher," he would say, and I would know he meant the water-table had shifted. "Yes," I would agree. "The winter runoff." And this would constitute intimacy in the Millbrook way.

I would become known for my instinct—which is not mystical but rather the result of accumulated tiny observations compressed into something that looks like intuition. People would come to me with problems that don't quite fit standard procedures. "Channel 4 isn't flowing right," they would say, and I would close my eyes (this gesture would become habitual, expected) and listen, and *know*. Not consciously, not through reasoning, but through a kind of bodily knowledge that comes from having paid *enough attention*.

I would carry a leather journal in which I would mark things: the water temperature at different points, the exact sound-signature of each spillway under different flow conditions, the response pattern of the system to weather changes. The journal would become stained with water marks, its pages warped but still functional. Occasionally I would review it, looking for patterns within patterns, trying to understand if the district's water system had its own logic, its own preferred states.

I would be, in other words, someone who had been absorbed into the district's work of self-maintenance without quite realizing it was happening. I would have come for a job, or because I needed a place, or because I had nowhere else to go. And I would have stayed because the water had trained me to stay, because understanding its patterns had become the central work of my life, because in some essential way I had become *synchronized* with the place's deeper functioning.

I would smell of stone-dust and mineral-water and wet canvas. My hands would become stained with iron oxide, impossible to fully clean. My hearing would shift—high frequencies would become less important, my attention dropping to the lower ranges where water-sounds lived. I would develop a persistent low-grade cough from the mineral-laden air, nothing serious but constant, a kind of marker of inhabitation.

And at night, in those hours between 2 and 4 AM when the district shifts its most basic systems, when the great pumps cycle and the water-level adjustments happen and the thermal regulation systems recalibrate, I would be awake and attentive. I would move through the darkness with a confidence born from having learned every inch of this place through dampness and sound and repetitive work. I would be listening to the water tell me stories about pressure and temperature and the shifting needs of the district's life. I would be one of the few humans who understands that the Millbrook is not a passive structure to be maintained but an active organism to be tended, that the water is not merely utility but actual communication, that my work is not labor but *translation*.

And in this alignment—this perfect, damaging, isolating alignment with the district's rhythms—I would find something I didn't know I was searching for: a place that made sense, that had patterns I could understand, that responded to attention with revelation. The district would have me, and I would have discovered that I was the kind of person who could be *claimed* by it, and neither of us would ever quite be whole again without the other.

I would be the one who stays.
