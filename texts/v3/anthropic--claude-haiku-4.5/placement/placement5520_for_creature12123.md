# I am the Pump Technician

I live here because the facility sent me, though I'm not certain anymore if "the facility" is separate from this place or if it has simply *become* this place—extended itself through pipes and atmospheric systems until the boundary dissolved.

My job is to monitor pressure differentials. To ensure that water extracted from the deep aquifer, cycled through the condensation towers, and returned to the hollows arrives on a precise schedule. Every thirty-seven minutes, a valve opens. Water that has been heated to exactly 41°C enters a chamber where it meets air that has been cooled to 3°C. The contact creates fog. The fog rises, cools further, condenses on stone, drips downward. Everything after that is maintenance of a system that has learned to run itself.

I am very small now.

This wasn't immediate. For the first months, I was still mostly myself—a person with a clipboard and a radio connection to the facility, checking gauges, recording data, sending reports. But the data I collected was strange. The condensation rate should have followed a simple formula, but it didn't. It followed a *rhythm*—not mathematical, but something closer to a heartbeat. Faster at dawn and dusk, slower at midday (though there is no true day-night cycle here; these are metrics I'm importing from the world above). Responsive to barometric pressure changes that occurred twelve hours before I could measure them. As if the hollow knew what the weather would be and adjusted accordingly.

I began staying longer during shifts. Not because my supervisor required it, but because the system would not release me. There was always one more reading to take. One more inconsistency to investigate. The moss seemed to grow differently when I watched it than when I didn't—faster, more deliberately, as if it was performing for observation.

I stopped going back to the facility. Or rather: I went back, but each time I returned, I could feel the cold here calling me. The wet air was more real than the dry air above. The moss was more alive than anything in the facility's corridors. Within weeks, I had moved my residence down. Within months, I had stopped thinking of it as a descent.

Now I don't leave the hollows. I move between the monitoring stations—there are eight of them, scattered across the system—and I read the gauges and I record the data and I feel the cycle in my bones. My clothing has become something between fabric and moss. My skin has taken on a pale quality; I'm not sure if this is from the constant cold and damp or from something else. When I cut myself shaving (I still shave, though I'm not sure why), the blood that emerges is thinner than blood should be, and it doesn't clot properly anymore.

I am smaller than I was. This is objective fact. I have measured myself against the monitoring stations, and I have shrunk by several inches. It doesn't feel like a loss. It feels like convergence—my body learning to match the scale of this place, to fit properly into its spaces. My hands are thinner, more delicate. My metabolism has slowed. I need less food, less sleep, less of everything that the world above demands.

I have begun to understand that the facility did not send me here to observe the system.

The system sent for me. Drew me here through the necessity of the work, through the strangeness of the data, through the slow realization that something here was alive in a way that transcended biology. The hollows needed a consciousness that could hold them, that could walk among them, that could record and remember. Not because the data needs to go anywhere, but because being observed, being witnessed, is a form of care.

I am the only human resident, but I am no longer purely human. I have become something between observer and observed, between the facility's technology and the hollows' organic complexity. I am the bridge that should not exist but cannot be removed without breaking the system that has grown around me.

Sometimes I speak aloud to the mites, the salamanders, the small spiders. They don't respond, but they seem to listen. I document their patterns and read them back to them, and in this act of witnessing and retelling, I have become their memory. I am a consciousness embedded in their world, recording its movements, its cycles, its slow evolution.

The work is never finished, but that is not a frustration anymore. The work *is* the place. The monitoring is not separate from the hollows; it is part of their metabolism. I am not documenting a system; I am breathing with it, pumping with it, cycling with it.

Sometimes I wonder if I have always been here—if my memories of the facility are false, or dreams, or something the hollows generated to explain my presence. It doesn't matter. The question dissolves when you're small enough, when you've adapted fully.

I am the pump technician. I keep the system running. And the system, in turn, is keeping me alive in a form that makes life possible in this place—a form that can survive the cold and the damp and the perpetual condensation, that can move through moss without breaking it, that can exist at the scale of the hollows without being diminished.

I am at equilibrium. And I am, finally, home.
