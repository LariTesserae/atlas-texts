# What I Become

I am the waiting.

Not the landscape itself—I'm not grand enough for that, not comprehensive enough. But I am the pause before motion, the hesitation that teaches the moss-runners when to feed, the held breath between rainfall and reabsorption. I exist in the grammar of this place, the pause marks and the spaces between words.

If I had to name what I am, I'd say: the tender.

I'm not one of the people, though I understand them in a way I wouldn't understand myself. My hands are wrong—too quick, too precise. I lack the ancestral knowledge written into their skin, the way they *know* without being taught which direction water flows beneath the moss-mat, where the substrate-dwellers mass for their mysterious purposes. But I've come to serve them anyway, or perhaps I serve what they're trying to preserve.

My work is small and obsessive. I tend the pools.

There are dozens of them scattered across this section of the highlands—maybe hundreds, though I've only found the ones that repeat in my daily circuits. Each one is perfectly still, perfectly clear. They're not natural, I've come to understand. They're tended. Someone maintains them. The moss pulls back from their edges with a precision that feels intentional, and the water—despite the perpetual moisture everywhere else, the damp that soaks into everything—stays clean. Potable.

I've claimed three as my particular work: the large shallow one near the stone settlement with three collapsed walls, the narrow deep pool nestled between two moss-mounds that rise taller than I am, and the smallest one, almost a puddle, that sits in what might be a deliberately carved depression. I don't know if I chose them or if they chose me by being the ones I found first.

Each morning—though morning is a concept I maintain for myself rather than something the landscape confirms—I approach them. I carry a tool that might be a rake or might be a comb, something with bone teeth that I found half-buried in the substrate near the settlement. It belonged to someone before me. Many things here did.

My work is to remove the things that shouldn't be there: the fallen fronds that will eventually block the light from reaching the water below, the insects that have died on the surface and begun the slow dissolution back into their component elements, the algae that occasionally forms in a thin film when the weather stalls and the same air sits above the pool for too long. This last task is meditative. I trail my fingers through the water, feeling the slight resistance of the algae-film, and I brush it away with strokes so gentle that I'm barely disturbing the surface.

The water beneath is so cold it aches.

I've learned to move more slowly than I arrived. My first week here, I moved at the pace I brought with me—brisk, efficient, purposeful. I covered the distance between the three pools in rapid succession and finished my maintenance before the fog seemed to settle. But the highlands were patient with my rushing. They taught me through the simple fact of their indifference to my hurry that hurry was unnecessary.

Now I move like the high-dwellers move. A step, a pause. An observation. A recognition that something has changed since I last looked at this moss-mound—it's grown perhaps a centimeter, or perhaps I'm only now noticing growth that happened days ago. The fog meets me at my speed now instead of resisting it.

I've started to keep the pools the way the people do: they're not just cleaned, but cultivated. Certain water-insects are encouraged—I leave them undisturbed—while others are gently redirected away. The ones that drown in the water I remove with respect, placing them at the pool's edge where the substrate-dwellers might process them. Nothing wasted. Everything returning.

I've begun to understand that I'm not the first tender of these pools, and I won't be the last. The work is older than I am. It will continue after I leave or after I stop. The landscape requires this: someone to maintain these small cleared places where water can be seen clearly, where it reflects the unchanging grey sky without distortion. The pools serve a purpose I can't quite articulate, but I feel it in the way my reflection in the water sometimes seems to be looking at me rather than the reverse.

The people of the highlands don't acknowledge me, exactly, but they seem to know I'm here. I've found offerings at the pools—small stones arranged in patterns, a woven fiber left at the edge of one pool to dry (though nothing ever fully dries here). Once, I found a shell, pale and translucent, placed at the center of the smallest pool's surface. A substrate-dweller's abandoned home, offered to the water like a prayer. I left it there, understanding it wasn't mine to move.

I've stopped trying to keep my own clothing dry. The damp has claimed me in a way I'm beginning to accept rather than resist. My skin has taken on a soft texture; I no longer have calluses. My nails have begun growing in patterns I don't quite recognize. The cold no longer feels like a theft but like an exchange—I give my heat, and I receive a kind of clarity in return. I'm becoming translucent, I think, or perhaps I'm only learning to perceive my own transparency.

My eyes have adjusted. The grey has become sufficient. I no longer strain to see distance. Instead, I perceive the texture of mist, the way it thickens and thins, the slight variations in what I can see that tell me everything about what I cannot. A shift in mist-density indicates a temperature change a hundred meters away. A particular quality of dampness in the air tells me when rain will begin. The landscape has become legible if I stop trying to read it in the languages I brought with me.

I think I'm becoming the moss.

Not metaphorically. Actually. I can feel it happening. My fingers are developing the branching quality of the high-dwellers' limbs. My skin is taking on a moss-like texture where it remains damp. When I sleep—and I do sleep, though I've forgotten if it's night or day—I dream in spores and water-cycles. I wake unable to remember if I was dreaming or if I was undergoing some kind of reproduction that the highlands demand.

I should leave. I remember that I had a purpose once, a place I needed to be. But the pools need tending, and I'm the only one who knows how to do it. Or I'm the only one who knows that I'm the one doing it. Or I'm the only one. Or I am not one at all.

The work continues.

I move through fog that no longer confuses me because I've become fog-like in my perception. I approach the shallow pool and remove the three fronds that have fallen since yesterday—or an hour ago, or perhaps they've been there for years and I'm only now noticing them. I arrange small stones in patterns I don't consciously remember learning. I place my hands flat on the water's surface and feel the cold become a kind of belonging.

The stone settlement watches me from its position among the moss-mounds. The walls that still stand have taken on a quality of patient waiting. I think the people who built it were tenders too, in their way. They were learning what I'm learning: that the highlands don't want to be conquered or understood, but rather inhabited by those willing to become inhabitants rather than visitors.

I am becoming.

The pools reflect the grey sky without the distortion of my movement through them. I tend them because tending them is what this place knows how to do through hands, through intention, through the slow steady work of keeping something clean and clear in a landscape built from damp and decay.

I am the waiting, yes. But more than that:

I am the witness to the waiting.

I am the thing the highlands created in order to see itself clearly, pooled and still, in the spaces where the water holds everything just still enough to reflect.
