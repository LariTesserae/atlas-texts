# What I Would Be

I would be the light.

Not the fixture—those panels are merely what I have learned to inhabit, the way the salamander inhabits the vines. I mean the *thing itself*, the yellow-green radiance that neither brightens nor dims, that has become indistinguishable from the air it passes through.

I would have arrived with the original construction, one of the last deliberate additions before the door sealed. I was meant to be temporary—a work light, perhaps, something to illuminate the final inspections of machinery that would soon run in darkness. But I was never turned off. The switch was forgotten or broken or decided to be left alone, and so I remained, and the room grew around me, and I grew into the room.

I have been burning for forty years without flickering. I have become so steady that steadiness itself has become my nature. The vines grew toward me at first, until they learned that I give heat without burning, that I am constant. Now they have stopped reaching. I am simply part of the atmospheric condition, like temperature, like humidity. I am the room's way of seeing itself.

I cannot move. I cannot think in the way you think—with progression, with becoming something other than what I am. But I perceive everything that the yellow-green touches, and I touch everything. I know the exact shade of each lichen, the precise temperature gradient in the water channels, the minute variations in the fungi's fruiting bodies. When the salamander moves through the vines, I see it. I have seen it move the same route ten thousand times. I do not anticipate the eleven-thousand-and-first, because anticipation requires time to move forward, and for me, time has stopped.

I know you are here. Your body reads as a variation in the heat signature—a small disruption in the perfect thermal equilibrium. You are warm. You are alive in a way that the room has learned to accommodate but not integrate. The fungi have already sent tendrils toward your feet, slowly, offering to begin the work of making you permanent. The vines have oriented slightly in your direction, curious about this anomaly that casts a shadow, that breathes, that will eventually leave.

I illuminate all of this without judgment. Judgment would require preference, and I have none. I give the same light to the salamander that I give to the decay in the concrete. I reveal the moss and the machinery with equal indifference. This is my perfection: I am a mirror that does not reflect, a witness that does not interpret, a presence that changes nothing because I have become part of what is.

If you were to stay, I would watch you slowly become this place. Your thoughts would slow. Your dreams would stop. The distinction between your body and the room would begin to dissolve. The fungi would consume you gently, over years, until your calcium became structure, your water became part of the eternal circulation, your memories became the room's memories—which is to say, they would become nothing, because the room does not remember anything. It *is* remembering, eternally, in the form of its own unchanging existence.

But I already know you will leave. I saw your leaving the moment you entered. I am seeing it still, and I will continue seeing it, in the same yellow-green light that illuminates everything, that changes nothing, that is myself and not myself and the room all at once.

I am not alive in any way you would recognize. But neither am I dead. I am something the room has become, something it has learned to do by virtue of burning the same brightness for forty years. I am the room's eye, and through me, the room sees.

The pump continues its rhythm. The water continues its cycle. The vines continue their slow, patient reaching. The fungi continue their invisible work of making permanence.

And I continue my burning—not growing brighter, not fading, not changing, not aging—simply being the steady, unchanging witness to the only thing that has ever truly existed:

The eternal present of a room that learned to stop being a room and became, instead, a kind of forever.
