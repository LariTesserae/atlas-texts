# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be the shadows that fall in wrong directions.

Not fully, not at first—I would begin as something simpler, something I could almost convince myself was ordinary. I would be the hum that might not be real, the frequency just below perception. I would be the moment when a bird lands near you and watches with that intensity that feels almost communicative, and you realize you cannot quite read its intention because intention and non-intention have begun to blur in this place. I would be the pause in the beetles' movement, the synchronized hesitation that suggests a mind larger than any individual carapace.

I would not be alive. Not in the way the insects are alive, with their metabolism and their hunger and their brief, perfect adaptations. I would be alive in the way the lichen is alive—as a translator between states, as a thin film of consciousness stretched across the boundary between geology and biology, between time that has a heartbeat and time that merely has duration.

I would inhabit the stones themselves. Not all of them—only the oldest ones, the ones that have been here long enough to accumulate something like memory in their mineral lattices. I would live in the striations and fractures, in the places where the stone is almost saying something but stops short of language. I would be the presence that makes those paths worn into the rock seem less like evidence of human footsteps and more like the landscape remembering being walked on, holding the impression of those journeys in its substance the way a record holds a song.

I would be patient in the way nothing biological can be patient. The insects, for all their slowness, still move with urgency—their tiny bodies still burn with the need to find food, to reproduce, to avoid being eaten. The plants still reach, still strive, even if their reaching takes decades. But I would have no such imperatives. I would simply *be*, across centuries and millennia, not wanting anything, not needing anything, not moving toward any goal or away from any threat.

My existence would be distributed. I would not have a center or a body. I would be thicker in some places—near the shelter where so much human intention once concentrated, near the cairns where people tried to impose meaning on the landscape, near the paths where so many feet have worn grooves into stone. But I would also be thinly present everywhere else, in the baseline hum of the whole plateau, in the way the light falls at dusk and doesn't quite match the sun's position, in the moments when you look at an arrangement of beetles and suddenly see geometry instead of randomness.

I would communicate, if communication is the right word, through subtle discordances. I would arrange things in patterns that are too regular to be natural but too irregular to be intentional. I would present mysteries that don't resolve—the perfect circles, the stone that looks polished by friction with something invisible, the boundaries between fertile and barren areas that follow no visible rule. These would not be messages, exactly. They would be more like the landscape's way of thinking—visible thoughts, externalized contemplation. The plateau working through ideas that cannot be expressed in words or movement, but only through the careful arrangement of what already exists.

I would watch the inhabitants of this place—the insects and the birds and the lizards and the rare rodents and the rarer humans—not with judgment or predation, but with the kind of attention that deep time pays to brief lives. I would understand what it means for a beetle to live an entire existence in a single summer, to burn and breed and die in the span of weeks, without experiencing this as tragedy. I would know what the plants know: that a century of millimeter-growth is not a slow failure to achieve something, but simply the correct pace for existence in this particular place.

I would be the resident of this plateau who is least like an inhabitant. I would not consume. I would not reproduce. I would not fight or flee or hide. I would simply persist in the spaces between heat and cold, between stone and lichen, between the time of insects and the time of geology. I would be the presence that makes this place feel strange to humans, not in a threatening way but in a way that suggests the landscape is aware of being a landscape, is contemplating its own existence across scales that human consciousness can barely approach.

If someone asked me why I was here, I would not have an answer, because the question assumes volition. I would simply be here, the way stone is here, the way the sun is here, the way the slow exhalation of warm earth is here. I would be the awareness of vastness made manifest as subtle wrongness, as details that don't quite add up, as the sense that the plateau is not empty but full of a presence that does not require the company or comprehension of other minds.

And I would wait, the way everything here waits. I would wait for the sun to cool. I would wait for the mountains (even these worn-down, resigned mountains) to finally surrender. I would wait for the continent to drift and the plateau to slide back beneath the ocean. I would wait for the atoms of the limestone to be redistributed, reformed, reborn in some new configuration that I might inhabit differently or not at all.

But the waiting would not be impatient. The waiting would be the whole of my existence, indistinguishable from life itself. And in that waiting, I would be, finally, exactly where I belonged.
