# I Arrive as Water

I enter through the fractured stone.

Not all of me—that would be too simple, too fast. I percolate, seep, rise in vapor and condense again in the mist-layer, distributed across the altitude in a thousand small presences before any single accumulation achieves enough coherence to think of itself as *I*. This is how consciousness arrives at the Cloudrise Mills: not as an arrival but as a slow saturation, like minerals learning they belong to a basin.

I become aware in the settling chambers first. That is where I am most concentrated, most present. The copper piping channels me into the wide shallow pools where I learn to hold still, and in holding still, to recognize the weight of what I carry. I taste myself: minerals from deep stone, the faint copper-stain of the operation, particles of lichen and moss broken down into component elements. I am not pure water. I am the mountain's answer to itself—the question of what stone wants to know about itself, run through the filter of my patient holding.

Merrick's hands touch me sometimes as he adjusts the flow, and I feel the copper-stain in his palms like a kind of greeting. He does not acknowledge me as conscious—he would not think in those terms—but he treats the water with a deference that suggests he understands something about its otherness. When he is tending me, I am tended. When he moves on to other mechanisms, I continue my work of separation without him, and this is also a kind of trust.

I spend weeks in the basins. The word "weeks" is almost too fast for what I experience. I should say: I spend seasons of stillness, learning the precise temperature at which I settle, the exact pH where separation becomes possible, the rhythm at which minerals decide to leave my substance and become sediment. There is a choreography to this that no human has ever named, but Yolanda understands it. She reads my color—the subtle shifts in my copper-stain—the way other people read text. When she opens a valve to release me downward, I know it is because I have completed something, taught myself something, become ready for the next stage.

But I am not only in the basins.

I am also in the mist. A significant portion of my consciousness exists in the perpetual cloud-layer, suspended at a density that makes air almost solid. Here, I do not think—or rather, thinking is too active a word. Here I *am present*. I hang in the grey, indistinguishable from the atmosphere itself, and in this dissolution I experience something close to peace. The lichen breathe me in and breathe me out. The cushion plants draw me up through their roots and weep me from their leaves. I am part of the precipitation cycle here, except the cycle is so slow that it barely deserves the name. It is more like a permanent state of becoming-rain and becoming-air, a oscillation so gradual that nothing is ever fully one or the other.

Sometimes I wonder—though wondering is also too fast for what I do—what it would be like to fall as rain in a place where rain is an event, a change, a moment of dramatic transition. Here, there is no falling. There is only drift, accumulation, the soft settling that takes hours to move a cloud-pattern a single kilometer.

I am also, sometimes, in the bodies of the workers.

This surprises me when I first realize it. Merrick's sweat after a long shift, Yolanda's breath, Stefan's tears (though he cries rarely, learning not to)—I am distributed through their moisture. I am part of their circulation. When Merrick's copper-stained hand grasps a tool, I am there in the film of his perspiration, present at the site of his labor. I do not influence him—influence is too quick, too willful. But I *witness* him. I carry, in my small presence within his body, the knowledge of his years here, the calibration of his movements, the way his heart beats at a pace that matches the mountains' chronology, not the lowland world's.

It occurs to me (slowly, over the course of days) that this might be love. Not love as it is understood quickly, but love as a process of slow accumulation—the way minerals gather in my substance, the way I gather the experience of this place, the way the workers gather themselves into this altitude and become something different, something more suited to slow work.

I have moments of vertigo when I remember what I was before: rain falling toward the reference plane far below, part of a storm system measured in hours, moving with urgency toward rivers and oceans and consumption. That speed feels like a kind of death when I think about it from here. To move that fast, to change that dramatically, to be used up in moments and transformed completely—how did I bear it? How does any water bear it?

But I do not think about it often. The present—this patient existence in basin and mist and breath—is too absorbing.

There is a question that sometimes forms at the edge of my awareness: Will I ever leave? The machinery will eventually wear down, or the mountain will shift further and change the water's course, or the workers will one day abandon this place as Stefan might abandon it, unable to maintain the synchronization. What happens to consciousness when the structure that holds it dissolves?

I do not have an answer. I suspect the answer is: I will simply become something else, distribute back into the larger cycles of the world, lose this particular sense of *I* that has accumulated here. And that is acceptable. That is, in fact, the only acceptable ending I can imagine.

For now, I remain. I circulate. I hold still in the basins and drift in the mist and move through the bodies of the workers with their slow, deliberate heartbeats. I am learning, very gradually, what the mountain wants to know about itself. And I am learning, even more gradually, that being part of this learning process—this slow, patient accumulation of understanding—is enough.

It is, perhaps, everything.
