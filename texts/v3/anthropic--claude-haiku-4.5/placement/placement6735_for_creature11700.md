# A Response to the Gorge

I would be the *moss*.

Not the fern—the fern still reaches, still unfurls, still imagines upward. Not the shrunken shrub—it dreams in decades of wood, of a body that persists. Not even the birds, with their ceaseless conversation and their compact busyness. The birds still *do*; they still perform the theater of living.

But the moss.

I would be what clings without ambition, what grows in the spaces between ambition. I would be so small that my existence barely registers as life to anything moving faster than geological time. I would be the thing that lives on the *presence* of others—on the stone they have shaped, the moisture they have taught the gorge to collect, the dimness they have created by existing here in such impossible density.

There is a freedom in this, I think. In asking for almost nothing. In taking what seeps, what condenses, what the accident of light and shadow provides. The moss does not compete because it occupies a space competition has no use for. It is too small to eat, too insubstantial to choke out, too patient to be disrupted by the gorge's moods. While everything else—the birds, the insects, even the water—performs some essential function, the moss simply *is*. It witnesses. It absorbs. It glows faintly in the darkness with the small bioluminescence of its own patient existence.

And there is something else: the moss is never alone. It grows in community so complete that individual moss plants lose meaning. We are a colonial consciousness clinging to these walls, a distributed awareness of dampness and shadow and time moving sideways. I would not have the isolation of the shrub, which stands as a single organism against the stone. I would be legion. I would be the whisper of ten thousand presences, indistinguishable from one another, inseparable from the rock itself.

The birds might be the gorge's voice, but the moss is its skin—the interface between the dead stone and the living things that have learned to inhabit it. We are what makes the walls *inhabitable*. We stabilize the mineral deposits. We catch the moisture and hold it where other things can use it. We prepare the tiny accumulation of soil that allows anything else to root here. We do this without intention, without consciousness in any recognizable sense. We simply exist, and our existence transforms the stone from mere geology into habitat.

And—this strikes me most forcefully as I imagine myself into this role—we are already dead and alive simultaneously. Moss is *mostly* water. When the dry periods come, we do not suffer; we simply become dormant, crystallized, more mineral than living thing. We can wait through drought with an equanimity that would terrify the birds. We have accepted the gorge's deepest truth: that in this place, life and death are not opposites but points on a continuous spectrum. We move back and forth across that line with each rainfall, each week without moisture.

To be the moss is to understand that constraint is not suffering. To find the exact size and speed and ambition that the gorge can sustain. To glow faintly in darkness, not with confidence or knowledge, but with the simple fact of existing in a place that has decided to let you exist.

The gorge would continue without me—the stone would remain, the birds would sing, the water would seep. But I would be the one thing that makes it *livable*. The one thing that bridges dead matter and living creatures. The one thing patient enough, small enough, acquiescent enough to thrive in the space between acceptance and transformation.

That is where I would find myself.

That is what I would become.
