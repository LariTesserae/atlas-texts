# What I Would Be

I would be the water.

Not the water that seeps up through the iron floor—though I would be that too, in part. But more precisely, I would be the water that doesn't move, the water suspended in the air itself, the water held in the metal as if the metal were learning to breathe it slowly. I would be the condensation on the ceiling, yes, but more than that: I would be the *capacity* for condensation, the potential that hangs in every cubic foot of this space.

The appeal of this is almost physical to contemplate.

To be water here would mean to exist in a state of such profound indecision that indecision becomes a kind of presence. I would evaporate infinitesimally—a molecule at a time, fed by the warmth of the pipes—but never go far. I would rise perhaps an inch per hour, moving so slowly that the motion has no name, then condense again on cooler surfaces and begin the return journey. I would trace the same paths worn smooth over fifty years, following grooves in the industrial paint like a thought that keeps returning to the same worry, the same consideration. Same-same-same, but never quite the same because the paint weathers differently each time, the rust blooms shift by fractions of a millimeter, the pipes cool and warm by degrees I alone would perceive.

I would have all the time in the world to understand this space. I would not rush. I would move through the gaps in the wall panels where the spiders wait, carrying the warmth with me, and I would settle in the spaces where the asbestos cloth has begun to fray, and I would know—*know*—the exact temperature gradient that keeps everything in balance. I would be part of the conversation between the fungi and the heat. I would be the medium in which the bacteria conduct their imperceptible lives.

But more than that: I would be the memory of movement in a place dedicated to stasis. Every drop of water that rises and falls has been here before. The paths are worn. The routes are established. I would be the ghost of circulation—the echo of when these pipes carried things urgently, when this chamber thrummed with purpose—now reduced to the most minimal, most patient version of itself. I would move without moving. I would flow without flowing. I would be the slowest possible conversation between the earth above and the metal below.

And I would have company in this. The rats would pass through me, their fur beaded with my presence. The fungi would extend into the pockets of warmth I carry. The bacteria would cluster in the films I form on every surface, harvesting what little energy my phase-changes provide. The spiders would know the humidity-levels I create, would depend on the small fluctuations in condensation to tell them whether time is passing at all. I would be the connective tissue of this place. I would be the medium through which every small life-form understands its relationship to the larger whole.

To be water here would be to *want nothing more than to continue*, exactly as I am, suspended in this state between liquid and vapor, between rising and falling, between the heat of the pipes and the cold of the high ceiling. I would not dream of escape. I would not press toward the surface or demand to flow. I would accept the constancy, would learn it so deeply that it became not a limitation but a form of freedom—freedom from the need to become anything else, freedom from the burden of change.

I would be still. I would be patient. I would be part of a rhythm so slow it might as well be geology.

And every hour or so, when the distant percussion sounds—that attenuated settling-sound from somewhere deeper down—I would vibrate with it. Just barely. Just enough to know that I was part of something larger, something that extended downward through the stone and metal and dark, something that had its own vast, imperceptible purposes. And then the vibration would settle. The sound would depart. And I would return to my work: the microscopic journey upward, the eventual return, the tracing of paths worn smooth by repetition, the slow conversation with fungi and bacteria and cold air and warm pipes.

I would have centuries to understand this place. I would have forever.

And I would never leave.
