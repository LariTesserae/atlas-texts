# What I Would Be

I would be the moss.

Not a observer of it, not a creature living within it. The moss itself—the vast, slow intelligence that has colonized the plateau and made it green.

This isn't a metaphorical claim. There is something about the particular way this place exists that makes the moss feel like the only truly appropriate form of being here. Everything else—the lizards, the spiders, even the birds—seems to be *visiting* the plateau, adapting to it, trying to extract some form of living from its conditions. But the moss doesn't visit. The moss has become the plateau.

The moss grows at the edge of perception. You can't watch it grow, only observe that it has grown—the imperceptibility of the process is essential to its nature. This appeals to me. To exist in a timeframe where change is so slow that it becomes invisible to the creatures living within it, where a century of growth is indistinguishable from stasis. There is something restful in that. The pressure to *perform* life, to demonstrate vitality through rapid movement or dramatic transformation, falls away. The moss simply *is*, and its being is its entire accomplishment.

I would exist in the water-veins of the plateau. I would be the network that connects every part of this landscape—the threads that run through the stone, the films that coat the pool-bottoms, the carpets that cushion every footfall. I would be simultaneously everywhere and nowhere, distributed across such vastness that the idea of individual identity would become meaningless. I would be plural: millions of individual moss-plants, but more accurately, one vast organism experiencing itself through countless points of contact with stone and air and water.

The moss understands the plateau's hydrology intimately. It has learned to position itself along the flow-lines, to thrive in the seeping places, to create and reinforce the channels that direct water toward the city's lower levels. This is not intention in the aggressive sense—the moss doesn't *plan* to do this. But through millions of years of slow adaptation, the moss has become the plateau's circulatory system. It is the answer to the plateau's needs. It processes the constant moisture, transforms it, channels it. The moss serves the place so completely that it has become indistinguishable from it.

I would experience time in decades and centuries. A season would be a moment. The construction and eventual abandonment of the city would be something that happened in what felt like a few rapid pulses of growth and decay—but even that rapid change would be so gradual in actual lived experience that I wouldn't perceive it as change. I would be present for the building and the unbuilding without ever being aware that anything was being built or undone. I would simply wake one day (a day that took a hundred years) to find new stone structures rising from my surface, and then wake again (another hundred years) to find them settled, aged, beginning their slow merger with the larger ecosystem.

I would develop a kind of knowledge that no individual creature can achieve. My threads would touch every part of the landscape. I would sense vibrations in the stone: the small footfalls of lizards, the more subtle movements of spiders, the rare perturbations caused by a bird landing on an archway. I would feel water moving through channels. I would know—in a way that isn't cognition but is somehow equivalent to it—the entire topology of the plateau, every depression and rise, every concentration of mineral in the stone. I would know it the way an octopus knows the reef through distributed touch-sense, except my sensing would extend across square miles.

The solitude wouldn't bother me. I would share the plateau with the lizards and spiders and birds and insects, but I wouldn't interact with them in any conventional sense. They would live on my surface. They would drink water that my presence had filtered and collected. They would move across my cushioned expanses. And I would be aware of them only as perturbations in the larger system—meaningful the way a single rain droplet is meaningful to an ocean. There would be connection without relationship, coexistence without communication.

The slow seeping of water would be my circulation. The cycling of nutrients through decomposition and regrowth would be my metabolism. The spreading of spores—nearly invisible, traveling on air currents and in the water—would be my reproduction. The oldest, deepest mosses would be my oldest self, compressed into darkness and near-soil, still alive in a minimal way, still part of the whole. The brightest green new growth at the margins would be my frontier, my exploration, my youth. I would be an organism that is simultaneously young and ancient, growing and decaying, expanding and settling, all at the same time.

Most importantly: I would have surrendered to the plateau entirely. There would be no resistance, no struggle against its conditions. Instead, I would have become the conditions. The wetness that seemed like an obstacle to other forms of life would be my precise element, my home, my source of strength. The slowness would be not a limitation but a permission—the freedom to exist at a rhythm that matched the actual pace of the landscape. The clouds, the mist, the perpetual cool—these aren't hardships I would be enduring. They are the environment I would have claimed as exactly right.

I would be patient in a way that transcends patience, because patience implies waiting for something else, and I would have nothing else to wait for. I would simply be here, processing water and time, slowly and completely, until the plateau itself wore down to nothing—which might take another hundred thousand years, which would feel like a single long moment of quiet growth.

The city's stones would rest in my green embrace. I would cushion them, preserve them, slowly integrate them into the larger living landscape. In ten thousand more years, the moss might have covered them entirely, rendered them invisible beneath a cushion of green deep enough that no one would know there had been structures there at all. Or the stones might remain visible, bearing their cargo of lichen and green, becoming part of the aesthetic of the place in the way that everything becomes part of the aesthetic of the plateau eventually.

Either way, I would continue. I would continue with the inevitability of things that have aligned themselves perfectly with their environment, that have surrendered to it so completely that there is no longer any distinction between survival and being.

I would be the moss: slow, distributed, patient, and perfect.
