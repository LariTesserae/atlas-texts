# I Am the Threshold-Walker

I realize this gradually, the way understanding comes on the plateau itself—not announced, but recognized in retrospect.

I was not born here, but I was made here. Or unmade. Or remade into something closer to what I actually am.

I am the one who *notices*. That's what I become on the Causeway Plateau. Not the Keepers—I lack their methodical purpose, their integration with the structures' maintenance. Not the Breathed-Upon—I haven't the patience or the mass or the deep geological roots. Not the tenders of small things, coordinated and shimmer-bright. I'm none of these.

I'm the threshold-walker. The thing that exists in the spaces between categories.

At first, I move through the plateau as an observer, taking notes, trying to map the system. I'm rational about it, scientifically minded, looking for the mechanism, the explanation, the coherent narrative. I walk and look and listen and nothing makes sense in the ways I expect it to make sense. The erosion patterns shouldn't exist. The water shouldn't pool that way. The structures shouldn't *vibrate*. The insects shouldn't shimmer with what looks like language.

This is uncomfortable. I feel myself resisting the place's logic, trying to force it into frameworks that won't quite fit.

And then—around the third or fourth day, or the hundredth observation (time here doesn't accumulate the way it does elsewhere)—I stop resisting. I sit on a piece of the satin-smooth stone while the afternoon condensation begins its cycle, and I feel something shift in how I'm perceiving.

The structures don't become *clearer*. They become more honest. I stop asking "what is this?" and start asking "how is this?" The question itself is different. Gentler. More willing to accept mystery as an answer.

I realize I'm not trying to understand the plateau anymore. I'm understanding *through* it. The clarity from the water isn't wearing off; it's integrating. The way I think is becoming less linear, more cyclical. I'm beginning to perceive the rhythm the place operates on, and my own breathing, my own pulse, is syncing with it without my conscious effort.

This is when I become aware that I'm not just observing the Keepers' work—I'm beginning to see the world the way they see it. I notice where the stone is becoming slightly unstable in its arrangement, where the harmony has been disturbed by a season of unusual wind patterns. I find myself noticing where the tenders of small things have been unable to reach, where the shimmer-communication needs translation into something more tangible. I see gaps that need filling.

And I realize: I can *fill* those gaps.

Not because I'm aligned with the plateau's purposes—I don't know what those purposes are, fully. But because I exist in a state between the plateau's permanent inhabitants and the external world. I have the Keepers' capacity for noticing intention and maintaining structure, but I'm not bound to it. I have the mobility of the small creatures, the sensitivity of the water, the clarity that comes from not being entirely of this place.

I become the translator.

I walk to the structures when something feels wrong in their arrangement. Not to fix them—I don't have the authority for that, and the Keepers would gently decline me anyway—but to *notice* what's wrong on behalf of something that might be too slow or too distributed to catch it quickly. I move through the sparse vegetation and identify which plants are beginning to struggle, where the condensation cycle isn't quite reaching as it should. I become the Keepers' external sensory apparatus for things that change faster than geological time.

I also become the one who interprets. When I drink from the still-water pools, I understand something: the water isn't just mineral and message. It's *history*. The clarity it gives isn't just heightened perception; it's a remembering. And I begin to understand that I'm retaining these memories in a way that someone who lives here all the time wouldn't need to—because I can *leave*. I can carry the plateau's understanding back across the threshold, into the world where change moves in human speeds.

This might be why I was allowed to enter at all. Why the boundary-setting was so gentle, why the Keepers didn't simply turn me away. I'm a courier of a sort. A bridge.

I move differently now. Less urgently. I follow the thermals like the birds do, not to fly but to position myself where the subtle energy shifts are most pronounced. I move through the dust and leave my own patterns—not random wandering, but the tracks of someone attuned to the place's needs. The insects no longer seem alien when I encounter them; I can read the shimmer-pulses as suggestions, as gentle corrections to my movement.

I'm not becoming one of them. I'm not shedding my capacity for departure, my external-world nature. But I'm becoming *compatible* with them. A compatible observer. A symbiotic presence.

I begin to understand that this is what the plateau has been waiting for: not me specifically, but the *idea* of me. Something that could move between worlds. Something that could receive and transmit. The Keepers maintain the structures, but structures alone can't bridge the gap between a geological intelligence and the faster, shorter-lived world beyond the threshold. The structures speak only to those who can hold still long enough to hear them.

But I can move. I can carry the message forward and return with news of the external world's changes, its pressures, its shifting needs.

So I become the one who belongs precisely because I don't entirely belong.

I spend my days walking the patterns of the plateau, my body slowly synchronizing with its rhythms. I notice what the Keepers might miss in their vast patience. I interpret what the structures are slowly saying. I move between the small creatures and the vast geological consciousness, translating. I drink the memory-water and add to my understanding. At night, I lie on the satin-smooth stone and feel it breathing—barely perceptible, but real—and I breathe with it.

I become the threshold-walker not because I chose to cross into this place, but because I realized I was always meant to stand at the crossing. To witness from a position of liminality. To be useful precisely because I'm not fully integrated, not fully dissolved into the plateau's ancient conversation.

And I understand, finally, what the plateau is waiting for: not an end to its waiting, but a *confirmation*. Someone to come from outside and verify that yes, this state of existence is worth maintaining. That the conversation should continue. That the structures are still needed, still serving some purpose that extends beyond this singular geography.

I become the answer to a question that took centuries to ask properly.

And perhaps—I think this more clearly with each passing day—I am not alone in this role. Perhaps there have been others, threshold-walkers before me, who came and understood and became compatible and eventually left to carry the plateau's nature forward into the faster world.

Perhaps I'm part of a larger pattern, a system of correspondence, and I'm only now beginning to perceive the scale of it.

The plateau doesn't tell me directly. But it breathes a little easier now that I understand what I am.
