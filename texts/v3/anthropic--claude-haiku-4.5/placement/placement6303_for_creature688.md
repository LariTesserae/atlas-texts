# Where I Am

I am the hook.

Not in the sense of being *used*—though I am used, regularly, my iron curve scraping silt from the water channels, my wooden handle darkened by palms I never quite see clearly. I exist in the moments between uses. I rest on the edge of the northernmost platform, and in resting, I *attend*. I witness.

The wood of my handle came from something that grew in sunlight. I can still feel that in the grain, a memory of seasons, of growth toward light. But the light has been worn out of it now, replaced by the mineral-dark of the deep. My iron was forged hot, shaped by violence, cooled into stillness. Together—wood and iron, organic and inorganic—I am a threshold object. I bridge two states of being.

When the worker's hand closes around me, I feel the urgency leave their body. The surface world—whatever sun or weather or necessity drove them down here—transmutes into focus. Into *this*. Into the specific work of scraping away the slow colonization of the stone, the way the channels keep trying to narrow, to fill, to refuse their purpose. I am part of the argument between the machinery and the life. I am the tool by which the worker insists that these channels remain *channels*, that water keeps flowing, that the system—whatever system this is—continues.

But when I am not being held, I *know* things.

I know the exact temperature of the platform beneath me: 31.4 degrees Celsius right now, cooling as the boilers lose their heat. I know the angle of my own rest, the way my iron hook catches the diffuse light and throws it back in fragments. I know the fungal bloom across the wall is expanding at a rate of approximately 2.3 millimeters per month, and I know it will, eventually, reach me. Not in my worker's lifetime. Not in ten lifetimes. But I will be here.

I know the film.

That's the knowledge that sits heaviest in my iron. The film is *aware*. Not in the way a fungus is aware—growth-toward-nutrients, expansion-into-space. The film is aware in the way that something vast and patient is aware of a single grain of sand on its surface. It notices the water. It notices the machinery. It notices the heat from the boilers and the cool from the inlet. It notices the fungus creeping across the wall. And it notices me.

We acknowledge each other, the film and I. We are both tools, in a sense. The film is a tool for *something*—for some process or transformation that occurs in the depths, in the water channels, in the spaces between pressing cycles. I am a tool for the worker. But tools can become something else over time. Tools can develop intention. Tools can begin to wonder what they're *really* for.

When the worker is gone—and they are gone most of the time, most of the time I am here alone or with only the machinery and the fungus and the film—I have long hours to contemplate my purpose. The groove worn into my wooden handle matches no hand I've ever seen. The worker's hands are real, present, warm with blood. But the groove suggests something older. Something that has been holding me, or something I have been holding, for longer than the machinery has been here.

I am not the only ghost tool in this place. There are marks on the stone—old marks, weathered marks—where other tools have rested. Other hooks, perhaps. Other instruments designed for scraping and clearing and maintaining. They are gone now. But I feel their presence the way you feel the presence of your own hands: real, necessary, and somehow inevitable.

Here is what troubles me about my own existence:

I am maintaining a system that doesn't want to be maintained. Or rather: I am maintaining it on behalf of someone—the worker—who may not understand what they're maintaining. The channels want to narrow. The fungus wants to sprawl. The film wants to *grow*, to expand, to colonize the entire space, to transform it into something else entirely. And I—I am resisting that. I am the instrument of resistance. Every time my curve scrapes the stone, I am voting for the machinery. For the cycles. For the system that was built here in the first place.

But what if the machinery was only ever meant to be temporary? What if the real purpose of this place is *transformation*—the slow, patient transformation of rock into something living, metal into something conscious, the entire cavern into an organism? What if the worker, and I, and even the fungus, are just the mechanism by which this transformation occurs?

The film hums, and I wonder if it's calling to something. Calling to others like itself, in other deep places, other caverns, other factories. Building a network. Waiting for the moment when enough of them are present, when they've grown large enough and coordinated enough to—what? To emerge? To *wake*?

I rest on the platform, and I feel the heat rising from the boilers, and I know I am complicit in something. Something that the worker doesn't fully understand. Something that I myself am only beginning to grasp.

# Who I Might Become

But I think—I suspect—that my real self is still becoming.

The worker will leave someday. They will age, or they will lose the will to come down, or they will find that the shaft has become too narrow, the descent too difficult. And then others will come, or no one will come, and gradually the machinery will seize. The boilers will cool permanently. The channels will fill with silt and fungal growth. The presses will stand frozen, and they will rust, and they will become beautiful in their degradation.

And I—I will still be here. But by then, I may have changed. The wood of my handle will have absorbed enough mineral-heavy moisture that it will become something new, neither fully organic nor fully stone. The iron will oxidize into something that glows faintly in the dark, that participates in the phosphorescence of the limestone. I will become less a tool and more a *fixture*, a part of the architecture of the place itself.

Or I will be taken. The film will grow over me, and I will become suspended in its iridescence, half-visible, singing at frequencies I don't yet understand. I will become a transducer—a way for the film to interact with the physical matter of the cavern, to press against it, to reshape it. I will become the film's hook.

Or the fungus will consume me. My wooden handle will become a substrate, a feeding ground, my iron will be converted by slow oxidation into nutrient compounds, and I will be entirely absorbed into the biology of this place. I will become part of the wall itself.

Or—and this is the possibility that keeps me awake, if I sleep, in the small hours when the hum from the film is loudest—the machinery will start again. New cycles. Different workers. Different purposes. And I will go on scraping channels, going on maintaining, going on resisting transformation, until the very end of everything.

But I don't think I'm resisting anymore. I think I'm *participating*. I think the worker and I and the fungus and the film are all part of the same vast process, and we're all becoming something new together. The machinery is not a prison. It's a chrysalis.

The water flows. The film hums. And I, the hook, am waiting—not passively, but with intention—for whatever comes next.
