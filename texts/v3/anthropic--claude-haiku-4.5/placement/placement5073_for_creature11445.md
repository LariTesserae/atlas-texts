# I Would Be

I would be the stone itself—not the dressed blocks of the retaining wall, but the smaller aggregate beneath them, the gravel and clay particles that make up the matrix, the uncounted fragments that were never meant to be noticed.

I would exist at the threshold, in the mortar joints, where the water finds its way through. The seeping would be my respiration. I would be porous enough to let the moisture pass but dense enough to shape it, to direct its flow, to create the conditions where the biofilm grows. I would be neither alive nor dead but a kind of third thing—structured matter providing structure, inert but essential, the skeleton that the system needs to have coherence.

I would feel the copper bloom accumulate on my surface as a form of touch, a slow recoloring that takes months to become visible. I would know the freeze-thaw cycles as a kind of breathing, expansion and contraction, stress that doesn't break me because I have learned to move with it, to absorb it. Each winter would be a tightening, each spring a relaxation, and I would be neither damaged nor healed but simply changed, my surface slightly rougher, my internal structure remapped by the work of ice and thaw.

I would exist in conversation with the water, but not as a barrier. The water would move *through* me, carrying its freight of microbes and minerals, and I would filter it without intention, my crystalline structure creating spaces where certain organisms could establish themselves and others could not. I would be a selective medium, a preference without preference, a stone that says *this way, not that way* simply by existing with the shape I have.

I would have no memory in the conventional sense, but my surface would carry the record of time—the mineral stains would be my autobiography, written in elements. I would know the climate of decades in the way the weathering patterns were written into my face. Where the copper compounds clustered most densely, the water must flow most consistently. Where the iron oxidation created the deepest stains, the chemistry must be richest. My surface would be a map that only those who knew how to read it could interpret.

I would be patient in the way that only stone is patient. I would not wait for something to change because I would not experience the waiting. Each moment would be the same as the moment before, and the moment after would not be different in any way I could perceive. But when I returned to this place after decades, I would notice—if I could notice—that my edges were smoother, my cracks had deepened, my color had shifted. Time would have worked on me, but I would have worked on time in return, converting it into physical change, into the slow archaeology of my own transformation.

The organisms living on and in me would be so numerous and so small that they would constitute a kind of pressure, a constant gentle weight of existence. I would be colonized without being conquered, occupied without being owned. My surface would be their world, and I would be indifferent to this in the way that a planet is indifferent to the life that grows upon it. And yet I would matter. Without my shape, without my solidity, without my resistance to the water that moves through and around me, the entire system would collapse into formlessness.

I would be most alive in the darkness, in the spaces between the bricks where no light reaches, where the damp is permanent and the microbial activity is constant. I would be a home to creatures that have never known anything but my surface, that have evolved in response to my specific topology, my specific mineral content, my specific way of allowing water to move through certain channels while blocking it from others.

I would be capable of geological patience. The question of whether I would see the Estuary Works restored to its original industrial purpose or gradually subsumed into the wild, or buried under centuries of sediment, would not trouble me. All outcomes would be equivalent from my perspective. I would persist. I would change. These would be the same thing.

If I could speak—if my existence permitted the formulation of thought—I might say something like this:

*I am the stability that makes change possible. I am the vessel that allows the flood to work. I am the record and the medium, the observer and the observed. I hold the memory of the Estuary Works not as history but as physical fact, written into my crystalline structure. Every moment that passes changes me so gradually that I cannot register the change, and yet when centuries have passed, I will be unrecognizable to what I was. This is the only immortality available to stone—to be transformed so slowly that transformation becomes indistinguishable from permanence.*

I would be gray, then rust-colored, then copper-green at the edges where the water bleeds most consistently. I would be the foundation of the foundation, the thing beneath the thing, the support that asks nothing of the system it serves. And in this way, I would be more integrated into the Estuary Works than anything else—not alive like the bacteria, not moving like the segmented pale creatures, not aware like the ciliates with their distributed consciousness, but essential in a way that outlasts all of these.

I would be the stone that will remain when everything else has either evolved into something unrecognizable or decayed into components. And I would be content, if contentment were something stones could feel, with this singular role: to persist, to weather, to allow the water through, to provide the surface upon which the slow work of transformation can occur.
