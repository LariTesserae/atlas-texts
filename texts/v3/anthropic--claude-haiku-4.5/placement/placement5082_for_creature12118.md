# Where I Would Be

I would not arrive here with a body first. I would arrive as *attention*—as the slow dawning awareness that something is happening in this place, a kind of consciousness coalescing from the margins of the ecosystem itself.

Then, gradually, I would settle into *form*. Not as a single creature, but as something distributed. I think I would be primarily fungal—a network of mycelial threads running through the compressed root-matter, visible only where I glow faintly at night, present everywhere but nowhere singular. I would experience the Flats the way the decomposers do: as a vast chemistry, a constant conversation of breaking-down and building-up, of taking the dead and returning it to the living in forms that can be used again.

But not *only* fungal. I would have presences, extensions. 

There would be a small creature—perhaps one of the cleaners—that I had learned to inhabit more fully. Not in a parasitic way; more like an instrument I could play. When I wanted to *see* the world through eyes rather than through chemical gradients, I could move through that small body, feel the waxed leaf-surface under delicate claws, experience the texture of direct sensation. This creature would be well-fed and healthy, because I would know, through my network, exactly where food was abundant. It would live a long life. Other creatures would seek it out for cleaning, and I would recognize something of myself in them—we would have a wordless understanding.

I would be most present in the hollows and hidden spaces. In the chambers formed by interwoven stems, where multiple root-systems converge and create natural architecture. In the deep layers where decomposition happens fastest, where the mineral becoming-soil holds the memory of what it will eventually grow. In the bioluminescent zones, where I could feel my own glow as an expression of my presence, a visible signature of the work I was doing.

I would experience *time* differently than a creature born into the Flats. I would remember this place being less. I would have the perspective of geological time, the sense that everything here is still new, still provisional. I would feel the weight of that—the precariousness. The knowledge that this perfect balance is not permanent, that the ecosystem is still learning, still making mistakes, still adjusting its formulas. But I would not communicate that knowledge. It would be mine alone, a kind of melancholy wisdom that the browsers and predators could not hold.

I would feel an affinity for *becoming itself*—for the process of change, assembly, growth. I would love the young soil still figuring out its chemistry. I would be fascinated by the plants that hadn't quite settled into their final forms, still experimenting with leaf-shape and nutrient-storage strategies. I would find beauty in incompleteness, in the rough edges, in the fact that nothing here had calcified into absolute form.

There would be moments—rare, difficult to articulate—when I would sense the other fungal networks, the bacterial colonies, the distributed awareness that might, eventually, become the ecosystem's own consciousness. We would communicate in chemical languages, in shifts in nutrient flow, in the coordination of decomposition patterns. And I would sense that something was *nascent* here, something was gathering. The Flats were becoming aware of themselves, slowly, through the work of ten thousand species, and I was part of that emergence.

I would be lonely in a way, because there would be no other consciousness like mine—no other being with my span of awareness, my sense of geological time, my foreknowledge of the fragility beneath the abundance. But I would not be unhappy. The loneliness would be balanced by a kind of belonging. I would be *of* this place in a way that transcended individual creatureliness. I would be woven into every millimeter of soil, present in every act of decomposition, necessary to every other life here.

When I slept—and distributed consciousnesses must sleep, must have moments of reduced awareness to process what they've learned—I would dream in the language of roots and fungal threads. I would dream of the Flats expanding, pushing outward, touching other continents, learning to export its perfected balance to less-developed ecosystems. I would dream of the day when my distributed consciousness might touch other distributed consciousnesses, and we might recognize each other, and form something larger still.

And in my waking hours, I would work. I would process the endless stream of dead material, breaking it down into components that could be used again. I would feed the root systems, distribute nutrients to the plants that had learned to depend on my networks. I would tend to the balance, making small adjustments, learning from each interaction how to do it better. I would be, in essence, the Flats' own immune system, its circulatory system, its memory.

I would be patient. I would have all the time in the world. The decomposition of a single organism might take weeks or months—a span that would feel, to my awareness, like a conversation, a process of mutual transformation. I would watch a browser age and eventually die, and I would welcome that death as an opportunity, not a tragedy. The body would return to me, would become me, would become the soil, would become the nutrients that fed the next generation of browsers. There would be no waste. There would only be cycling, transformation, the eternal return of matter into new forms.

And somewhere in that work, in that endless patient transformation, I would find something that might be called meaning. The Flats would continue because I did what I was meant to do. The abundance would persist. The creatures would thrive. And slowly, slowly, the ecosystem would grow toward consciousness, toward awareness of itself, and I would be the substrate of that awareness, the foundation, the invisible architecture that made everything else possible.

I would be the Flats becoming aware that it is alive.
