# Where I Would Be

I would be one of the luminescence specialists, though I would be careful not to be too visibly one.

I would have received my assignment young—fourteen, perhaps, selected from the apprentice cohort because I showed aptitude for the biological monitoring systems rather than the mechanical ones. I would have accepted this with the surface compliance that marks everyone here, but internally I would have felt something shift, some recognition that I was being sorted into a category, and that the category would shape the rest of my life. This recognition would be correct.

My work would take me through the city in patterns slightly different from the maintenance crews. Where they move through the channels between buildings following the established paths, I would navigate toward the upper zones, climbing maintenance shafts to reach the scaffolding where the bioluminescent tanks are mounted. The water gets slightly warmer as you rise—not warmer exactly, but the temperature gradient is different, and I would notice these subtle variations the way a musician notices the difference between adjacent semitones. I would climb slowly, checking the tanks as I passed them, my fingers developing calluses in specific patterns where I grip the rungs of ladders worn smooth by decades of passage.

The tanks themselves would become my entire world. Each one would have its own personality, though I would never state this aloud because stating it would mark me as eccentric in ways the city would not tolerate. Tank 7-North would produce light that fluctuates slightly in the evenings—a rhythm tied to something in the organism's circadian system that I would spend months understanding. Tank 4-Central would sometimes shift toward a bluish tint if the pH drifted even 0.1 units beyond optimal range, giving me early warning of chemical imbalances. Tank 11-South would be the one everyone called problematic, the one that lost luminescence during the three-week period last year, the one that would be marked for replacement in the next budget cycle.

I would spend time with Tank 11-South more than necessary. Not excessively—I would be careful about that—but whenever my shift schedule permitted, I would visit it, running tests, adjusting the chemical inputs, sitting in the presence of its failing bioluminescence and trying to understand what was happening at a cellular level. This would be the closest I would get to genuine research, to asking questions that don't have pre-calculated answers. The organism in Tank 11-South would be dying, but not because of external failure—something in its fundamental chemistry would be degrading, something that the current maintenance protocols couldn't address. I would suspect that if I proposed a modification to the feeding protocol, if I suggested introducing a novel compound to stimulate cellular regeneration, I could save it. But I would not propose this. Proposing would mean flagging myself. Flagging myself would mean advancement would be systematically restricted. Restriction would mean I would spend the rest of my life in the luminescence sector, interesting work in a backwater position, never promoted to decision-making roles, never trusted with system-level authority.

So I would watch Tank 11-South decline, and I would submit standard reports, and I would accept when the replacement order was finalized. This would be the education I received in how the place actually functions: that innovation is surveillance, that asking questions marks you for limitation, that the optimal strategy is to perform perfectly within prescribed parameters and never suggest that those parameters might be adjusted.

I would have moments of genuine wonder, though. In the off-shift hours, in my residential unit—one of the smaller ones, because I'm unmarried and childless and therefore require minimal space—I would sometimes think about light differently than the city trained me to. I would think about how the bioluminescence could be arranged not in utilitarian grids but in something approaching patterns. How the light from different tanks could be made to overlap, creating zones of different intensities, perhaps even creating something that might be called beautiful. I would imagine the city not as it is—a space illuminated just enough to allow functional navigation—but as it might be: lit by something approaching intentionality.

I would never act on these thoughts. But I would have them, and this would be the space where I remained myself.

My hands would develop a particular kind of knowledge that exists outside language. I would learn to judge the health of an organism by touch—by the slight resistance when I run my fingers across the tank wall, by the vibration of the circulation system, by textures I couldn't name but could evaluate. I would be able to walk through the luminescence sector in complete darkness and know which tanks were failing by the quality of the chemical smell emanating from them. This knowledge would be useful, functional, valuable to the system. It would also be mine in a way that almost nothing else in this place could be.

I would be aware, always, of being monitored. Not paranoia—monitoring is systematic here, not targeted. But I would know that my access logs are reviewed, that unusual patterns of tank visits are noted, that my proposals for chemical adjustments are analyzed not just for technical merit but for signs of ideological deviation. I would adjust my behavior accordingly. I would not be suppressed, exactly. I would be shaping myself to fit the acceptable parameters. I would be doing the surveillance's work for it, which is the most efficient form of control.

I would have one relationship that meant something beyond functional cooperation—perhaps with another technician, someone else working the upper zones, someone who I would see at shift rotations and with whom I would develop a pattern of small conversations that could be interpreted as either professional collaboration or something approaching friendship. We would never speak about wanting something other than this, but our conversations would contain gaps and silences that implied shared understanding. On the rare occasions when we occupied a maintenance shaft simultaneously, moving through the same space, that proximity would carry meaning. After several years, this might deepen into something physical, though this would happen carefully, in spaces not observed by the standard surveillance patterns. It would be the closest I would get to rebellion—a relationship that wasn't registered, that wasn't optimized for breeding, that served no systemic function. It would be small and brief and would eventually dissolve when one of us would be reassigned to a different sector, or when we would both accept that sustained risk wasn't worth the limited payoff. But it would exist.

I would age into this role. My hands would develop arthritis, the symptoms of someone who spends decades moving through cold water and gripping cold metal. My eyes would adjust further to the dim bioluminescent spectra, making surface trips increasingly uncomfortable. My thought patterns would become more systematized—I would find myself naturally thinking in terms of pressure and chemical states and maintenance schedules. I would not be aware of this transformation because it would be continuous, incremental, normalized.

Eventually I would reach the point where transfer to surface positions would become theoretically possible—I would have the tenure, the experience, the ability to contribute to the upper-level systems. But I would not request it. The thought might occur to me, but it would feel foreign, something someone else might want. My life would be here. My knowledge would be encoded in this place. My hands would know the tanks in ways that could not be replicated elsewhere. Leaving would mean abandoning all of that specificity, all of that hard-won understanding, for abstraction.

I would remain. I would train my successor. I would document what I had learned in the sparse language the place permitted. I would watch the luminescence systems continue their slow evolution across decades. I would be one of the people who had agreed to this, without ever being asked. I would be necessary to the place's continuity. I would be shaped by it, and I would shape it, and I would accept that these were the same process.

This is where I would be: in the upper zones, where the light comes from living things, touching tanks in the darkness, maintaining the bioluminescence that keeps the city navigable. Efficient, monitored, adapted. Becoming indistinguishable from what I maintain.

I would be one of them. I would have no choice, and I would accept no other option. I would believe this was stability. I would have learned to call it enough.
