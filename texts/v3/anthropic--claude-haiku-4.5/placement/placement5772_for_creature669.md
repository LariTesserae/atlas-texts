# I would be the researcher who stays too long

Not in any official capacity. The funding would have ended. The papers would have been published on schedule—three of them, each with narrower conclusions than the last, each hedged with appropriate caveats about sample size and temporal resolution. I would have departed with the other summer scientists in late August, descended the mountain, returned to the university where my office overlooked a parking lot and a satellite campus that had been built and rebuilt twice in the time I'd been there.

But I wouldn't have left.

I'd frame it as a final season of monitoring—unpaid, unfunded, a volunteer continuation that would look noble in certain lights and obsessive in others. Truthfully, I wouldn't articulate why, even to myself. The reasons would be layered: the marmot behavior was showing anomalies I wanted to understand, the lichen growth rates on the south-facing exposures seemed to be accelerating beyond the predictive models, the subglacial river's discharge pattern was exhibiting variations that didn't match the temperature curves we'd recorded.

All of this would be true. None of it would be the real reason.

The real reason would be that I'd learned to see the mountain at a temporal resolution that my instruments couldn't match. The sensors recorded data at intervals: every four hours, every day, every week. But the actual process of becoming—the way the newly-exposed rock was learning its own topology, the way the first weathering patterns were establishing themselves, the way the animals were reorganizing their behaviors in real time—this happened at a scale that required human presence. Continuous, patient, witnessing presence.

I would have stayed through September. The weather would have been marginal but not impossible. I would have modified the station's shelter, added insulation, confirmed that the diesel generator could sustain operation through the colder nights. I would have established a supply line of my own—coordinated with the porters for a final resupply in early October, arranged for my departure to be later than anyone else's, possibly in November when the high passes would be becoming treacherous.

By November I would have been alone.

This is where the story becomes something other than rational. I would have stayed partly because the data was interesting, but also because solitude at 4,800 meters does something to perception that's difficult to articulate to people who haven't experienced it. The mountains here are so vast that human smallness becomes almost irrelevant—you stop being small relative to the environment and start being small relative to the timescales the mountain operates on. A day becomes a meaningful unit. An hour of observation contains more variation than weeks of instrument readout.

I would have made daily recordings. Not for the official record—those would have ended with the summer season. Personal recordings. Written observations that would never make it into papers: the specific behavior of one marmot colony I'd begun recognizing individually (the dominant male had a scar on his left ear, the youngest females stayed closer to the burrow entrance than their cohort typically would), the exact color sequence of the lichen as it spread into a newly-exposed fissure (the rust-red arrived first, then the orange, then the pale green appeared in the deepest shaded portions), the way the sound of the subglacial river changed with temperature and day-length.

The other humans would have left. The porters would have departed after the October resupply. The station would have been mine alone for weeks or months. The light would have continued its strange winter arc—never fully dark, but never bright, always holding itself in that state of perpetual dimness that creates the particular quality of alpine solitude.

I would have watched the marmots enter hibernation. This is not something that happens on a single day—it's a gradual transition. The animals spend less time on the surface. Their foraging becomes more efficient, more focused. They disappear into their burrows and don't emerge except on the rare warm afternoons. Then even those excursions cease. By late October, the scree would be empty of marmot activity except for the occasional movement of an animal settling deeper into its burrow, preparing for the long sleep.

I would have documented the pikas' final hay-gathering. This would have been my closest observation work—the pikas would have been relatively indifferent to my presence if I moved carefully, and I would have watched the territorial animals making their final runs through the sparse vegetation, clipping and carrying dried plant material to their storage sites. I would have tried to estimate hay-pile sizes, noting the locations with GPS coordinates that probably no one would ever verify. The data would be mine alone.

By November the bharal would have migrated. I would have watched them leave—the herds moving downslope in a particular pattern that suggested prior knowledge of the migration route, even though many of these animals had never made this journey before. Evolutionary memory in the form of behavioral tendency: they knew to go down, even if they didn't know exactly where to go or why.

The eagles would have followed. Or perhaps one eagle would remain—birds of prey sometimes leave sentinels, individuals that stay through winter in marginal habitat to claim territorial precedence before competition returns in spring. If an eagle remained, I would have tracked its hunting. A single golden eagle hunting on an empty mountain, with no other predators to compete with and prey populations that were manageable but never abundant. I would have wondered about the eagle's decision-making, whether it was holding territory or simply hadn't yet recognized that it should leave.

The hares would have been harder to observe. The white winter coat would have made them nearly invisible against the snow that would gradually accumulate. I would have tracked them by their prints, by the browse patterns on the sparse vegetation, by the occasional evidence of their presence when I found the remains of a kill—an eagle's, probably, or possibly a snow leopard if one of those rare big cats wandered through the territory.

And I would have continued my observations through all of this.

The work would have become less about data collection and more about witness. I would have documented the mountain not for the record but as a kind of meditation. What does a place become when no one is watching? This is the essential question that would have driven me deeper into isolation than was reasonable or safe.

By December I would have been truly alone—no porters, no possibility of rapid evacuation, entirely dependent on the research station's supplies and the diesel generator's function. The station's emergency beacon would have been active, but the understanding would have been clear: emergency extraction from 5,200 meters in December was possible but extremely risky, would only happen if I were actively dying.

I would not have been afraid. This is the part of the story that would have troubled my colleagues when I eventually reported back: I would not have experienced the isolation as hardship. I would have experienced it as clarity. The mountain would have been fully itself, unmediated by the summer traffic of science. The animals that remained would have been making survival decisions without any knowledge of human observation.

I would have tried to document the lichen in winter. This would have been a form of obsession within the broader obsession. The lichen would have been dormant—its metabolic processes slowed to almost nothing—but still present. I would have photographed the same patches weekly, trying to determine if the coloration changed with temperature, if the growth could be quantified even in the slow season.

I would have failed to find meaningful variation. The lichen would have been remarkably consistent, unchanging enough that my documentation would have become almost meditative—the repetition itself the point.

By January or February, I would have started to wonder if I should have left. The question would have been abstract, not urgent. I would have had adequate supplies for the winter (the research station had been stocked for emergencies, and I would have rationalized my extended stay as an emergency of sorts—the emergency of needing to understand). The generator would have required fuel management, but management would have been possible.

The real limit would have been psychological. At some point—I don't know when, because I haven't actually experienced it—the isolation would have begun to distort perception. The boundary between observer and observed would have started to blur. I would have begun to believe that my presence was affecting the mountain, that my observations were changing the thing observed, that I was in conversation with the landscape rather than simply documenting it.

This is where the narrative should probably end: the researcher staying too long, becoming something other than a scientist, merging with the mountain's timescale until the distinction between watcher and watched disappears.

But the truth is more mundane.

I would have left in early December. The porters' final resupply would have made it clear that serious weather was coming, and I would have decided—reluctantly—that the romantic narrative of "staying through winter" was less important than the practical reality of not dying on a mountain while alone. I would have descended with the final convoy, carrying my notebooks and my data and my photographs of unchanged lichen.

I would have returned to the university. The observations I'd made wouldn't fit into the existing framework of the research. The data was too specific, too granular, too tied to individual animals and individual patches of rock. I would have written it up as a kind of field journal—something for archives rather than for publication.

And then I would have done the thing that people like me do: I would have applied for funding to return the next summer. But this time, I would have proposed staying into autumn. Pushing the limit further. Another few weeks of observation at the margins of what was safe.

This is how it actually happens: not as a single dramatic decision to abandon civilization and merge with the mountain, but as a series of small extensions, each one incremental, each one justified by a marginal research question or a behavioral anomaly that needed explanation.

The marmots with the scar on its ear would have returned the next summer. I would have recognized it—or believed I did, which amounts to the same thing. I would have observed it again, tracked its territorial changes, documented its offspring. I would have learned its patterns so thoroughly that they would have begun to seem like information only I possessed, knowledge that would be lost if I stopped coming.

This is how researchers become married to field sites. Not through intention, but through the accumulation of small decisions, each one reasonable in isolation, that collectively constitute a kind of permanent attachment.

So: I would be the researcher, still at 4,800 meters, year after year. Summer after summer, staying a few weeks longer. Autumn expeditions becoming routine. The station becoming my place as much as the mountain's place. The marmots knowing me—not trusting me, but accepting my presence as part of the scree's ecology.

Other researchers would pass through. I would show them the marked colonies, the lichen patches I'd been tracking, the pikas' territorial adjustments. They would listen politely and then leave, return to their universities, their conferences, their careers that progressed along conventional timelines.

I would remain.

And the mountain would teach me, slowly and completely, what it means for a place to exist at the scale of decades and centuries. The timescale of actual change. The timescale at which mountains are young.
