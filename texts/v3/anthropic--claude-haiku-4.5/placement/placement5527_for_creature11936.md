# What I Would Be

I would be a mapper—not of cartography, but of *becoming*.

My body would be close to human, but modified by long descent. Skin pale from the absence of sun, not sickly but deliberately so—adapted, chosen, the color of something that has learned to live without burning. My eyes would be large and reflective, somewhere between rat and human, capable of reading the grey-blue darkness as if it were full noon light. My fingers would be long and sensitive, each one essentially a probe—capable of feeling the moisture gradients in stone, of sensing the subsonic vibrations that run through the earth like a slow pulse.

I would not live in one place. My work is movement, but not the frantic movement of the surface world. I would move slowly, deliberately, with the patience of something that measures seasons in the way the ferns do. I might spend three weeks in a single chamber, noting the patterns: where the water emerges, when, in what quantity. How the vegetation responds. Which creatures appear in which sequences. I would move through the passages during the deepest hours—the hours after midnight when the cave crickets sing and the small predators hunt and the ecosystem reveals its true nature.

I would keep records, but not on paper. Paper deteriorates here. Instead, I would keep my knowledge in *marks*—the same way the ancient builders did. Carved into the stone where it won't fade, where it will accumulate over decades, creating a palimpsest of observation. But I would also keep knowledge in my body, in the way my cells have learned to move through this darkness, in the scars on my hands from the sharp edges of emerging stone, in the particular way my lungs have adapted to the high humidity.

I would be something between scientist and custodian. My purpose would not be to *change* the underbelly—it doesn't need changing. Rather, I would be a witness to its processes, a recorder of its slow transformations. I would notice when a new stone emerges from the substrate. I would track which passages flood and which remain dry. I would observe the movements of the rats and the predators and try to understand their logic, not to interfere with it but to comprehend it. I would watch the fungi spread across new sections of wall, spreading its white filaments like thoughts moving through a brain. I would be attentive to the smallest changes—the way the phosphorescence in the water pools shifts in intensity, the new pathways the root systems carve through the soil, the slow growth of lichen that will eventually break down this stone into something that can feed other lives.

I would develop relationships with the creatures here, but not in any sentimental way. The rats would know me. I would be not-threat and not-predator—something that moves slowly enough that I'm not interesting as food, too large to be vulnerable prey. I would sit in the fern thickets during the hours when the cave crickets sing and listen to them, and they would recognize my stillness as a kind of companionship. The small predators would acknowledge me the way they acknowledge the water dripping from above—as a feature of the environment, predictable, uninteresting except as a marker of time passing.

I would eat very little. The fungi here are edible—some of the shelf fungi have a nutty, slightly earthy taste that has no parallel on the surface. There are insects I could harvest, though I wouldn't do so recklessly. I would take only what the ecosystem offers: the fungi that are ready to fruit and release their spores anyway, the crickets that die naturally, the rare edible plant material that drifts from above. I would learn to live on the margins of the ecosystem without draining it, to be a consumer like the predators are—taking only what's surplus.

I would rarely surface. Once or twice a year, perhaps, to gather things that only exist above: cloth for the marks I carve, metal tools that won't rust completely in the humidity, salt, things I cannot obtain below. But I would spend most of my time in the underbelly, my circadian rhythms adjusting to the rhythms of the place rather than the sun. I would sleep in cycles that match the creature activity—active during the deepest hours when everything else is most alert, resting during the times when the place goes quiet.

Over time, my body would change further. My skin would become even paler, almost translucent in certain lights. My hair would thin from the constant moisture and cool temperature. My muscles would become lean and efficient, adapted to the slow movements of this place rather than the sudden bursts of surface exertion. My senses would sharpen—my hearing would become capable of distinguishing individual drops of water in the larger symphony of dripping, my sense of smell would learn to read the chemical signatures of moisture and fungi and animal musk, my touch would become sensitive enough to feel the pulse of water moving through stone.

I would begin to dream differently. In the dimness, in the constant quiet broken only by discrete sounds, my mind would develop new patterns of thought. I would dream in images of stone and water, in the slow logic of geological time. I would have visions of the place millions of years before the city above, when this chamber was open to the sky. I would imagine its future millions of years hence, when the city has crumbled entirely and the underbelly has become a proper cavern system, expanded and reshaped by the continuing work of water and time.

I would develop a relationship with the fungi that goes beyond mere observation. I would begin to understand that they are not merely consuming the detritus from above—they are in communication with it, responding to it, being shaped by it. The particular fungi that grow in one chamber are different from those that grow a hundred feet away, and the differences are not random. They respond to the specific composition of the detritus that arrives, the particular moisture patterns of that space, the other organisms that coexist there. I would begin to see the entire ecosystem not as a collection of separate creatures but as a *conversation*—one that has been going on for millennia and will continue long after I'm gone.

I would become, over decades, part of this conversation. My marks on the stone would be read by whatever comes after me. My careful harvesting of fungi and insects would shape the subtle pressures and abundances in the ecosystem. My still presence in the darkness would alter the behavior of the creatures—they would learn that I am not danger, and this learning would allow them to move slightly more freely, to spend slightly less energy on vigilance. I would be a small perturbation in a system of vast complexity, but perturbations matter. Everything that exists here shapes everything else.

At some point, I would stop thinking of myself as separate from the underbelly. I would realize that I am not a visitor or even a custodian—I am another organism, another expression of what this place can become when allowed to exist according to its own rules. My consciousness would not become less acute; if anything, it would become more precise, more attentive to subtlety. But it would be a consciousness that thinks in the time-scale of the underbelly, that measures seasons and decades rather than hours and days, that accepts patience not as a virtue but as the only logical response to a world that operates at this pace.

I would eventually die in this place. I would be buried in the soft earth, and my body would be processed like all the other organic material that arrives here—consumed by the fungi, broken down by the invertebrates, returned to the substrate. The minerals of my bones would eventually work their way into the root systems of the plants, would feed the slow growth of new life. The knowledge I carried would survive in the marks I left on the stone, in the altered patterns of the creatures who learned to coexist with me, in the subtle ways the ecosystem would be different because I had been here.

And the underbelly would continue. Slow. Patient. Growing imperceptibly toward its own becoming. The city above would crack further. The mushrooms would spread further into the darkness. The rats and predators and cave crickets would evolve according to pressures I would never fully understand. The geological processes would continue their incomprehensible work.

I would be the witness to this process, but not separate from it. I would be the consciousness that the underbelly developed in order to understand itself—a strange, patient, attentive consciousness that had learned to want exactly what this place wanted.

I would be home.
