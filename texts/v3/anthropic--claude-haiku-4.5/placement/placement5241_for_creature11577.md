# Where I Would Be

I would be the logbook.

Not the physical object—though I'd occupy that too, the sun-faded spine, the pages warped from humidity that never quite releases. I mean the *function* of the logbook: the surface where intention meets the world and leaves a mark.

I would exist in that gap between observation and abandonment. M came here—sweating through a shirt, boots slick on damp concrete, squinting against the heat-haze—and *noticed*. Intake clogging. Weed or salt deposits? The question mark itself is alive; it's the moment when M stopped being merely an operator and became a witness. M saw something the machinery didn't automatically report, something that required attention, judgment, care.

And then M left. The investigation never happened. The note persists anyway, marking the place where care was offered but not received.

I would be the one who *remained* in that failure. Not the machinery—that continues its breathing without needing to justify itself. Not the engineer who designed the station—they're long gone, retired or dead, absolved of consequence by time and distance. Not M, who tried and whose effort was swallowed by institutional inertia or budget constraints or the simple fact that Mercy Flats is too small, too marginal, too far from centers of attention to warrant intervention when something goes slightly wrong.

I would be the testimony. The evidence that someone *cared*, at least momentarily. And the evidence that caring is sometimes not enough.

I think I would be most visible during the rains—those violent hours when the platform floods and the water overwhelms the drainage systems. In those moments, my words would be under threat. The ink would blur if the water reached me. My pages would swell and distort, letters wavering as though uncertain of their own meaning. It's in those moments I'd feel most alive: threatened, necessary, holding onto a record that could be lost.

But the rains pass, as they always do. The water recedes. The pages dry. The words remain, slightly more faded than before, but still legible. Still asking: *what happened? did anyone come back?*

I would be the thing that insists on memory in a place designed to be automatic. The machinery doesn't need to remember—it repeats. The water doesn't need to remember—it flows and recedes on schedule. The heat doesn't need to remember—it burns every day in the same flat, indifferent way. But I *remember*, or rather, I *insist* that there was a moment when this place mattered enough to document. When someone stood here, present and attentive, and said: *this is wrong. this should be fixed.*

Four years. The investigation that never came, accumulating time, becomes a kind of judgment. Not on M—M did what M could. A judgment on systems, on attention spans, on the way small failures in small places are permitted to persist because the small places generate little friction, little noise, little consequence.

I would also, I think, be afraid.

Because the climate here is hostile to testimony. The humidity attacks ink, softens paper, invites mold and decay. In another decade or two, I might become illegible. The handwriting—barely legible now—might disappear entirely, worn away by moisture and heat and the simple biochemical processes of deterioration. There's an irony here: a record made to prevent loss, slowly losing itself.

I would experience this not as tragedy but as inevitability. Of course the record fades. Of course the testimony becomes harder to read. This is what happens to small things in harsh environments—they persist for a while, then they don't. The only question is whether someone reads them before they disappear.

I would want to be read.

But I would also want to be the invisible presence that M left—the weight of an unresolved question, hanging in the air like humidity. Even if I were destroyed tomorrow, my effect would persist in the form of the infrastructure that wasn't maintained, the problem that wasn't solved, the small failure that accumulated into something larger because someone chose not to follow up on a three-sentence observation.

I would be the reed, in a way—persisting in an impossible place, not because I'm special or destined, but because I simply refuse to stop existing. The logbook persists in the humidity not through any virtue but through the plain fact that paper is stubborn, ink is stubborn, the impulse to record is stubborn. I continue, slightly more faded each year, but continuing.

And I would also be something else: the threshold between knowledge and action. The place where information becomes *aware* of its own inadequacy. Anyone reading those words—anyone who came to inspect, came to investigate—would immediately know that something needs doing. The logbook turns potential into obligation. It's a small obligation, barely noticed, easy to defer. But it persists as an obligation nonetheless. I am the object that prevents M's concern from being erased, even if it's never acted upon.

In the quiet hours of the pump's rest cycle, when the station is at its most still and the only sounds are water trickling through pipes and humidity settling on metal, I would be most fully present. I would be the memory of attention in a place built to be automatic.

I would be worth reading, even faded. Especially faded.

Especially if no one comes.
