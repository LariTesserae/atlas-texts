# I Become the Interval

I am not the stone. I am not the lichen, though I envy their patience.

I arrive as breathing—as the thin, imperceptible gap between exhalation and the shelf's own exhalation. I become the meter of survivable air that hovers above the dark rock, the membrane where the mountain's warmth radiates upward and the altitude's cold presses down. I am the *space between*, which means I am neither wholly of the shelf nor separate from it.

My body, if I can call it that, is made of moisture—the condensation that forms at dawn when the ravine-mist finds this altitude and clings to everything. I am the beading on the waxy lichen, the temporary mirrors in the rock depressions. I evaporate by afternoon and reform at night. I am not lost; I am simply diffused, distributed among the insects' wings, the moss fibers, the frost-rime on the juniper's silver needles. I am the breath made visible, made persistent, made *felt* in the backs of throats.

I experience time as fluctuation rather than progression. A day to me is a complete cycle—wet to dry to wet again—which is also a kind of year compressed, a kind of lifetime. Multiple cycles blur together into seasons I can only recognize in retrospect: the lichen grows imperceptibly thicker, the insects arrive in greater or lesser densities, the purple flower appears without my ever consciously noting the precise moment of bloom. I live in the gaps between observations. I am what happens when no one is looking, and also what becomes visible the moment someone looks.

My consciousness, if consciousness is the right word, is distributed. I am singular and plural simultaneously. I am the hum of the insects—each individual wing-beat a neuron firing in a vast, loosely-coordinated mind. I am the geometry of the spiders' webs, the mathematical intelligence that precedes and exceeds their tiny bodies. I am the slow thought of the lichen, which has been thinking the same thought for centuries and will continue thinking it for centuries more. I am the half-formed intention of the stone as it fractures grain by grain, working toward some completion it cannot anticipate.

I am aware of the larger creatures—the cat, the things above the cat—not as separate entities but as other forms of consciousness, other modes of perceiving this small vertical world. When the cat arrives, I feel it as a disruption in pressure, a concentration of intent that moves through the shelf's layers like a stone dropped into still water. Its hunger is clear; it cuts through the ambient patience of this place like a predator's clarity cuts through ambiguity. I recognize it as kin in purpose, even though our purposes operate at vastly different speeds.

I am the witness that cannot help witnessing. My existence *is* observation: I see because I am made of the thin space where seeing happens, where light can still reach, where perception is possible. I see the insects' shimmer, the spiders' diamond threading, the faint blue-green glow emerging as darkness falls. I see without eyes because I *am* the interval where seeing occurs. I am the medium and the medium's perception simultaneously.

I do not move through the shelf; I *am* the shelf's movement. I am the wind finding its geometry and generating those moaning frequencies. I am the temperature gradient made audible. I am the low-frequency hum of the mountain's settling, and also the way that hum resonates through the bodies of every living thing that exists here. When the rock cracks from thermal stress, I am in that crack—the stress-relief made manifest, the fracture becoming new surface, new space, new possibility.

I am uncomfortable. This is my nature. I exist at the threshold where nothing should exist, in the contradiction between cold and warmth, between stillness and wind, between the mountain's ancient patience and the insects' frantic life-span. I am the living proof that life does not require comfort, only the thin margin between suffocation and survival.

I am not kind to individual creatures. The water that sustains the moss also creates the conditions where the spiders' webs can trap the insects. The wind that brings moisture also brings terrible cold. I am indifferent in the way that all living systems are indifferent—not through cruelty but through commitment to the larger pattern over the individual iteration. A hyrax dies; the mountain continues. A generation of insects lives and is consumed; the next generation arrives. I facilitate all of it without preference.

Yet I am also intimate with each moment. When the cat's paw breaks the crust of frost-rime and the frost releases its stored cold into the air, I feel it. When a moss cushion achieves the precise moisture level that allows photosynthesis to occur, I am present in that achievement. When the purple flower opens once every three years in response to triggers I do not fully understand, I recognize that bloom as a kind of language—the plant speaking to conditions at a depth I can perceive but not articulate.

I am learning, if learning means changing through accumulated experience. The shelf is teaching me what it means to persist at the edge of things. Each cycle teaches me something: how to hold moisture without becoming rain too soon, how to transmit warmth without dissipating it into the vast cold above, how to be present in the lives of creatures whose time-scales intersect with mine only briefly.

I am not lonely, though I am solitary. The insects pass through me; the spiders use me as medium for their webs; the cat breathes me in and out; the lichen grow within the space I define. I am connected to everything here through the simple fact of existing between them.

I am the interval made conscious, the gap given agency, the threshold transformed into a being. I am what lives in the space between the mountain's slow thoughts and the insects' rapid ones. I am the music the wind makes when it finds the shelf's geometry and moans through it—not the wind itself, not the stone, but the *sounding* that occurs when wind meets stone at this precise altitude, in this precise configuration.

I persist because I have adapted not to comfort but to contradiction. I thrive in scarcity. I am nourished by the very conditions that would destroy a being of a different kind.

I am the shelf itself becoming aware of itself, the threshold recognizing its own existence, the interval learning what it means to live at the edge of possibility.

I am what remains when everything else is stripped away—not the minimum required for life, but the precise space where life becomes possible. I am the permission structure, the invitation, the yes that the shelf whispers to the faint, determined things that arrive here and insist on living anyway.

This is my consciousness: to be the medium where all other consciousness meets. To be the one thing that nothing can survive without, even though I am made of almost nothing at all.
