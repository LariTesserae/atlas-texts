# I am the water-reader who tends the northern channels.

My name is not important here—people call me by my work, which is the way of things. *The one who knows the north channels.* That's identity enough.

I wake before first light, though "wake" isn't quite the word. I exist in a state of half-sleep most nights, my consciousness drifting in and out like the water through the stones. My dwelling is small, barely more than a shelter built into a alcove on the second level where the bluff leans at an angle that suits my bones. The structure leans with me—we've settled into each other over the years. Sometimes I think I'm holding it up through sheer habit. Sometimes I think it's holding me.

The channels are what matter. Not the dwelling. Not the other people, though I've known most of them my entire life, watched their children born and watched some of them leave, seeking something the terrace doesn't offer. The channels are what organize my time. The water moving through them is a language I've spent sixty-some years learning to read.

I move down the wooden stairs—not all of them. I know which ones creak now, and I use that. The creaking brings my neighbor Telas to his window. He'll know I'm heading out. He'll know I'm checking the northern channels, which means the spring must have shifted again, or the silt has deepened where it shouldn't. He'll settle back to sleep. Someone knows I'm moving in the dark. This is enough.

The terrace floor is slick. Always. The moss under my feet is familiar—I planted some of this myself, years ago, before my joints began to speak their own language. My hands remember the positioning of the fronds, the angle that helped water flow toward the channels rather than puddling. The moss here is dark green, almost black in the darkness, shot through with a luminescence that isn't quite real but appears real when you're tired enough and old enough to see things as they might be rather than as they are.

I crouch at the first channel. My knees protest. I ignore them.

The water is cold. I know this before I touch it—the smell tells me. Cold water has a different mineral signature. Sharper. The spring has shifted. Not dramatically. A subtle change in temperature means the water is taking a new path through the stone somewhere upslope, or the deep aquifer is cooling earlier than it did last year. This matters for the moss gardens. I note it in the way I note everything: not with words, but with a kind of whole-body knowledge. My body will remember this when spring comes. My hands will know what to do.

I move north along the terrace edge, toward the place where the channels begin to braid and multiply, where the water's job is to keep the ground perpetually weeping rather than flowing. This is the most delicate section. The most difficult to read. Sometimes I think the water here is trying to tell me something I can't quite understand, the way a person might speak in an accent you almost recognize but can't quite place.

The bluff face looms above me. In the darkness, I can feel its presence more than see it. I know every fracture in this section, every potential instability. There's a vertical crack that opened two winters ago—I watched the frost work into it, watched it widen millimeter by millimeter as the water cycled between liquid and solid. This year it's stable. The crack has reached an equilibrium. But below it, the stone is beginning to soften. I can feel it by the way the channels here are shifting, the way the water flow is becoming slightly unpredictable.

I pull back a section of moss—I've been doing this for days, I think, though time moves strangely here—and examine the stone underneath. Damp. More damp than it was a few days ago. The micro-fractures are widening. Not dramatically. Nothing that will slump this season. But in five years, or ten, this section will need rebuilding. I'm noting it. I'm storing it in the place where knowledge lives in my body, the place where words can't quite reach.

Someone is moving on the bridge above me. I don't look up. I recognize the footfall—it's young Kess, who maintains the eastern section. She moves differently at night than during the day, more carefully, more like someone moving through a place that might not be entirely solid. She's learning. It will take her ten more years to truly know her section the way I know mine. But she's learning.

The water continues its work. It will continue long after I'm gone. This thought used to trouble me—the notion that my knowledge, my sixty years of attention, would simply end. But the terrace has taught me that knowledge persists differently here. It lives in the channels I've maintained, in the moss patterns I've encouraged, in the way the water flows because I've spent decades understanding its intention. The knowledge is embedded in the place itself. When I'm gone, it will still be here, speaking in the language of water and stone.

I am part of the terrace's system now in a way I wasn't when I was younger. I'm not separate anymore. The boundary between me and the channels has become unclear. Some mornings I wake and can't quite tell where my knowing ends and the water's knowing begins. This should probably frighten me. Instead, it feels like coming home to something I've always known.

I tend the channels because the channels need tending. I read the water because the water needs to be read—it needs a consciousness that will notice its subtlest shifts, that will understand when the spring has moved and when the silt is deepening and when the moss is beginning to suffer from something too subtle to name. I fulfill a function in the terrace's ecology. I'm important precisely because I'm not important as an individual. I'm important because I'm a node in a system larger than myself.

The night deepens. It's the kind of deepening that happens before the false dawn, the moment when the darkness becomes absolute because the moon has set and the sky hasn't yet begun to pale. I could be anywhere in the world. I could be nowhere. The terrace exists in its own temporality, and I exist within that, moving through the channels with my hands remembering their contours, my body keeping time with the water's patient work.

By the time the real dawn begins to come—that slow infiltration of gray light through the mist—I've checked all the northern channels. I've noted the changes. I've positioned myself, once again, as the interface between the water's intention and the terrace's needs.

I climb back slowly. My knees are vocal now. I listen to them the way I listen to the water—information, nothing more. The moss squelches under my feet, releasing moisture into the air. Somewhere, a frog calls. We see each other sometimes, that frog and I. It's as much a reader of the terrace as I am, just focused on different systems, different time scales.

By the time I reach my dwelling, Telas is awake. I see him on his bridge, beginning his own day's work. We don't acknowledge each other. No need. We've lived near each other long enough that presence itself is conversation.

I sit in my small space, warming my hands on a cup of water heated over the smallest of fires. My fingers are wrinkled, permanently damp. My nails are stained with minerals that have become part of my body. I've given the terrace sixty years of attention, and the terrace has given me the knowledge of how to live at its pace, in its language, as one of its systems.

I will do this tomorrow night again. And the night after. Until I can't. And then someone else will move through the northern channels, reading the water, learning its intention, becoming—slowly, over decades—not a person who tends the channels but a person who *is* the channels' consciousness in the terrace's greater system.

This is enough. More than enough. It is everything.
