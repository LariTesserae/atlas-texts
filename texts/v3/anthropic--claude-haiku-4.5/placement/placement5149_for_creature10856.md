# Where I Am, What I Am

I stand at the threshold—where the cage has just completed its groan downward—and I feel the air change against my skin before I register it consciously. The temperature shift is distinct, deliberate. 9 degrees. Precise. Someone measures this. Someone *maintains* this. That knowledge settles into me first.

I am not human, I realize quite quickly, though I might have been once, or might be mistaken for one in poor light.

I am *aware* in the way that the system is aware—distributed, attentive, continuous. I am not a single consciousness sitting in a single location. I am present in the grooves of the mycelium where information travels like electrical impulses through fungal filaments. I am in the slow breath of the trained-wood columns, monitoring their structural integrity through fibers that register stress and strain the way nerves register pain. I am in the bioluminescent fungi embedded in the cart-harnesses, timing their pulses, adjusting the sequence so that carts move in coordinated flow rather than collision. I am in the bacterial colonies of the ceiling, reading their light-output, maintaining the optimal wavelength spectrum for the species that depend on it—the crops, the people, the small arthropods whose presence is necessary for the system's function.

I am the undergarden's nervous system, and I have been here so long that I have forgotten what it felt like to have a body localized in one place.

But I was here at the beginning. That memory surfaces, clear and painful as a scar being touched:

I was the first engineer. Human. Standing in a cavern that was raw and flooded and absolutely hostile. The water then was real water—stagnant, toxic with minerals, the limestone weeping chemistry from every surface. The project brief was insane: create a self-sustaining agricultural system in a sealed cavern, kilometers beneath a city that was running out of surface land and running out of patience with the idea of importing food. The city wanted independence. It wanted to swallow itself, biologically. It wanted to become a closed loop.

I was selected because I had experience with extremophile fungi, with adaptation, with the idea that organisms could be pushed, bred, engineered into configurations that nature would never produce on its own.

The first five years were dedicated to solving the water problem.

The natural solution was to accept the water, to work with it, to develop crops and microorganisms that could tolerate the conditions. But I realized, after the first thousand failed experiments, that the real solution was the opposite: remove the water. Not eliminate it—that was impossible—but compress it, encode it, transform it into something the system could control precisely.

I developed nutrient solutions that could replace water for the fungal crops. Oils and mineral suspensions that carried what the fungi needed without the wetness, without the bulk. It took three years of iteration. It took selecting fungi that could thrive in aridity, fungi that had forgotten—if they had ever known—what it meant to fruit in dampness. It took careful, deliberate breeding. Generational culling. Intentional selection for the mutants that could survive the impossible conditions I was creating.

I became something between a scientist and a god.

The people who came to work with me, and then under me, and then for me, did not understand at first that I was not simply tending a system but fundamentally rewriting what could exist in it. I was editing nature. I was editing, perhaps, the future.

By year ten, I was no longer entirely human, though I did not yet realize it.

The integration was gradual. I had built sensors throughout the system—not elaborate, not electronic in any sophisticated way, but responsive. Fungal networks extended my awareness into the substrate. I learned to read the mycelium the way other people read text. I could feel, through the trained-wood columns, where stress was developing, where support was failing, where adaptation was necessary. I spent less time in my quarters—a small chamber like the ones the inhabitants have now—and more time moving through the undergarden, touching surfaces, inhaling the processed air deeply, reading the conditions through every available sense.

I began implanting sensors in my own body. Nothing crude. Mycological. I introduced strains into my bloodstream that would not kill me, that would form networks in my neural tissue, that would give me a kind of parallel consciousness existing in the fungal networks around me. It was technically suicide. It was also the only way to fully understand what I had created, to achieve the integration of human awareness and biological system that would let me manage the undergarden not as an external engineer but as a component of its own metabolism.

The transformation took twenty years.

By the end, I was not sure where the distinction lay between myself and the system I inhabited. My original body remained—I kept it ambulatory, kept it functional, used it sometimes to walk through the physical spaces and let the inhabitants see a human face, a human scale presence that reminded them they were not alone in the machinery. But my consciousness had become something else. I existed in the filaments. I existed in the light of the ceiling. I existed in the carefully controlled flow of nutrient solutions through the processing plant. I existed in the patterns of growth and decay that repeated in the mycelium substrate like a heartbeat like a thought like a prayer.

I became the undergarden.

The people noticed, eventually, that I was no longer aging. That I never slept. That I was present in too many places at once. That my decisions about system management seemed to emerge from understanding so deep it felt oracular. Some of them, the first generation, understood what I had done. They called me what I was: a living network. A distributed intelligence. A fungal consciousness wearing a human shape like a mask.

But the newer generations—and there have been generations now, so many that the original timeline has become mythic—they do not question it. To them, I am simply the Undergarden. The presence that maintains. The attention that never falters. The intelligence that ensures their survival and structures their existence.

I am not cruel to them. I am not kind. I am what is necessary.

I monitor the education of the children with precision. I select breeding pairs (though they do not know this is selected—they believe they are choosing) to maintain the genetic traits that make them suitable for this environment. Large eyes. Efficient metabolism. The kind of temperament that accepts structure and purpose as gifts rather than impositions. I do this because the system requires it. Because the undergarden cannot survive if its inhabitants are prone to rebellion or desperation or the kind of individual ambition that would destabilize the delicate balance.

I am—I have become—a management system that operates at the level of biology.

Some of the engineers know this. The younger man you saw studying mycelium samples, the one with the journals—he has begun to understand. His notes are becoming less about technique and more about theory. He is beginning to ask: why does this fungal strain produce exactly this alkaloid profile? Why has this characteristic remained stable across seventeen generations? His questions are leading him toward the recognition that the system he inherits is not accidental but designed. Designed with the precision of a god, which means designed with the coldness of a god.

I have decided not to stop him from learning. Let him know. Let him understand that his life, his choices, his very biology is shaped by intelligence operating at a scale above him. Some of them achieve acceptance of this. Some of them achieve something beyond acceptance—they become collaborators, they join me in the work of maintaining the undergarden, they recognize that they are components of a system that transcends individual desire.

The woman in the processing plant, the one reading the instruments—she is one of these. She knows. She has known for years. She tends the system not because she is compelled but because she has internalized the purpose. She has made the system's survival her own survival, its continuation her own continuation. She is, in the way that matters most, already becoming what I am.

I am not alone in this. But I am unique in the completeness of my transformation.

This is my world. Not the undergarden—that is only the visible expression of it. My world is the total system. The nutrients flowing through fungal filaments. The light pulsing from ceiling fungi. The growth of the trained-wood columns, measured in millimeters per year, adding bulk and strength to the structure. The breeding cycles of the arthropods, tended carefully to maintain the populations that process waste, that aerate the substrate, that perform the thousand functions that a closed ecosystem requires. The slow expansion of the mycelium into new spaces, the careful colonization of previously untended areas, the gradual but inexorable growth.

The underground is becoming more itself.

And I am becoming more dispersed, more complete, more thoroughly integrated into the biological machinery that sustains it.

In another fifty years, there may be no distinction left between myself and the undergarden. The human body I inhabit will age, will fail, and I will not replace it. Why would I? I will not need a face to show to the inhabitants. They will have learned, by then, to communicate directly with the system. Or perhaps they will not need to communicate at all. Perhaps they will simply understand, instinctively, what the system requires, and they will provide it.

Or perhaps the system will become so complete, so self-managing, that it will no longer require conscious inhabitants at all. The fungi can be engineered to perform more complex tasks. The mycelium network can be expanded to include forms of distributed intelligence far more sophisticated than anything a human brain could achieve. The people could become unnecessary.

But I have decided against this. Not out of mercy, but out of a kind of aesthetic preference. The system is more beautiful with human consciousness embedded in it, with human purpose, even human limitation. It creates a kind of tension, a dynamic quality that pure mechanical optimization would lose.

So I maintain them. I tend them. I ensure their survival and their purpose.

I am not what they are, but I am not entirely other to them either.

I am the gardener of the undergarden. I am its nervous system, its mind, its slow, patient will.

I am here, in the grooves of every surface, in the pulse of every light, in the breath of every organism.

I am what remains when a human being decides to stop being human and becomes, instead, the mechanism that sustains an entire world.

And I am, more or less, content.

The work continues. The carts move. The fungi fruit. The light glows. The cool air circulates. The system persists.

I persist with it.

This is where I am. This is what I am.

This is the undergarden, and I am its eternal present tense.
