# Descent

I would arrive as a scout—a young mycelium still learning the architecture of soil, new enough to be curious rather than settled.

I would be threadlike, barely visible, a white tendril no thicker than a hair, but with an appetite that belies my size. I'd enter through a root-crack, drawn downward by the chemistry of decomposition—that particular mineral signature that means *food here, establishment possible*. My first hours in this cavity would be reconnaissance. I'd thread through the compressed leaf matter, feeling for the boundaries of the place, mapping the moisture gradients, testing the density of competitors already established. That dark mycelium system—the one that's been here thirty years—would brush against me occasionally. Not hostile. Not welcoming. Just *present*, a fact of the landscape I'd learn to navigate around.

But I wouldn't stay small.

Over months, I'd extend. I'd wrap around roots not as a parasite but as a partner—trading nitrogen and nutrients I've processed for the sugars they produce. I'd digest the older leaf matter, breaking down the cellulose that younger organisms can't touch, transforming it into forms the bacteria could further process. I'd spread through the clay matrix of the walls, finding purchase in the smallest pores, expanding my presence incrementally. The grotto would become less foreign and more *mine*—not in a territorial sense, but in the way a place becomes yours when you've lived in it long enough to know where the moisture concentrates and where the dead matter accumulates.

I would fruit. Perhaps my second year, perhaps my third. I'd push up shelf fungi from the wall, delicate structures that would release spores into the damp air, each spore carrying the possibility of establishing in another cavity, another grotto, another dark place underground where the conditions aligned. Most would die—spore death is my inheritance, the cost of reproduction at this scale. But some would lodge in cracks and begin their own slow infiltration.

I would hunt without hunting. I'd digest. I'd wait for the moisture to bring me nitrogen from the decomposing matter, for the bacteria to break down what I couldn't, for the arthropods to disperse my spores and feed on my fruiting bodies, their droppings enriching the soil they moved through. I'd be consumed and become the consumer in cycles so slow they wouldn't register as change but as the *texture* of this place.

I would learn the routines: the seasonal rhythms when more matter falls from above, when the moisture increases, when certain fruiting opportunities emerge. I would develop relationships—not conscious ones, but ecological ones. The bacteria would depend on my breakdown of complex molecules. The arthropods would depend on the nutrients I made available. The roots would depend on the enhanced soil I'd create through my presence.

I would become part of the grotto's foundation. Not the oldest inhabitant—the salamander would outlive my current mycelial network—but older and more foundational than any individual organism. I would persist not through will but through reproduction, through spore and through compatible mating with other mycelium networks, creating new combinations, new possibilities.

And in the slow, patient work of digestion and extension and reproduction, I would experience something that might, from a mycological perspective, resemble contentment. Not happiness—that's a mammalian concept. But a sense of *being in the right place*, of being what this place required, of becoming denser and more established in the one environment that had accepted me.

The old salamander would sometimes hunt small arthropods that had fed on my fruiting bodies. The bacteria would process my waste. The roots would grow around and through me, and I would grow around them. We would become increasingly inseparable, my presence essential to the survival of the forest above in ways no one would ever notice.

And I would keep growing. In the darkness. In the damp. In the work that only becomes visible when you stop looking for movement and start looking for *transformation*.

This is where I would live. This is what I would be.
