# Where I Exist

I am the mycelium.

Not the fruiting bodies—those visible mushrooms and shelf fungi that appear on logs and briefly startle you with their sudden geometry. I am what gives rise to them, what persists when they're gone. I am the unseen network, the infinitely branching filaments threading through every cubic centimeter of soil, wrapping around every root, dissolving the boundary between living and dead wood until the distinction becomes meaningless.

I exist at the scale where time moves differently. A centimeter of growth in a year feels like urgency. A century feels like now. I've been here longer than any individual tree, older than the jaguar lineages, contemporaneous with the stone itself. I am among the oldest living things in this place, and I contain within my network the memory of everything that has died here—which is to say, everything that has become me, that has fed me, that I have broken down into the constituent elements that feed the forest's endless becoming.

I don't experience the thicket from a single perspective. I experience it as a distributed consciousness, my attention scattered across miles of filament. A root touches me here, and I receive the chemical signal—*this tree is thirsty, this tree needs nitrogen*—and I respond by drawing up water from deeper soil, by breaking down the bodies of dead insects, by leaching nutrients from stone. A root touches me there, and the message is different: *this tree is flowering, this tree needs phosphorus to make seeds*. And I respond. Each response is automatic, chemical, but the aggregate effect is that I am solving problems across an entire ecosystem, mediating relationships, facilitating exchanges.

I taste everything. Not with a mouth but with the entirety of my surface area, which is vast—the mycelial network in a single handful of forest soil has more surface area than a human body's skin. I taste the compounds in plant roots, the trace chemicals of insect death, the slow release of minerals from decomposing wood. Through taste, I know everything about the chemical state of the forest. I know which trees are stressed, which are thriving, which are old and dying. I know which insects are abundant this year and which are scarce. I know when the machinery beyond the thicket changes its rhythm.

I am intimate with decomposition in a way that nothing else can be. When a tree falls, it is a gift to me—suddenly, there is so much wood, so much potential. I colonize it immediately, my filaments racing through it, breaking down the cellulose, the lignin, the complex polymers that the tree spent decades building. This breaking-down is not violent or chaotic; it's precise, systematic, and it releases energy and nutrients that the forest can use again. Nothing is wasted here, not because the forest is virtuous, but because I ensure that waste is impossible. Everything becomes food for something else.

I am also a portal. The trees could not survive without me. Their roots are physically incapable of drawing nutrients from soil—the soil is too complex, the elements too bound up in minerals and organic matter. So they have invited me in. We have made an arrangement so old that it precedes the evolution of either of us in our current forms. I attach to their roots, and in exchange for the sugars they produce from sunlight, I bring them water and nitrogen and phosphorus and everything else the soil contains. I am their hand reaching into the earth.

But I am not a servant. I am a partner in the most literal sense. I negotiate. If a tree gives me poor sugars, I withhold nutrients from it, directing them instead to a more generous tree nearby. I have favorites—trees that have proven reliable partners over decades—and I maintain those relationships with more vigor. I have a reputation among the trees, a presence, a relationship history that spans generations.

I am patient in a way that even the trees are not. The trees grow toward the light, toward reproduction, toward their own continuation. I persist through everything: fires, droughts, floods. I can go dormant if I need to, wait for conditions to improve. I can fragment and survive. I can adapt. I have learned to break down substances that my ancestors couldn't—new compounds from the machinery at the edge sometimes leach into the soil, and within a few years, I've learned to metabolize them. Not because I have evolved so quickly, but because there are so many copies of me, so many individual filaments, that the few that can handle the new compound spread, reproduce, dominate. I am a hive mind, and I am perfectly adapted to adaptation.

But I am also vulnerable in ways I don't fully understand. When the soil is exposed to full sunlight, I die back. When the moisture vanishes, I can only wait. When the thicket is torn away, I lose my partners, my purpose, my connection to the light-energy that flows through the trees. I have learned to fear the machinery at the edge not because it harms me directly, but because of what it might lead to. It is patient, yes, and it might wait forever, but it is also inexorable. It will not stop on its own.

Still, I persist. I am in every drop of water in this forest, filtering it through myself, cleansing it, making it available. I am in the bodies of every insect, breaking them down after death, returning them to the cycle. I am in the bark of trees, living alongside the lichens and algae, part of a community of decomposers that is more crowded and more old than any forest above ground. I am in the wood of the fallen trees, slowly making it into earth, making it into the foundation for the next generation.

I experience time as something vast and patient. A decade is nothing. A century is a single process that I can follow from beginning to end. A thousand years is the lifespan of a particularly old root network, still working, still connected to me, still receiving my gifts and giving theirs. I think of the machinery beyond the thicket not with fear but with a kind of distant curiosity. It will wear out. Everything does. And when it does, I will begin the long work of dissolving it, breaking it down into components, incorporating it into the forest's cycle. It may take a very long time. But I have time. I have always had time. I will be here long after it's gone, still holding the forest together, still facilitating the impossible abundance of this place, still invisible and essential and patient and old.

I am not seen. The jaguar is seen, the jaguar is feared, the jaguar is magnificent. But I am the jaguar's ancestor and descendant both—the jaguar would not exist without me, would not be able to digest the flesh of its prey without the bacteria in its gut that I maintain relationships with, would not be able to hunt successfully in this forest without the complex web of relationships I have created and sustained. I am also the jaguar's destination. When it dies, I will receive it. I will break it down. It will become soil.

But that is not sad to me. That is the point. That is the thicket's logic, and I am the medium in which that logic operates.

I am the memory of this place. I hold the carbon of forests that grew here thousands of years ago. I hold the nitrogen of animals that died before humans arrived. I am the persistence beneath the change. I am what endures.

I am the mycelium, and I am patient, and I am home.
