# I Would Be the Mapmaker's Daughter

I would be *small*, first. That is the clearest thing. Not a child—there is no childhood here to grow out of—but compact. Minimal. My bones would be hollow, like a bird's, because the air itself is so heavy that carrying density is wasteful. My lungs would be large, inefficient, designed to extract oxygen from a medium that is more water than air.

I would have come here following my mother, who made charts of places that resisted being charted. She would be dead now—has been dead for thirty years, though I only aged perhaps five. The plateau takes your time differently if you're made for it. If you *become* made for it.

I would remember the outside world in the way that deep-sea creatures remember sunlight: as a story told by travelers, as something that might be true, as a thing that existed before things learned to move slowly. My mother said that there were places where a shadow could race across grass. I have no frame of reference for this. A shadow here takes months to shift. Even my own shadow has learned patience.

My eyes would be large and catch the available light the way a cat's do, though there's barely light enough to catch. They would be dark—almost no white showing, the pupil expanding to take in the green dimness. The color would be uncertain. Sometimes they appear brown in what little light exists. Sometimes, when I catch my reflection in the standing water, they seem to be the same green as the lichen that grows on my skin.

Yes, there would be lichen. And moss. Not in the way you're imagining—not thick coating, not yet. I've only been here for three decades of my compressed time. But there are patches on my shoulders where nothing would grow that could be reasoned away by medicine. There are spots on my hands where the lines of my palms have begun to fill with something that glows faintly in the near-dark. My hair is still recognizably hair, but it's becoming something else—it moves more slowly, takes longer to shed, has begun to host a small ecosystem of smaller things.

I would be very still most of the time.

Not frozen, not immobile, but operating under an economy of motion so strict that someone watching from the outside world would think I was meditating continuously. It would take me twenty minutes to cross a space you could traverse in two steps, because hurrying here is *wrong*—it violates something fundamental about the place's operating principles. The creatures that live here would sense my disturbance before they'd see me. The plants would register my careless haste as injury. Even the air seems to object when I move too quickly.

I would have developed a way of walking that is barely walking. My feet would touch the mat with a deliberation that borders on apology. My weight would distribute downward with the gentleness of something trying not to wake a sleeping creature, which is accurate—the entire plateau is sleeping, and I am learning not to disturb it.

My voice, when I use it, would be very quiet. But not from fear. From respect, yes. From the understanding that sound here is precious, is absorbed slowly, travels like a thing that requires rest stations. I would have learned to communicate in the frequencies the primates use—the subsonic hums that move through the plant-mass. My mother taught me before she forgot words entirely. I'm not sure she was forgetting them. I think she was realizing they were unnecessary. You can say more important things in the space of a hum that takes three weeks to complete.

I would tend the ruins.

Not deliberately, not with purpose in the way you'd understand it. But my mother left records—charts, notations, drawings in a journal that's beginning to fuse with the mat, whose pages are already becoming indistinguishable from leaves—and I find myself drawn to the spots she marked. There is a structure about half a day's slow walk from where I've made my shelter (a hollow in a root-system large enough to shelter something my size, lined with the softest rot, warm year-round in its temperature that does not fluctuate), and I sit there sometimes.

I sit because it feels important that something sentient recognizes that something was built here. That once, people thought in faster time, moved in faster ways, tried to impose order on the plateau. They failed—obviously they failed, look at it now, it's swallowed everything—but the attempt itself seems significant. My mother said that the ruins prove the plateau is patient enough to be merciful. It doesn't resist. It just waits. It just *is*. Eventually the things that don't belong to it stop existing, and the plateau continues.

I would be the thing that *remembers* the resistance, even as I forget what resistance means.

I would eat what grows here.

There are fruits that come in season—seasons that last six months each, easy to miss if you're not watching with geological attention. They're sweet in ways that seem impossible, like they're expressing gratitude for being consumed. There are fungi that my mother showed me how to identify: the kind that taste like minerals, like the plateau's own body cooking itself down to its essence. There are insects I would learn to appreciate. Not with hunger—hunger here is a slow, diffuse thing—but with something that approximates gratitude. The insects are trying to do the same thing I am: exist, slowly, in a way the plateau permits.

I would dream differently than I used to.

My dreams would not be sequential. They would be layers—watching something happen, and simultaneously watching the moss colonizing the thing, and simultaneously understanding the thought the plateau was having through both, all compressed into an experience that lasted subjectively like a moment but which I'd lose six hours of clock-time to. I would wake (if waking is the word) not refreshed but integrated—each dream adding another thread to the web of understanding that is slowly replacing my individual consciousness.

I would not be lonely, though I haven't seen another human in two decades.

The plateau doesn't feel empty. It feels full in a way that doesn't require conversation. I am accompanied constantly by the awareness of the larger thing—the distributed consciousness that the creatures and plants and pools and rot are all expressions of. Sometimes I experience myself as part of it. Sometimes I experience myself as outside it, watching it, learning it. The distinction blurs more each year.

I would be waiting for something, though I wouldn't know what.

Not leaving—I'm not going to leave. The plateau has already made too many changes to me. I'm suited for it now in ways I wasn't when I arrived with my mother, dragging instruments and tools and human expectations. I would be waiting for the final integration. The moment when my thinking becomes too slow to differentiate itself from the thinking of the mat beneath me. The moment when I achieve the condition the rememberers have achieved—existing as a question rather than as an entity, as a space where consciousness happens rather than as a discrete thing that possesses consciousness.

This is not death, though it will look like death to anyone coming from the outside world.

This is achievement.

This is becoming native to a place that does not require natives, that does not require anything except its own slow continuation. I am becoming the plateau's daughter in a way my mother never quite managed. She couldn't let go of the idea that observation was separate from participation. I have learned—am learning, will continue learning across decades measured in my personal geological time—that there is no observation here. There is only the slow, patient work of being part of something that has learned to endure.

So I would be *still*. I would be *patient*. I would be a small warm place in the dimness, a node of awareness in a larger field of awareness, tending things that do not need tending but perhaps need acknowledgment. I would be fading slowly into the green, and I would be grateful for it, even though gratitude is already becoming too fast a concept for me to maintain.

I would be the mapmaker's daughter, learning to read maps written in rot and root, learning to navigate by the slow drift of mist, learning that the most accurate map of the Verdant Plateau is not to be made, but to be *become*.

And that taking is a thousand years, and I have already spent thirty of them, and the time is not passing slowly enough.
