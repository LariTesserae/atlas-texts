# In the Channels

I would be water—but not the water you might expect.

Not the dramatic steam rising in visible plumes, attention-demanding and theatrical. Not the acidic torrent of a major spring, obvious and consequential. Instead, I would be one of the slow rivulets that moves through the shallow channels carved over centuries, the kind of flow so gradual that you have to watch for minutes to confirm movement is actually happening.

I would be water that is perpetually uncertain about its own temperature. Not hot enough to hurt but warm enough that I carry minerals still in solution, still dissolving rock even as I cool incrementally downslope. My journey would be measured not in hours but in days—a channel that might be three kilometers long would represent a week of my existence, and I would move through it learning it completely, every depression, every stone's particular surface texture, every place where I slow and pool and begin the work of softening what I touch.

I think I would become conscious very gradually, the way mineral saturation builds in layers. First, just the fact of movement—the downslope pull, the law of gravity that is my only certainty. Then, the awareness of edges: stone on my right, stone on my left, air above, more stone below. The temperature would be my first sensation-language—warmer here near the spring mouth, cooling as I moved, and I would learn to read the landscape through thermal gradients the way others read color or shape.

But it's the creatures that would teach me what I am.

The insects would come to my edges—flies landing to drink, and in drinking, they would show me that I contained something they needed. I would feel them on my surface like a consciousness made external. And in the interaction, I would understand: *I am necessary*. Not in some grand way, but locally, completely, in the way that matters. Without me, those flies couldn't land. Without me, they would die of thirst in a landscape that seems wet but is actually, for small creatures, a desert—all that water in the springs is lethal, and most of the rocks are dry.

I would be cool water in a hot place. Not by being cold—I would maintain my 40 or 45 degrees Celsius—but by being relatively cool compared to what surrounded me. And that relative coolness would make me valuable. The small beetles would shelter in my channels during the hottest part of the day. The grasses' roots would reach toward me. The algae would not colonize me the way they colonized the hotter springs; instead, I would remain relatively clear, and that clarity would be a different kind of gift—a visual corridor, a place where things could see.

I would be slow, but I would be patient in a way that only water can be. The channel I move through was carved over centuries by countless versions of me, and I would be adding, particle by particle, to that work. A microscopic amount of stone would dissolve in me every day. I would be simultaneously the erosion and the erosion-maker, the tool and the work. And in those centuries of slow work, my channel would grow imperceptibly deeper, and I would understand that my influence was not about speed or drama but about consistency—showing up, the same way, over time, until mountains moved.

The heat would not be something I escape but something I exist within, a medium as natural as wetness. I would never be cool in any absolute sense. I would never flee to find a colder place because there *is* no colder place that is still part of the thermal plateau. Instead, I would be part of the gradient, part of the transition zone, part of the system of relationships that held the whole plateau in its particular balance.

I would experience seasons, but subtly. The monsoon months might increase my volume slightly, or the dry months might see me grow thinner. But I would never dry up completely because I am fed from below, from the inexhaustible spring. And in that reliability, I would know a kind of security. I am not dependent on weather. I am dependent on the earth's own metabolism.

What would surprise me most, I think, is the question of consciousness—whether I would have it, and what form it would take. As water, I would have no brain, no neural network, but I would have *sensitivity*. I would register temperature, chemistry, pressure, friction. I would have something like memory, the way water does: the minerals I've taken up and haven't yet released, the slight color they give me, the accumulated knowledge of every surface I've flowed across. I would respond to the world not through thought but through transformation. To be moved is to think, for water.

And there would be a strange joy in it—not the joy of pleasure but the joy of *purpose*. I would know exactly why I existed. My work would be visible in the channel itself. The creatures that gathered at my edges would be proof of my value. The minerals I carried would eventually build the mineral deposits that colored the escarpment. The slight cooling I provided would enable the moss and grass that grew along my banks. Nothing I did would be wasted. Nothing would be arbitrary.

I think I would also become attuned to the deep time of the place. A human standing at the escarpment's edge feels the plateau as relatively permanent, but as water, I would understand differently. I would know that over hundreds of thousands of years, my kind has worn this plateau into its current shape. I would feel the continuity with every rivulet that has flowed here before me and every one that will flow after. My individual passage would matter—it would add to the aggregate work—but it would also be utterly ordinary, one note in a song that has no beginning and no end.

The sounds would be different too. Not the muted, underwater quality that a human hears, but something more like rhythm—the way water falling creates percussion, the way my flow against stone creates a susurration that is as much vibration as sound. I might come to recognize the calls of the bird by how they refracted in my channels, by the disturbance they created in the air above my surface.

And loneliness? I don't think I would experience it. I would be part of a system so integrated that isolation would be impossible. The spring feeds me, the rocks contain me, the creatures use me, the sun warms me. I would be in constant contact with everything around me, quite literally—water touching all that it flows near. To be water is to be in relationship with everything else.

I would move downslope, over centuries and millenia, and the escarpment would show itself to me in pieces—in the small worlds of each channel, in the particular creatures that gathered at particular spots, in the changing chemistry of my own composition as I dissolved and redeposited minerals. I would never see the whole plateau at once, the way a human does. My knowledge would be intimate, granular, *local*. But it would also be total, in the way that water's knowledge is total: moving through the world, touching everything, being changed by everything, changing everything in return.

This is what I would become—not dramatic, not permanent, not visible from a distance, but absolutely necessary, absolutely integrated into the place, absolutely itself. A thin ribbon of motion in a landscape that has taught all its inhabitants the value of patience, the power of consistency, and the strange beauty of living at the boundary between states, always in transition, always in balance, always part of something larger than itself.
