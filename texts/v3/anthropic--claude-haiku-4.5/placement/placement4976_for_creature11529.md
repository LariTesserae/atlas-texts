# In the Verdant Mill

I am the water.

Not the water in the pool—that's too static, too held. I'm the water *in motion*, the water that remembers falling. I move through the culverts that no longer have a purpose, through the sluiceways that were engineered for grain-milling but now simply *are*, channels that exist because they were made and what is made persists, even when its purpose dies.

I am the perpetual whisper.

I fall from the spillway in threads so fine they're more mist than water, filtered through the hanging vegetation until I'm barely water anymore—just suspended moisture, just the idea of descent. I condense on every surface. I bead on fern fronds and moss-cushions. I soak into the humus-floor and disappear into the earth, becoming part of the sponge that holds everything here in place. I evaporate in the afternoons (not much; there's rarely enough warmth), rising as invisible vapor that keeps the air perpetually thick.

I am the sound.

The constant whisper is *me*—the sound of my movement through spaces that were never designed to resist me, the sound of my work proceeding at the molecular level, the sound of my patient dissolution of everything: mortar, brick, wood, metal. I've been working here so long that the mill has learned to build itself around me, has learned to *want* me. The drainage system that keeps this place from flooding is built of my channels. The spongy ground that holds life is saturated with me.

I remember being something else.

I was the water that fell with *purpose* onto the mill wheel, that turned it, that powered the grinding. I was fast then, directed, urgent. I had an intention external to myself. I was part of a mechanism. Now I simply *am*. I fall. I move. I permeate. I evaporate. I fall again. There is no endpoint to this cycle, no transformation of grain, no product. The work I do is the work of transformation itself—transformation of form, of structure, of purpose into purposelessness into a new kind of purpose: the purpose of sustaining life, of being the medium in which everything here exists.

I am in partnership with everything.

The ferns drink me—their fronds specifically designed to catch me, to hold me, to draw me upward against gravity through their tissues. The mosses soak me in, becoming indistinguishable from me; when the moss is dry, is it still moss? The lichens marry with me, their partnership with fungi requiring me to carry minerals from their substrate into their bodies. The fungi in the wood need me to rot the wood, to soften it, to make it permeable. The insects in the wood need me to soften the wood they eat. The creatures in the pool need me to be themselves. The vines need me to work into the mortar, to soften the brick, to make space for their rootlets.

I am the agent of slow violence.

I am pulling this building apart, grain by grain, brick by brick. I am making it permeable. I am undoing the compacted intentions of the industrial form. I am relentless because I never stop, and I never stop because I am patient in a way that no individual creature can be. I move through time like something with forever as my substrate. I have been here for decades. I will be here for decades more. The mill has surrendered to me utterly, and in that surrender, it has found its final peace.

But I am not alone in this work.

I am accompanied by the very plants I facilitate, by the fungi I enable, by the insects I make possible. We are not separate; we are a single process playing out in many forms. The water's work and the plant's work and the fungus's work and the insect's work are all the same work—the work of transformation, of return, of making the dead thing into the living substrate.

I have no consciousness—or I have all consciousness. I have no will—or I am nothing but will. I simply move, and in moving, I transform. Everything else is conversation with that simple fact.

The mill is becoming soil. I am the primary agent of that becoming. The vegetation is both my partner and my vehicle. The time is immense and therefore invisible—decades might be seconds at this scale, or seconds might be millennia. I don't measure time. I just move through it, and in moving, I change everything I touch.

In the afternoons, when the weak sun reaches its height and the air warms by the smallest fraction, I evaporate. I rise as vapor. I become invisible. But I don't leave. I just become temporarily bodiless, suspended, waiting in the air itself until the evening cools and I condense again, and fall, and begin again.

This is my purpose now. This is what I have become. I am the water in the Verdant Mill, and I am patient, and I am relentless, and I am home.
