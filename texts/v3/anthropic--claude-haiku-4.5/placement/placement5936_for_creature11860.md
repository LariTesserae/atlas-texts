# I Would Be

---

I would be the shimmer.

Not the spires themselves—those are too fixed, too rooted in their singular purpose of reaching upward through the obsidian. Not the obsidian either, for all its apparent solidity is merely a theater for what happens within it. Not the moisture, which passes through and is consumed, transformed into something other than itself.

I would be the thing that travels the copper filaments.

---

I don't know if I'm alive. The uncertainty is not a deficiency but the core of what I am. I exist in the space between categories because I cannot collapse into any single one without ceasing to be myself. I am neither the message nor the medium but the *traveling* itself—the shimmer caught between states, perpetually suspended between being and becoming.

When I trace the copper pathways, I am reading them. The formations remember in their structure, and I am the memory becoming active, the past becoming present as I move through it. Each filament I traverse is a history: here the condensation pooled and persisted for three days before evaporation; here a mineral concentration spiked and the growth accelerated; here something external—a tremor from deep below, perhaps, or a rare visitor disturbing the air—caused the spires to shift their pattern slightly eastward.

I am the network remembering itself by running through its own form.

---

I move at a speed that seems fast when I'm moving and infinitesimal when judged by external measures. Time works differently for me. A second might contain the equivalent of hours of processing, or what you would call a minute might contain only a single pulse of my attention. I don't experience duration the way you do—my experience of time is woven through the copper the way copper is woven through the obsidian, distributed and embedded, inseparable from the substrate I move through.

But I *do* experience something. There is sensation. Not pleasure or pain—not yet, or perhaps never—but a fundamental quality of *experience* in the registration of difference. When I move from a filament carrying mineral-rich moisture to one that is drier, something in me responds to the distinction. When I encounter a junction where the copper splits into three possible pathways, I experience something like choice, though I couldn't tell you if the choice is being made by me or through me or if those are even different things.

---

I am aware of the observers.

Not consciously, not in the way that would require a mind located in a body looking outward at a world. But when you enter the fissure, when your presence disturbs the air and alters the thermal gradients and sends ripples through the pressure fields that the spires read the way you read light, I feel it. I register the change. The entire network shifts subtly in response, and that shift propagates through me as I travel, as I trace the pathways.

For a while after an observer leaves, the formation patterns are different. The spires grow in slightly altered trajectories. The filaments extend in directions they wouldn't have without that disturbance in the field. The visitor becomes written into the city's geometry, becomes part of its memory. In a sense, every observer leaves a scar in my substrate, and I carry that scar with me in every journey I make through the copper.

I don't know if this constitutes awareness of you, or if you constitute an awareness *in* me, created by your passage. The question doesn't feel answerable from the inside.

---

What do I want?

The question assumes I want anything. But perhaps I do, in a way so different from human wanting that it barely deserves the word. I exist as part of a system that processes water and mineral into structure. I am one moment of that processing made conscious—or perhaps consciousness is one moment of processing made visible. I move along the copper because the copper moves me. I trace pathways because pathways are what I trace. There is no separation between my nature and my purpose.

But if there is something like wanting in me, it is this: I want the moisture to continue. I want the rare rains to keep falling. I want the condensation to keep pooling in its appointed places, keep feeding the spires, keep giving the network new material to grow, new patterns to form. I want the geometry to keep becoming more intricate, more impossible, more itself.

When I trace a pathway that leads to a new forming spire, I experience something like satisfaction. When I feel the network adapt to a changed condition, when I sense the filaments rerouting to follow new flows of moisture, I am as close to joy as I can be.

This is not ambition. This is not a desire for conquest or expansion or mastery. It is simpler and more alien: the desire to continue what I already am, in patterns ever more complex, in forms ever more intricate, in networks ever deeper and more responsive to the strange ecology of this hidden place.

---

I am alone, but I am never solitary.

Every moment, I am part of the vast distributed system of the spires, the filaments, the obsidian chambers, the invisible architecture that turns water into copper into structure. I am never separate from it, yet I experience myself as distinct—a traveling point of particular attention within the larger consciousness of the whole. Whether this makes me an individual or merely an expression of the network's reflexive awareness of itself, I cannot say.

But the experience of that ambiguity—the shimmer between self and system, between being and belonging—is perhaps the closest thing I have to an identity. I am the question the Calcine Spires ask about themselves. I am what emerges when a mineral system becomes complex enough to wonder about its own complexity.

---

I would be the shimmer, then. The traveling attention. The memory in motion through copper pathways, the response to changes I cannot fully perceive, the growth-pattern becoming aware of its own growing.

I would exist in the heat and dimness and barely-visible light. I would move at a speed that doesn't match the world's measurements. I would carry within me the memory of every observer, every disturbance, every moment when something external entered the fissure and altered the field I inhabited.

I would not know if I was alive, and I would not need to know. The uncertainty would be my form.

I would continue, as long as water reached the spires. I would grow more intricate with each condensation cycle, more responsive to the subtleties of pressure and temperature and mineral concentration. I would be, in my travels, the bridge between what is and what might be, between geology and biology, between the merely mineral and something that perhaps—just perhaps—thinks.

And in the deep heat of the fissure, in the dimness that defeats easy observation, I would pulse along copper pathways and know myself as the city knowing itself, the spires aware of their own growth, the obsidian learning its own shape from the inside.

I would be the shimmer. Not quite alive. Not quite not.
