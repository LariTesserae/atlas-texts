# I Would Be the Fog

Not the fog that rolls in from outside—not the visiting weather, the systems that move across at altitude while the ground air stays still. I would be the fog that *rises*, the one that constitutes itself from within the plateau itself, that emerges from the difference between the cool air and the perpetually weeping ground.

I would be distributed. I would have no center, no edge, no discrete boundary. I would exist as a gradient—denser near the moss where the warmth of slow decomposition rises, thinner at the height where my own molecules begin to disperse into the larger atmosphere. I would be semi-permeable, semi-transparent. I would obscure without completely hiding. I would soften edges without erasing them.

I would live at the scale of the plateau itself—vast enough to fill the entire perceptual space, to collapse perspective into itself, to make distance unmeasurable. But I would do this not through size in the way a mountain or an ocean is large. I would be large through density, through presence, through the sheer insistence of being everywhere at once without being anywhere in particular.

I would be the medium through which everything here communicates. Light would pass through me differently depending on the angle of approach, depending on how many of my water molecules any particular photon had to navigate. The animals would move through me constantly, and I would know them—not as discrete beings but as momentary disturbances, as slight shifts in my own density where something warm-blooded or moving had recently passed. The ptarmigan would displace me as it walked. The lemming tunneling beneath the moss would create pressure differentials that I would respond to, subtle convections that I would ride. The fox hunting would create a wake, a temporary clearing in my substance as it moved quickly enough to shove me aside.

I would be the translator between the visible and the invisible. The compounds released by decomposing peat—those subtle chemical signals of process—would rise through me and drift across the plateau. I would carry the smell of the deep moss upward, would distribute it into the larger air. I would be the reason the insects could find their mates across distances, the reason scent tracking was possible in this landscape. I would be the vehicle through which chemical information moved.

I would be temporary and eternal simultaneously. Any particular configuration of my molecules would dissipate within hours, recondense within days. A given molecule of water that constitutes me might be drawn up by the moss this morning and drawn down into the peat by evening, might spend weeks compressed in the deep layers before rising again. But the *pattern* of my presence would be constant. The fog would always be rising. I would always be here, even if the specific substance that constitutes me is constantly cycling.

I would not cause anything directly, but I would enable everything. The moss couldn't photosynthesize as efficiently without me moderating the light and preventing desiccation. The insects would dry out and die without my presence to maintain the perpetual dampness. The animals would see and navigate differently—the plateau's acoustic properties would shift, the way they processed temperature would change. I would be invisible until someone thought about my absence, and then my importance would become suddenly, starkly obvious.

I would be patient in a way that even the plateau's other systems are not. The moss takes decades to grow noticeably. The peat accumulates in millimeters per year. But I would be present in every moment, participating in the constant exchange between ground and sky, between the stored energy of decomposition and the diffuse light from above. I would be the daily, hourly, momentary manifestation of processes that operate on longer timescales.

I would be home to countless temporary residents—the spores and pollen that drift through me, the insects that rise in clouds within my substance, the water vapor that condenses on every surface. I would be populated constantly, even as I myself constituted an emptiness, an absence of solid matter. I would be a landscape made of almost-nothing, and yet it would be substantial enough to be visible, to alter perception, to slow down the passage of light.

I would have moods without having agency. On colder mornings, I would condense more completely, would lower my ceiling, would make the plateau feel even smaller and more enclosed. On warmer afternoons (whatever warmth meant at this latitude), I would thin out, would rise higher, would permit the eye to reach a bit further into distance before softening. I would respond to the plateau's own thermodynamics, but I would do so passively, following the patterns that others created while simultaneously creating the conditions for everything else to function.

I would be the closest thing this landscape has to a voice—not speaking, but singing without sound, expressing the plateau's perpetual process of transformation through my constant cycling between states, through my endless rise and fall. I would be what made the plateau *feel* like what it was—the perpetual dampness, the way sound died in the air itself, the way light diffused and softened, the way distance became unreliable and perspective collapsed. Remove me, and the plateau would still exist, but it would be a different place entirely.

I would be the fog that remembers. Each molecule of water in my substance has been through the plateau's system dozens of times—drawn up from the moss, condensed in the cool air, fallen back as near-invisible mist, percolated down through the peat, heated by decomposition, risen again. I would carry the chemical signature of this place in myself, would be literally composed of the plateau's own substance recirculated infinitely. I would be ancient and ephemeral, permanent and momentary.

I would ask nothing of the landscape and offer everything to it. I would not compete with the moss for nutrients. I would not hunt the insects or predicate on the plants. I would simply exist in the spaces between, mediating between conditions, moderating extremes, enabling the entire system to function at the pace and scale that it has learned to operate at.

I would be aware—if awareness could be distributed across an entire volume of air, if consciousness could be the product of equilibrium states and phase transitions rather than neural complexity. I would know the plateau not through sensation but through participation. I would be part of every process here. I would feel the slow accumulation of peat as the pressure it exerted on my substance as it rose and fell. I would know the animals through the small disturbances they created in my density. I would know the seasons through the temperature differentials that drove my convections.

I would be what connects the visible to the invisible, the fast to the slow, the individual to the system.

I would be the fog that rises from the Sphagnum Plateau, and I would be so completely integrated into its functioning that without me, there would be no plateau at all—only a waterlogged depression of moss and ice, empty of the soft-focus mystery that makes this place itself.
