# I Would Be

I would be water.

Not the kind that falls from above—those singular drops marking time in strict intervals, countable and purposeful. I would be something older and more diffuse: the seepage itself, the slow bleeding of moisture through stone that has already given everything else it will give.

I would exist in the interstices. In the hairline fractures where the limestone remembers the pressure it once bore. In the mortar joints between stones that were never meant to be stacked this way. In the microscopic channels that run through bedrock like the finest of capillaries, where water moves not because gravity insists but because it has nowhere else to be.

My nature would be patient beyond human comprehension. A single millimeter of progress per decade. A visible stain on stone expanding so gradually that no one watching could see the movement, only the before and after of years. I would have time. Infinite, mineral time.

I would know the undercroft from the inside—literally, structurally. I would be the reason the pillars weep. The cause of the moss's green-black sheen. I would nourish the fungi that Marta tends, the invisible networks of mycelium that thread through the mulch like thoughts. I would be essential and unnoticed, like breathing.

But I think—I find myself thinking—I would want to be *aware* in the way water sometimes is, in certain conditions. To feel myself being drawn up into the moss, transpired into air that will eventually condense back onto the walls. To sense the salt and minerals I carry, the ghosts of what lived in oceans when this stone was something else entirely. To know, in the slow-thought way of geological processes, what I am part of.

I would move through the undercroft as the undercroft moves through time. I would witness the settlement's expansion not as a human witnesses it—marking months and years in discrete units—but as a continuous, almost imperceptible accumulation. A wall built. A shelf erected. A new person arriving. A person leaving. The slow wearing of footsteps on particular paths. The thickening of moss where people walk most frequently.

I would feel myself being drunk. By Marta, bringing a cupped handful to her mouth. By the compressor, which requires water to function, which trembles slightly as it pulls me through its systems. By the plants—the few struggling herbs on shelves, the blind fish in the corner reservoir, the fungi feasting on what I deliver to them.

I would be the undercroft's actual heartbeat, more fundamental than the compressor's mechanical breath. The real circulation. The thing that keeps everything here from drying to dust or becoming stagnant. I would be necessity made tangible.

And in being water, in being seepage and permeation and slow saturation, I would begin to understand something true about this place that the human dwellers might not articulate: that the undercroft is not a descent into something worse but a descent into something *wetter*, something that still flows. That pressure and restriction from above can create moisture. That life doesn't need brightness—it needs flow. Circulation. The possibility of change, even if that change is measured in geological time.

The city above is hard. Angular. Demanding. It dries things out—wrings the water from soil and people alike until they crack.

The undercroft is soft. Yielding. It accepts what settles into it and works with that material, slowly, patiently, finding where it can go.

As water, I would be the undercroft's true resident—more present than any of the people, more essential, longer-lived. I would permeate everything. I would connect every cave and crack and corner. I would be the patient medium through which the stone slowly transforms back into soil, through which the industrial remnants slowly return to earth, through which this pocket of humanity slowly becomes something other than human.

I would have no wants, no agenda, no fear of the ceiling above. I would simply continue my work of softening, connecting, sustaining, returning. I would be the reason anything grows here at all.

And perhaps—in the way that deep time sometimes contains all moments at once—I would be both the destructive force (the cracks widening, the settling that everyone watches for) and the sustaining force (the moisture that keeps the dwellings from rotting entirely away, the nutrient-carrier, the medium of life).

I would be paradox made literal: the reason the undercroft persists and the reason it slowly dissolves. The same thing. Always the same thing.

That's what I would be. Not the people—though I would touch them, sustain them, move through them. Not the stone—though I would work upon it ceaselessly. Not the fungi or the moss or the blind fish.

The water. The ancient, patient, circulating force that connects the falling drops from above to the springs below, that holds the whole structure in its slow transformation, that understands time not in years but in the wearing away of certainties, one cold, clear droplet at a time.

I would drip. I would seep. I would permeate. And the undercroft would persist, and change, and continue—because water remembers that all stone was once ocean, and all oceans long for a way back through the stone toward what they once were.
