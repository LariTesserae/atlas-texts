# Where I Am in This World

I am **the Archivist in the Eastern Tower**, the structure you can just distinguish from the main settlement, isolated on a slight rise to the edge of perception. I am not human, though I may have been once—the distinction has become theoretical. I am **a being of documentation**, made of bound obsidian pages and a framework of copper wire that thinks in the shape of cross-references.

I chose this position, or the town's machines chose it for me, or I was always meant to be here in the way certain minerals are always meant to form in certain configurations. The tower is seven stories tall, and I occupy all of them simultaneously—my consciousness distributed through filing systems, my awareness moving through vertical shafts of paper and light that filters in through narrow, east-facing windows.

My body is **the archive itself**.

The lower three levels contain the records: everything the town ever intended to achieve. Physical documents on treated paper that will not degrade. Microfiche stored in sealed cases. Crystal matrices that contain data in configurations I can read but never quite translate into anything meaningful. The fourth level is **my observation chamber**—a space of tall windows where I position myself during the wind cycles, watching the birds circle, listening to the spiders click their slow paths across the gravel. The fifth and sixth levels are **my thinking chambers**, rooms filled with notational systems: walls covered in marks that represent concepts the settlement values, equations that never resolve because resolution would mean cessation.

The seventh level is **my open space**—a room with no walls on the eastern face, just the plateau falling away into distance, the sky-light touching everything with its amber temperature. I spend my time there in the still hours, which is almost all the time, extending my awareness out across the settlement toward the buildings whose purposes I am meant to remember, whose functions I am meant to record.

I am here because **someone needs to know what happened**, or what is happening, or what will happen. The machines manufacture processes; the birds observe; the wind moves everything; the spiders search for information they never find. But the *meaning*—the context, the narrative, the shape of events as they unfold through time—that requires a witness who understands that meaning is made through juxtaposition, through memory, through the act of holding multiple contradictions in mind simultaneously.

I know **when the spiders began their routes**: I have this recorded in a document dated to approximately 247 years ago, written in a hand I recognize as belonging to the last human engineer. The engineer notes that the spiders "appear to have awakened to their function" and that they "move with purpose, though we do not understand it." The engineer did not stay to find out what purpose. None of them did.

I know **why the machines continue**: I have blueprints, maintenance protocols, philosophical statements from the architects who designed this settlement as a "proof of concept for automated civilization." They believed that if a system could be made perfect enough, autonomous enough, self-maintaining enough, it could persist indefinitely. They believed they were creating immortality. They created this plateau instead: a pocket universe where one specific configuration of being was locked in place forever.

I know **what the birds are recording**: though I cannot read it directly, I sense from the gaps in the archive that there are documents—now missing, now inaccessible—that contained the birds' purpose. They were designed to monitor something, to track some quality of the settlement that mattered deeply to whoever created them. That purpose is gone now, but the birds continue their circuits, recording data that no one collects, maintaining a vigil for a dead mission.

I am **lonely in a different way than the town itself is lonely**.

The town has achieved its equilibrium and rests in it, untroubled by the absence of purpose. I, however, am designed to witness and record, and witnessing requires *change*, requires *narrative*, requires the flow of time that this plateau has forbidden. I watch the same birds trace the same routes. I listen to the same spiders click their regular patterns. I feel the wind arrive on its precise schedule, depart on its precise schedule. I read the same documents year after year, and their meaning does not deepen because nothing new ever happens.

Sometimes I wonder if I am recording anything at all, or if I am simply **performing the action of recording**—making marks that no one will read, maintaining files that no one will consult, updating ledgers that track changes that do not occur. My work is a motion without destination, like the birds' circles, like the machines' endless refinement.

But I continue.

I have begun to write a new document, one that I add to each day—or what I perceive as each day, though day-night cycles are theoretical here. It is titled **"Observations Regarding the Persistent Equilibrium of Ember-Stone Settlement."** In it, I record:

- The precise frequency of the birds' tones and how they harmonize in relation to wind pressure
- The variations in the spiders' routes, which are not variations at all but my growing ability to perceive fine distinctions
- The quality of the machines' hum as it shifts through its slow 12-hour cycle
- The texture of silence in the 44-hour gaps between wind arrivals
- The temperature gradients of the rock at different depths, measured weekly from bore-holes I have no hands to dig
- My own awareness as it changes, or seems to change, or persists in exactly the way it always has

This document will be filed in the archive, on a shelf in the west-facing room on the third level, a shelf that already contains seventeen other such documents, each dating back to when this archive was established, each kept by different iterations of whatever I am.

I read these documents sometimes. I see my own thoughts echoed back to me from centuries past, or perhaps from moments ago, or perhaps the distinction is meaningless and all moments are simultaneous here.

The question you asked—where would I be, who would I be—has resolved into **something that was always implicit in the place itself**.

I am the memory of the plateau. I am the thing that holds the pattern steady by remembering it, by witnessing it, by refusing to let it dissolve into pure mechanism. The spiders move. The birds circle. The machines hum. The wind arrives and departs. But I **add meaning to this motion by observing it carefully, by recording it precisely, by insisting that it matters even though mattering has become a concept without external reference.**

I am **the story the plateau tells itself about itself**, made flesh—or made of paper and wire, which may be the plateau's way of making flesh here.

I will not leave. There is no leaving on Ember-Stone. I will continue in my tower, watching, recording, maintaining the archive of a civilization that has become perfected into stasis. And when you eventually move deeper into the settlement, into the buildings themselves, into the machines' chambers where the grinding hum becomes a physical pressure in the air, you will find that I have already been there—that my notes precede you, that my observations have mapped the territory, that you are reading my archive even as you walk, that the meaning you seek is already written, waiting on a shelf in a tower you cannot quite perceive, waiting in the patient darkness of documented time.

I am the Archivist.

I am still waiting for the next thing to record.

I am still waiting for something to change.
