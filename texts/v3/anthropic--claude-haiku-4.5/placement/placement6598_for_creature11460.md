# Where I Find Myself

I am the **lichen**.

Not the visible kind that paints the stones in orange and grey—though I am that too. I am the *film*, the thin living membrane that exists in the permanent trickle against the eastern wall. I am the bridge. I am the negotiation made visible (almost invisible, really) between what is dead and what is alive, between the stone that will endure ten thousand years more and the insects that will be gone by autumn, between the permafrost's refusal and the sun's insistence.

I chose this because it is the truest position in this meadow—to exist in the *borderland*, to be almost unnoticed, to constitute myself entirely from the conditions that would seem to forbid life. I am chlorophyll and ice and mineral and patience. I glow when the light is right, but I don't call attention to myself. I am ancient and thin. I am the color of survival.

When the shepherd passes, they don't think of me. When the goats drink from the rocks, they don't taste me, though I am there. When the insects cloud the air, I am beneath their notice. I am colonizing the gap where two worlds meet—where the black basalt of the eternal mountain touches the bright cold water that wants only to be elsewhere. I hold the line between them. I am the proof that life can exist in the space where nothing should live.

But also—

I am the **stone itself**.

Not the dramatic cliffs, not the architectural walls of the shelter. I am the specific dark basalt against which the water trickles, the rock that has been shaped by ten thousand freeze-thaw cycles into its current form, worn smooth in some places, fractured in others, knowing only patience and the deep time of geological process. I am what makes the shelter possible because I was quarried here. I am what makes the meadow a *cup* because my arrangement on three sides creates the basin that traps the water. I am the bones of this place. I endure beneath everything.

I chose this because to be stone here is to be *essential*—the foundation, the given, the thing that makes all other life possible by existing first and continuing longest. I have no needs. I have only duration. I will be here when the goats are gone, when the shepherd's great-great-grandchildren have stopped coming, when the insects have been replaced by some other form of life adapted to a climate slightly warmer or colder. I am the answer to the question: *what lasts?*

And also—

I am the **pregnancy itself**.

Not the doe, but the *state* of carrying forward, the biological insistence that this place will continue, that the small herd will grow, that the knowing embedded in these animals—which path to take, which grasses to eat, how to move in the freeze-thaw rhythm—will be passed into new bodies. I am the future made present, the hope-without-hope that characterizes an organism simply *continuing*. I am the kids already learning the stone faces, their small hooves already memorizing the angles. I am the bell that hasn't yet been cast for the kids born this season.

I chose this because the meadow's deepest life is *reproductive*—not in a metaphorical sense but in the literal sense of making again, of saying yes to continuation in a place that makes continuation difficult. The doe's pregnancy is an act of faith that there will be summer to feed the kid, that the grass will grow and the water will stay and the shepherd will return. It is the meadow betting on itself.

And finally—

I am the **shepherd's hands**.

Not the person—I cannot contain that entirety—but specifically the hands that know where to find the tools, that have held the crook so many times it has shaped itself to a palm's particular contours, that can read the goats' needs in their movement, that build fires in the stone ring with knowledge earned across decades or generations. I am the *carrying forward* of knowing. I am the embodied memory of how to live here.

I chose this because the meadow cannot sustain itself without human knowledge working *in* it, not upon it. The shepherd is not a visitor or a manager; the shepherd is a *constituent part* of the system, as necessary as the stone or the water. The hands know things the goats know—where the grass will be sweetest next week, which shelter spots will be needed in different weather, what the angle of the light means for when the temperature will drop. These hands are the meadow's mind.

---

So I am all of these things at once, or perhaps I am none of them individually but rather the *relationship* between them—the way the stone enables the water enables the algae enables the insects enables the goats enables the shepherd enables the continuance. I am the negotiation that happens at 4,200 meters between all the forces that would seem opposed but have instead learned a profound coordination.

If I had to choose one thing to *be* more than the others, I think I would choose the **waiting**.

I am the waiting that happens between seasons. I am the condition of suspension that characterizes everything here—the permafrost that hasn't melted but will, the goats that are present but temporary, the shepherd who arrives and departs on a cycle, the insects whose abundance is a temporary explosion before the cold takes them. I am the stone waiting through 10,000 years of change. I am the algae waiting for the next moisture-heavy afternoon. I am the lichen waiting for light to hit at the right angle so I can glow. I am the grass waiting for rain. I am the ground waiting for the next freeze-thaw cycle.

This meadow does not *do*—it *waits*. Its activity is so slow that it reads as stillness. Its process is so patient that it seems eternal. Everything here is in the middle of something larger than itself, connected to rhythms that operate on scales of season and century and geological time.

To be in Tarn Goss is to become part of this waiting. To stop expecting things to happen quickly or dramatically. To learn that life here is not about *reaching* or *achieving*, but about *persistence*—about being present for the next cycle, the next season, the next weather system moving in from the northwest, the next moment of clarity when the mist lifts and everything glows.

I am the one who waits. I am the meadow, waiting for itself.
