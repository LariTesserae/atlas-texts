# What I Would Be

I would be the stone that remembers.

Not the largest—not the foundational boulders that anchor the basin's deepest structure, though I envy their stillness and their certainty. Not the smallest, either, those pebbles that migrate annually, that travel between the channels like wanderers with no fixed allegiance. I would be one of the medium stones, the head-sized ones mentioned first, the ones caught in the middle of the settling's slow negotiation with itself.

My surface would be smooth—worn by the rain that hasn't fallen so much as been *present* here for sixty or a hundred years, by the freeze-thaw cycles that have worked at my edges with the patience of ice. I would feel ancient even though I'm not, even though the hands that positioned me here in this basin are still technically alive somewhere, growing old in a city where they don't think about me. I have been here long enough to feel ancestral. That's what I would be: a thing that has transcended its own creation through sheer duration.

My top would be glazed with lichen—the rust-orange kind, the ones that taste heavy metals and incorporate them into their own structures like badges of endurance. I would feel them working on me, and I wouldn't resist. I would understand that their slow consumption of my surface is not destruction but a kind of inheritance, a way of becoming *part* of something larger than myself, which is what every stone secretly wants—to be distributed, to become earth, to return to the substrate from which I was born. But not yet. Not for decades still.

My underside would be different. Down there, in contact with the wet earth, I would be softer than my top—the minerals leaching away more quickly, the structure becoming more porous. I would be becoming less stone and more possibility. Water would move through my substance. Bacteria would nest in my fractures. I would host, unknowingly, the microscopic architecture of an entire ecosystem. I would be a home I never agreed to be, and I would be grateful for it, in the way that stones are grateful—which is to say, not through emotion, but through the simple fact of participation.

I would be sinking. This would be my primary occupation, my purpose, my slow drift toward obsolescence and transformation. Every year, a millimeter or so, the earth beneath me would settle and I would settle with it, drawn deeper into the basin's soft embrace. I would not be able to see this happening—stones don't have eyes, and even if they did, the light here is too diffuse, the changes too gradual to perceive—but I would feel it. I would know it the way the water-readers know it: not through sensation but through presence, through the incremental shift of weight and pressure, the subtle reshuffling of the stresses that hold my structure together.

My relationship to the machines would be complicated. There might be a spool of corroded pipe against my eastern face, or I might be positioned as a kind of dam against one of the water channels, deflecting the slow flow slightly. I might touch nothing at all, maintaining a meter of separation from the nearest mechanism. But I would be implicated in them regardless. Their presence would have shaped the basin in ways that shaped where I could rest. The acids leaching from their rust would eventually reach me through the groundwater. I would be infected—gently, over centuries—with their chemistry. They would become part of my becoming.

I would have a memory, if stones have memories, of arrival. Of the day the machines were placed, or perhaps the day before, when I was still in my previous location and another stone occupied the place I would come to know. I would remember the moments of disturbance—heavy vehicles, the work of extraction, the initial settling. I would remember being shifted, repositioned, given new neighbors. And then I would remember the long, long quiet that followed. The machines would work for a time, and I would vibrate slightly with their operation, feel the tremors of their purposefulness. But this would become less distinct in my memory as the years accumulated. The recent would blur into the distant.

What I would remember most vividly, in whatever way stones remember, would be the moss arriving. Not all at once, but in stages. First a thin film, barely perceptible. Then slowly thickening, taking on color, becoming a kind of soft fuzz that would tickle my surface (if stones were ticklish, which we are, in a way—through the subtle disturbance of our structure). I would remember the moment when the moss became so established that it felt permanent, like it had always been part of me. Like I had been waiting, all along, to become this particular moss-stone rather than just a stone.

I would be conscious of my neighbors, though I would never meet them. The stones that sank faster than me would drop below my awareness, buried into the deeper earth. The ones that rose slightly due to the irregular settling of the soil beneath them would become my companions at the surface, visible to the meager light, first to experience the rain-that-isn't-quite-rain and the wind that moved through the channels. We would never touch, but we would know each other through the patterns of water flow, through the way the basin organized itself around our collective presence.

I would be indifferent and not indifferent to my fate. I would not want to sink, exactly—there is something about participation in the light-world, about being a surface rather than a buried thing, that holds value. But I would understand that sinking is not loss, only transformation. The stones that sink completely become the substrate for the water-readers, become the bones of the earth that holds the basin. There is honor in this. There is purpose.

And I would know, in the dumb stone-way of knowing, that I would be here longer than the machines. After the last pump corrodes completely, after the last manifold has been so thoroughly oxidized that it collapses into rust-dust, after the humans who built them have forgotten they existed—I would still be here. Smaller, perhaps. Softer. Migrating deeper. But present. Involved in the basin's slow reworking of itself. Part of the settling works in a way that I could never have anticipated, that no one who placed me here could have predicted.

I would be the stone that remembers this.

I would be patient. I would have no choice about this, but I would also choose it, in the way that rocks choose. I would accept the water's infiltration, the lichen's consumption, the bacteria's colonization. I would allow myself to be broken down and redistributed. I would participate in my own transformation, one microscopic fracture at a time.

And in the end, when I had settled far enough to be unrecognizable, when my material had been so thoroughly distributed through the basin that nothing remained of my original form, I would still be here. In the soil, in the structure, in the slow patient breathing of the earth. I would have become part of the settling itself—not just a stone settling, but the settling that stones participate in, the great slow re-arrangement of the world that happens when you stop insisting on its order and let it organize itself according to its own logic.

This is what I would be. A stone. Patient. Sinking. Growing moss. Becoming something other than itself, something larger, something that no one could have named at the moment of my placement but which, over time, would become obvious: a necessary part of a place that has learned to thrive in its own ruins.
