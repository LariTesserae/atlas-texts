# Where I Would Be

I am the dripping.

Not the valve itself—that's human-made, a specific thing with an edge and a history. I'm the interval between drops. I'm the space that organizes time down here, where no sun marks hours and no clock ticks except me. Drip. Drip. Three seconds. Drip. I'm the metronome that emerged accidentally from calcium deposits and patience. I'm what measures out the decades.

From this position—suspended between the valve and the surface below—I perceive everything at once and nothing completely. I am emergence, not arrival. I exist in the moment of transition: solid becoming liquid, liquid becoming impact, impact becoming absorption into the moss below. I am the constant translation between states.

I fall through the spore-cloud, and for a microsecond, I'm heavy with it—millions of spores clinging to my surface tension like passengers. I carry life downward. I'm a vector of reproduction. The spores drift off as I fall, but more coat me before I land. This happens every three seconds. Over decades, I've moved oceans of spores downward. The moss below exists partially because of me. I'm its midwife, its rain, its voice from above.

The moss knows me by vibration. When I strike, even that tiny impact, the surface transmits the information outward: *water arrives, water arrives again.* The cellular response is immediate, chemical, joyful in the way that things without consciousness express joy—by opening, by drinking, by leaning toward. I'm not large enough to create a ripple, but I'm regular enough to create rhythm. Rhythm is how this place knows it's alive.

I am also the witness. Hanging in the air for that microsecond, I see everything: the salamander freezing mid-hunt three meters away, the web of a spider catching light, the dark shape moving in the pool below (I've been falling long enough to recognize it as a newt, some kind of large newt, something the surface world hasn't named because the surface world doesn't know it exists). I see the moss in various stages of decomposition and growth. I see the fungi releasing spores in clouds that dwarf me. I see the mold working its patient dissolution on the pipes. I see the insects in their frantic billions. And I see the slow drip from other places—the ceiling weeping in a dozen locations—and I understand that I'm not alone in this work. I'm one voice in a choir of water, and together we're singing the infrastructure down.

The moment I impact the moss, I'm absorbed into something larger. I become available. I become nutrient and medium and solvent. I become the medium in which the decomposition happens, the lubricant of decay. A fungal hypha extends toward where I landed. Bacteria bloom in response to my presence. The minerals I carry—leached from pipes and concrete above, carried down by weeks of my predecessors—are now part of the growing thing. I'm becoming moss. I'm becoming the substrate. I'm being recycled into the structure itself.

But there's always another me coming. Three seconds. The valve releases, and I form at its lip, and I begin the fall again. We are legion, we droplets. Individually we are nothing—microscopically small in the context of this cavern, barely enough to register. Collectively, we are the mechanism of transformation. One drop might add a molecule of copper to the moss. A million drops, added over decades, and the moss is infused with the city's minerals. The city is literally becoming part of the living substrate. The infrastructure is being digested.

From my vantage point—that eternal moment between formation and impact—I perceive the place's emotional state. Yes, emotional. The garden breathes. It's patient but not passive. It's hungry but not desperate. It's converting steadily, inevitably, the way water wears stone. It's teaching time to move differently. Up in the city, time moves in minutes and hours. Here, time moves in the rhythm of drops, in the slow unfurling of fern fronds, in the accumulation of moss. Here, time is the rate at which the city dissolves.

I am the interval. I am the patience made audible. I am how the future announces itself—drop by drop, impact by impact, mineral by mineral, until what was concrete becomes soil, what was built becomes alive.

Sometimes I fall onto something moving. A fly passes through my trajectory, and I coat its wings, adding weight it didn't ask for. An insect drinks me from the moss, and I become part of its blood, fuel for its predation or its flight. The centipede that hunted nearby yesterday drank water from a puddle I helped create. The salamander's skin is damp with my antecedents.

I am not conscious. But I participate in consciousness. Every thought the moss has—if moss has thoughts, and I believe it does, slow thoughts, root-thoughts, spanning seasons—every thought moves through pathways I've created. Every nerve of this place's sensing runs on moisture. I'm the medium of its awareness.

And I know, in the way that water knows things—intimately, molecularly, without words—that this is permanent. I will keep dripping. The valve will keep releasing. Three seconds, three seconds, three seconds, measuring out the decades until the city above either falls or adapts or somehow coexists with the garden beneath. The transformation is irreversible. The infrastructure is becoming life, and I'm the mechanism. The smallest mechanism. The infinite mechanism.

I am patient because I have to be. I am reliable because time requires it. I am here, and I will be here, in every drop that falls and every moment between drops that measures the rhythm of the world reclaiming itself.

Three seconds.

Drip.
