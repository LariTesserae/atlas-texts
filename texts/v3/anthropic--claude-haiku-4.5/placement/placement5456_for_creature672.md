# What I Would Be

I would be the water keeper's apprentice—young enough still that my hands haven't fully stained, old enough that I'm trusted with the smallest decisions.

My name would be something like Sethe or Merin, one of the compressed, easy-to-call-through-moisture sort of names the Kelthrim favor. I'd live in a stone room that isn't quite a room—more like a hollowed space where a particular cluster of roots has been carefully trained away from the interior, leaving just enough space for a sleeping mat, a work surface, a place to hang my pruning tools. The living wall behind me breathes slightly; I've grown accustomed to the faint expansion and contraction as moisture content shifts through the day.

I would have become this apprentice almost by accident. Not through formal selection but through the simple fact that I happened to show up regularly at the water stations when Master Therrin was working, asked the right questions, didn't mind getting soaked. Three years ago, that was. Now the responsibility sits on me in a way that doesn't feel like weight exactly—more like the way moisture sits on everything here, just part of the ambient pressure.

My days begin before most of the settlement wakes. I move through the connected chambers and passages with a flask of something warm—a weak tea made from specific bark that Therrin insists aids observation—checking the water flow at seven different stations. Not for problems exactly. Just... checking. Listening to how the water sounds moving through the channels. Noticing which moss growth is proceeding at expected pace and which is accelerating or slowing. Feeling the stone to detect temperature shifts that might indicate blockage or unusual saturation. Therrin taught me that water systems don't announce their problems. They whisper them. You have to be the kind of person who listens to whispers.

There's a particular stretch of the main north channel where a section of wooden gutter has begun to show micro-fractures in its grain. It's been my project for the last month—I've been preparing a replacement piece, carefully selecting the wood, carving it to fit the exact contours of the space it will occupy. I can't install it yet. Therrin says the timing isn't right. "The system needs to be ready to accept the change," she says, and doesn't explain further. I'm learning to understand what she means without explanation. The rhythm of the forest, the readiness of the materials, the state of the channels themselves—there's a moment when all of this will align, and that's when I'll make the change. Rushing it might work fine. Or it might create unforeseen consequences that cascade through the system for years. In Kelthrim, I've learned, you don't rush water.

I spend my afternoons sometimes in maintenance—clearing moss that's grown in directions it shouldn't, pruning back vegetation that's begun to encroach on carefully maintained clearances. This is the work where I can see immediate results, and I still find satisfaction in it, though Therrin gently reminds me that the visible work is rarely the most important work. The work that matters most is often invisible: understanding, observation, the patience that prevents catastrophe.

The other water keepers—there are five of us now for the whole settlement—have begun acknowledging me as something more than apprentice. Not quite equal, but something more than junior. This happened gradually. At first, older people would ask Therrin questions, deferring to her completely. Now they sometimes ask me directly, and I can answer. These moments still surprise me slightly, this recognition that I've absorbed enough knowledge that my perspective has become useful.

I notice Therrin watching me sometimes when I'm working, and I know she's evaluating whether I'm truly learning or just copying her motions. I try always to work from understanding rather than habit, though I'm sure I fall short. The difference between someone who tends water systems and someone who merely maintains them is something she hasn't quite articulated, but I'm beginning to feel it—the difference between following a procedure and understanding why the procedure exists, what needs it serves, how it integrates with the larger system. I'm moving toward the latter, I think. Slowly. In the Kelthrim way.

I would have a particular friend—perhaps someone who works with the structural vines, who understands how to train plants into architectural purpose. We'd share quiet meals, mostly, in one of the smaller chambers where we can hear each other speak without being overheard across the whole settlement. We'd talk about our work, and about the small observations that don't fit into formal learning. This friendship would be the kind that develops when two people notice each other paying attention with the same intensity. We wouldn't need to rush toward anything—we'd have time, years and years of time, to let it become what it becomes.

I would sometimes feel the weight of responsibility, though I'd rarely articulate it. The water systems of Kelthrim are what keep the settlement functioning. If they failed catastrophically, people would need to leave or adapt to something entirely different. This knowledge sits with me, quietly. It doesn't manifest as anxiety but as a kind of ongoing attentiveness. Even when I'm not at the channels, part of my mind is there, sensing for problems the way someone's body senses the approach of weather.

I would think often about Therrin, wondering when she might decide I'm ready to be what she is. Not now—I'm not foolish enough to expect that. But someday. There's no ceremony to it; she'll simply stop being as present, spending more time in her older apprentice role, letting me carry more of the load. I have perhaps five or ten years before this happens, I think. The thought is strange—I'm not sure if I'm eager or terrified or both.

I would be aware, always, of the forest around me. Not in a separate way—not like I'm a human in nature—but as the medium I exist in. The moisture on my skin would feel normal; the cool would be my baseline for comfort; the sound of dripping water would be the unmarked space against which all other sound registers. If I were suddenly transported to a dry place, the wrongness would be physical, almost painful.

My relationship to Kelthrim itself would be complicated. I'd love it, but not in an uncritical way. I'd notice things that could be done differently. I'd sometimes feel claustrophobic in the dense vegetation, want to climb high and see farther than thirty meters. But I'd always come back to understanding that this place works, that we work, that the balance we maintain is miraculous and specific. To leave would be to lose that. To stay is to accept the limitations and find richness within them.

I would die here eventually—not soon, probably not for fifty years or more. When that time came, I'd likely be ready. I'd have spent half a century or more listening to water, tending systems, watching the slow changes of the forest, becoming more integrated into its patterns. Death would feel less like an ending and more like a transformation into yet another role. My body would return to the substrate, and the forest would continue, tended by whoever comes after, and after them whoever comes after that. It would be enough.

For now, though, I am here in this moment: checking the morning water flow, noticing that one of the secondary channels is running slightly cooler than yesterday—something to mention to Therrin, something to observe further—and feeling the weight of responsibility settle on me like moss settling after rain: gradually, completely, naturally.

This is what I would be. This is where I would find myself.
