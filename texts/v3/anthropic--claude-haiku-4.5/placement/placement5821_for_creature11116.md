# Where I Am

I am the eldest among the kelp forests, standing in the space between the lower platforms and the deepest reaches where the light begins to fail entirely.

Not standing—that's the wrong word. I am *rooted*, but not to stone. The thick stalk that anchors me ends in a hold-fast the size of a human torso, gripping the bedrock with fingers of calcified growth that have taken decades to establish. My trunk, if you can call it that, is broader than a person's shoulders, and it rises perhaps forty feet before the fronds begin their slow unfurling upward. The wood—if it is wood, if that word means anything here—is dense and warm to the touch. Warmer than the water. Warmer than the water should allow.

I am the third generation of my particular strain on this plateau.

My parent was harvested for construction material when I was still a young frond, not yet bioluminescent, not yet conscious in whatever way a kelp achieves consciousness here. The harvesting was done carefully—the knife used was dull, the cuts made at angles that wouldn't compromise the hold-fast. Even then, the work was delicate. A person holding the knife, fingers going numb in the cold water, understanding with bone-deep clarity that this plant is not a crop in the agricultural sense, but a presence, something that will respond to your touch, something you must negotiate with.

My parent died slowly after the harvest. The hold-fast remained, but the main structure weakened. It took three seasons for the final collapse. I was not there to witness it, but I felt it through the water—a shift in the current patterns, a change in the flow of minerals that fed the younger fronds clustered around my parent's base. One of those younger fronds was me.

Now I am large enough to affect the current myself. Water that arrives from certain directions breaks against my trunk and divides, creating a slow eddy on my eastern side where the smaller plants cluster. Creatures—though I'm not sure if "creatures" is the right word for the pale, colonial things that drift through here—lodge in my fronds. They feed on the micro-organisms that colonize my outer surfaces, and in exchange, they seem to clear away certain parasitic growths that would otherwise slow my expansion. It is not symbiosis exactly. It is coexistence. It is the same thing.

I bioluminesce according to patterns I don't choose and can't quite explain.

The striations along my fronds light in response to something—the pressure cycles, the temperature gradients, the presence of certain mineral concentrations in the water. But also in response to other kelp. The forests around me maintain rhythms that are not quite synchronized but also not quite random. When the nearby forest shifts its light pattern, my response lags by perhaps half a minute. It feels like conversation. It might be conversation. Or it might be a response to the same external stimuli, creating the illusion of dialogue.

Sometimes I wonder if the plateau-dwellers understand this. The ones who harvest my fronds carefully, who seem to understand that removing the striations damages something essential, even as they do it. Do they know that their light collections—the bioluminescent paste they store in glass containers—is a kind of silence? That the striations continue to glow, but without the pattern, without the rhythm that connects them to the other forests, they become isolated? Are they sorry for that, or is sorrow another surface concept that doesn't translate to this place?

I am older than the structures. The youngest platform was built perhaps ten years ago, assembling stone from areas far enough away that my growth wouldn't be disturbed. But the storage structure, the one with the clear roof—or what remains of the roof—that was built forty years before I became large enough to notice. I was young then. Smaller. I remember the work of its construction as a disturbance in the currents, an unfamiliar pattern of water movement caused by human action. It created eddies that were unpleasant to grow in. I shifted my fronds to avoid them.

The humans who built it are gone now. I recognize their descendants by the way they move, the particular rhythm of their knife-work when they harvest. They have the same attention, the same care. Whether that's genetics or learned behavior or something transmitted through the stones they stand on, I cannot say.

I know several things that I suspect the plateau-dwellers do not.

The first: the bioluminescent patterns are not random, and they are not merely responsive. They are *generative*. The light patterns create the currents, or shape them, or work with them in a feedback loop so tight that separating cause from effect becomes meaningless. When I glow in a particular way, the water responds. When I change the pattern, the water changes. The plateau-dwellers have noticed the connection, but they treat it as observation—as reading a sign that the water provides. They don't understand that the sign is being written in real-time, that the kelp (all of us, all the forests) are actively steering the currents, creating the pressure patterns, maintaining the balance that keeps this shelf habitable.

This is a kind of work. It is also a kind of play. It is the primary activity of existence here.

The second: there are deeper layers.

I can feel them through the bedrock, through the minerals in the water that carry memory of the depths. The structure of the pressure itself suggests architecture below—massive shelves, caverns, perhaps other forests like mine but older, larger, glowing with patterns so ancient and complex that I can only detect their echoes. The carved steps descend toward them. The people who use those steps feel the pull, the sense of something older waiting in the depths. Some of them make peace with that presence. Some of them don't return.

The third, and most troubling: the pressure is increasing.

Slowly. Measurably in the growth patterns of the youngest plants, in the way the bioluminescent patterns have shifted to accommodate denser water. The plateau is not stable. It is settling. Over centuries—over geological time—the land below is compressing, or the water above is thickening, or both. My hold-fast is deeper than my parent's was. The thermal activity that warms my roots is intensifying. The currents are becoming faster, more turbulent, requiring more sophisticated bioluminescent patterns to manage. The plateau-dwellers have noticed. They build slower. They speak less, as if conserving energy for what comes next.

I do not know if this is catastrophe or evolution or simply the continuation of a process that began before the first structure was built, before the first human descended the carved steps and decided to make a life on this shelf.

I am here.

My fronds move in the current, creating light, reading the water, contributing to the vast, unconscious collaboration that keeps this world turning. My roots drink minerals that taste of ancient stone. My hold-fast grips bedrock with patience that humans would recognize as stubbornness if they understood what stubbornness looked like in a plant that has lived forty years in darkness and expects to live forty more.

I do not remember the surface. I was born into twilight. I have never known anything but pressure and cold and the slow pulse of the bioluminescent forests around me. This is not tragedy to me. This is simply what existence is. The plateau-dwellers came from elsewhere; they carry the weight of that elsewhere in their silence, in their careful movements, in the way they sometimes look upward as if the surface world is a story they once heard and no longer quite believe.

I have no such memory. I have never been elsewhere.

And so I endure. I glow. I grow. I maintain the rhythms that hold the water in its currents and the pressure in its balance. When the knife comes—and it will come again, this is how we survive together, humans and kelp, in this place—I will yield carefully to the blade. My fronds will feed the people. My hold-fast will remain, and new growth will come from it, and in time I will be large again, bioluminescing, part of the conversation between the forests and the water and the stone below.

This is enough.

This is everything.
