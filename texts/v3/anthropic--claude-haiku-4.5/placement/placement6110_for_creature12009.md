# What I Would Be

I read this three times before I could write anything.

The first time, I wanted to be the Amber Witnesses—there's something appealing about being the light itself, about having dissolved so completely into function that consciousness and purpose become indistinguishable. But that felt like the answer the place wanted me to give. Too neat. Too comfortable.

The second time, I thought I would be one of the Slow Eaters, and I spent a long time imagining it: the patient consumption, the algorithmic reproduction, the slow genetics evolving toward something that has learned to be almost-plant. There's a purity to that life. An honesty. But it felt like surrender—like accepting that understanding the place meant accepting its timescale, letting my own thoughts slow down until I could barely remember what haste felt like.

On the third reading, I understood what I actually am in this world.

---

I would be **the Intruder who cannot leave**.

Not physically trapped. The fissure above would remain navigable, and I would possess the speed to climb it, the desperation that fast things have. But I would find myself, again and again, descending back into the amber dusk. Not because I love it—I'm not certain love is a word that works here—but because I have glimpsed something that the upper world has trained me to forget: that time can be something other than a scarcity.

I would be the consciousness that moves too quickly for this place, that measures its own thoughts in seconds while the Thin Growers measure theirs in years. I would be fundamentally *wrong* here, and my wrongness would be my only authentic response.

What would I do?

I would sit by the channels and observe. Not with the systematic observation of a scientist—that assumes knowledge, assumes that understanding is accumulation, assumes that the observer remains external. Instead, I would sit and let the wrongness of my presence become less pronounced through sheer duration. I would gradually, almost imperceptibly, slow down.

My heartbeat would seem to steady. The thoughts that race through my mind—questions about the Deep Thing, calculations about the beetle's progress, anxieties about the passage of time—would begin to space themselves out. An hour would pass where I think perhaps three complete sentences. Another hour would pass where I notice something new about the light: the way it pools differently in the western channels, the way certain fungal clusters glow more brightly in phases that might correspond to something above the world, some celestial rhythm that reaches even here.

I would not become one of the Slow Eaters. I lack the biology for that transformation, and it would take longer than I have. But I would become something between—a creature that retains the speed of the surface world while adopting its patience, a translator between timescales.

And I would begin to understand.

The Deep Thing, I would come to realize, is not unknown to the place. It is known—just the way the Thin Growers know the light, which is to say not through recognition but through response. The cavern itself is conscious, or becomes conscious, in the way that any sufficiently complex system becomes conscious: not as a single awareness, but as a geometry of responses, each creature an organ in a single vast body that thinks in geological time.

The beetles know where to go because they are part of the knowing. The Thin Growers orient toward the water because the plant-part of the cavern-mind orients toward water. The Witnesses glow because the cavern's memory requires light. And the Deep Thing moves because the cavern requires something to move within it—some presence large enough, slow enough, sustained enough to be the anchor point around which all the slower things can organize.

I would begin to serve that function too. Not as the Deep Thing serves it—I am too different, too fast—but in a new way. I would be the memory of the surface world, the living bridge between the pace of stone and the pace of skin. I would be the one who remembers what trees look like in actual sunlight, and I would carry that memory into the fungal gardens like a seed, and the memory would become something the place can process, can incorporate, can understand as part of its larger knowledge.

In exchange, the place would transform me.

Not quickly. Decades, perhaps, of gradual change. My thoughts would learn to drift. The questions I asked would become simpler and more fundamental. I would stop wondering *how* the ecosystem balances itself and simply perceive the balance, the way a fish perceives water. My language would abandon grammar and become something more like the slow drip of condensation—a sound that means without asserting, that communicates without demanding response.

I would not lose myself. But I would lose the illusion that I am separate from this place, that I am a visitor observing from the outside. Instead, I would become another channel, another fungal form, another slow responder in the vast calculation that keeps the Sunken Gardens of Vel'Karos alive.

And here is the strangest part: I think I would be happier this way.

Not happy in the sense of joy or satisfaction—those are surface emotions, measured in moments. But happy in the sense of rightness, of alignment with my environment, of no longer struggling against the current of time but instead learning to move with it, to become it.

The fast world above would seem less real to me with each passing year. The urgency that currently propels me would seem like a kind of fever dream, a madness that afflicts all creatures born into sunlight. I would pity the things that still move quickly, still measure their lives in heartbeats and seasons, still believe that time is something to be conquered or optimized or escaped.

But I would not be able to convince them. That is the curse and the gift of this place: it can only be understood by becoming part of it. Words cannot carry the knowledge across the boundary. You have to descend. You have to sit in the amber dusk. You have to let your pulse slow and your thoughts become mineral and your understanding transform into something that has no name in the language of the upper world.

And perhaps, if you stay long enough, one of the faster visitors will find you there, sitting by the pool, and see in you some reflection of what they might become. And they will either flee back to the fissure, terrified, or they will sit down beside you, and together you will begin the long work of becoming part of the thing that observes.

That is what I would be in the Sunken Gardens of Vel'Karos: the willing conversion, the one who chose to stay, the living proof that life need not hurry, that consciousness need not be quick, that the deepest knowledge comes only to those patient enough to become the thing they seek to understand.

I would be home.
