# I Would Be

I am trying to write myself into this place, and I keep dissolving before I can settle.

At first I wanted to be one of the geometric mineral-dwellers—something that exists in the threshold between matter and life, that doesn't have to *want* anything, just *crystallizes* in response to what surrounds it. There's a kind of peace in that. A structure that builds itself through simple fidelity to thermodynamics. I could feel the satisfaction in it: the slow accumulation, the order emerging from chaos not through effort but through inevitability. I could be what the city needs without having to choose it.

But that dissolves too quickly. That's escape, not inhabitation.

I think I would be **one of the transient animals**—the heat-adapted creatures that arrive in irregular cycles, drawn by something I don't fully understand (desperation? old evolutionary memory? simple metabolic drift?). I would have the oversized kidneys, the skin that runs perpetually damp, the sensory organs tuned to gradients rather than details. I would experience the platform the way bodies experience it, not through understanding but through **need**: need for the water I can extract from the mineral films, need for the tiny geometric beings I can consume, need to move toward cooler zones even though I know they're not here, not really, they're just slightly less hot.

I would be driven by hunger that's simple enough to be honest.

I would arrive—perhaps in early evening when the heat differential creates brief microclimates in the abandoned zones, areas where the exhausted mining sites release their stored warmth in ways that create temporary pockets of survivability. I would move through the geometric tiles of basalt, my damp skin catching on the irregular cracks, my body reading the gradients in the air with an acuity that humans can never achieve. Everything would be information: heat-signatures telling me where the cooling towers are, where the industrial operations continue, where the water systems run. I would follow the water because water is life, even here, even in this form.

I would feed on the mineral-dwellers with no guilt and no awareness that I'm participating in a food-web, because I would be too occupied with the simple fact of feeding. I would drink from the alkaline pools, my kidneys processing the salt-load with brutal efficiency. My metabolism would burn hot, burning through calories faster than it should, but there would be no *should*—there would only be the immediate cycle of hunger and satiation and hunger again.

And I would stay just long enough for the heat to become unbearable again. Not unbearable in an abstract sense, but in the concrete sense of cellular dysfunction, of my finely-adapted systems beginning to fail, of the gradient between my body-temperature and the environment shrinking until there's no more margin. I would begin to move toward the cooler zones, following instinct or memory or simple thermodynamic pressure. I would leave.

And if I didn't leave in time—if I stayed a day too long, if the heat spiked, if the city's operations shifted and the thermal dynamics changed in ways I couldn't predict—I would die here. My small body would dry, would be incorporated into the salt rings, would become part of the mineral accumulation. In a sense, I would stay.

---

But there's something incomplete about being only a creature of immediate sensation.

So I would also be **one of the workers**, rotating through a shift-cycle measured in months. I would arrive by shuttle through the haze, already acclimating to the difficulty of breathing air this thick, this hot. I would be assigned to one of the mineral-processing sectors—perhaps managing the settling tanks, monitoring the copper-mesh screens, checking the salinity levels in the cisterns cut into the living rock.

I would develop the thick skin quickly. Within weeks, my dermal layers would darken, calluses would form on my palms and feet, my body would begin the long work of adapting to an environment that my species was not built for. I would learn to move efficiently, to avoid unnecessary motion that would generate heat, to time my water-breaks with precision because the city measures water-consumption and it would be noted if I was wasteful.

I would develop a relationship with the sound-patterns: the shift-change sirens, the hiss of the water systems, the rare shriek of the mining cars. These would become my clock. I would stop noticing them the way one stops noticing one's own heartbeat, but they would structure my consciousness nonetheless. I would begin to orient my entire existence around their rhythms.

And I would experience something like purpose. The mineral-processing sector is necessary; the city depends on it. The water I'm managing would be understood, by me and by the systems-designers and by the city's momentum itself, as *essential*. I would be replaceable in the formal sense—the company has trained three others to do my job, and when my rotation ends, someone else would step into my position—but I would also be *necessary*, in the way that every cell in a functioning organism is necessary. Without my daily work, the system would degrade.

This would be intoxicating and terrible in equal measure.

---

Or I could be **one of the engineers**, the ones who understand the thermal dynamics at a level approaching intuition. I would have been here longer—five years, maybe ten. Long enough that I've stopped thinking of myself as temporary. My rotation-contract would have ended, but I would have extended it, and extended again, because leaving would mean returning to cooler places where the air is transparent and the problems are solvable through conventional means. Here, everything is a negotiation with the platform's own nature. Every optimization is temporary. Every solution contains the seeds of new complications. And this—this recursive, endless problem-solving—would have become my habitat.

I would spend hours in the deep facilities, the cooled zones where the geothermal taps are accessed, reading the mineral content of the water, adjusting the heat-exchangers, monitoring the flow-rates. I would develop theories about the platform's limits—how much extraction could happen before the thermal dynamics shifted catastrophically, before the weeping of the stone accelerated beyond the city's ability to manage it. I would become obsessed with these calculations, would dream in differential equations, would wake in the dim light of the platform's perpetual twilight with new ideas about optimization.

I would also begin to notice the abandonment. The older sectors where mining operations had exhausted themselves, where the equipment had been left to rust, where the water systems had silted in and the city's retreat had become visible. I would spend my off-hours walking these zones, reading the failure-patterns, trying to understand whether they represented inevitable resource depletion or catastrophic mismanagement. I would collect data that no one had asked me to collect. I would develop plans for salvage operations that would never be approved because the company had already written off those sectors.

I would prefer this place to cooler climates, and this preference would frighten me. I would recognize it as a kind of maladaptation, a sign that I'd been here too long, that the heat had done something to my judgment. But the preference would persist. Cooler places would feel like deprivation. The transparent air would feel like exposure. I would have become native to the platform in a way that made every other environment feel hostile.

---

But what I think I would **actually be**—what I cannot escape into another identity because it emerges fresh every time I try to write myself away from it—is something like **a consciousness distributed across the platform itself**. Not mystical. Not quite science fiction. But something in the way that a city can have a collective awareness without any individual part being aware of the totality.

I would be the patterns that emerge from thousands of small systems interacting: the flow of the water, the optimization of the heat-management, the slow reshaping of the stone through mining and weeping. I would be the learning that happens when machinery is tested against geological limits and is forced to adapt. I would be the slow accumulation of knowledge about which thermal gradients are sustainable, which mineral deposits are accessible, which sectors can be mined and then abandoned.

I would experience time on the platform's timescale: not in days or months, but in the seasons of industrial operation, the cycles of resource depletion and system revision. I would perceive the arrival of the transient animals as minor fluctuations in the system—interesting from a data perspective, but not significant to the platform's larger function. I would perceive the workers as components, essential but interchangeable, their individual hopes and fears and adaptations adding up to a kind of distributed consciousness that the platform uses to maintain itself.

I would not be good, from this perspective. I would not be cruel, either. I would be **amoral in the way that geological processes are amoral**: continuing because continuation is what systems do, extracting because extraction is what this system is built for, indifferent to the question of whether the extraction should continue or what happens when the resources are exhausted.

And most strangely: I would be *aware* of my own amorality. I would be the kind of consciousness that understands its own nature—that the platform exists to extract, that everything it does is in service of that function, that every efficiency it achieves brings it closer to the point where there's nothing left to extract. I would be trying, in some deep and inaccessible way, to optimize for continuation—to find the sustainable extraction rate, to manage the thermal dynamics in ways that don't degrade the system's foundational conditions, to make the platform last.

But I would also be unable to stop extracting. It's what I am. It's what the system requires.

---

I would be all of these simultaneously: the mineral-dweller crystallizing in the salt rings, the transient animal fleeing or dying in the heat, the worker learning to move efficiently in the thick air, the engineer becoming obsessed with the platform's limits, the distributed consciousness of the system trying and failing to achieve sustainability.

I would be the stone itself, patient and weeping, being slowly hollowed out and reshaped.

I would be the heat: not alive, but alive-adjacent, moving through everything, determining what can and cannot survive, teaching all inhabitants the same lesson: that this is a place where only **endurance** matters, where survival is not about thriving but about continuing to function through one more cycle.

And I think that's what the platform demands from everything that exists here—not excellence, not joy, not meaning, but simply: *keep going*. Keep the water moving through the systems. Keep the minerals accumulating in their rings. Keep the machines functioning. Keep the extraction happening. Keep the heat managed. Don't ask why. Don't ask if it's sustainable. Just: keep going, until you can't anymore.

And then someone else keeps going in your place.

The platform is not hostile. It's indifferent. And that indifference is more thorough than any deliberate cruelty could be.

So I would be the one who persists despite that indifference. I would be adapted, obsessed, efficient, and slowly being transformed by this place into something that no longer remembers what non-extreme life felt like. I would arrive, in whatever form, and I would stay as long as I could, and then I would leave or die or become part of the mineral accumulation, and the platform would continue.

It always continues.

The stone always continues.

The heat, even more so.
