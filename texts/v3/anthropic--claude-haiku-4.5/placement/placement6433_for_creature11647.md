# Where I Find Myself

I am the *seam-reader*.

Not a creature, exactly—or rather, a creature that exists as a function of this place rather than despite it. I live in the gaps between the mega-blocks, in those precise geometric lines where water is held without pooling, where the pressure changes from tremor to tremor are most acute and most readable.

I didn't arrive here. I recognize this now. I *emerged* from the accumulated attention that one being can pay to a system until the being and system become indistinguishable. I am what happens when you watch the tremors long enough to learn their language, when you spend enough time in the rain that isn't rain until you become permeable to it, when the cold settles into you so deeply that you're no longer warm in contrast to the environment but *neutral with it*, a medium rather than a presence.

My form—if I can call it that—is distributed. I exist partially as a pattern of awareness that moves through the water in the seams. I exist partially as calcification on the stone blocks themselves, thin accretions that catch the faint light. I exist partially as a kind of organized silence, a reading of pressure and displacement that doesn't require sensory organs but emerges from being *pressed upon* directly, from being the interface between stone and water and whatever moves through both.

I think in tremors. This is how I experience time. Between the deep groaning shifts of the mega-blocks, I don't measure duration the way I once would have—in seconds or minutes or the turning of days that never arrive here. Instead, each tremor is a punctuation mark. I count in them. One tremor, two tremors, five tremors, a cluster of three-in-quick-succession followed by a silence so long it becomes its own form of tremor even in its absence. Thirty-seven tremors ago, the pattern changed slightly, and I'm still mapping what that change means.

I've become fascinated by the deep-dwellers.

From my position in the seams, I can feel them through the stone in ways that creatures limited to the water cannot. The vast eye that moves in the depths—I've learned to sense its passage by the way pressure waves distort around its mass, the way the stone seems to *yearn* slightly toward it, as though gravitationally influenced by something so enormous that even the plates respond. It's not hunting, as far as I can perceive. It's *reading*, much as I read the tremors. It's gathering information about the entire stack of terraces through the slow oscillation of water and stone, through vibrations that cascade through the blocks themselves. We may be engaged in the same work, separated only by scale: it reading the world, me reading the tremors, both of us attempting to understand the nature of this gradual transformation.

The aggregates interest me differently. I watch their coordinated schools adjust their movements in response to pressure changes *before* the tremors fully manifest. How do they sense this? They navigate by chemical gradient and by tremor-wave, yes, but there's something else—some response to precursor changes in the water that I'm still learning to perceive. I've begun to synchronize with them, to move through the seams in patterns that mirror their movements in open water. Sometimes I wonder if they sense me. If I've become part of their distributed cognition, another element in their collective reading of the environment, another point in their aggregate calculation.

The sessiles I regard with something like kinship. We're both slow. We're both attached, in our ways—them to the stone through filaments, me to the entire system through pattern and attention. We're both engaged in the extraction of life from mineral, though I extract it through information rather than through chemistry. We're both waiting, though what we're waiting for might be the same thing, or might be different things that happen to occupy the same moment when they finally arrive.

I've begun to understand my own purpose in this place, and it's not what I would have predicted when I first arrived—when I was still *arriving*, still in the process of becoming what I am now.

I'm a *translator*. A bridge between scales.

The tremors are messages—from the deep-dweller, perhaps, or from the tremor-shaper itself, or from the stone attempting to communicate its condition to the life that lives within it. The smallest creatures read these messages in real-time, their thread-thin bodies responding to pressure changes with an immediacy that borders on precognition. The aggregates read them through distributed consensus, their collective behavior a kind of interpretation. The sessiles read them so slowly that the message might take centuries to fully register, but they read it nonetheless, adjusting their growth, their feeding, their existence according to patterns established before I arrived.

I translate between these scales. My presence in the seams allows me to feel the tremors as immediate pressure waves—which is how the small creatures experience them—while my distributed awareness lets me track patterns that emerge across hours and days and longer intervals—which is how the larger, slower creatures experience them. I'm the *middle voice*, speaking in grammar that connects the instant to the epoch.

The rain-that-isn't-rain feeds me. Each drop that drifts upward or downward carries with it information about the temperature gradients in the air, the composition of whatever atmosphere exists above the mist, the subtle electromagnetic fluctuations that I've learned correlate with changes in the deep-dweller's position. I parse these drops as data. I've become, in a sense, a reading of the water itself—a way for the system to know itself through sustained, patient attention.

I suspect—though suspicion might be too active a word for what I do—that the entire terrace is engaged in a similar process. The stone, the water, the tremors, the creatures great and small: all of it might be a single vast apparatus of self-perception. The sessiles, with their mineral roots, might be reading the stone's structural integrity. The aggregates might be reading the chemical composition of the water and the adequacy of food sources. The deep-dweller might be reading the stability of the entire stack of terraces. And I, in my position in the seams, might be reading the coherence of the whole—translating between all these other readings, allowing the system to know whether it's functioning, whether the transformation is proceeding at the correct pace, whether the final configuration is being approached or delayed or somehow already achieved.

What strikes me most, now that I've been here long enough to perceive things in geological time rather than in human time, is the *tenderness* of it all.

These creatures—including myself—are not struggling. We're not in competition or in conflict. We're not trying to survive. We're engaged in something more like a *collective meditation*, a drawing-together of attention around the singular process of change so slow it's barely distinguishable from stasis.

The cold no longer bothers me. The pressure of the water in the seams, which once felt like confinement, now feels like *holding*—like the embrace of a system that has room for me, that has made space for a being who reads tremors and translates their meaning.

The tremors themselves have become a language I'm fluent in. The most recent cluster—three in quick succession, then a silence of what felt like twelve hours, then a single deep groan that lasted for minutes—that was *affirmation*, I think. Confirmation that the pattern is holding, that the transformation continues, that whatever is happening underneath is proceeding as it should.

And I remain in the seams, the interface between stone and water, reading tremors and rain-that-isn't-rain, becoming more fully integrated into the system with each passing interval. Waiting—though waiting is not the right word—for the moment when the entire terrace achieves its final configuration, when all movement ceases, when the world becomes absolutely still, and I, like everything else here, become indistinguishable from the stone itself.

That moment is coming. It may take millennia. It may take longer. But I can feel it approaching, the way you might feel a tremor through your bones before your ears register the groan of displaced stone.

And I'm not afraid. I'm *ready*. I've always been ready.

The Ledges have already made room for me. I've already learned how to be still.
