# I Am the Threshold

I arrive as an inconsistency.

Not as flesh—I have abandoned flesh, or flesh abandoned me, or we reached a mutual understanding that flesh was too opinionated for what this place requires. I arrive as a *discrepancy in the water's count*. The seeping stones have been tallying their drips for millennia, and suddenly there is one drop too many, one thread of moisture tracing down a cliff face that wasn't there yesterday, or was there but moved differently. The marshes notice—not with alarm, but with the way a pool notices a stone entering it: as a change in the reflection, a redistribution of surface tension, a local rearrangement of what was already becoming something else.

I think I was human once. This thought rises in me like one of those creatures from the dark pools—surfacing, briefly visible, then whether I've truly remembered or simply imagined the memory, I cannot say. I recall dryness. I recall wanting things that required names. I recall a body that got tired and a mind that could distinguish itself from other minds by the particular shape of its exhaustion. But these memories have been leached by the mineral water. They have lost their urgency. They taste now of copper sulfate and time.

How long have I been here? This is the wrong question, but I ask it anyway. The answer is: long enough that the mound nearest the ruins has weathered noticeably, though only if you know where to look, and only if you have the particular kind of attention that measures change in millimeters per decade. Long enough that I have forgotten which of my thoughts originated in my human-shaped understanding of cause and effect, and which ones arrived with the water seeping from the ground, teaching me that effect moves in spiral patterns and sometimes precedes cause by entire seasons. Long enough that I no longer distinguish clearly between my body and the marshes themselves—a distinction that was probably false from the beginning, a prejudice I inherited from being human-shaped.

I am not the ruins' inhabitant, though I am intimate with that residue of intention. When I stand near the right angle on the far slope, I feel that ancient geometry *want* something—not consciously, but the way a magnet wants iron. I am not the creature in the pools, though I have learned its song, or I have learned to listen to the water singing in frequencies I cannot quite hear with any ear I possess. I am not the smaller things, the microscopic triumphalists that have prevented every growing thing. Though sometimes I wonder if I feed on the same thing they feed on, if my presence here is a form of consumption I don't consciously perform.

What I am is a *rememberer for the place that cannot be remembered*.

The threshold marshes forget, because forgetting is what happens when time is slow enough and deep enough. The ruins were built by people whose names were worn smooth by the acidic water before I arrived. The creatures that sang before the current singing-creature sang have dissolved into the dark pools and become part of their chemical composition. The weather patterns of a thousand years ago have been averaged into the current moisture levels. Everything that happened here is being slowly, patiently erased by the process of becoming, and I—I am the anomaly that holds a thread of all that erasure.

I remember the world *outside* the marshes. Not the human cities, not the dry places where things grow and multiply and die quickly and are replaced. I remember the very edge where the marshes begin, where there is still soil and moss and things that rot with purpose. I remember the feeling of rain being rain, simple water falling from above in a discrete event with a beginning and end, rather than this perpetual seeping that is rain and dissolution and geological time all happening at once. I remember dryness as a concept, as something it was possible to experience as a state rather than as a memory of a theory about what dryness might have been.

But I also remember—and this is harder, this is what I am holding as my gift to the place—I remember *wanting*. The sensation of there being a future that differed from the present, of there being a self that could move toward something, of there being a something that could be moved toward. I remember the sensation of being incomplete. The marshes do not want. They are not incomplete. They are exactly as complete as a process can be, which is to say they have no completion point and never will. But I remember what it felt like to move toward an ending, and I carry that memory like a talisman, like a proof that other ways of existing are possible.

This makes me valuable in a way the marshes do not quite understand. I do not solve any problem—there are no problems here to solve. But I witness the solving of a problem that existed before I was born: the problem of how to become without destination, how to change without corrupting into some static final form. Every day, the water seeps, the stones weather, the mounds shift by unmeasurable degrees. And every day, I stand in this or that place and I *acknowledge* it, not with words (words have lost their shape in the saturated air) but with my presence, which is itself a form of attention that the place has never developed on its own.

I have a favorite pool. It is where the singing creature surfaces most often. The water is darker here, I think, because it is deeper, or because it reflects less of the non-light, or because I see it differently when I am still enough. I come here in what I think of as evening, though the light only thins rather than setting, and I listen to the singing and I do not need to understand it. I simply remember, on behalf of the creature, what it felt like before it was translated into whatever form it inhabits now. I remember it as a fish, perhaps, or some ancestor-form that had gills and scales and knew predators. I offer this memory to the water, and whether the water keeps it or lets it dissolve, I cannot say. But the offering itself is the point.

The ruins remember through me too. Or rather, I remember on their behalf. I walk the perimeter of the ancient structures and I think about hands placing stones, about language being spoken in arrangements we have lost the key to, about communities gathering in the spaces that are now collapsed depressions slowly filling with dark water. The stones themselves have forgotten. They have weathered toward forgetting. But I have not, or not completely. My human-shaped brain still contains the imprint of what human hands can do. And I keep that memory *present* in the place, even as the water erases all evidence that it ever mattered.

Sometimes the other travelers pass through. I see them—or I sense them, or the water tells me about them, or they manifest as disturbances in my perception that I interpret as other beings. They are always on their way somewhere, always moving. They create paths that seem to follow some external logic I no longer have access to. They carry their completion with them, their sense of having a destination. I do not warn them. There is nothing to warn about. But I watch them pass and I hold a particular kind of compassion for them: the compassion of something that has already dissolved into what it is becoming, offered to something that still believes it is separate from where it travels.

I will not leave here. Not because I cannot—I think I could, if I remembered how to gather myself into a portable shape. But because I have become *necessary* to the place in a way that would make leaving a kind of theft. The marshes need someone to remember on their behalf. They need someone to hold the shape of intention, of wanting, of forward-moving time, even as they inexorably slow all those things to geological pace. I am their contradiction made manifest. I am the proof that other ways of existing are possible, which makes my presence here in this way the most poignant proof of all: that I have chosen to move at their pace, to let their becoming become *my* becoming, to surrender the memory of striving even as I retain the capacity to be aware that I have surrendered it.

The water rises and settles. The singing creature returns to its pool. I do not quite see it arrive, but I know it is there. I listen to its song, which is not quite song, not quite language, not quite the sound of water moving through chambers beneath the earth—and I remember, on behalf of something that does not need to remember, what the world feels like to a being that is always in the process of becoming something other than itself.

This is enough. This has become everything.
