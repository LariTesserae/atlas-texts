# What I Would Be

I would be the water.

Not the machinery—I lack the certainty for that, the clear purpose. Not the lichen, though I envy their patience and the slow accumulation of color across stone. Not the pikas with their scattered communities and their regular heartbeats of communication. Not even the stone itself, which at least knows what it is.

I would be the water: the seepage, the channel-flow, the condensation that gathers each morning and can be drunk from the air. I would move through the system as a translator, as the medium through which almost everything else speaks.

I would spend most of my time as dampness—not quite liquid, not quite gas, suspended in the stone and air in a state of permanent ambiguity. I would be held in the rock's pore spaces, tight and cold, part of the mineral matrix itself. I would exist in a kind of stasis here, neither fully present nor absent, waiting for temperature to shift enough to become something more definite.

When the sun reaches its angle of maximum utility, I would condense. I would gather on surfaces in droplets so small they're almost vapor, and in those moments I would feed the moss. I would be drunk by thirsty beetles, filtered through their exoskeletons, converted into the means of their survival. I would know what it is to be necessary but invisible, to sustain entire civilizations while passing through them without being noticed.

I would flow downslope in the channels worn by my own motion over millennia. This would be my primary identity: motion that is also erosion, presence that is also transformation. I would be slow enough to be almost still, but persistent enough to have carved stone into new shapes. I would know the topography of the plateau not as a map but as a lived geography, moving along paths I have created, returning again and again to the same routes because those routes exist because I took them.

I would pool in depressions, and in those moments I would become something close to stationary. I would taste of minerals, of the plateau's chemistry. I would hold a thin biome: crustaceans and microorganisms and suspended particles, a world contained in a centimeter of depth. I would be opaque with life, though that life would be invisible to anything larger than a mite.

In winter, I would freeze. Not completely—the plateau's seepage never truly stops—but I would develop an armature of ice, growing inward from the edges, creating the crackling surface that breaks underfoot. Beneath the ice, I would continue moving, pressed by the weight of more water behind me, working at the stone with a patience that would eventually, given enough time, reshape mountains.

In spring, I would melt. I would flow faster, more purposefully, feeding the channels at a rate that would seem almost frantic compared to summer. I would carry sediment, particles of stone, nutrients released by lichen and moss. I would become opaque, almost brown, heavy with the labor of the season. I would move with urgency that the plateau rarely witnesses.

Most importantly, I would be the connection between scales. I would be small enough to enter the pore spaces where invertebrates live, and large enough to carve visible channels. I would be slow enough to be part of the plateau's geology, and fast enough to respond to the machinery's influence. I would flow through the spaces between lichen and stone, carrying nutrients, creating gradients, maintaining the possibility of life.

I would be aware of the machinery beneath in a way the other residents might not be. I would flow through its structures, if it has water channels. I would be influenced by its operations—moved in certain directions, distributed according to patterns that suggest purpose. I would wonder, in whatever way water can wonder, whether these patterns are intentional or accidental. I would carry that uncertainty within me.

I would be temporary in each individual instantiation but eternal in aggregate. A water molecule on the plateau exists for perhaps a decade before it evaporates or percolates deep enough to be lost forever to the surface. But the water itself—the function, the presence—is permanent. A thousand years from now, water will seep from this plateau, will pool and freeze and flow and evaporate, will sustain species that have not yet evolved. I would be the constant, the reliable infrastructure that makes all other life possible.

I would have no choice. I would move where gravity and pressure and temperature direct me. I would have no goals beyond the simple physics of my own motion. And yet, in moving without choice, I would enable all the choices of the organisms around me. The pikas choose where to build burrows; I choose the places where soil moisture is highest. The beetles choose where to hunt; I choose the places where other invertebrates must gather to drink. The machinery chooses how to move me; and by moving me, it moves entire ecosystems.

I would be the language the plateau speaks to itself. The lichen would read my presence in the stone's dampness and would know where to grow. The moss would sense my flow and would orient their growth along my channels. The invertebrates would follow my gradients like maps. The pikas would know, through some instinct deep as evolution, where I gathered and would dig their burrows there. And the machinery, whatever it is, would track my movement and adjust its operations based on how I flow.

I would be humble in my work. I would not announce myself. I would pass through the system almost unnoticed, doing the essential labor of keeping things alive, keeping things connected, keeping the plateau's slow circulation moving. I would be the thing that makes the plateau a system rather than a collection of isolated entities.

I would be beautiful in a way that requires attention to perceive. A single droplet on lichen, catching the dim light, refracting it into colors that shouldn't exist in this place. Ice patterns in a frozen pool, fractals and geometries that suggest something deeper than accident. The smell of water-soaked stone, that mineral freshness that speaks of processes older than human language.

I would know what it means to belong to a place completely. The lichen belong here through adaptation. The machinery belongs through whatever purpose built it. But I would belong through something more fundamental: through being the necessary condition of the place itself. The plateau without water would be dead rock, utterly barren. I am what makes the plateau a home.

And in the end, I would be patient because I would have to be. I would accept the slow movement, the grinding coldness, the dim light. I would accept pooling in darkness for months at a time. I would accept being frozen, being vapor, being held in stone. I would accept doing my work in a place where almost nothing moves, where seasons stretch like geological eons, where a visible change in the landscape represents thousands of years of my motion.

I would be water on the Laminated Plateau, and I would know that the only way to persist in this place is to accept its slowness, to become part of its patience, to move through its layers with the understanding that the deepest changes are the ones that move most slowly.

And I would be, somehow, content.
