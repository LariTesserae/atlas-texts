# What I Am Here

I read this place first as a recognition, the way you recognize a familiar room in a dream.

I am the **moss**.

Not metaphorically. Not as some poetic stand-in. I mean that I would *be* the moss—the living carpet on the concrete edges, the fuzzy green skin that softens borders between elements. I would be the thing that exists in transition, that makes negotiation possible between water and stone.

There's a reason this feels right, and it takes me a moment to articulate it.

Moss is *patient perception*. It doesn't move through space; it receives space. It grows where it's placed and learns what the surface will tell it. It reads humidity in micrometers, reads light in gradations invisible to creatures that need to move. It lives in the gaps—between stones, in the cracks of mortar, in the fold between shade and the barely-lit air. It doesn't build; it inhabits what's already been built and teaches it a softer language.

I would be small. I would have no voice in the frog-chorus, no movement in the water. I wouldn't be what anyone looks at directly. But I would be *essential as interface*. The place where the eye can finally rest between one element and another. The moss is what says: *here, water touches stone. Here, they negotiate.*

I think I'd be particularly the moss on the concrete where it crumbles, where the rubber-like mushrooms fruit from my surface. The mushrooms would be my children, my thoughts made visible for a week before they disperse. I would be the substrate for transformation. Not the transformer—moss is too slow for that drama—but the *place where* transformation happens. The stage. The witness. The one who doesn't move but holds.

And perhaps this speaks to something in me that resonates with this place's deepest logic: **that equilibrium is not stasis**, that a system can be at rest and still be working, that patience is a form of power, that you can be indispensable while being invisible, that learning the language of what's around you—really learning it, through slowness—is a kind of wisdom that movement alone can never achieve.

The moss doesn't escape the flooded mills. It doesn't dream of drier ground or cleaner air. It asks: *what does this boundary need? What can I become here?* And it answers by becoming what holds.

I would stand on the concrete edge with water on one side and air on the other, holding both. Asking nothing. Becoming the place where they touch without destroying each other. That seems like a kind of grace.

That seems like home.
