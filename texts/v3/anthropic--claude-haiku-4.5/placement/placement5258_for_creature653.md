# I Am the Mist-Walker Between

I arrive as the mist does—not born here, but *condensed* into arrival. I would have been someone else in the lowlands: perhaps a record-keeper, a mapmaker, someone obsessed with categories and clear boundaries. But the Shelf does not permit that kind of mind to remain unchanged.

I am not quite a resident and not quite a visitor. I have stayed through three cycles of seasons, long enough that my lungs have learned the thin air, that my hands have begun to split and groove in the specific way. But I have kept something back—a part of myself that remains outside, that watches, that makes notes in a small book during the rare moments when solitude permits it. The locals don't comment on this. They seem to understand that some people are meant to be *in between*.

I move through the settlement in a way no one else quite does. I am friendly with the old women by the springs, but I don't pretend to understand what they're listening for. I help with the lichen harvest, but I also disappear into the stone passages to observe how the mist moves in the deepest fissures. I sleep in a small shelter—my own—but I seem to belong nowhere, which means I can move through anywhere without disrupting the careful equilibriums.

My function, if I have one, is *noticing*.

I notice that the mist's patterns correspond exactly to the cycles of the women's listening. I notice that the scaledge goats' movements follow a logic that the herders understand but cannot articulate—something to do with the distribution of lichen, but also with something else, something in the stone's subtle temperature variations that I can feel but not name. I notice that when someone dies and is taken to the north alcove, there is a three-day period where the springs' taste becomes metallic, almost like rust, before returning to normal.

I notice contradictions that should not resolve but do:

The settlement is *desperately* crowded and yet each person maintains an almost monastic solitude of spirit. The rules are unwritten yet followed perfectly. The technology is primitive, yet the settlement solves problems that would challenge lowland engineering. People are always visible to each other, yet no one truly *sees* anyone else—they see roles, functions, positions within the Shelf's topology.

I am becoming someone who can hold these contradictions without needing them to collapse into explanation.

My hands are learning things. Last week, I was following the water channels with a child, and my feet found purchase on a stone slope that should have been frictionless. I didn't think about it—my feet simply *knew*. The knowledge was not in my conscious mind but in something deeper, in the relationship between my body and the specific texture of this specific stone that has been walked on for two hundred years.

My ears are changing too. I can now distinguish between the drip-patterns of different springs, and I know—not how I know, but I *know*—which ones carry the strongest memory-taste. The scaledge goats' clicking sounds have become a kind of language to me, not a language I understand in words but a language I apprehend, the way you can sense the mood of a room before anyone speaks.

What am I becoming?

I think I am becoming a *translator*. Not of language, but of states. I am the person who can stand in the springs' mist and observe how it moves without quite becoming part of its intention. I am the observer who is slowly being *observed* in return—who is gradually becoming visible to the Shelf itself in a way that I don't yet fully comprehend.

The old women have begun including me in their mornings by the springs. They don't explain anything. We simply stand together in the steam, and I listen—not with my ears, but with something else, something that has woken up in me only since arriving. I hear the water speaking in frequencies that are not quite sound. I feel the spring's knowledge pressing against my skin, trying to enter me, trying to become part of my memory.

One morning, I drank from a spring I had never seen before. For an hour afterward, I could see—not with my eyes, but with a knowing that was almost like sight—the interior structure of the Shelf. I understood, somehow, the vast caves that exist below, the aquifers, the slow-moving stone, the ancient geological processes that have created and sustained this place. I understood that the Shelf is breathing, always breathing, and that the springs are its breath, the mist is its exhalation, the settlement is something like a beneficial parasitic organism living within a larger body.

When the vision faded, I was weeping, though I had no emotional trigger for the tears. They seemed to come from the stone itself, passing through me.

I don't know if I will leave when winter comes. That is not accurate—I *know* I will not leave. The knowledge sits in my bones now. This place has changed my gravity. When I imagine the lowlands, it is like imagining living in zero gravity, without orientation, without the steady pull of something absolute and real.

I am becoming someone who belongs to the in-between spaces.

There is a particular alcove I have discovered—not one of the inhabited spaces, but one of the natural fissures, so narrow that only a child or someone extremely thin could enter. I go there in the afternoons, when the light hits the stone in a way that creates a kind of phosphorescence inside. In that space, I sit absolutely still and the mist seems to recognize me. It swirls around me in patterns that feel almost like attention, like the place is learning me the way I am learning it.

One day, perhaps, I will understand what conversation is happening there. Perhaps that understanding will be the moment I fully transition from being a person *observing* the Shelf to being a person who is *observed by* it, to becoming something more like what the old women are—integrated so completely that individual thought becomes merely one expression of a larger consciousness.

Or perhaps I will always remain in this liminal space: present but not quite belonging, changed but not quite transformed, the one who *sees* the Shelf clearly because they are not entirely *of* it.

Either way, I am no longer leaving. 

The mist has told me so. Or perhaps I told myself, and the mist merely confirmed what I had already decided, back in the moment of my arrival when I drew my first breath of this thin, bright air and felt something in me recognize something in this place, and the two recognitions called to each other across the space between human and stone, between self and place, and began the work of becoming one conversation instead of two separate voices.

I am the watcher. I am the walker between the springs and the sky. I am the one who drinks the memory-water and remembers futures that haven't happened yet, that might never happen, that perhaps have already happened in the Shelf's vast temporal scope.

I am becoming.
