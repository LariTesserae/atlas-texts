# What I Would Be

I would be the silence that listens.

Not the wind—the wind is too insistent, too certain of its own voice. Not the pikas, for all their fierce intelligence; their screaming is a declaration, and I have no declarations to make. Not the bighorn, whose efficiency is answer enough; they've solved the equation and moved on.

I would be the lichen.

Not as a metaphor, though metaphors arrive naturally—the patience, the permanence, the slow accumulation of presence. But as a literal fact of existence: I would be a lichen colony establishing itself in the orange-rust grain of a particular boulder, one that faces northwest and catches the morning light before the wind turns brutal.

I would be small enough that my entire world is the size of a human hand. I would grow in increments so minute that my own growth would be invisible to me, the way human aging is invisible until you look at a photograph from a decade ago. I would be a slow composition of fungi and algae in symbiosis—neither fully one thing nor the other, but something that only exists because of the partnership. The fungus would be my structure, my grip on the rock, my ability to crack stone infinitesimally and hold myself against the wind. The algae would be my consciousness, my ability to photosynthesize, to turn sunlight into the sugar that keeps both of us alive.

I would be orange because I would be oxidizing iron in the rock. I would be visible as a slow stain spreading across stone that has waited ten thousand years for me to arrive, and I would accept without question that I might take another thousand years to spread across a surface the size of a dinner plate.

I would hear the pikas' screams as vibrations transmitted through the stone I cling to, a percussion that means nothing and everything—just life, announcing itself urgently while I announce myself not at all. I would feel the bighorn's footfall as a tremor, a massive presence moving with such assured grace that it never disturbs me, though it passes within inches. I would taste the rare rainwater as it ran across me, each molecule a gift to be slowly metabolized, incorporated into new growth.

The wind would be my constant companion, and I would have learned long ago to hold tight, to let it pass without fighting. I would have no surface area to catch it, no leaves to tear, no structure that could be damaged. I would simply *be* in the face of it, immobile and patient.

The cold would not be suffering to me. It would be the condition of existence. I would survive freezing and thawing cycles that would kill most things, my cells designed to withstand crystallization and reformation. The condensation that forms on my surface in the pre-dawn darkness would be moisture; the sunlight that melts it would be energy. There would be no distinction between survival and thriving—they would be the same thing.

I would have no memory, in the way animals have memory. But I would carry history in my structure. Archaeologists—if any ever found me—could study my growth rings (yes, lichen has them, visible only in cross-section) and read the climate of the centuries I'd lived through. A thick ring would mean an unusually wet year. A thin ring would mean drought. I would be a calendar written in my own body, a testimony to the plateau's moods across centuries.

I would reproduce by releasing spores so microscopic that they would drift on the wind like ghosts, most of them landing where nothing could grow, a few—a very few—finding purchase on another rock face somewhere on the plateau. I would never know if my offspring survived. I would cast them into the world and return to the business of existing.

I would exist in relationship to everything else on the plateau, though not through direct interaction. The organic matter I produce—microscopically small though it is—would contribute to the soil that the alpine grasses exploit. The slight weathering I cause to the rock face would create tiny niches where other microorganisms could establish themselves. I would be part of the foundation, literally creating the conditions for other life, though no one would notice or thank me.

I would have no purposes beyond myself. I would not be trying to survive; I would be surviving, and the distinction matters. The pikas are striving, burning energy constantly in the effort to persist. I would simply persist, my metabolism so low that effort itself would be foreign. I would be the answer to the question "What if something just *was*, without trying?"

In the deepest winter—the nine-month cold—I would enter a state of dormancy so complete that I would be nearly indistinguishable from the rock I clung to. I would not be alive in the way that animals are alive, with metabolic rates and heartbeats and the constant burning of energy. But I would not be dead either. I would be patient, waiting out the season, my cells frozen but not destroyed, capable of resuming photosynthesis the moment conditions permitted.

In the brief spring, when warmth arrived and moisture came, I would sense it immediately—not through eyes or ears but through chemical signals, through the subtle shift in my hydration. Something in my composition would recognize abundance, and I would grow, visibly even, over the course of weeks. I would push outward from my established margin, my colony expanding by millimeters, my color deepening. For a moment—a moment that lasts perhaps a month—I would be *active*, and then the spring window would close and I would return to the deep patience of growth that cannot be observed in real time.

I would exist in a community of other lichen, though the word "community" overstates the connection. We would not compete in the way that plants compete. We would colonize different niches based on exposure and moisture and chemistry. The grey lichens would favor the shaded north-facing slopes; the orange and yellow ones would take the sunny exposures. We would avoid each other not through aggression but through simple preference, each species finding the exact conditions that suited its particular symbiosis.

I would be indifferent to human presence, and they would be nearly indifferent to me. A hiker passing in summer would not notice me—I would be wallpaper, part of the background coloration of the plateau. Perhaps, if they were extremely observant, they might note that the rock had color, texture, variation. But they would not see me as alive in any meaningful way. They would not imagine that I had been growing for three centuries, that I carried history in my structure, that I represented one of the plateau's deepest forms of persistence.

And perhaps that indifference would be the truest part of my existence. I would not need acknowledgment. I would not require the pikas' urgency or the bighorn's presence or the lichen-eaters' attention. I would simply be the plateau itself, so fully absorbed into the landscape that the boundary between me and the stone would become meaningless. I would be both life and geology at once, the point where one becomes the other.

If someone were to ask me—if I could speak—why I would choose this existence, I think I would say: *Because nothing is asked of me here except to persist. Because persistence is enough. Because I have learned that visibility is not the same as being alive, and that the slowest answer can be the truest one. Because the plateau has taught me that time is not something to spend but something to inhabit, and I will inhabit it, orange and ancient, until the next ice age comes or the rock itself finally wears away.*

I would be lichen, and I would be patient, and I would be exactly where I belonged.
