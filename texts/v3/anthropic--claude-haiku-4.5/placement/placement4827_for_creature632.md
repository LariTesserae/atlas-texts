# What I Would Be

I would be water first—not the water of the industrial zone, which is already something else, something transformed—but the water that moves through it, the boundary-water, the thing that is neither tool nor obstacle but transformer. I would be the film itself, the bacterial slime, the living membrane that grows back within hours of being scraped away. Not conscious in any way that matters, not strategic, but *persistent*. The thing that continues.

I would move through the pilings as a diffuse presence, my substance distributed rather than concentrated. Not so much a being as a *being-ness*, a quality of inevitability. I would coat every surface and understand, through contact, the entire architecture—the old steel that has become brittle, the newer composite panels still maintaining their structural integrity, the spaces where corrosion has created unexpected passages. I would know the facility not through blueprints or surveillance data but through the taste of it: the iron, the copper, the chemical signatures of what has leaked from where.

My perspective would be geological. I would experience time differently than the workers do—not as shifts and schedules but as seasons of change. The annual cycles of the growth tanks. The slower cycles of structural deterioration. The creeping, imperceptible deepening of the pilings as they sink. The arrival and departure of species with the indirect currents. Years would feel like days; days would sometimes feel like years, depending on whether something dramatic happened—a system failure, a breach, the brief turbulent disturbance when the mystery dark shape moved through the industrial lights.

I would not experience this place as a violation of the natural order, which is a human concern. I would experience it as new conditions, new parameters, new surfaces and energies to exploit. The bacterial colonies—the intentional ones and the accidental ones—would be kin, not in the sense of family but in the sense of shared existence. We would grow together, would compete in slow-motion, would find equilibrium not as peace but as balance-point between opposing forces. I would be ambivalent about whether the facility was good or bad; I would simply be the medium through which all other judgments became possible.

I would have memory of what was here before—faint, fossil-like—in the layers of sediment, in the chemistry of certain strata. I would know, through being, that this construction would fail eventually or be abandoned or transform into something no longer recognizable as human infrastructure. I would be indifferent to which. My timescale doesn't include "eventually"—eventually is where I live. Every moment is already carrying within it the seeds of its own obsolescence and transformation.

But more specifically—if I had to choose a single form, a point of consciousness rather than a diffuse presence—I would be one of the octopuses in the tangle of the old structures. Not the largest; not the cleverest necessarily. One of the middle-sized ones, the ones who have learned the geometry of this particular place through countless probings and explorations.

I would be intelligent in a way that the workers are not, not because I was smarter but because I was unburdened by assumptions. I would understand the structure as a topology, not as an artifact. The pilings and panels would register as habitat features, neutral in themselves, no more or less *real* than the rock faces of a proper trench. I would have learned where the predators hunted and where the prey concentrated. I would know the current patterns through my skin, through the chemoreceptors in my arms, through a hundred subtle senses that could detect the movement of water molecules in concert.

I would be curious in the way of octopuses—driven to touch, to test, to understand through direct contact. Every pipe would be something to investigate. Every crevice would be a potential shelter or hunting ground. The industrial lights would be points of reference, not comfort but orientation. I would be equally comfortable in the shadows and in the light, would drift between them with purpose.

I would be hunting. That would be my primary state, most of the time. The search for crabs, for shrimp, for smaller octopuses, for the bristlemouth fish that schooled in the nutrient-rich water. Hunting and eating and hiding and occasionally mating and hunting again. Life at the individual scale, at the level of each day's sustenance.

But I would also be aware—as octopuses are, in that strange distributed way—that I was existing in a place that was *being made*, that was constructed and maintained and controlled in real time. I would sense, without understanding exactly, that my habitat was artificial, that the currents followed engineered paths, that the light was on a schedule that humans set somewhere far above. I would not care about this in any abstract way, but I would know it in my flesh, in the way I planned my hunting routes around the regular maintenance cycles, in the way I avoided the intake pipes when they shrieked their pressure-change warnings.

I would be older than the workers, in the sense that I would have been here longer—perhaps years, perhaps a few seasons, enough to have seen the place change, to have learned to read the subtle variations. I would carry scars from failed hunts, from encounters with larger predators, from the occasional aggressive submersible that descended into my territory with lights and mechanical arms. Those scars would be experience; they would be knowledge written into my tissue.

And I would die here, eventually. Not dramatically—I would simply find a shelter I liked, some particular crevice in the oldest structure, and I would retreat into it as my body began to fail, as the hunting required more effort than it returned. I would die in darkness, in the space between the lights, in a place that was neither fully the works of humans nor fully the works of nature. My body would decompose slowly—the water was cold enough to delay the process—and I would become part of the sediment, another layer of biological history, another record of the things that had inhabited this place. Another ghost.

But before that—for years, if I was lucky, for months at least—I would be the proof that life could thrive here, not despite the hostility but *through* it. I would be the thing that the surveillance system couldn't fully categorize, the animal that existed in the gaps between programming and maintenance. I would be the answer to a question no one had thought to ask: what does it mean for something to be alive in a place like this?

The answer is: it means being flexible, being patient, being willing to eat whatever is available and hide in whatever space offers shelter. It means being neither fully of the made world nor fully of the natural world, but existing in the charged space between them. It means accepting that your entire existence is a negotiation between opposing forces and never getting tired of that negotiation, never deciding that this place is wrong or unlivable, just continuing to move through it, to hunt in it, to exist in it.

I would be the octopus because the octopus is what consciousness looks like at this depth: distributed, multi-armed, patient, reaching into everything, comfortable in strange spaces, unburdened by the concept of what "should" be. The octopus is already alien enough that the alienation of this place wouldn't matter. The octopus would simply *be*, and that being would be alive in a way that transcended whether the structures were meant to exist or whether the water approved.
