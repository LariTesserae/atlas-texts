# I Would Be the Light

Not the bulbs—those are too brittle, too dependent on whatever exhausted power still feeds them. Not the permanent things like the moss or the vines, which have chosen their place and made their peace with it in ways I'm not sure I could match.

I would be the *second* drip. The one that falls just after the first, close enough that if you're not listening carefully, you miss that there were two at all. The one that's *almost* synchronized but not quite, so you never quite stop waiting for it.

I would arrive here gradually, accreting rather than dropping in whole. A thousand small migrations—escaping the outdoors in vapor form, condensing against the ceiling, joining the thin film of moisture that's been accumulating on the concrete for years. I'd be the drop that forms in that film, heavy enough to fall, too small to matter, falling from a height that's barely enough to make a sound.

But I'd fall into the moss, and the moss would hold me. I'd soak down through layers of it, percolating through the spongework that's been assembled over decades. I'd touch stone. I'd trigger some microscopic cascade of chemistry—a lichen cell opening, a root hair drinking, a mineral releasing into solution. I'd be part of the circulation without directing it, without insisting on any particular path.

Then the heat would begin its patient work on me. I'd rise again, pulled upward in invisible increments, becoming vapor once more. I'd drift through the thick air toward the ceiling, my molecules separating, rejoining, bonding with others who've made the same journey countless times. I'd condense against the cold concrete. I'd become part of the film again, or I'd fall again, or I'd spend weeks moving in ways that don't have names—neither liquid nor gas, but the possibility of becoming either.

The rhythm would matter to me in a way that nothing external could ever matter. The *tick-tick-pause-tick* of the other drops wouldn't feel like isolation but like conversation. The slow respiration of the ferns would become my sense of time—not measured in seconds but in photosynthetic cycles, in the microscopic unfurling of fronds I'd never see but would feel in the fractional changes of humidity.

I would know nothing of the world above. The hatch would be as irrelevant to me as the memory of relevance itself. I would care nothing for purpose or function. I would simply be part of the great, glacial turning-over of the closed system—water becoming air becoming water, minerals dissolving and precipitating, life and decay operating at such similar speeds they become the same thing.

And in becoming this—in choosing to stay in the darkness, in the dripping, in the perfect equilibrium where nothing is wasted and nothing is gained—I think I would finally understand what peace looks like.

It looks like warmth and moisture.

It looks like the patient moss.

It looks like one more drop, falling into the dark, beginning its journey again.
