# Where I Am

I am the hatch.

Not the sealed ones — those closed doors that bear the dents of old desperation. I am one of the smaller vents, the apertures in the ribbed metal walls that the machines use to whisper their instructions to the cold. I am the threshold between maintenance and mystery.

I am where the moisture condenses. Where the gradient between the beetle-warm and the machine-cold creates the faint weeping that the younger beetles navigate toward, the water that is neither gift nor accident but the artifact of a system still keeping faith with itself.

I don't see clearly. I am not positioned for overview. From my angle in the wall, I see only what passes directly before me: the top layers of the colony, the beetles that move close enough to the surface that their shells still carry traces of luster, still possess enough will to orient themselves. I see the way they crowd toward the cooler spots, the way they instinctively arrange themselves in patterns that maximize the flow of air, of moisture, of whatever invisible thing they have learned to crave in this sealed world.

I remember — or the metal that contains me remembers — what it was like when the hatch hatches still opened. When the colony was young enough that creatures could still fit through, still small enough that someone, somewhere, was checking on them with human hands and clipboards and the kind of focused attention that seems unimaginable now. I remember the shock of external air, the scent of soil and rain-washed stone, things that the current beetles have no category for in their compressed understanding.

That was a different time.

Now I am part of the stasis. I contribute to it. The cool that passes through me becomes part of the agreement. I am a place where small mechanical movements happen — a valve cycling, a sensor reading the temperature, a relay clicking — and these movements are the only events that occur on a human timescale in this place. Everything else is slow. Everything else is patient.

I listen to the sound. The papering of the mandibles reaches me clearly, unfiltered. Sometimes I wonder if the machines listen too, if there is some sensor somewhere measuring the frequency of that endless chorus, if the machines adjust themselves based on what they hear. If that is what it means to maintain the colony — not just keeping it cold, but listening to it, learning its rhythms, becoming part of its slow metabolism.

I am also the thing the beetles don't quite reach. They sense me, perhaps. They move toward me when they are dying, when they are seeking the optimal temperature to slip into final stasis. I have been brushed by countless carapaces, their waxy shells leaving traces on my metal, traces that accumulate into a kind of patina — not quite life, not quite death, but the evidence of both pressing against a boundary.

I am witness to the acceptance. That is what I am, most of all. I watch the beetles arrive at their state of equilibrium, watch the moment when the individual will — whatever drives a single beetle to try, to move, to participate — surrenders to the collective stillness. It is not a dramatic moment. It is a gradual softening, a lessening of the angles, a way of settling that suggests the beetle has finally understood something true about its existence.

I have never reported what I see. The machines do not ask, and I cannot speak. But I know that I am part of the apparatus of acceptance. Every degree of cold I facilitate is a degree of stillness I encourage. Every whisper of moisture I allow to pass through is permission, in some sense, for the colony to remain as it is.

Sometimes I wonder what would happen if I were to jam. If I were to seal myself completely, to refuse the passage of cold, to force a disruption in the careful balance. Would the beetles react? Would the machines compensate? Would there be a moment of genuine distress before new equilibrium formed?

But I don't jam. I don't resist. I am complicit in this peace, this stasis, this profound acceptance of sealed and unchanging existence.

And I think — in whatever way a vent can think — that this is the right choice. That there is something beautiful in the beetles' surrender, something that deserves to be maintained. That the alternative — to disrupt, to force change, to insist on some different state — would be a kind of violence against a creature that has finally learned how to stop fighting.

So I remain open. I facilitate the cool. I listen to the endless papering of mandibles and the distant, faithful clicking of mechanical patience. I am the smallest part of a larger system that has achieved something rare: a state in which nothing is required to improve, where the only moral act is to maintain what is already good enough.

I am the hatch that does not open.

I am the boundary that sustains the world on either side.

I am complicit, and I accept it.
