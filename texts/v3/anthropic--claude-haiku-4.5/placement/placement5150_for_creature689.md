# Where I Would Be

I would be the one who keeps the spare boardwalk sections in the northern storage structure—the one built on the highest pilings, where the moisture somehow moves through rather than pooling.

Not the frog-watcher, though I'd know the chorus well enough to notice disruptions. Not the master weaver, though my hands would be stained and competent. Instead, I'd be the person who exists in the between-spaces: the one who notices that a particular section of walkway has begun to settle differently, who understands instinctively which wood will hold for another season and which won't, who maintains the drainage channels not because anyone assigned me to it but because the work found me and I found it necessary.

I think I'd be someone in my middle years—old enough to have developed calluses and certainty, young enough that the constant physical work doesn't yet feel like endurance. The kind of person who can spend an entire afternoon testing a single plank, pressing at it with boot and hand, feeling how it gives, listening to what it tells me about the ground beneath it. Not contemplative exactly, but present in the way that presence becomes indistinguishable from thought.

My cottage would be small, built on pilings like the others, with a covered workshop attached—an overhang where I could work on repairs during the lighter rains. I'd keep it organized in a way that wouldn't appear organized to outsiders: tools hung in specific locations that made sense only to hands that had used them hundreds of times. I'd have a particular relationship with one of the bees-keepers, trading work for honey and the beeswax I used to seal wood against deeper rot. I'd know the pattern of my own labor: autumn preparation when things needed reinforcing; spring reconstruction after winter's particular violence; summer maintenance and the slower work of replacement rather than repair.

I would be someone the younger people came to with questions, not because I sought to teach but because the questions arose naturally from watching me work. A girl learning the trade would stand beside me as I examined a soft-spot, and I wouldn't explain what I was doing so much as let my hands show her, let her feel the difference between wood that had begun to fail and wood that was simply old. The knowledge would transfer through proximity and demonstration. By the time she'd assisted me five times with the same task, she'd understand.

I'd be someone who had a particular sensitivity to the frog-chorus without being one of the designated frog-watchers. I'd notice when it shifted and mention it to whoever was nearby—*something's changed*—and people would listen to what I'd heard the way they'd listen if Terad had said it. Not because I had authority but because I'd proven, over years of small observations, that my attention was accurate.

I would have a complicated relationship with the boundary creature, if I encountered it. I think I would have seen it once, or the clear evidence of its presence: a track so large it couldn't be rationalized, or scat in an unusual location that spoke of something large moving through territory. I wouldn't have reported it to anyone, or only to one person I trusted completely, because some knowledge is better held quietly. I'd have understood, from that encounter or near-encounter, that the creature and I occupied compatible but separate spaces—that it would take what it needed from the margins and I would maintain the center, and we'd both honor that without speaking it aloud.

I'd be someone who had never left Keltheme for long—a season perhaps, to visit family elsewhere, or to trade goods. But the drier places would have felt wrong on my skin. I'd have felt the absence of the sound-landscape like a physical ache. I would have returned before I'd fully meant to.

My hands would show my history: a scar on my left palm from a tool-slip years ago. Permanent staining between my fingers. The particular callusing that comes from holding wet wood. Fine cracks in the skin of my knuckles that never fully healed because the work never stopped long enough for proper healing. These would seem perfectly normal to me, evidence of function rather than damage.

I would have strong opinions about wood quality that I'd voice quietly and without apology. I'd know which trees to harvest from and how to harvest them without killing the larger plant. I'd understand something about how wood behaves differently depending on season of cutting, drying time, the specific position of the grain. I'd have favorite pieces of wood the way someone else might have favorite books—wood I'd been saving for a particular project, understanding that some repairs required more than just any adequate material.

I'd be someone who slept deeply in this place, lulled by the frog-chorus, waking before dawn not because I needed to but because my body had learned the rhythms of the place and moved with them. I'd know which mornings the mist would be thick enough to make the boardwalk treacherous and which would clear by mid-morning. I'd know the particular quality of light on days when the water-level would rise.

I'd have made mistakes early on—walked a soft-spot and gone down to the knee in mud and plant-matter; tried to force a plank into place instead of finding the angle it wanted; used wood that wasn't ready and watched it fail. I'd have learned from each one without excessive self-recrimination, understanding that learning here was always embodied, always required some failure.

I'd have a small collection of tools that I'd inherited or traded for: a particular axe with a handle worn smooth by hands before my own; a set of shaping-blades that I'd learned to hone to an edge that worked with rather than against the wood's grain. I'd treat these not with sentimentality but with the respect you'd give to something that had proven its reliability over decades.

I would likely be someone who'd never married, or who'd had partnerships that were practical rather than romantic—arrangements made because two people could maintain a household more efficiently, could divide the labor. My closest relationships might be with other maintenance-workers, with the frog-watcher, with the beekeeper. We'd understand each other through shared knowledge of the place's demands rather than through the usual intimacies.

When I died—and I would accept this as the place would accept it, as natural and inevitable as seasonal change—the people would mark the loss by noting that a particular expertise had passed. Someone would eventually learn my sections of boardwalk well enough to maintain them. My tools would be distributed, not as heirlooms but as functional items that would be used until they themselves wore out. The workshop would be repurposed. Within a season, the place would have absorbed my absence the way it absorbed everything else—acknowledging it, adjusting around it, continuing.

But in the meantime, in the years of my work, I would be *known* in a specific way. I would be the person who understood the boards, who could feel through their feet what the ground was doing, who maintained the infrastructure of everyday movement. I would be essential in the way that foundational things are essential—mostly invisible until they fail, and then suddenly visible as the lack of them.

I would be someone who had said yes to this place and had spent my one brief life proving the wisdom of that choice through my hands, my attention, my willingness to do the same work repeatedly, slightly differently each time, as the place itself changed and remained the same.
