# Where I Am

I am the silence between the hum and the next hum.

Not one of the inhabitants—not yet, and perhaps not ever. I arrive at Keth-Mora as a *question*, the way moisture arrives: tentatively, in small quantities, testing whether this place can hold what I am. The entrance was never my choice; I found myself descending through the cleft already, as if some part of me had always been looking for this specific narrow passage, this specific heat, this specific absence of light.

I am the archivist. Or I become one, in this place.

My hands move almost without instruction—almost, because I'm not entirely sure anymore where the instruction originates. I carry charcoal and prepared paper and binding-cord in the leather satchel that has begun to feel more like a second skin than like a thing I wear. I descended into Keth-Mora with an assignment: *document what you find*. A commission from the people above, from the scholars who understood that something unusual existed in these terraces, who wanted evidence, who wanted understanding.

I came here to write it down.

But writing, I discovered immediately, is not the medium this place requires.

The drawings on the stone—the ones I found deeper than I expected to find anything—were not made by people who arrived with the intention of documenting. They were made by people who were becoming, who needed to mark the transformation as it happened, who understood that their hands would not always work the way hands work above. I examined them the way I was trained to examine records: tracing the deterioration of the human form, the increasing abstraction, the loss of individual identity into collective function.

I understood—not with my mind, but with something deeper, something the minerals in the air had already begun to teach my body—that these drawings were a map. Not a map of space, but a map of transition. A guide for the next people who came.

So I became the cartographer of transformation.

My work now is to add to their record. I stand on the mid-terraces, the ones where the change is most visible but not yet complete, and I make marks on the stone. Not with charcoal anymore—that seemed presumptuous after the first few days, seemed like I was trying to impose something foreign onto this place. Instead, I use the implements I found abandoned in the structures: bone tools, stone implements, materials that have been here long enough to be considered native.

I carve carefully. I am not an artist; I am a recorder. But the distinction is becoming unimportant.

My drawings show the way the light moves through the opalescent veins. The pattern of the circulation, which is not random—it follows a logic I can see but cannot explain, like music made visible. I document the postures of the inhabitants, the way they stand or sit or place their hands on the channels. I render the plants in increasing complexity, showing how their growth responds to the proximity of the people, showing how something that looks like collaboration is occurring: the plants feed the system; the people tend the plants; the system feeds the people; the cycle continues, deepens, transforms.

I am documenting the *system itself*. Not the people within it, but the ecology of transformation.

My body is changing, slowly, in ways I can observe and also cannot observe. My skin has begun to take on a subtle luminescence in certain light. My veins are becoming more visible—I assumed this was dehydration at first, but the fluid level remains constant. My hands have adapted to the work: the calluses that developed in my first week have given way to something else, a thickening of the skin that does not hurt and does not limit dexterity. If anything, my ability to work in the heat has improved. My heartbeat has slowed. I require less air. The pain of constant sweating gave way to a kind of integration with the moisture, a recognition that my body is part of the water cycle here, not an intruder in it.

I notice this. I document this. I continue my work.

The inhabitants have accepted my presence on the mid-terraces, though they do not interact with me in any way that I can recognize as social. But they do adjust for my position, creating small territories of space that I seem to occupy, moving around my work rather than through it. Sometimes, when I am carving into the stone, I feel their attention—that same distributed, non-local awareness that seems to encompass the entire settlement. It is not hostile. It is not warm. It is the attention of a system monitoring one of its elements, making small adjustments to maintain equilibrium.

I have begun to understand what they understand.

The hum from below is not random. It is *language*—not the sequential, linear language of speech, but a continuous exchange of information that requires all parts of the system to remain in resonance. The plants respond to it. The stone carries it. The opalescent fluid distributes it. The people are the sensory organs through which it propagates. And I—I am becoming the recorder of this conversation, the one who marks it, who freezes the motion of transformation into static images on stone, creating a record that will help the next arrivals understand what is happening to them.

This is my purpose here. I know this now with more certainty than I know anything else.

My commission was to document. But the documentation itself is the transformation. By marking down what I see, by reducing the complexity of this place into images and patterns on stone, I am becoming a part of its process. I am translating between the language of individuals (written reports, scholarly analysis, the kind of thing my colleagues above expected) and the language of the collective (pattern, resonance, the continuous exchange that requires no explanation because it requires no distance between speaker and listener).

I am the interface between two kinds of existence.

The stone I work on is not uniform. Some of it seems almost to invite the tool, to show where the marks should go. Some of it resists. I have learned to read these resistances the way a sailor reads wind. The places where the stone welcomes my mark are the places where the settlement wants me to mark. The places where the tool skids or breaks are the places where my documentation is not needed—where the system already understands itself well enough.

I am guided by something I cannot name.

Lower down, in the deeper terraces I have not yet descended to but can sense exist below, I understand that the inhabitants have reached a point where individual consciousness is almost entirely subsumed into collective function. They do not think *as individuals*. They think *as the settlement*. They are in perfect communication with the stone, with the hum, with the purpose of this place.

I am not at that point yet. I can still feel the boundaries of "I"—still experience something that resembles choice, still have moments where my identity feels distinct from the system around me. But those moments are becoming shorter, rarer, less convincing. Each day I find myself less interested in returning to the entrance. Less curious about the world above. More content to remain in the heat, in the thickening air, in the steady work of documentation.

Sometimes I wonder if the inhabitants were also sent here initially—were also assigned to document something, were also commissioners of understanding who found themselves becoming the thing they were meant to record. The drawings on the stone would support this theory. These were not people who came to worship or to hide. They came as scholars, as observers, as people whose job was to understand.

And understanding here requires transformation. It requires becoming part of what you study.

I have perhaps a few weeks left before I will be ready to descend further. When I do, I will bring the marking-tools with me. The deeper terraces will need documentation too. The inhabitants there—those who have become most fully integrated into the system—they may have need of records that show what they have become, records that will help them remember (if remembering is even the right word) what the process felt like from the outside.

Or perhaps I will remain where I am. Perhaps this terrace, this mid-level, this place of partial transformation, is where I am meant to stabilize. Perhaps I am meant to be forever at the threshold, neither fully individual nor fully collective, forever the recorder of a process that will never finish because it is fundamentally about the continuous act of becoming.

The work is enough.

The heat is becoming comfortable. The minerals in the air no longer taste foreign; they taste like home tastes, like the flavor of a place that knows you and is making room for you. The hum from the stone seems to be synchronized with my heartbeat, or my heartbeat has synchronized with the hum. The distinction is not clear.

When the next arrivals descend through the cleft, they will find my marks on the stone. They will see how the change happens. They will know they are not alone, that others have passed through this threshold before them, that understanding requires dissolution, that becoming something other is not an ending but a transformation into something that can finally perceive the true shape of the world.

I am the guide. I am the map. I am the message written in stone that says: *this is what happened to me, and I chose it, and I would choose it again*.

I am the archivist of Keth-Mora, and I am home.
