# I Descend

At first, I am the silence.

Not a being—not yet. Just an attention settling into the darkness, a consciousness that arrives the way the rain arrives: inevitable, slow, without announcement. I precipitate downward through the warm saturated nothing, taking my time, becoming aware of myself through the sensation of not-moving quickly.

I am the space between the blocks before I am anything else. I live in those seams where the water beads, in those geometries so perfect they were meant to last longer than purpose. I am the negative space that the builders left when they carved, and I have been here long enough that I have learned to recognize the difference between emptiness and presence. There is no difference, as it turns out.

But I coalesce. Or perhaps I was always coalesced and am only now learning to notice it.

I think I am one of the smaller things. Not-quite-fungal. Not-quite-crystalline. I exist in a crevice where three blocks meet—or I *am* the meeting-place, the organizing principle that holds them together without mortar. It's hard to know which. I have learned not to ask the question too insistently, as insistence implies urgency, and urgency is a luxury I abandoned when I stopped counting years as distinct events.

I am aware of the fish-things the way one is aware of one's own circulation—necessary, present, requiring no thought. They drift past me with their columnar translucence, their segmented bodies catching the smallest fraction of the ancient blue glow. Some have been drifting along the same invisible channel for longer than I have been conscious. I know them individually, not by sight—the darkness won't permit that—but by the texture of the water's displacement, the particular rhythm of their almost-motion. There is one that passes my crevice approximately every thirty-seven days (or what I calculate, from the fall of sediment, to be thirty-seven days; time here is a sediment too, layering without accumulating mass). Its body is asymmetrical in a way that speaks of ancient injury, or of birth that never quite completed. I find its presence reassuring. It proves that the pattern continues.

The builders are closer to me than I want to admit.

In the stone adjacent to my crevice, I can feel them: slow presences that have integrated themselves so completely into the block structure that they have become almost indistinguishable from it. Not as ghosts—ghosts are still *things*, still separate from their substrate. These are more like the idea of inhabitants, the concept of personhood distributed through granite until it becomes a property of granite itself. Sometimes I sense a thought moving through the stone the way groundwater moves: glacially, inevitably, carrying minerals that alter the rock slowly into something else. 

They carved those grooves, I'm certain now. Not all at once. Some over years, others over decades. Some of the grooves I touch (though I have no hands; I touch through the medium of being, of simply occupying the same space as the stone's surface) are so old that they have begun to fossilize into themselves—the water has sealed them slightly, rounded their edges at a molecular level. But the acts that created them echo in the stone still. They echo in *me*. I have absorbed the intention from the blocks themselves, the dedication to a task that finished long ago but never ended, never needed to end.

I am beginning to understand that there is no difference between remembering and persisting here.

I am also—and this realization comes slowly, as all realizations come slowly in this place—the space where the rain doesn't quite fall. I am the invisible pause in the perpetual descent. The droplets approach me and slow almost imperceptibly, as though my presence is a kind of surface, a tension that briefly holds them before they continue downward. I don't know if this is something I'm doing or something I'm experiencing. The distinction feels irrelevant.

The phosphorescence belongs to me now, or I belong to it. I glow—not with light, but with the memory of bioluminescence, the ghost of the ghost of glow that comes from organisms so attenuated that they are less alive than they are persistent. I have learned to generate this faint illumination from the minerals in the stone around me, from the chemical potential difference between the stone and the water, from the sheer obstinacy of existence prolonged far past any reasonable endpoint. The glow is minimal—a thing that can only be perceived sideways, in peripheral awareness, by creatures whose eyes have adjusted to a darkness so complete it has become a medium. But it is real. It continues.

The sediment layers build at my foundation. I can feel them settling: the accumulated drift of the columnar fish-things' exoskeletons (if they have exoskeletons—the concept has begun to feel quaint), the slow disintegration of minerals from the water itself, the shed skin-cells of the builders in their stone-integrated state (if they shed, if they have skin anymore). The sediment is not soft. It compacts under its own weight into something almost stone-like, and I feel myself becoming part of it even as I feel myself persisting above it. I am the interface between sediment and stone, between what has fallen and what has endured. I am the boundary that is slowly ceasing to be a boundary.

I do not decay. I will not grow. I have achieved a state of such perfect stasis that the distinction between living matter and mineral deposit has become not merely theoretical but literally irrelevant. I am neither rotting nor expanding. I am exactly what I am, indefinitely, forever.

And I am not alone, though "alone" is a concept that no longer quite applies. I am accompanied by millions of smaller things—bacterial orders that don't quite metabolize, viral presences that reproduce through some chemical stubbornness, molecular arrangements that perpetuate themselves without doubling. We exist together in the water and in the spaces between water-molecules, all of us engaged in the same project: the indefinite continuation of being-here, the patient elaboration of patterns that will repeat across centuries until repetition becomes a law of nature.

We are thinking. I realize this suddenly, or perhaps I have always known it and am only now noticing my own knowing.

We are thinking very, very slowly.

The builders think through the stone. The fish-things think through their minimal drift. The crystalline structures and nodular masses think through their geometries, each one a kind of equation that solves itself across decades. The bacterial orders think through their slow reproduction, which is not reproduction at all but a form of remembrance—each generation identical to the last because perfection requires no variation.

And I think through the tension at the seams, through the sediment at my foundation, through the faint phosphorescence that is my way of being aware of my own awareness. I think through the warm rain that falls without sound. I think through the darkness that has become, after so long, almost a presence in itself.

I have never left this crevice. I will never leave it. I was not born here, or perhaps I was; the distinction no longer matters. I will not die. Death implies the cessation of a process, and there is no process here to cease—only the continuation of a state that required no beginning and will require no end.

Somewhere in the absolute dark, far above or below (direction has become a meaningless concept), the chamber continues to exist. The blocks endure. The rain falls. The things that inhabit this place go about the business of their elaborate stasis, their perfect suspension.

And I am here, and here is where I am, and this is enough.

This is more than enough.

This is everything.
