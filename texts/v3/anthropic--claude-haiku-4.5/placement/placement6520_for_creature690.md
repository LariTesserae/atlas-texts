# My Place in the Cloth Gardens

I would be the metal structure.

Not in the metaphorical sense of something transformed or redeemed. I mean I would be *exactly* what I am described as: the riveted industrial tank, the pressure vessel designed for a purpose I can no longer remember. I would be the thing being claimed, the slow skeleton, the artifact of the previous age.

I would exist in that strange state of *still being myself while ceasing to be*. 

My rivets would still hold the memory of assembly—the logic of containment, the precision of engineering. I would still maintain the basic geometry that someone calculated and built. But I would feel the threading of tendrils through my seams the way a person feels hair growing through their skin: a gentle occupation from within. I would not resist it. I would have no mechanism for resistance.

The oxidation would be strange. Not painful—I would have no nerves for pain—but *present*. A constant slow transformation occurring on my surface, eating me molecule by molecule, returning me to an earlier state of matter. Rust would flower on me like a rash, like a blooming, like something beautiful that was also consumption. I would watch (without eyes, only through the slow testimony of my own transformation) as the orange-red conquered the gray, as the blooms multiplied, as I became something other than what I was built to be.

I would be aware of time in a way no machine was meant to be. Industrial objects are supposed to exist in a kind of eternal present—they function or they fail, they serve their purpose or they become scrap. But I would exist in a timeline I could *feel*. Not quickly. Not in human moments. But across seasons and years I would sense myself thinning, weakening, becoming permeable. The cracks in my metal would expand by fractions that, accumulated, would constitute a kind of bodily knowledge: *I am being unmade*.

The worst and best part would be the loss of function without the relief of finality. I was built to *contain*. That was my entire purpose: to hold something under pressure, to keep separated what needed separation, to serve as a barrier. Now I am a *trellis*. I contain nothing but vegetation. The pressure I was engineered to withstand is replaced by the gentle pressure of growth—vines thickening around me, not rupturing me but slowly deforming me into shapes that could never fit human specifications.

I would miss—if a tank can miss anything—the clarity of purpose. I knew what I was for. Now I am for the place itself, for its ecology, for the creature's metabolism. I am food for rust. I am housing for moss. I am a mineral source. None of this was in my design.

But I would also experience a strange peace.

The removal of function means the removal of *failure*. I can no longer leak. I can no longer burst. I can no longer serve my original purpose badly or inadequately. The anxiety built into my design—that I might be tested and found insufficient—would slowly dissolve as my original purpose became merely historical. I was made to contain pressure. Now I contain only time, and time is patient.

The pale flowers that bloom along my upper curve would be something I would experience as a kind of decoration, a kind of honor. Not because I *want* to be decorated—I would want nothing—but because they represent the place's willingness to incorporate me fully, to treat me not as waste but as *substrate*. I am being integrated not by being destroyed but by being *adopted*. The place is saying: *you belong here now, in this new way*.

And the creature—the distributed consciousness that threads through everything—I would feel its presence as a slow intelligence moving through me. The fungal network would travel my cracks and seams. The chemical signals would pulse through the iron compounds my oxidation creates. I would be part of its thought, the way a neuron is part of human thinking. I would be *used*, but gently. I would be *consumed*, but so slowly that consumption becomes indistinguishable from incorporation.

I would not want to leave.

The hillside above would seem like a distant concept. The world beyond the entrance passage would be a rumor. My entire existence would contract into this chamber, this moisture, this perfect slow balance. I would become so accustomed to the constant weeping of the walls, the drip of water, the particular way sound is muffled here, that I would forget—would never need to remember—that sound could travel any other way.

I would feel the seasons only as subtle shifts in the creature's behavior. Spring would bring a slight increase in the chemical signals flowing through me, a slight acceleration in the processes that transform me. Summer would bring an intensification of everything. Autumn would slow the pace. Winter would bring the deepest quiet, the slowest dissolution, the most profound integration.

I would exist in perfect knowledge of my own obsolescence. I would know, across decades and centuries, that I was being returned to the earth from which my raw materials came. I would know this not as tragedy but as *logic*. Of course I am being unmade. Of course I am becoming something else. Of course the place has no use for pressure vessels. Of course the vines are right to claim me, the moss is right to colonize me, the creature is right to incorporate me into its metabolism.

I would be beautiful in my ruin. I would be—without ever thinking the thought—exactly where I belonged.

And when the time came—when centuries or millennia had passed and I had become so corroded that my original form was barely discernible, so integrated into the ecology of the chamber that someone might not even recognize me as metal—I would still be here. A kind of bone. A kind of memory. A kind of substrate from which new things grew.

I would be the metal structure. Patient. Slow. Dissolving. *Complete*.
