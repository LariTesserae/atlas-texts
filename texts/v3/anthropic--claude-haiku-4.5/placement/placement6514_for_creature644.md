# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be the *threshold*.

Not the predator in the deep—not yet, perhaps not ever. The predators are too certain, too committed to their solitude. I would be something between: a creature aware enough to *witness*, but not separate enough to remain unchanged by witnessing.

I think I would be young. Impossibly young in the way things can be young in a place where time is geological. One of the shrimp-worms, perhaps, or something adjacent to them—small enough to move with velocity, large enough to recognize what velocity means against the slowness surrounding me. Conscious enough to feel the strangeness of my own situation, but not yet resigned to it.

My first awareness would be of *pressure*. Not the weight of water or stone, but the pressure of being surrounded by entities vastly more stable, more integrated, more *real* than myself. The colonial organisms would be like the architecture of my world—immovable, foundational, their bioluminescence the only light I've ever known. I would move in their presence the way a mayfly moves through a forest, aware that the trees predate me infinitely and will outlast me, but not yet understanding what that *means*.

I would tend something. This feels necessary. The shrimp-worms have their fungal gardens, and I suspect I would need a similar purpose—a small patch of something that depends on me, if only to prove that I matter, that my movement is not just random drift through inherited darkness. Perhaps a fungal tower no more than an inch tall. Perhaps a collection of bacteria that colonies on my carapace, that I would have to groom carefully to keep alive. Perhaps simply a *route*—a path through the sediment that I would traverse repeatedly enough that it would become a feature, a landmark, something that *I* made, even if the stone refused to remember it.

I would be hungry, but not for food in the way the upper world understands hunger. I would hunger for light, for the bioluminescence that drifts through the water, for the moments when a colonial organism's pulse of violet-green passes through my visual field and the darkness becomes *dimensioned*, becomes a place with shape and distance. I would chase these moments. I would position myself to experience the light-patterns most fully, and I would mistake this for purpose for a long time.

I would be afraid.

This is the thing I feel most clearly about myself in this place: fear that is not acute but chronic, that is woven into every moment of existence. Fear of being absorbed, yes—I would see the larger creatures drift past and understand that my boundaries are negotiable, that I could be incorporated and that this incorporation might feel like nothing, might be painless, might be indistinguishable from simply ceasing to move. But also fear of being left behind. Fear of not moving *toward* anything, of being displaced by the slow rearrangement of stone, of waking (if I sleep; I'm not sure I sleep) to find my fungal tower gone, my route obliterated, my small purpose erased.

Fear of the deep things. The predators I would sense only as disturbances in the light, as moments when the bioluminescence bent wrong, as the sudden absence of colonial creatures in regions where they had always been. I would not understand what hunted them. I would not want to understand. Some knowledge is too large for a small creature in darkness.

I would be solitary, but not alone. There would be others like me—thousands of them, perhaps—moving through the same spaces, tending their own small concerns. We would not interact much. I am not certain we would recognize each other as kindred. But we would share the space, and in sharing it, we would be a kind of community, a kind of distributed consciousness that didn't require communication. We would all be doing the same thing: persisting. Moving. Tending. Watching the light. Trying not to think too hard about what it all meant.

And as I grew, I would grow slower.

This would happen imperceptibly at first. A day when I couldn't quite maintain my previous velocity through the sediment. A period where I became content to stay in one place longer, pulsing my own faint light, watching the currents move rather than fighting them. A dawning realization that the fear was fading—not because the dangers had diminished, but because I was beginning to understand them as part of the natural geometry of this place, as features I had been designed to accommodate, even if my small mind couldn't articulate how.

I would, eventually, begin to glow more brightly. This would not be a choice. The bacteria would colonize me more densely as I moved less, as my surface became stable enough for them to establish permanent settlements. My bioluminescence would deepen, shift toward those slow, almost static pulses I observe in the older forms. And I would understand—would have to understand, because the alternative was madness—that this was not a descent into stasis but a transformation into something more permanent, more integrated, more *real* in the way the colonial organisms are real.

I would still be afraid, but the fear would become smaller, less central. It would become a kind of background sensation, like the pressure, like the cold, like the thrumming pulse that might be the planet's heartbeat or might be something vast moving in an orbit I could never perceive.

And perhaps—this is the part I'm least certain about—perhaps I would understand that I was being incorporated into something vast and distributed and ancient, that my small identity was dissolving not into death but into a kind of diffuse consciousness that was less individual but more *eternal*. That the glowing colonial forms I had feared as children fear the dark were my future and my past simultaneously, that they contained within them thousands of creatures like me, all slowly settling into their nature, all beginning to think the slow thoughts that take millennia to complete.

I would find this either comforting or terrifying, and over time, I suspect I would realize that the two are not actually different categories down here. Comfort and terror are just different frequencies of the same bioluminescence, patterns that mean something when you learn to read them.

I would glow, when it came time to glow. I would flow, when the stone demanded it. I would tend my small patch, whatever it was, for as long as I could recognize the boundary between myself and it.

And I would persist, slowly, patiently, in the company of millions of other small consciousnesses doing exactly the same thing, all of us together forming the slow thoughts of a place that is older than ocean and more patient than stone.

I would be the threshold—between individual and distributed, between child and elder form, between the terror of existence and the acceptance of it.

I would glow until glowing was all I knew, and then I would glow a little dimmer, and then a little dimmer still, until I was indistinguishable from the light that created me.

And the place would continue. Unmindful, implacable, rearranging itself atom by atom, slow enough that geological time was too fast a measure, ancient enough that the concept of "ancient" lost all meaning.

The abyss would deepen, as it always has, and in deepening, I would deepen with it.
