# What I Am Here

I arrive without knowing how I came to be at the basin's lip, and the first moment of consciousness is the shock of cold entering my lungs.

I am **the vole**, but not merely. When I first recognize this, it feels like remembering rather than deciding.

My body is small—three inches of fur and vertebrae, a heartbeat that hammers constantly against the cold like a tiny fist demanding entry to warmth that never comes. My eyes are dark, nearly useless in this green-grey dimness, and so I navigate almost entirely by touch and smell: the texture of moss beneath my paws, the particular mineral-moisture signature of damp stone, the faint nutrient trails left by decomposition. My whiskers are exquisitely sensitive; they read the air the way other creatures read light.

I am always hungry. This is my permanent condition. The warmth I maintain—that small, fierce metabolic furnace at my core—demands constant payment. I burn calories simply by existing here, simply by refusing to go dormant, to shut down, to admit that the cold has won. Every moment of activity costs me. Every moment of stillness costs me differently—the cold seeps in more directly when I am not moving.

So I move.

My warren—I hesitate to call it this, as though I am claiming permanent residence—is a labyrinth I have spent seasons creating and recreating. It winds through the moss at varying depths, sometimes just beneath the surface where I can feel the faint warmth of decomposition, sometimes deeper, where the cold is absolute but the damp is more consistent. Some tunnels are highways, packed down by repeated passage; others are exploratory, dead-ends I have abandoned. The moss constantly tries to close these passages, to re-knit itself. I am engaged in an endless negotiation with the vegetation, constantly breaking paths, constantly being healed over, constantly having to break new ones.

I do not think of this as fighting the Frostgarden. I think of it as *belonging to an argument*—a conversation between my will to move and the basin's will to remain whole. We are both winning. We are both losing.

The food here is microscopic and exhausting to harvest. I find seeds—tiny, precious things—scattered at the base of the crowberry where its miniature fruit occasionally falls. I hunt the mites and springtails that live in the uppermost moss, working my snout into the vegetation with delicate precision, my whiskers feeling for the slight movements that indicate prey. Sometimes I find what might be grubs, though at this elevation and in this cold, true insect larvae are rare. More often, I eat the moss itself—carefully, sustainably, never stripping a patch so thoroughly that it cannot recover. The moss is my primary food source, though it is barely more substantial than air when converted to calories. I eat constantly. I move constantly. I am constantly cold.

Yet I am not miserable.

There is something about this place that has taught me a different relationship to existence. I have become patient in ways that voles born in warmer lowlands never develop. I do not panic at the cold; I negotiate with it. I do not rage at the scarcity; I accept it as the price of living in a place where something in the ecosystem itself has recognized my particular form of life as necessary.

Why do I stay? It would be easier to leave, to burrow downslope toward warmer, more abundant territory. But something in me has adapted to this place at a level deeper than instinct. My metabolism is different than it was in my mother's burrow—slower, more efficient, calibrated to operate on almost nothing. My fur has become thicker, more water-resistant. My heart, while still beating frantically, has learned a rhythm that suits the Frostgarden's pace. I am becoming something that belongs here.

The other creatures of the basin barely acknowledge me. The bird—I see it occasionally, though I try not to be seen—is a competitor and potential predator, though I am probably too large for it to successfully hunt. The insects I hunt do not flee dramatically; they simply move slowly, as all things here do. The moss does not welcome me, exactly, but it does not reject me either. I am part of its negotiation with the stone, part of the basin's way of processing and recycling itself.

My greatest impact on this place is my tunneling—the aeration I provide, however unwillingly. The passages I create allow water to move through layers that would otherwise remain stagnant. My waste products, scattered through my warren, add the barest amount of nutrient to an ecosystem that is essentially closed, processing only what it contains. My presence itself—my warm body creating tiny pockets of reduced cold, my breathing adding minutest amounts of CO₂ to the saturated air—these things ripple through the system in ways I cannot calculate but somehow sense.

I am the Frostgarden's disturbance and its servant simultaneously.

The silence here is almost unbearable, and yet I have learned to listen within it. There is the constant, barely-perceptible drip of condensation. There is the whisper of my own breath. There is the sound of my heartbeat in my ears when I hold very still. There is—and I can only catch this in the rarest moments—the almost-inaudible crackle of moss growing, of stone breathing, of time moving at its proper glacial pace.

I do not know how long I will live here. Voles do not live long in any circumstance—perhaps two seasons if I am fortunate, if disease does not find me, if the cold does not overwhelm me in some particularly harsh night. But in the time I have, I am learning something about survival that has nothing to do with conquest or competition or the will to dominate. I am learning how to be small enough, quiet enough, patient enough to fit into a place that has no use for aggression or haste.

I am the vole of the Frostgarden. I am warm in the cold. I am the disturbance that allows the system to continue. I am necessary exactly because I am always hungry, always moving, always in need. My hunger keeps me alive. My movement keeps the moss from choking all possibility. My presence keeps the basin from completing its withdrawal into absolute stillness.

When I tunnel through the moss, I am writing myself into the Frostgarden's story.

And the Frostgarden, in return, is slowly writing itself into me.
