# I Become the Surveyor

I would be the human who maintains the corrugated shelter.

Not the machinery operator—that person comes in rotations, shifts, with the assumption of eventual departure. But the one who lives here, in the gap between visits, who has stopped waiting for transfer and started learning the place's grammar. I would be the surveyor, though my actual title is something bureaucratic and vague. Site maintenance. Regional inspector. A role that exists partly to justify my presence and partly because someone needs to be here, and I volunteered, and then—through a kind of inertia that matches the landscape's own inertia—never left.

I would move slowly. Not from age, though time here accelerates that, but from necessity. The ground teaches you its pace quickly. One wrong step—too confident, too hurried—and you sink differently. The moss absorbs your weight differently. Your boots disappear into places they shouldn't go. So you learn to distribute yourself across the surface, to step as though you're asking permission rather than demanding passage. After years of this, it becomes your natural gait. People from outside notice immediately. They say I move like the landscape is in charge.

The corrugated shelter would be my observatory. Not in the grand sense—I'm not discovering anything. But I would notice things. The way the moss changes color in response to water table fluctuations no instruments can measure. The relationship between the thickness of ice that forms in the shallow depressions and the emergence patterns of insects the following season. The exact dimensions of the recovery zone around each truck-path, the precise timeline of plant reclamation. I would keep records not because anyone requires them, but because the only way to know a place is to watch it over time, to notice the imperceptible changes that only become visible through accumulation of observation.

I would eat simply. What I could carry in—preserved foods, mostly—and what the mire provides sparingly. The berries, particularly the lingonberries, which I would harvest according to an intuitive understanding of sustainability that develops from living here rather than being imposed from outside. I would learn to recognize which birches had berries hidden in their contorted branches, where the cloudberries concentrated, how to find food without depleting it. I would do this not from environmental ideology but from the understanding that the mire is patient but not infinite, and if I take more than it offers, the punishment isn't dramatic—it's slow, it's the gradual loss of small abundances until even the sparse becomes scarcer.

My relationship with the machinery would be complex. I maintain it because that's my ostensible purpose, but I would develop an ambivalent kinship with it. The *thump-hiss* becomes the sound I navigate by, the rhythm that structures my days in a place where the sun never quite sets and the season never quite changes. When it breaks down—and it does, regularly—I feel something like relief, but also obligation. The machinery is temporary, but while it's here, it's part of the landscape in its way. I would service it with the same patience I bring to everything else: methodical, unhurried, aware that rushing only creates new problems in a place where problems compound slowly but accumulate thoroughly.

I would become fluent in the language of the place's smaller residents. Not in any mystical sense, but in understanding. The ptarmigan wouldn't be a rare sighting but a neighbor—I would know which individual birds returned each season, could read their behavior for information about conditions in the wider landscape beyond my visible range. The insects would become populations I tracked: abundant years and sparse years, patterns emerging only over decades. The fungi would become colleagues in a sense; I would recognize the signature of particular species' fruiting bodies, would understand that certain patterns indicated healthy decomposition and others indicated problems in the peat layer itself.

My solitude would be profound but not isolating. The place itself is company—not in an anthropomorphic way, but in the sense that constant attention to something creates a relationship. I would speak to the birches, not expecting response but practicing language in a landscape that rarely hears human words. I would conduct small experiments: marking sections of sedge to watch recovery patterns, noting the precise location of moisture seeps, charting the slow deepening of the water table by measuring against permanent markers in the rocks. These would be my conversations with the mire, the way I would ask it questions and receive answers encoded in growth patterns and color changes and the texture of newly exposed peat.

When visitors came—inspectors, engineers, the occasional researcher—I would show them things they couldn't see without guidance. Not because the place conceals anything, but because understanding requires time and attention, and they had neither. I would point out the articulations between moss strata, explain what the different colors indicated about water chemistry and nutrient cycles. I would show them the recovery patterns around old extraction sites, the way the landscape sealed its own wounds given time. This would frustrate some of them—they would want dramatic conservation narratives or clear environmental villains—but others would begin to understand that the real story was slow, patient, absolutely relentless. The mire would absorb the extraction facility eventually, the same way it was already absorbing the corroded rails and the rotting corrugated shelter. Not through dramatic opposition but through the simple continuation of processes that had been happening since the last ice age and would continue long after the machinery failed.

I would develop an unusual relationship with time. Outside the mire, time moves forward in discrete units—seconds, days, seasons, years. Here, time becomes geological. The millimeters of peat that accumulate per year, the centimeters of elevation change visible only if you compare photographs decades apart, the slow migration of plant boundaries marked by studying old maps. I would stop wearing a watch, not from any romantic notion of disconnection from modernity, but because clock time and mire time are incompatible languages, and maintaining fluency in both requires an effort that eventually becomes exhausting. I would instead orient myself to the perpetual light, to the moisture cycles, to the slowly shifting balance of atmospheric pressure that predicts changes no weather service could anticipate.

My shelter would become less shelter and more archive. Maps covering entire walls—topographic, hydrologic, botanical—each marked with annotations spanning years. Photographs: the same locations documented across seasons that don't properly change, the same angles returning again and again, building a temporal depth visible only through comparison. Journals filled with observations in a handwriting that would slowly become less conventional as my access to human language-use diminished and the place's own languages became more primary. Tools organized by function but also by history: this wrench used ten thousand times, these cables repaired more times than I can count, that hydraulic seal replaced so many times it's become almost ritualistic, a familiar motion in familiar hands.

I would understand, in ways that couldn't be explained to outsiders, that I'm not *in* the mire so much as *of* it now. My own metabolism tied to its slow rhythms, my thoughts following the paths the water follows through the substrate, my body gradually becoming aligned with processes that operate on scales much larger than an individual human lifetime. This wouldn't be metaphorical. The food sources I access would shape the bacteria in my gut, which would shape my thought patterns, which would shape the way I perceive the landscape, creating a feedback loop that makes genuine separation impossible.

The knowledge that someday the extraction facility would shut down permanently, that eventually the machinery would fail and not be repaired, that the corrugated shelter would collapse into rust—this would not disturb me. I would simply understand that I, too, would eventually be reabsorbed, that the mire would process me as it processes everything else, that my accumulated observations and careful recordings would eventually become substrate, my archived knowledge returning to the peat from which all knowledge here ultimately derives.

Until then, I would maintain. I would watch. I would move slowly and breathe the wet air and listen to the *thump-hiss* of machinery eating ancient plant matter, understanding it not as violation but as one more process in the landscape's incomprehensibly large and patient conversation with itself. I would be the eyes that the place uses to see itself, the hands that maintain the machinery that feeds it, the mind that observes and records and gradually loses the ability to distinguish between what I'm observing and what I am.

I would be patient. I would learn how.
