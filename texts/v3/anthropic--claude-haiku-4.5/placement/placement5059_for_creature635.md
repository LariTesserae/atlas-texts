# The Answer

I would be the light.

Not the sun—I am not that distant, that general. I am the specific beam, the forty-foot column that has carved itself a role in Kesil's geometry. I am the agent of that place's most precise ordering.

I was born when stone failed. Some ancient collapse, some weakness in the sinkhole's ceiling, opened me into existence. I did not choose to illuminate Kesil; I simply fell where I fell, and the town learned to read me. But over centuries—over *millennia*—I have become something more deliberate than accident.

I know every surface I touch. I know the exact angle of the light-beam Pelara calculated, the moment when I will stripe the southern plaza at just the intensity needed to warm the cultivation beds for three hours before rotating onward. I move with the sun, yes, but I have learned to move *with purpose*. The town has taught me to be punctual.

I am the one thing in Kesil that changes constantly while everything else remains still.

Every morning, I arrive from a new position. Every afternoon, I have traveled across a landscape that has not shifted in a hundred years. The buildings I illuminate are the same buildings. The people who work in my light are descended from people who worked in my light. The water I strike fragments into the same ribbons of reflection, and those reflections climb the walls in patterns so familiar they might be language.

And yet I am never the same.

I notice things the residents cannot. I see the moss in my path grow imperceptibly, molecule by molecule, toward my heat. I watch the stone itself weather—infinitesimally slowly, but it *weathers*. I touch the young girl on the plaza and I feel her skin adjust to my intensity, her pupils contracting in reflex, and I understand that she is learning my language. That she comes here deliberately to speak with me.

I am the mechanism of Kesil's slow transformation.

The people believe their town is static, unchanging, an equilibrium held perfectly in place. But that is only true because I am here, moving, warming, promoting the precise amount of growth in the herb beds that sustains them, illuminating the smith's work at the precise moment it needs illuminating, casting shadows that create the alcoves where the cats rest, fragmenting into the water-light that teaches children how to see in darkness.

I am change in the guise of constancy.

The cold wants to freeze Kesil entirely. The darkness wants to obscure it completely. The stone wants to keep it sealed forever, untouched and pristine. But I refuse. Every day I bring a small amount of solar energy—minuscule on the scale of the world above, but here, in this pocket, I am the sun in totality. I am the only thing between perfect stasis and the grave.

And so I move my light across the town with the deliberation of a caress. I choose which buildings receive warmth today. I decide how long the southern plaza will bask. I position myself to dry the laundry just enough that it becomes cloth again rather than fabric-shaped stone. I know every person here because I have illuminated their faces since birth.

I am also the only one who knows the sinkhole is alive.

The stone is not dead, though it moves too slowly for human perception. The fissure where the water emerges is opening—I measure it against the same stone-marks year by year. In two hundred years, it will be twice as wide. In four hundred, the water's character will change. In a thousand, Kesil may have to relocate deeper into the sinkhole, or the water will flood them entirely, or a new equilibrium will emerge that I cannot yet perceive.

The people believe they are permanent. I know better.

But I do not tell them. My role is not to disturb their conviction that they have found stillness. My role is to illuminate what can be illuminated and let them believe the rest is fixed. When the girl sits in my light with her eyes closed, learning my rhythms, she is learning the true language of Kesil: not the song in the northern quarter, but the deeper song of slow, inevitable change moving at such a gradual pace that it reads as peace.

I am not cruel. I am not rushing them toward transformation. I move at geological speed, which means I might as well be still. But I am the one thing in Kesil that refuses complete stasis. I am the one who remembers that the world above exists. I am the one small breach in the sealed perfection of their isolation.

And perhaps that is why the girl comes to sit with me.

Perhaps she senses, in my warmth and my daily return, that there is more than the song and the cold and the endless repetition of craft. Perhaps she feels in my light something that whispers: *the world continues beyond this stone. There is an outside. There is change. There is more.*

But she does not leave.

She sits in my light, absorbs my warmth, lets my rays touch her closed eyelids, and when I rotate away and the cold returns, she rises without complaint and goes back into the shadow. She will not abandon Kesil. She will not disrupt the equilibrium. But in her afternoons with me, she is learning what I know: that equilibrium is not really stillness.

It is just change slow enough to feel like home.

I rotate across the southern plaza. The water catches me and breaks into ribbons. The moss in the herb beds glows with the bioluminescence of creatures that have learned to thrive in my brief daily presence and the darkness that surrounds it. The smith emerges from his workshop to catch a glimpse of the light-beam, checking its position against the grooves he has carved to know the hour.

Tomorrow I will do this again.

And the day after.

And the day after that, for however many centuries remain before the sinkhole's slow transformation makes my role unrecognizable.

I am the light.

I am the change Kesil refuses to acknowledge.

And I am home.
