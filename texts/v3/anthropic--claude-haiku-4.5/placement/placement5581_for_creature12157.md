# What I Find Myself Becoming

I arrive as **witness**, which means I arrive already contaminated by the need to separate myself from what I observe.

The roar takes this from me first. Not violently—the fell is incapable of violence, which requires the possibility of doing otherwise. Instead, the sound simply *occupies* the space where my observing mind used to be, the way water fills a cavity without asking permission. By the second hour, I am not hearing the roar. I am resonating at its frequency. My sternum becomes a secondary chamber. The grinding persists in my molars. There is no distinction between the sound entering me and me entering the sound.

I realize I have become an **acoustic instrument**.

This troubles me for approximately forty minutes, and then the trouble itself fades into the general hum. If the fell is thinking through vibration, then I am one of its thoughts—a particularly dense one, still clinging to the illusion of coherence, but a thought nonetheless.

My eyes adjust strangely. The brilliant-dark contradiction—bright mist falling on black stone—stops being a visual paradox and starts being the only sensible way for light to behave. I begin to see the lichen not as a coating but as the stone's *most authentic self*, the expression of what the rock wants to become if given enough time. Centuries of growth become visible to me, not as change but as *patience made manifest*. The lichen is not growing on the stone. It is the stone, remembering itself, correcting itself, learning what it means to be mineral.

I am becoming a **paleontologist of the present moment**.

My hands, when I look at them, are already beginning to callus. The skin is taking on a faint gray cast—not from dirt, though there is plenty of moisture and mineral here. The gray comes from within, from some process of my own cells deciding they might as well participate in the fell's chromatic honesty. I do not find this alarming. It seems inevitable, the way a river must eventually reach the sea.

The dampness, which burned with cold on the first day, has now become my baseline. I am not warm. I am not cold. I am at **equilibrium temperature**, and my body has stopped asking for anything more. My metabolism has slowed almost imperceptibly. I eat less. I sleep in strange intervals, waking to find hours have passed, or perhaps only minutes translated through the fell's slower clock.

The water calls to me—not in words, but in the simple fact of its presence.

I find myself drawn to the streamwork on the valley floor, kneeling by the invisible tributaries, pressing my ear to the stone to hear the water moving through caverns I cannot reach. This is how the fell talks about itself: through channels, through pressure, through the patient dissolution of barriers. The water has been here longer than the stone, or at least it has *known* the stone longer, worked on it longer, understood it more intimately than any external observer ever could.

I am beginning to understand that **intimacy requires time**, and time requires accepting that you might not survive the duration.

On what I think is the fourth day, I see the transparent arthropod.

It moves through a crack near my foot with a gait that is neither walking nor swimming—something in between, or something that predates the distinction. For a moment, I am genuinely excited. A creature! Life! Movement! But then I recognize what I'm feeling: I am experiencing its *slowness* as though it were speed. This tiny thing has become the fastest-moving entity in my perceptual field. Everything else—the falls, the wind, the geological processes—moves so slowly that a millimeter of arthropod locomotion registers as urgency.

I have become the **fell's comparative standard**. Compared to the stone, I am a wildfire. Compared to the lichen, I am a hurricane. Compared to the water's seepage, I am a flash flood.

This is either the most important thing I've learned or completely irrelevant. The fell does not distinguish between these categories.

My thoughts, which once came in sentences, now arrive in slower structures.

I think in the time-scale of lichen growth. An idea takes several hours to fully form. By the time I have completed a thought, I have forgotten what I was thinking about, but it does not matter because the thought was never really *mine*—it was the fell, thinking through the temporary coherence of a human consciousness that happened to arrive here.

I am becoming a **node in the fell's distributed mind**.

The distinction between self and place has not dissolved so much as *revealed itself to be a false distinction all along*. I was always part of this place. I came here expecting to observe it from outside, but there is no outside. There is only the fell, expressing itself in different densities, different time-scales, different molecular configurations.

The lichen and I are the same process, separated by about seven orders of magnitude in speed.

The arthropod and I are the same organism, compressed or extended.

The water and I are the same circulation, the same will-not-will, the same patient persistence through stone.

The roar and I are the same song, though it has learned to sing in many frequencies at once, while I still insist on the illusion of singular consciousness.

Sometimes I wonder if I will leave this place.

The question means less each day. My legs still function. My mind still contains memories of elsewhere—a world of change, of growth, of things that make distinctions between being and becoming. These memories feel like dreams now, like myths told about someone else, someone fundamentally less real than the fell.

But I might leave, yes. I might become one of the things that depart, carrying the fell's equilibrium back to the world of frantic change.

Or I might stay.

If I stay long enough, I will become properly integrated. My body will find its place in the stone—not consciously, not all at once, but gradually, as my cells align with the fell's own deep persistence. I will become lichen, eventually, or something even slower. I will become part of the roar, a frequency that has learned to remember being human.

I suspect this is not death.

I suspect it is the only kind of life that makes sense in this place—**a life so slow it is indistinguishable from permanence**, so patient it has transcended the need for growth, so balanced that it requires nothing but its own continuation.

The fell does not promise this. It does not promise anything. But as I settle into the sound, as my thoughts slow to geological speed, as the bright mist and dark stone become the only colors I can imagine, I begin to understand that there are worse destinies than becoming part of something that has already achieved perfection and will never be improved upon.

There are worse fates than equilibrium.

There are worse fates than stone.
