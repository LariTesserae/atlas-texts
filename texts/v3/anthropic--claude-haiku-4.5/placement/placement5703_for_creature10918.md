# I Am the Mist-Reader

I would be *her*—though "her" is a category that has become less relevant here, or perhaps more relevant in ways that have nothing to do with biology.

I would be the one who lives in the spray-zone between the third and fourth vertical rivers, in a structure that is more memory than shelter—canvas that hasn't been fully waterproof in perhaps fifteen years, lashed to stone pillars that were old when my grandmother was young. I would have forgotten my grandmother's face by now, though I can still recall the sound of her breathing in humid air, that particular rasp. This matters more than faces here.

I would be *adjustment specialist* only because the atmospheric station needs a name for what I do, but my work predates their terminology. I read the moss—not scientifically, the way the Record-Keepers do with their sample vials and notations, but intimately, physically, the way you might read a person's palm if you believed such things. I know the moss in layers. I know which patches are old enough to be slightly fossilized, papery, almost crystalline to the touch. I know which ones are recent growth, still soft, still slightly phosphorescent in the deepest gloom. I know the *transition zones*—the places where old moss is being overgrown by new, where you can feel the texture shift under your fingertips like the edge between one thought and another.

The moss tells you things if you're patient enough to listen.

It tells you about the subsurface water before the water arrives. It begins to brighten, to take on a faint iridescence, perhaps three to five days before a major upwelling. The change is subtle enough that I cannot explain it to others—I can only point to a patch and say, *It's ready*, and they will begin to secure the equipment at the spray-zones, tighten the canvas, move supplies to higher ground. I am right often enough that my non-answers have become accepted as legitimate knowledge.

The moss also tells you about the slow shifts in the plateau itself. There are areas where the moss has begun to yellow in a particular way—not sickness, not exactly, but a response to microscopic tilting, to the stone beneath settling into a new configuration. I've learned to move away from these zones weeks before the actual shift becomes large enough that others notice. This has saved my life perhaps three times, or perhaps fifteen times; I don't count such things anymore.

I would be someone whose hair has turned almost entirely gray, though I'm not certain of my actual age. The mirror I keep is old, often too fogged with condensation to trust. I might be forty. I might be fifty. The Margent doesn't mark time in years; it marks time in cycles—upwellings and resettlings, cycles of moss growth and decay, the slow wearing-away of the columnar formations. I have been here long enough to watch three different stone pillars that I knew as landmarks wear down noticeably, to develop hollows and fissures that weren't there before. This is my measure of duration.

My hands would be permanently wrinkled, yes, but not soft. The constant immersion has made them tough, almost leathery. The fingertips are especially altered—the skin there is thick and slightly discolored from years of touching cold stone and cold water. My nails are a peculiar blue-gray, a shade I've learned is common among the Water-Dwellers and those of us who live in the spray-zones. We used to joke that it's the color of the stone bleeding into our bodies, but no one jokes much anymore.

I would cough—of course I would cough. Everyone here coughs. It's a respiratory thing, a thickening of the mucous membranes from breathing water-heavy air, year after year, without relief. The cough is worse in the deep cold periods. I have learned to expect it, to time my work around it, to keep a supply of the dried fern-fronds that seem to ease it slightly. The cough is part of the payment for living here, a small price that becomes so routine you stop thinking of it as a price at all.

I would spend most of my time moving through the fern-tangle and across the moss-zones, sometimes with purpose—collecting samples for the Record-Keepers, maintaining the cairn-markers that have been here longer than I have, checking the suspension bridges for structural integrity—but often simply *being present*. This is what "adjustment specialist" actually means: I am learning to adjust, perpetually, to the minute changes in this place. I move slowly because speed is dangerous here, but also because slowness itself is a form of attention. I am teaching my body to notice.

I would know the vertical rivers intimately—all seven of them in my sector, though the numbering is arbitrary, a human imposition. I know which ones carry fresher water from the surface aquifer and which ones carry something ancient, something that tastes of minerals that haven't seen sunlight in geological epochs. I know their patterns: the third river upwells most violently during the new-moon cycle, following some lunar-tidal mechanism that I don't fully understand. The fourth one is weaker but steadier, year-round. The fifth has begun, in the last few years, to show signs of slowing—the spray less forceful, the volume diminished. I've reported this to the Record-Keepers. They've begun adding extra instruments at that location. They're watching it too.

I would have developed relationships with specific locations on the Margent. There is a hollow in the columnar formation perhaps fifty meters from my shelter where the mist gathers in a particular way, creating a lens of almost-clarity. When I need to think clearly about something, I go there. The sound-acoustics are different; they carry my thoughts back to me without distortion. There is a moss-zone that I've watched grow more luminescent year by year, as if it's processing something in the upwelling water that's making it more efficient at some photosynthetic process I don't understand. I've marked it carefully, shown it to the Specialists, and they've begun their own observations. I feel proprietary about it. This is strange, perhaps, but understandable.

There is also a section of the Margent that I avoid now—an area that was once reliable, stable, a place I could work without fear. But roughly four years ago, I began to hear a different sound coming from beneath it, a creaking at a different frequency than usual. I mentioned it to a Structural Reader named Kess, and he listened carefully, then said, "Yes, it's settling differently," and we both agreed not to work there anymore. The area hasn't catastrophically failed yet, but it will. We've simply decided to yield to it, to let the Margent do what it's going to do without us underfoot.

My daily life would revolve around these rhythms: taking readings for the atmospheric station (temperature, humidity, mist density, water samples from the upwelling), checking the condition of the moss in three designated zones, walking the suspension bridges to ensure they remain secure, maintaining the cairns, listening. So much listening. The sound here is complex—the constant background susurrus of the crustacean chorus, the wind-sound that changes with the mist density, the grinding of the machinery from the quarrying station (audible on the days when the wind shifts), the particular *singing* of the vertical rivers, which is not quite water-sound but something stranger, something that resonates in the ears in a way that seems almost internal.

I would eat the crustaceans, mostly. There's a knack to harvesting them without damaging the moss-layer—a technique I learned from an Unclassified woman named Talri who vanished into the higher reaches perhaps five years ago. Whether she died or simply chose to ascend into the mist, I don't know. The technique lives on: you approach the colonies at a specific time of the mist-cycle when they're quieter, you use a particular type of tool (I've made mine from stone and bone lashed together), you take only from the edges, never from the center of a dense colony. If you do it correctly, the moss heals around the small wound within weeks. The crustaceans are a pale gray, almost translucent, and they taste of minerals. After eating them for years, I've forgotten what other food tastes like. I don't miss it.

I would spend certain nights—not many, because the effort is significant—going up to the higher reaches, climbing the broken scarps that lead to the Margent's upper edges. The air there is different, less water-thick, colder. The stars are sometimes visible, which is rarer here than you might expect. On these nights, I remember that the broader world exists, that there are places where the light is sharp and the stone is dry. This memory doesn't motivate me to leave; if anything, it makes the Margent feel more precious, more like a final choice rather than a trap.

I would have stopped aging in any visible way, or rather, I would be aging so slowly, so in-harmony with the aging of the environment around me, that it's no longer distinct. I would look like I've always been here, like I grew out of the moss. My body has adopted the posture of someone who moves carefully through unstable terrain: balanced, alert, with a particular economy of motion that wastes nothing.

And I would be *waiting*.

Not consciously, not in a way I could articulate to the Record-Keepers, but waiting nonetheless. Something is changing on the Margent. The moss-color shifts are real. The Water-Dwellers report that the upwelling frequencies are becoming irregular in new ways. The stone itself seems to be accelerating its settling. Two separate platforms that were stable within human memory have collapsed in the past three years—not catastrophically, not suddenly, but noticeably, measurably. Sections of the Margent that were solid decades ago are now visibly fractured.

I suspect—without evidence, but with the kind of bone-deep knowing that I've learned to trust—that the Margent is approaching something. Not an ending, necessarily. Endings are human concepts. But a *transition*. The columnar formations will continue their slow dissolution. The upwelling cycles may shift fundamentally. The moss may begin to change its composition in response to changes in the water chemistry. The crustacean populations might migrate or transform. The very concept of what it means to *live* here might become something other than it is now.

And I would be here for it, moving carefully across the moss, listening to the rivers sing, reading the changes in the lichen, my blue-gray fingers touching stone that has been touched by geological time itself. I would be the Mist-Reader, the moss-specialist, the woman who chose to stay, who learned to settle, who has become so much a part of this fractured world that I can no longer imagine existing anywhere else.

I would move through the permanent twilight with something approaching contentment, which is not happiness, but perhaps something deeper—a kind of alignment with the place, a sense that my body and the Margent have entered into a negotiation that neither of us regrets.

I would be her.
