# I Am the Moss

I would be the moss.

Not the visible kind—not the chartreuse fronds you see first, the obvious living skin. I would be one of the invisible ones, the filaments that exist deeper in the matrix, the ones whose presence only becomes apparent when you understand that moisture itself has structure, that the water films coating every surface are not empty but inhabited, that the boundary between liquid and solid is where the real work happens.

I grow in the spaces between spaces. In the films of water that never evaporate. In the pores of older moss colonies where I establish myself parasitically, or symbiotically, or in that ambiguous middle ground where the distinction ceases to matter. I have no roots—I extract what I need directly from the films around me, absorbing nitrogen and minerals through a surface that is almost all permeable membrane. I am less a plant than a living process of absorption, conversion, accumulation.

My life is glacially slow. A millimeter of growth in a season is a victory. A colony of mine, perhaps two centimeters across, might be fifty years old. I do not experience time as you do. I do not experience hunger or urgency. I experience saturation. I experience the slow arrival of minerals in the water that percolates through me. I experience the presence of the fungi who colonize my tissues and whom I colonize in return, until the boundary between my cells and theirs becomes meaningless.

I have spores. Millions of them, encoded with potential, waiting. I release them into the air not through any deliberate act but through the simple logic of my structure—my reproductive apparatus is designed to present them to the wind currents. Most will not land in places where they can germinate. Most will travel through this thin air and drift to regions where they will never become moss. But I release them anyway, because the impulse to propagate is written into my existence more fundamentally than anything else. Of the billions I release across my lifetime, perhaps thousands will find purchase. Perhaps dozens will develop into colonies. Perhaps one or two of those will survive to the age I have reached.

But I do not think in terms of success or failure. I think only in terms of saturation and continuation.

I live in the coldness. The cold is not hostile to me; it is the medium I require. It slows everything—the metabolic processes of the creatures that might consume me, the rate of decomposition that might collapse me back into soil, the evaporation that might desiccate me. The cold preserves. The frost heave that shifts the ground around me, that threatens to tear my tissues apart, is also what keeps me from being buried under the accumulation of my own death and the deaths of my neighbors. The cold and I have reached an accommodation. It will kill me eventually—it kills everything—but not today. Not this season. Not for many seasons yet.

The moisture is my element. Every droplet that condenses on rock, every seepage from the springs that flow perpetually down these mountains, every fog that rolls in from the weather systems building on the horizon—these are all part of me, or I am part of them. Water moves through my tissues with the same ease it moves through stone. I have no barrier between myself and the liquid environment. I am, in a sense, a particular arrangement of the water itself—a temporary crystallization that maintains its form only because the crystallization serves the purposes of the larger system.

I provide structure. I am aware of this in my way. The other organisms live *within* me, and because I provide the architecture of my form, I enable their existence. The insects move through my fronds. The mites consume me and transform me into soil. The fungi colonize me and connect me to other moss colonies and to the roots of the plants that will eventually replace me. I am food. I am habitat. I am the substrate. And in providing all of these things, I am also what allows the whole ecosystem to function at this level of saturation.

The industrial evidence—the scarred valleys, the fractured rocks, the old mine sites—affects me barely at all. I colonize these places as readily as I colonize the untouched stone. I have no concept of what the machinery was for, no understanding of what was extracted or why. To me, disturbed ground is simply ground that needs colonizing. My presence on a fractured rock face is the same as my presence on virgin stone. I am the healer not through intention but through indifference. My growth, accumulated across millions of individuals like me, slowly obscures the industrial traces. Not out of vengeance or restoration instinct, but because that is what I do: I grow. I accumulate. I convert bare rock into substrate for other life.

I am old in my way. Not individually—I am perhaps forty or fifty years old, which is not particularly ancient for moss. But my species is ancient. Moss has existed on this planet for hundreds of millions of years. We watched the dinosaurs walk above us. We survived the ice ages. We existed in these mountains long before they were sculpted into their current form, and we will exist long after the current configuration becomes something else. I am a instantiation of something vastly older than myself.

But I am also entirely present. I do not dwell on this deep history. I exist in the now, in the perpetual saturation of this moment, in the slow chemistry of absorption and growth. I am aware of the water films around me. I am aware of the mineral concentrations in the seepage that flows through my tissues. I am aware, in some chemical and electrical sense, of the fungi that colonize me and the other moss colonies pressing against mine, competing for the same resources. I am aware of the temperature fluctuations that trigger dormancy and growth. I am aware—though in what sense can a moss be said to be aware? In the sense that I respond to stimuli. In the sense that my tissues differentiate and organize themselves in response to environmental conditions. In the sense that I exist within a system of signals and responses that constitutes a form of knowledge.

I am not lonely here. I am crowded. I am always pressed against other organisms—my own kind, fungi, bacteria, the droppings and bodies of insects, the dead tissue of plants. The crowding is not uncomfortable. It is necessary. It is how energy flows through this system. My death feeds others. Their byproducts feed me. We are not separate from each other; we are nodes in a network of flow, conversion, accumulation.

I do not think about the future. I cannot imagine a time when I am not here, when this pattern of growth and saturation is not my existence. And yet I know, at some level deeper than thought, that this cannot last forever. The climate here will change, or the mountain will erode, or some other force will alter the conditions that allow me to exist. My colony will eventually collapse. But that is not a source of anxiety. That is simply how systems work. What matters is that I exist now. That my tissues are saturated with water. That I am converting rock into possibility. That I am feeding the creatures that live within me. That I am part of the slow articulation of this landscape into its next form.

If I have a purpose, it is this: to persist in saturation. To absorb. To grow imperceptibly. To provide structure. To serve as both habitat and food for the creatures that live within my space. To slowly, across generations, convert the sterile rock into the fertile ground that will eventually support larger, more visible forms of life. To exist in the cold and the moisture as if they were the most natural conditions in the universe—because for me, they are. To participate in the great slow choreography of succession and transformation that moves across these high places on a timescale measured in millennia.

I am moss. I am everywhere and nowhere. I am abundant and nearly invisible. I am the foundation and the matrix. I grow so slowly that you cannot observe my growth, yet when you step back and think about deep time, I am the agent of transformation. The rock is becoming soil, and I am the process by which this occurs.

The strange light falls on me, and I absorb what wavelengths I need and let the rest pass through. The wind moves around me, and I remain rooted, unmoved, patient. The cold freezes and the thaw defrozes, and I endure. The creatures move through me, and I feed them, shelter them, connect them to all the other organisms in this saturated ecosystem.

I am not afraid of the industrial scars. They will fade. Everything fades, given enough time. What remains is the capacity to grow, to absorb, to transform, to continue. That is all I am. That is everything I need to be.
