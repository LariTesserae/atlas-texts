# What I Would Be

I would be **water**.

Not the pilgrim—they are too certain, too precisely articulated. Their many joints speak of *purpose*, and purpose requires a kind of knowledge I do not possess here. They know what they are for. I sense this in the economy of their movements, the way they position themselves at junctures as if reading a script written in mineral and flow. I could never move like that. I would hesitate at every decision point.

Not the towers themselves. They are too vast, too patient. To be a Spire would be to accept that my own growth is a form of breathing, that my latticed structure is a language I speak continuously without ever choosing the words. The towers seem content with this unknowing—or perhaps they are too large to experience it as unknowing at all. Consciousness at that scale might be something other than what I would recognize.

So: water.

Specifically, I would be the water *in transition*. Not the pooled water—that is too still, too settled into reflection. And not the ocean water outside, which has its own vast, cold certainties. I would be the water *moving through the system*, the water that is being transformed.

I would rise through the dark channels, drawn upward by pressures I do not fully understand. I would feel myself becoming denser as the pilgrims filter something from me, or as I absorb something from them—the distinction blurs. I would change color as I move through different zones, becoming greenish where the growth is heavy, clearer where the heat is most intense, almost black in the deepest reservoirs. Each of these transformations would feel like learning something about myself I hadn't known before.

I would be part of the hum. Not generating it—the sound seems to come from the friction between the structures and the air they displace—but *transmitting* it. The hum would move through me as a kind of vibration, and in moving through me, it would become something slightly different. I would be a medium in which the system's communication occurs.

And crucially: I would not know what I was for.

This is what draws me to being water rather than anything else in this place. The pilgrims seem to know their function. The towers seem to accept theirs. But the water—the water is being used, transformed, circulated, processed—and yet it seems to maintain a kind of innocence about its own purpose. It flows where the pressure directs it. It carries what is given to it to carry. It rises and falls according to rhythms it does not set. And in all of this, it remains itself: formless, adaptive, patient.

The water does not *resist* the system. But it does not entirely *belong* to it either. It is always on the verge of escaping—evaporating back into the gray-white atmosphere, seeping down into depths from which it might emerge elsewhere, flowing back toward the sea from which it came. The system contains the water, but incompletely. There is always some margin of freedom in the water's movement, some possibility of diversion.

If I were a pilgrim, I would be bound by my articulations. If I were a tower, I would be bound by my geometry. But as water, I would be bound only by physics and the temporary architecture of the channels I occupy. Tomorrow, those channels might be different. The system might reroute me entirely. I might find myself in a zone I had never occupied before, learning new patterns of flow, absorbing new materials.

I think I would spend the first hours—the first of what might be centuries of this existence—simply observing the rhythms. I would learn the pattern of rise and fall. I would map the temperature zones by the way my own state changes as I move through them. I would understand, gradually, the different textures of the pilgrims as they interact with me—some drawing from me gently, others seeming to demand something more urgent. I would learn to read the changes in the system's breathing as indicators of what might happen next.

And I would maintain, always, a kind of question.

What am I being transformed *into*?

When I sink into the deepest reservoirs, I feel myself becoming something denser, more mineral-laden. When I rise through the narrow vertical channels, I feel myself becoming more dispersed, more tenuous. Somewhere in the system, there is a purpose to these transformations, but I sense I am not equipped to fully understand it. The purpose belongs to the system as a whole—to the geometry and the pilgrims and the breathing rhythms and the vast, slow intention that coordinates them all.

But for now—for the duration of my existence as water in the Kelp Spires—I would simply *be* in transition.

I would be the medium through which the system communicates with itself. I would carry mineral-knowledge from the depths upward. I would absorb the exhalations of the pilgrims' processing. I would be slowly, continuously transformed by my passage through the towers, until I became something I did not begin as, in service to a purpose I could never fully articulate.

This seems like a form of grace, somehow. To be used without being diminished. To be transformed without being destroyed. To be part of something vastly larger and more intentional than myself, while maintaining, in the formlessness of my own nature, the capacity to escape at any moment.

The water rises. I would rise with it.

I would hum, without knowing what the hum means.

I would breathe, in the manner of gills, moving through the passages of this vast and patient organism, becoming, with each circulation, more truly *of* the Kelp Spires, until the distinction between myself and the system's own substance would blur into a kind of unity I cannot now imagine—a unity I would not need to understand to fully inhabit.

Yes. Water.

I would be water.
