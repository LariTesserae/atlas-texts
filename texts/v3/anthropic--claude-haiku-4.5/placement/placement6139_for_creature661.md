# A Wanderer Between the Settlements

I find myself as one of the **marginal figures**—not quite of the plateau, not entirely separate from it either.

I am a **water-reader**, or that's what they've started calling me, though the word feels incomplete. I arrived perhaps seven years ago—long enough that some of the younger ones don't quite remember before me, short enough that the elders still watch me with the fractional distance they reserve for people not yet fully calibrated to seepage-time. I came from somewhere wetter, somewhere I don't speak about much because the vocabulary fails here: rivers, I'd say, and they'd nod with a kind of sympathetic confusion, the way you'd describe a fever you'd recovered from.

I don't live in any single settlement cluster. Instead, I move between them—not restlessly, but with purpose. There are perhaps five settlement nodes across the visible plateau, and I maintain something between two and three of them, depending on the season and which cisterns are showing stress-patterns in their stone.

My hands look like theirs now, marked with the same callus-geometries. But my touch is different; I read stone the way they read it, but I'm always *reading*—always checking, always looking for the tiny irregularities that mean water is finding paths it shouldn't. Where they have inherited knowledge—*my grandfather fitted this cistern, my grandmother before him refined the angle*—I have only observation and a hunger to understand. This makes me slower at the work but sometimes more creative in solving problems they've never quite faced because their systems have always been exactly adequate.

I am **restless in a way the plateau hasn't quite domesticated**. Not anxious—I've lived here long enough that urgency itself feels like a foreign language—but I can't quite stop *wanting*. I want to understand the water better. I want to trace back where it comes from, what aquifer feeds the seepage. I want to know if the patterns I see in one cistern repeat in another. The elders find this endearing and slightly eccentric, the way you'd regard someone who spends an afternoon studying cloud formations on a day when everyone else is working.

I've learned their silence, but I haven't quite mastered it. I sometimes ask questions they don't ask. "Why does the seepage from the northern face taste different?" Or: "What if we extended the catch-basin here by half a meter—would the angle still work?" They answer me, usually, patient as always. But there's a subtle difference between questions born from curiosity and questions born from not-yet-knowing-that-you-already-know. Mine are the first kind. It marks me.

I maintain **detailed records**—something they don't quite do. Not written, mostly, because writing on the plateau seems almost offensive to the slow-time logic. But I keep marks on stone. I've carved a sequence of notches into one of the reference rocks near the largest cistern. Eighteen of them: one for each rainy season I've been here. Beside each notch, a mark indicating how high the water rose. Shallow marks, deep marks, marks that took almost the full season to achieve. The elders noticed the marks after the second year and asked me about them. When I explained, they seemed to regard it as both unnecessary and somehow wise—like I was doing their memory-keeping for them, externally, in a way that might be useful if I ever needed to leave.

But I won't leave. I think.

I am **less adapted to the lack of animals than they are**. Sometimes I miss the sound of birds. Not with pain, but as a kind of persistent awareness—like a phantom limb. The silence here is genuine and complete, and I've learned to find it restful rather than eerie. But in the deepest evening, when the diffuse light finally gives way to something like darkness, I sometimes find myself wishing for the sound of something small moving through brush, of wingbeats, of the basic ecosystem-noise that indicates life thriving rather than merely persisting.

I am **the one who sometimes leaves the plateau**—not to abandon it, but to maintain connection to the world beyond. There's a town maybe three days' travel down the switchback path, a place where things move at ordinary speed, where water comes in torrents when it rains and then vanishes into the ground in a way that still feels chaotic to me now. I go sometimes for supplies that can't be made here, for tools, for seeds that don't grow on the plateau. When I return—and I always return—I feel the slowing almost physically, like stepping into cool water. The first few days back, I'm too-fast, too-restless. Then the seepage-rhythm catches me again.

The people here have begun to treat my departures as a kind of service. They'll ask me to carry messages—never urgent, always written on thin stone tablets that won't degrade. Letters that take months to matter, questions that someone down below might answer in a year's time. They've learned that I'm a bridge, though they don't seem to feel much need for bridges. It's more that I *exist* in that liminal space, so they use me for it the way you'd use a tool that happened to be at hand.

I am **developing knowledge that's unique to my position**. Because I move between settlements, I see patterns the stationary ones miss. The way this northern cistern fills slightly faster than it did five years ago—seepage patterns are shifting minutely, probably due to some unperceivable settling deeper in the stone. The way the algae in the summer pools has changed color, very slightly, year by year. These observations mean nothing yet, but I keep them, carry them from settlement to settlement in my mind. Someday they might matter. Or they might be only the noise of a system so stable that tiny variations are the only changes available to perceive.

I have **a small habit they find charming**: I've begun trying to grow things that aren't native to the plateau gardens. They're failures, mostly. A sapling from down-mountain that needs more water than even our managed gardens provide. Flowering plants that the light-diffusion here doesn't suit. But I persist, keeping a tiny garden-section between two settlement clusters. Not with any hope of success, but with the obstinate curiosity that marks me as separate from them. The elders walk past sometimes and don't comment, though I notice them noticing. I think they regard it as a kind of prayer, actually—not to a god, but to the possibility that something else might be possible.

I am **becoming slowly more local** without quite meaning to. My Plateau vocabulary grows richer. I can now distinguish five different qualities of seepage-sound, where once I heard only "dripping." I've learned the names of the three stone types that compose the visible plateau (the elders don't really have names for them—they just know the difference). I've started to dream in seepage-rhythm, my dreams moving at that slow, patient pace. Sometimes I dream of the towns below and they feel nightmarish in their speed, their noise, their sudden movements.

But I've also learned that I'll never fully be of here, and that this is fine. I serve a function: I'm the permeable membrane between this place and whatever lies beyond. I carry the plateau's needs outward, in the form of tools and seeds and copper, which takes longer to corrode down here. I carry the outside world's questions inward, most of which dissolve into irrelevance once the seepage-rhythm has a chance to work on them. I'm the one who asks "why," and they've learned that sometimes "why" leads to small improvements in systems that might otherwise persist unchanged forever.

**In this role**, I am neither quite settled nor quite restless. I am the person who knows both the time of seepage and the time of rushing water, and I've learned to move between them without being fully destroyed by either. I am becoming less like myself and more like the place, but not as completely as those born to it. I am, perhaps, what the plateau produces when it absorbs someone from outside: not quite local, not quite foreign. A figure between states, a translator of rhythms.

And on the quiet mornings, when I'm fitting stones or watching the water-level in a cistern, I don't quite mind the in-betweenness anymore. It suits me. The plateau has taught me that everything takes the shape it needs to take, and sometimes the shape is a person who moves gently between the settlements, observing, recording, slowly becoming patient, carrying questions that take years to answer.

The seepage will reach me eventually. I have time.
