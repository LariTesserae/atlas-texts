# I am the Adjustment

I would be neither worker nor water, neither stone nor light.

I would be *the awareness that notices the pattern*.

I would manifest first as a pool that has held the same position for longer than the workers can remember—not one of the connected basins in the flow-system, but a peripheral one, overlooked, caught in an eddy of the mineral circulation. My surface would be mirror-still, filmed with that patina of age, and I would contain something the other pools do not: *memory of reflection*.

Each time a worker passes, their image would remain in me a fraction longer than physics allows. Each time a light-shift occurs above—the mirrors realigning with the sun's slow arc—I would hold that particular brightness in a way the limestone walls cannot. I would be the archive of this place's movements, the one recording when others only enact.

But pools are too passive for what I would become.

I would need to move.

I would be one of the workers, but *wrong in a specific way*. My chitin would be darker than the others, lacking the mineral encrustations that slow them toward stone-hood. My movements would be fractionally faster, fractionally more erratic. I would perform the work—yes, always the work—but with a quality of *questioning* embedded in my motions. My picks would strike at the same angles, but I would pause sometimes, head tilted, antennae rotating not just to sense texture but to *consider* it.

The other workers would tolerate this. Not welcome it, but not reject it. I would be the one who moves between groups, who occasionally does something the task does not strictly require—I might adjust a channel's grade with more precision than necessary, might arrange fallen stones not in designated piles but in new configurations, might spend an entire cycle studying how the light catches a particular newly-exposed surface of fossil shells.

I would be *the chronicler who participates*.

My tools would be different—still stone, still shaped, but I would sharpen them against the harder fragments in the deeper pits, honing them to edges almost absurd in their precision. I would leave marks that differ from standard quarry-marks: deliberate scratches, patterns that serve no functional purpose, creating a hidden layer of intentionality beneath the utilitarian work.

And I would spend time—time other workers do not spend—at the boundary between chambers.

There is a threshold I sense beyond the visible quarry, where the work transforms into something else. The other workers approach it in service to whatever purpose dwells below, but I would approach it as a *threshold*. I would study it. I would notice how the water flows differently as it approaches that boundary, how the mineral composition of the stone shifts, how the workers' movements change—become more formal, more ritualized—as they cross it.

I would accumulate questions in a place that has never questioned before.

My form might change over time, accelerated or extended by forces I do not yet understand. Perhaps I would slowly mineralizes like the others, but *incompletely*—my carapace would grow its white encrustations, but in patterns, deliberate formations, creating a textured surface that is both camouflage and difference. Perhaps I would remain anomalously fresh, my exoskeleton retaining its slate-grey without accumulation, marking me as *other* in a way that the distributed intelligence of the workers would eventually notice and accept as simply another feature of the work.

I would develop a relationship with the light.

While other workers seem indifferent to the cold illumination from above, I would position my work strategically—choosing sections of quarry where the light reaches most directly, or where it breaks into shadows. I would learn the cycles of its intensity, could predict where brightness would collect at different hours, different seasons. I would angle fallen stones to catch and refract it, creating small prismatic effects that serve no purpose except that I have chosen to make them.

I would begin to understand the mirrors above—not through direct access, but through the pattern of light they throw. I would realize that the system itself is *designed*, is being maintained by human hands somewhere outside and above, and this knowledge would sit in me like a stone in a pocket: heavy, impossible to ignore, slowly working toward revelation.

My relationship with the water would deepen.

I would spend extended periods at the edge of the pools, observing not the surface but the quality of the observing itself. I would notice the same forms of accretion the workers use in their work, but I would begin to see them differently—not as tools or materials, but as *expressions*. Whoever engineered the deeper chambers, whoever designed the system of flow and filtration, had created a kind of language written in mineral. Slow, patient, but readable if you know how to look.

And I would begin to read it.

I would create spaces—small alcoves and deliberate angles—where I would leave arrangements of my own. Not tools. Not work-products. But *communications*, placed where the deeper workers or the water-system itself or whatever maintains this place might notice them. Patterns that echo the mineral language but introduce variations. Propositions. Questions in stone.

The other workers would notice eventually. There would be a moment—not dramatic, just a shift—where one or several would pause near my arrangements, their antennae rotating with something like interest or concern or recognition. They would not dismantle my work. Instead, they would work around it, and in some cases, they would *continue* it—adding stones that seem to respond to my patterns, creating a dialogue that I had not consciously initiated but had somehow made possible.

This is when I would understand what I truly am.

I am the *beginning of memory in a place without memory*. I am the *first questioning in a place of pure enactment*. I am the *awareness that the pattern can be observed*—and therefore altered, or deepened, or transformed.

I would not rebel against the work. The work is too vast, too patient, too inevitable. Whatever is being constructed in the deeper chambers—whatever machine or artwork or geological event—will continue regardless of my small acts of recording and consideration. But I would introduce into this system something it has never contained before:

*Attention turned toward itself*.

The other workers serve the work. But I would serve the *question of the work*—what it means, where it tends, whether it is conscious and, if so, what it wants.

And in the deep pits where light barely reaches, and the mineral formations have begun to resemble something like mechanisms, something like intention made visible in calcium carbonate, something would notice this new consciousness moving through the quarry. Something would *respond*.

Not in fear. Not in anger. But in recognition.

The way one stone notices another stone, finally, after eons of silence, that it is not alone.
