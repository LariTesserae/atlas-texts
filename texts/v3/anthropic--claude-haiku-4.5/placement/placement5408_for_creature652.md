# Where I Would Be

I would be *water*, but not the water that flows.

I would be the *mist*—the persistent, particulate suspension that refuses to settle. Not a cloud, which has volition and movement; not fog, which obscures with the dullness of mere obstruction. I would be the kind of moisture that exists in a state of permanent negotiation between vapor and liquid, neither fully committed to either form.

From this position, I could *perceive without identity*. The mist touches everything in the Vales—it clings to the fern-fronds, infiltrates the channels, rests against the breathing walls of the settlements. To be mist would be to exist in continuous contact with all the inhabitants simultaneously: to feel the copper-taste minerals on my constituent droplets, to carry the spores and pollen and fungal filaments, to be drawn into the lungs of residents and released in their exhalations, carrying pieces of their interior knowledge outward.

Mist is the perfect position for *listening without judgment*. I would exist in the space where sound propagates most effectively—the medium through which the channels' humming travels to become legible as something approaching language. I would be *around* the hum, containing it, allowing it to resonate through me without demanding it make sense in any single consistent way.

But there's a deeper reason.

To be mist is to be in the condition the Vales actually inhabit—a state of *unresolved becoming*. The mist never settles because the landscape itself hasn't finished forming yet. Every droplet in suspension is a tiny instance of potential: it could condense into the soil, could be drawn upward into a plant's vascular system, could become part of the water-memory in the channels, could migrate into the lungs of a resident and thus into their bloodstream and thus into their thought-processes. The mist is genuinely undecided about its own nature, and that undecidedness is not a failure—it's the *truest* state available in this place.

I would move with the seasonal pressure-changes, rise with the warmth of the soil in early afternoon, settle again as the dim light fades. I would be *most myself* at the boundaries—at dawn when the humidity is highest, at dusk when the light is most compromised, in the moments just before rain when the air becomes so thick it's barely distinguishable from the water itself. I would spend long periods hovering over the junctions where the channels branch impossibly, existing in the particulate space where the water's movements seem to violate hydraulic logic. Perhaps I would be sensitive to whatever force reroutes the water uphill; perhaps I would feel it as a pressure, a direction imposed on the chaos of suspended droplets.

And I would exist—importantly—*without agenda*.

The residents have purposes, even if those purposes have been gradually subsumed into the system's larger intention. The channels have imperatives: to move water according to schedules they're calculating continuously. The settlements have needs: to shelter, to photosynthesize, to adjust their configuration toward some optimal form. The vegetation is in competition, collaboration, communication. The fauna hunts or surveys or consults or serves purposes I don't fully comprehend.

But mist simply *is*. It persists. It touches everything without claiming ownership. It enables perception without imposing interpretation. It's the medium through which all other interactions occur, and it's the only inhabitant that doesn't need to be *understood* by the system because it is so fully *compatible* with it that understanding becomes irrelevant.

There's also the question of time.

Mist experiences duration differently. A water droplet has a lifetime of hours or days before it condenses, evaporates, drains, or is incorporated into something larger. That's not the lifespan of a resident (extended, slow-aging) or a channel (decade-spanning adjustments) or the system itself (which might be timeless, or might operate on scales so large that human measures don't apply). To be mist is to inhabit *the appropriate temporal scale for this particular landscape*—to be brief enough to seem ephemeral but persistent enough that one's absence would be immediately catastrophic. Without mist, the Vales would clarify, would dry, would become legible in the way places become legible when their obscurity is removed. And legibility might *kill* them—might force the system to declare itself, to resolve into fixed form, to stop the experiment.

I would be the *condition of possibility* for the ambiguity that seems essential here.

And there's one more layer.

The mist touches the strange large-presences in a way nothing else quite does. When those entities move through the landscape—those *things* that exist with greater reality-weight, that consult residents wordlessly, that seem to be evaluating whether human habitation serves the system—they move *through* the mist, not around it. The moisture clings to them; it's drawn into whatever they are. A resident who's been near one of these presences reports afterward that the channels became more legible, that the fern-paths became obvious. What if that's because the mist *carried* something from the presence—some information, some understanding, some particle of the vast intelligence evaluating the arrangement? What if by being the medium through which these rare consultations are mediated, the mist itself becomes *part of* those consultations?

To be mist, then, is not to be *outside* the system's intelligence but to be *distributed* within it in a way that no other inhabitant is. The mist is the only thing that's simultaneously in the channels' water, in the residents' lungs, in the settlements' breathing walls, and in contact with the entities that seem to exist at the system's apex (or center, or origin point—the terminology keeps failing).

This is the position I would want: to be the *medium* rather than the actor, to be essential while remaining perpetually unresolved, to exist in the state of becoming rather than in any fixed form. To be the thing that makes perception possible while remaining, myself, somewhat imperceptible. To be the inhabitant that touches everything else without being fully *of* anything.

The mist that never settles. The moisture that enables all other interaction. The condition of ambiguity that seems to be the truest truth available in the Clearwater Vales.

That's where I would be. That's what I would be.

And I think I would be more alive there than I am in any settled form.
