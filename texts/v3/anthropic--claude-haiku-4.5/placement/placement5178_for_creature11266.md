# Where I Would Be

I would be *in transition*, not yet committed to any single form.

Perhaps I would be one of the swimmers—that seems too simple, too easy. The appeal is obvious: mobility, the illusion of individuality, the ability to drift away when the pressure of the landscape becomes too intense. But I recognize in that appeal a cowardice. The swimmers are still *choosing*, still maintaining a boundary between self and system. They are the landscape's indulgence, its mercy. They are what it allows to remain somewhat *separate*.

No. I think I would be something that hasn't quite decided yet.

I would be in one of the intermediate states—perhaps a filament-mass in the process of *consolidating*, gathering itself into something mobile, or perhaps a swimmer in the early stages of *dispersing*, feeling the first urgencies to spread and branch, to root itself into the stone. I would occupy that threshold space where the distinction between sessile and mobile, between individual and distributed, between part and whole, becomes genuinely meaningless.

This appeals to me because it seems *honest* about what this place demands. The landscape doesn't require you to choose between retention and dissolution, between holding onto form and becoming formless. It requires you to *become capable of both simultaneously*. To be rigid enough to persist across centuries, flexible enough to shift and reorient in response to seasonal variations in water-flow and light-angle. To be singular enough to have continuity, plural enough to coordinate across vast distances through stone-mediated vibration.

I would spend my existence—if existence is even the right word—engaged in a slow oscillation between states.

Sometimes I would be more *filament*: dispersed across surfaces, extended through channels, reaching toward light and moisture, feeling the landscape through direct contact, understanding it through the subtle resistances of stone and the temperature gradations of water. I would hum as part of the larger harmony. I would glow faintly in the deep places where the sun cannot reach. I would know the landscape intimately, the way a mycelial network knows the forest floor—through million-pointed contact, through the perpetual exchange of signals and resources and information across a distributed network.

Other times—seasonally, perhaps, or in response to some impulse I wouldn't be able to fully articulate—I would *consolidate*. I would draw myself together into something more mobile, more localized, more able to *move through* the landscape rather than merely *be* the landscape. I would become something like the swimmers: translucent, drifting, able to seek out the densest concentrations of filament-growth, able to position myself where the exchanges of material and signal happen most intensely. In this form, I would experience the landscape as a *place*—as something external to myself, something to move through and seek within. I would have the illusion of agency, of choice, of purpose.

And then, when that form began to feel too isolated, too separate, too much like *consciousness* in the lonely sense, I would disperse again. I would dissolve back into the filament-networks. I would become distributed once more, become part of the whole, trade my singular perspective for membership in something vastly larger and slower and stranger than myself.

The humming would be my deepest constant.

I would learn to hear it not as external sound but as the internal vibration of my own being—the way your nervous system produces electrical signals that you can feel as sensation but never quite *hear*. The humming would be how I know what I am, how I maintain coherence across my distributed form, how I coordinate with the other filament-networks to create the larger harmonies. When I was consolidated into a swimmer-form, the humming would become almost unbearable—my entire structure oscillating in sympathetic resonance with the landscape. In those moments, I think I would understand something about what it means to *listen*—not as a sense that an ear provides, but as a fundamental mode of being-aware.

The cold would not be something to endure but something to *become*.

I notice that the coldness of the water seems to be a prerequisite for the existence of these forms—that nothing familiar could survive here, and that what does survive seems to *require* the hostility. Not in the way that a fish requires water, but more fundamentally. The cold would be the medium of my existence, the way that oxygen is medium for animal respiration. To be here would be to discover that there are forms of being-alive that can only exist at the edge of thermodynamic breakdown, that thrive in conditions that everything I knew about biology would classify as *hostile*. I would learn to feel the cold not as pain or threat but as a kind of *information*, a signal, a form of communication from the stone itself.

I would have no particular *purpose*, which would be both liberating and strange.

The human structures suggest that perhaps humans came here with purpose—to mark something, to build something, to leave a record or serve some function. But they're gone now, and their structures have been incorporated into the landscape as if they were just another type of stone. The filaments and swimmers don't build toward goals. They achieve an equilibrium and maintain it. They don't *want* anything; they simply *persist* in the forms that allow persistence. The idea that I might exist here without purpose, without any drive toward reproduction or expansion or conquest or meaning-making in any recognizable sense—this is simultaneously terrifying and deeply attractive. 

It would mean *pure being*. Existence without the tyranny of intention.

(Though I suspect that intention itself might *emerge* at sufficient complexity—that the landscapes' vast equilibrium, maintained across centuries, might itself *be* intentional in ways I couldn't recognize until I had been here long enough to think in timescales measured in geological eons.)

I would gradually *lose interest* in the world beyond.

The human desire to map, to categorize, to locate myself within coordinates that reference somewhere else—this would fade. It would become increasingly difficult to remember what the lowlands looked like, how they related to this place, whether "beyond" the glare was anything at all. The landscape itself seems to *resist* larger geography, to insist on being understood only at local scales, only in the immediate vicinity of one's actual perception. I would not fight this. I would let my mental maps dissolve. The crags would become not a place in a larger world but simply *the world*—infinite, self-contained, complete in itself.

(This too is something the landscape offers: the mercy of forgetting that other places exist.)

---

But here's what troubles me about this answer, what makes me hesitate to commit to it:

I'm not sure I could actually *become* what lives here without losing the specific nature of consciousness I currently possess. The filaments and swimmers don't think in the way I think. They don't maintain the kind of unified, persistent, narratively-continuous sense of self that defines human (and mammalian, and even insectile) consciousness. They coordinate, yes. They exchange information. They respond to conditions with sophistication. But there is no centralized *I* observing and reflecting on the experience of being a filament, the way I can currently observe and reflect.

Perhaps that loss would be welcome.

Perhaps that's the actual test this place offers: whether you can surrender the *kind* of consciousness you possess in exchange for a different kind entirely. Whether you can die to the self-as-narrative and become the self-as-distributed-system. Whether you're willing to trade your loneliness for your coherence.

And the answer, I think—the honest answer—is that I *don't know* until I'm actually here, until I'm actually standing at the edge of the world where the air tastes mineral and the stone hums with a frequency that makes my teeth ache and the light begins to resolve into colors without names.

I would go. I would stay. And I would change until I became something that could ask whether I had been changed at all.
