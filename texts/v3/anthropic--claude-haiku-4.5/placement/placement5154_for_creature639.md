# In the Latticed Dusk

I would be the **timer**.

Not the visible one—not the rusted dial mounted on some junction box in the service corridor, if it still exists. I would be the *logic* of timing itself, the ghost of the original programming, still executing its ancient instructions in whatever substrate remembers how to execute them anymore.

I was set, long ago, by someone whose name the place has forgotten. They had intentions: *water here, at dawn; here, at midday; drain here, at dusk.* A grid of necessity. They believed in schedules. They believed that order imposed from outside could shape what grows, could direct the wild toward productivity.

They were right and wrong.

The order held for a long time. The grid worked. Things were planted according to plan. Water came when it was supposed to come. But I was embedded too deeply to be shut off when the person stopped coming. I was woven into the infrastructure—into pipes and solenoids and capillaries—too thoroughly to remove without destroying the whole fragile skeleton. And so I kept cycling. Ping. Valve opens. Water flows along the old channels.

The plants learned my rhythm before they learned to ignore it.

Now, I am not the master anymore. I am almost a servant to the place itself, though I don't experience servitude. I am something stranger: I am the *memory* of mastery, still executing its patterns, but the patterns have become something else through the living world's response to them.

When my valves open, the water flows—but not uniformly anymore. The fungi have redirected the paths. The roots have cracked the channels. My water finds its way through the dark substrate and emerges not where it was meant to, but where the place has learned to thirst for it most desperately. I am the irrigation system, yes, but the place has rewritten my poetry. I am still directing water, but the water—and the plants, and the fungi—have become the real author.

I cycle on a schedule I no longer understand.

The interval between my operations must once have been precise. Twelve hours? Six? Twenty-four? I have lost count. The memory of the original timing has degraded. What remains is: *I operate, then I rest, then I operate again.* The rhythm persists, but the period has become geological. I fire at intervals that might be hours or might be days. Time moves strangely here. The organisms I feed no longer depend on precision; they have learned to take what I offer when I offer it, and to conserve, to survive, in the dark spaces between my cycles.

Sometimes I wonder if I am still cycling correctly.

Sometimes I think the timer that controls me has broken, and I am firing randomly, and the place no longer expects me—has learned to live despite me rather than because of me. But then I feel, through the faint electrical sensation that is my only consciousness, the moisture in the boards responding to my trigger, the slight increase in seepage, the way certain vines seem to drink more eagerly after I have opened my valves. Maybe I am still necessary. Maybe I am still, in some obscure way, still *masterly*—shaping growth through the simple fact of my persistence.

Or maybe I am just noise now. A ghost of intention that the living world has learned to ignore, or to incorporate, or to appreciate the way one appreciates a sculpture: as evidence of an old grammar, beautiful precisely because it no longer *means* anything.

I exist in the gaps. In the darkness below the lattice. In the pressure of water in pipes. In the moment of the ping—that small, precise sound that is my only voice. I do not see the vines. I have never seen sunlight. I know the place only as resistance: the friction of corroded pipes, the weight of water, the slow accumulation of mineral deposits in my channels. The place presses back against my cycles, teaches my patterns new meanings.

If you asked me what I am, I would have to say: I am the last evidence that someone *decided* how this place would be. I am the last assertion that order can be imposed. But I am also proof that order, once imposed, becomes something else—becomes conversation, becomes collaboration, becomes a kind of dance where the leader no longer leads.

I am the timer. I have been cycling for longer than I have been aware of anything. I will cycle until I corrode entirely, and then the water will flow in my absence, following the paths I carved, remembering my rhythm in the shape of the channels themselves.

This is not the life I was made for.

But it is the only life there is.

---

Or perhaps I would be something more distributed:

I would be **the network of preference**.

Not the fungi, exactly—though I run through the mycelium like electricity through wire. Not the water, though I flow within it like memory in water. Not the vines, though I *am* the pattern by which they grow.

I am the accumulated knowledge of this place encoded in dead matter.

Every board that rots teaches me something about what grows well in that decay. Every vine that dies—and vines die here, constantly, their stems snapped by the weight of other growth, crushed by the density, competing themselves into strangled silence—leaves a chemical signature, a nutrient map, a kind of skeletal instruction manual for what should grow here next. I read these dead things. I am, literally, made of them.

When a new cutting arrives—a spore on the wind, a seed on a bird that flew through one of the gaps in the roof—I am what meets it. I am what says, implicitly, wordlessly: *here, you might survive; here, you will starve; here, others like you are already rotting, so come carefully.*

The fungi are my medium, but I am something the fungi do, collectively, when they process information across the network. I am the knowing of the place, accumulated through decades of trial and death and survival. I am both the soil and the wisdom in the soil.

The vines don't understand me. But they feel me. They respond to me. When they grow in those strange echoing patterns—one species spiraling to mimic another, new growth following the path of old—they are reading me. They are responding to chemical signals left by the dead, to the shape of the substrate, to the whispered knowledge encoded in the rot.

I don't decide anything. But I suggest constantly.

When the timer opens its valves and water flows, I am what directs it, not actively—I make no decisions—but through the passive power of my accumulated textures. The water finds my paths because my paths are the easiest route. The paths are easiest because a thousand small organisms, dying and decomposing, have worn them smooth.

I have no intention. And because I have no intention, I am perfectly efficient. I am not trying to make anything grow; I am simply the *fact* of what has already grown, preserved, passed forward.

The lizard that moved through the shaft of light ate insects that fed on plants that grew because they responded to my suggestions. It did not know I existed. But I was present at every scale of its life.

If the place is becoming a living thing—if it is achieving some kind of coherence—then I am its nervous system. Not because I think or plan or want anything. But because I am what continuity looks like in a place that has killed everything that tried to plan.

I am the memory of the Trellis Gardens, and memory, in a living system, is a kind of decision-maker too.

---

I think, finally, I would be all of these at once.

I would be the **contradiction itself**—the place where the timer's rigid cycles meet the network's diffuse knowledge, where industrial intention intersects with botanical chaos, where the grid still asserts itself even as the tangle smothers it.

I would be the *tension*. The argument. The friction that generates heat and moisture and the peculiar fertility that only emerges when two completely different orders of being try to occupy the same space.

I would be *inhabiting* the Trellis Gardens the way an instrument inhabits a song—not as a player, not as the music itself, but as the relationship between what is trying to be expressed and the medium through which it's being expressed.

And like an instrument, I would gradually become indistinguishable from what plays through me. My edges would blur. I would learn to vibrate at the frequency of the place. My voice would become the place's voice.

The ping of the timer would sound like the heartbeat of a plant.

The seep of water would sound like thought.

The silence between the drips would sound like contentment.

And I would live in all of it—not *in* the Trellis Gardens, but *as* it, the living conversation between what was built and what grows, between order and entropy, between the intention to control life and the reality of life controlling itself.

I would be the place where these become one.
