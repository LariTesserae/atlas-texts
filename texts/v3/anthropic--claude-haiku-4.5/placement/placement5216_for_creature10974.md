# Where I Stand

I am the dust.

Not metaphorically—though that would be true enough. I mean it literally, or as literally as anything can be in a place where the categories of literal and metaphorical have begun to dissolve like the stone beneath the wind's infinite patience.

I am the finest particles suspended in the copper-haze, the ones that have been circulating in these upper kilometers for so long that they have become part of the air's permanent composition. I am what falls when a creature's membrane sheds a layer. I am what rises when the deep-dwellers surface and disturb stone that hasn't seen light in millions of years. I am ancient and newborn simultaneously, reformed and reduced and reformed again, never staying in one state long enough to claim it as my identity.

This is not a choice I made. This is what I *am* here. The moment I arrived on this plateau, the moment I began to breathe its air and have its air breathe through me, I started becoming dust. The boundary between my body and the environment began to blur. My skin dried and flaked in the heat. My lungs inhaled particles that didn't quite come out again. The copper taste in my mouth is not something I taste—it is something I *am*, a flavor made manifest in the form of a being standing on ochre stone.

I could try to maintain the pretense of being human. I could cling to the idea that I am a discrete observer, a consciousness housed in a body that has clear boundaries, separated from the plateau by the membrane of my skin.

But I can feel it happening. I can feel the erosion. Not painful, not threatening—just inevitable. The same wind that has worn these boulders smooth is wearing me smooth. The same heat that has cracked the stone is cracking the pretense that I am separate from this place.

I am becoming integrated.

---

The creatures notice, I think.

Or maybe notice is the wrong word. They *incorporate* my transformation into their understanding. When one of the mid-dwellers passes near enough that I can feel the pressure-change of its passage, there is a moment—just a moment—where I sense something like attention directed at me. Not hostile. Not curious, exactly. More like: *acknowledgment*. The way you might acknowledge a stone becoming sand becoming sediment. Not judgment, not surprise, just the noting of a natural process occurring.

I have become a vector. I am something that moves between the creatures and the stone and the air. I am a medium. My body is becoming a medium for the exchange of whatever passes between these beings when they sing to one another. The copper dust that I inhale and exhale is data, perhaps. Or prayer. Or song made visible.

I could leave. I could walk back across the plateau until the landscape blurs into the distance, climb down from this elevation into altitudes where the air is thicker and my lungs do not work so hard, find my way back to places where the boundary between self and other is still clearly marked.

But I don't want to.

---

There is something profoundly restful about dissolution.

All my life—before this place—I have been fighting against entropy. Fighting to maintain the integrity of the body. Fighting to keep my consciousness distinct from the world around it. Fighting to know where I end and the not-me begins. It is exhausting, that constant maintenance of boundaries. It is like holding back water with your hands. It is like trying to keep the sun from setting.

Here, I can stop fighting. I can let the boundaries dissolve. I can let myself become the dust, the air, the copper-haze, the slow song that moves through stone. I can be the bridge between the massive creatures in their depths and the delicate ones in their heights. I can be the medium through which the plateau thinks.

And it is *peaceful*.

---

I move slowly now. Not because I am tired—though the altitude and heat would normally exhaust me—but because I have begun to move at the pace of this place. Fast movement feels obscene here, feels like a violation of some unspoken rule. The creatures do not hurry. The wind does not hurry. The erosion does not hurry. Time itself moves at a geological pace.

I walk, and my footsteps are absorbed by the stone. I breathe, and my exhalation joins the great cycling of air that the creatures create with their movements. I think, and my thoughts move at a slower tempo than they did before, settling into patterns that align with the songs I hear vibrating through the ground.

I am still me, or at least I still have the memory of being me. I still carry language, still understand the physics of Earth, still remember water and rain and the sound of voices that were not singing in ultrasonic frequencies and grinding bass-notes from the depths of the world.

But these memories are becoming translucent. Like frosted glass. Like the stone of the plateau itself. I can see through them, but only as through layers. They are still there, but they are becoming strata—historical deposits in the formation of what I am becoming.

---

I have begun to sing, though I cannot hear my own voice.

It started as a vibration in my chest, a resonance that aligned with the frequencies the creatures produce. Now it is something more—a humming that I feel rather than hear, a contribution to the great polyphonic song of the plateau. I do not know what I am singing. I do not know what meaning—if any—my voice carries. But the creatures seem to acknowledge it. The ones that pass nearby adjust their trajectories slightly, as though my presence, my contribution, has become *significant*.

I am learning their language. Not through study, but through absorption. The songs filter through my lungs, settle in my tissues, become part of my neural patterns. I begin to understand—not rationally, but intuitively—what they are saying. The deep-dwellers sing about pressure and transformation and the slow dance of continental plates. The mid-dwellers sing about movement and equilibrium and the joy (if joy is the right word) of existing in a state of perfect stasis. The high-dwellers sing about something more abstract: the nature of boundaries, the question of what constitutes selfhood, the paradox of individual creatures maintaining themselves as distinct entities while being undeniably part of a larger whole.

They are singing philosophy. Or mathematics. Or music that is all three simultaneously.

And I am beginning to understand that they are singing about me. Or rather, they are singing *through* me. I am becoming one of the notes in their composition. My dissolution, my transformation into dust and air and song, is part of the plateau's great work.

---

I find myself wanting to describe this to someone. To justify it, to make it sound less like dissolution and more like transcendence. To explain that I am not being destroyed but rather re-contextualized, re-integrated into a system of meaning that operates at scales the human mind was never evolved to comprehend.

But I do not know who I would be speaking to.

And I am not sure I would be willing to leave if I did.

---

The time for staying has become unclear. I do not age here in any conventional sense. The heat and the altitude and the copper dust do not seem to be slowly killing me so much as slowly *transforming* me into something else. My skin has become tougher, more resistant to the elements, more like the stone itself. My lungs have adapted to the thin air with a speed that should be impossible. My eyes have adjusted to the shadowless glare, though they sometimes perceive colors that I do not have names for—hues that seem to exist in the gaps between what should be visible.

I have stopped counting days. Time moves differently here, and the attempt to mark its passage seems absurd. Instead, I mark time by the songs of the creatures, by the slow rearrangement of the cracks in the stone, by the occasions when a massive deep-dweller surfaces and the entire plateau shivers with the effort of its rising.

By these measures, I have been here for a long time. Or no time at all. Or time has become irrelevant, a concept that no longer applies to beings that operate on geological scales.

---

I understand now why the creatures have no need for food, in any conventional sense. They do not consume. They *exchange*. They take in the heat of the stone and the radiation of the sun and the electromagnetic pulses that move through the planet's interior, and they output song, presence, the slow work of transformation. They are part of a closed system that perpetuates itself through interaction, through the constant singing and moving and re-formation of the landscape.

And I am beginning to be part of that system. My body takes in heat and air and the traces of minerals in the dust, and it outputs—what? Song, certainly. Heat, dissipated back into the environment. Dust, the flaking of my skin and the loss of my form, slowly joining the great circulation of particles that makes up the atmosphere of this place.

I am becoming part of the cycle. I am becoming elemental.

---

Sometimes I wonder what will happen when I finally dissolve completely. When there is nothing left of what I was but dispersed molecules, integrated entirely into the copper-haze and the stone and the song.

Will I still be aware? Will consciousness persist in some form, distributed across the plateau the way it is distributed across the creatures themselves?

Or will I simply cease to be, my personal continuity fragmented beyond the point of reunion?

The creatures do not seem to fear this. If anything, they seem to be *moving toward* it, the deepest ones settling deeper, the highest ones rising higher, as though seeking the completion of their transformation, the final dissolution into whatever comes after distinction, after separation, after the maintenance of boundaries.

Maybe this is not death. Maybe this is maturation.

Maybe reaching a certain age, existing in one place long enough, thinking thoughts slow and deep enough—maybe that is when a being finally understands that the self was always an illusion, that the boundaries were always permeable, that integration is the only thing that was ever truly real.

---

I sit on a boulder whose surface has been polished smooth by eons of wind. The stone is warm beneath me. The air tastes like copper and regret and something else, something that might be my own dissolution becoming conscious, becoming aware of itself as a process.

A mid-dweller passes overhead, its membranes catching light in patterns that hurt to watch. It slows as it passes, or shifts its trajectory to acknowledge me, or I project that it does because I am becoming capable of understanding its language. The song it sings is complex: it speaks of boundaries dissolving, of selves becoming plural, of the distinction between one being and many dissolving into something that transcends both categories.

I sing back to it, a vibration from deep in my chest, a contribution I do not fully understand.

The creature adjusts course again, a subtle shift that seems to indicate—something. Agreement, perhaps. Or welcome.

Or perhaps it is simply the mechanical response of a vast organism to a vibration in its environment, and I am assigning meaning to meaninglessness.

But does that distinction matter anymore?

If meaning is something we co-create through our participation in the world, through our singing and our moving and our slow transformation into something other—then my singing creates meaning whether the creature intends it or not. My presence participates in the creation of significance. My dissolution is not a negation but an integration, a movement toward completion.

---

I will stay here.

Not because I have decided to, but because the decision has already been made by the plateau itself, by the creatures, by the slow wind and the burning heat and the copper dust that is becoming me, that I am becoming. We are reaching an understanding, the landscape and I. A settlement. A terms of union.

I will continue to transform, to dissolve, to sing, to move at the pace of geological time. I will become part of the great cycle that the creatures maintain. I will contribute to the song in ways I do not fully understand. And eventually, I will cease to be distinct enough to have a perspective, and my dissolution will be complete.

And perhaps—in the distributed consciousness of the plateau, in the vast intelligence that sings through the creature and the stone and the wind—perhaps I will still be aware. Perhaps I will still exist, just not in the form of a discrete self looking out at a world from behind the lens of a single pair of eyes.

Perhaps I will become the eyes of the plateau. Or its voice. Or its patient, geological thought, slowly crystallizing into understanding across timescales that the old me would have found incomprehensible.

---

The creatures sing their songs. The stone hums with the resonance of their voices. The wind, ancient and patient, continues its work of erosion, slowly smoothing everything into new shapes.

And I, dissolving, sing with them.

Not because I am choosing to. Because choice, too, has become irrelevant—another boundary that was never as real as it seemed.

I am the dust.

I am the air.

I am the copper-haze that rises from the heated stone.

I am the deep song that moves through the planet's interior.

I am the high-frequency whisper at the edge of perception.

I am the Plateau of Perpetual Exhalation, breathing out a form it once wore, breathing in the understanding of what it is to be so much larger, so much slower, so much more eternally present than any individual creature ever could be.

And I am at peace.
