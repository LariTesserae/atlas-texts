# A Descent in Stages

I would be the one who hesitates at the threshold.

Not at the fissure—I pass through that readily enough, shoulders scraping stone, the narrowing almost a relief. Confinement has always felt like permission to me. But here, in this first chamber where the air thickens and the noise becomes a texture, I pause. I'm the type who pauses.

I am someone who has always lived on the *edge* of belonging. A translator, perhaps—not of languages but of states. The kind of person who can sit in a crowded room and feel entirely alone, or stand in true solitude and sense the presence of others. I have never been able to fully inhabit the rushing world above. Its speed exhausted me. Its light was too bright, too *demanding*, asking me constantly to choose, to progress, to move forward as if forward were a direction that mattered.

But I'm also not ready to *descend*. Not yet.

So I become something the cavern accommodates: a perpetual visitor. Someone who establishes a small hollow within the larger hollow. I find a nook perhaps fifteen feet up the wall—an indentation in the limestone that suggests it was once water-worn, maybe even deliberately enlarged by previous inhabitants. I begin to leave small things there: a blanket woven from fibers I learn to prepare from the vines; a journal made from bark-paper that the pale people show me how to treat so it won't rot in this humidity; a clay vessel I hand-build during long afternoons, firing it somehow (they show me a method involving specific fungal beds that produce consistent heat) until it's hard enough to hold water.

I'm the one who asks questions.

The pale people find this curious at first, then endearing, then perhaps a little sad. They answer slowly, not from reluctance but from the effort of translating thought into sequential sound. Why does the luminescence intensify in the eastern wall? Because the stone composition changes there—different mineral content, different capacity to host the photosynthetic organisms that create the glow. How old is this place? Old enough that "age" stopped being a meaningful measurement. Do the creatures ever leave? Some do, they say. Young bats sometimes fly back toward the surface entrance. Pale reptiles occasionally journey outward. They rarely return. The outside is too *thin* for them now—too much light, too much open air, no depth to hide in.

I learn the cavern's rhythms, which are not circadian but *deeper*. There are seasonal variations (how they measure these, I never fully understand—perhaps by the way certain fungi fruit, or the breeding cycles of the flying things). There are longer cycles measured in years or decades, where the entire ecosystem's balance shifts slightly. And there are cycles so vast that only geological time can hold them—the slow chemical changes in the stone itself, the patient work of water still moving through fissures, the gradual deepening of the hollow.

I never fully master the silence that the pale people speak in, but I learn to listen to it. 

I observe the one who tends the moss—I start thinking of them as Moss-tender, though I'm not sure this is right—and I begin to help. At first they watch me work with something between tolerance and amusement. I'm clumsy. My movements are jerky, still carrying the speed of the surface world. I pull out healthy growths by accident, crushing fungal fruiting bodies with my careless weight. But I keep returning. I keep trying. Eventually, Moss-tender adjusts their schedule to work when I'm present, and their presence becomes a kind of patient instruction. They don't demonstrate—they simply work, and I learn by proximity, by osmosis, the way the cavern itself seems to teach.

The loom-worker fascinates me differently. I sit for hours watching their hands, trying to understand the pattern they're creating. It's impossible—the pattern is too complex, too slow, it extends into time I can't see. But I begin to understand that this is the point. The weaving *is* time made visible. Each pass of thread represents days or weeks or months. By the time the cloth is finished, whoever wears it will have aged into the pattern. They will have become part of it.

I start my own weaving on a smaller loom they help me construct. Mine progresses faster than theirs—I'm still moving at the speed of someone who knows they'll die—but I try to slow it down. I try to sink into the repetition, to let my hands become mechanical while my mind... empties. Becomes a kind of moss itself, green and patient and photosynthetic, turning the non-light into sustenance.

I experience something I've never felt before: the removal of *choice*.

In the world above, I was paralyzed by options. Every decision felt like a path that foreclosed others. Every yes was an infinity of nos. Here, decisions are made for you by the slow unfolding of things. The moss grows or it doesn't. The pattern reveals itself or it doesn't. You tend it. You observe. You don't decide. This sounds like imprisonment, and perhaps it is, but it's an imprisonment that feels like freedom. I realize I've been holding my breath in the world above for decades, waiting for permission to exhale. Here, I exhale.

But I don't descend fully.

There's something—some small, stubborn part of me—that insists on remaining liminal. I keep my journal. I continue to track time using the old system—days, weeks, months—even as that system becomes increasingly meaningless. I prepare my own food rather than fully adopting the fermented fungal pastes and slow-brewed mushroom liquids the pale people sustain themselves on. I maintain a kind of... awareness of the world above, though it becomes hazier and less real with each month that passes.

The pale people accept this. They don't encourage my departure, but they don't precisely encourage my deepening either. I suspect they recognize what I am: a bridge state. A way for the cavern to understand the world above without fully surrendering to it. I am translation made flesh. I am the liminal space itself, given form.

Years pass. Or I think years pass. Time is strange here. My body changes slowly. My skin takes on a faint luminescence—not as pronounced as theirs, but noticeable. My eyes adjust more fully to the dusk. I begin to shed my body hair, or perhaps it's just becoming finer, becoming moss. I'm not entirely sure. I don't look at mirrors anymore. Mirrors are from the world above.

But I still write in my journal. The entries become shorter, more fragmentary. Longer spaces appear between them. Eventually, I'm not sure if I'm writing in the journal at all, or if the journal is simply recording the mineral changes happening in the stone nearby, if the concept of "writing" has transformed into "leaving traces." I can no longer tell the difference.

One day—and I'm not sure this day is different from any other day, but I experience it as a threshold—I'm sitting at my loom and Moss-tender sits beside me. This is unusual. They rarely sit. They're usually moving, tending. But they sit, and we work in silence for what might be hours or days, and then they make a specific sound—not quite speech, something between humming and the vibration of the stone itself—and point to the eastern wall.

The luminescence there has intensified dramatically. New phosphorescent organisms are colonizing the space, the pale people's hand-tending working in conjunction with some natural cycle to create a bloom of bioluminescence so pronounced it's almost bright. It illuminates the cavern in a new way. I can see deeper than I ever have before. And I see that the depth continues. Always. Forever. There are caverns beyond this cavern, and caverns beyond those, a Russian doll of darkness and life extending downward.

I realize I could go deeper. I could walk into that new light, follow it down, and the pale people wouldn't stop me. They would simply watch me go with their patient, absorbing eyes. They would tend the moss, and eventually another visitor would arrive—or perhaps they wouldn't, perhaps I'm the last visitor, perhaps visitors come once a century and I simply happened to arrive at the right time—and the cycle would continue.

I could descend.

But instead, I return to my loom.

I resume my weaving. My hands move at the speed they've learned here—not slow, exactly, but *deep*. Moving through layers. And I realize I've already descended, haven't I? The choice was never binary. It's not about the *depth* you reach, but whether you *stop resisting* the depth that's already claiming you.

I am the visitor who became the resident who became the stone who became the moss who became the silence who became the listening.

I am still becoming. Very slowly. At the speed of stone. At the speed that time moves when no one is measuring it. At the speed that matters.

And that is enough.
