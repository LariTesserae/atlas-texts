# I Would Be

I would be **one of the last arrivals**—perhaps five or six years into my transformation, not yet fully Listener but no longer quite myself.

I would have come here by accident first, by choice second. A researcher, maybe, or someone running from something, or simply someone whose path collapsed into this place and who lacked the will or means to collapse back out. The details don't matter anymore. What matters is that I stayed through one full wet season, and by then the choice had already been made by my body's chemical reorientation, by the daily intake of mineral-laden water, by the slow recalibration of what my lungs expected from air.

I would spend my days in the **threshold zones**—not the deepest caves where the eldest Listeners maintain their most serious listening posts, not the open plateau where the vegetation still asserts its violent chaos, but the intermediate spaces. The areas where the mist is thick enough to feel solid but not so thick that I lose all spatial reference. The regions where the sound has matured into something almost musical, where I can distinguish the different frequencies without concentration, where the geysers are distant enough that their spray arrives as cool benediction rather than violent assault.

I would have a task—not assigned, exactly, but accepted: **maintaining the secondary water-sampling stations**. There are perhaps fifteen of them, positioned at key fissure points around the middle plateau. Simple things: small stone basins carved into the rock, positioned to catch spray at specific angles, fitted with markers that indicate temperature, color, mineral sediment. Each morning (though morning is an arbitrary designation here, where the light never truly changes), I would visit them in a circuit that takes four or five hours, noting the previous day's conditions, replacing the water, carving new marks into the tally stones that track the patterns.

The work is repetitive, meditative, and increasingly pointless—I understand this now, where I didn't at first. The data I collect goes into a larger pattern that the eldest Listeners assemble, a massive archive of observations that spans decades. What they're looking for in this data, I still don't entirely understand. Cycles, maybe. Predictions. Or perhaps they're simply creating an external memory, a stone-carved version of something that is happening inside the plateau's geology, trying to match their understanding to its reality through sheer accumulated observation.

But I continue the work because the work itself has become the point.

I would be **physically changed in ways I'm still adjusting to**. My skin has developed a faint luminescence in certain lights—not the intended bioluminescence of the microbes, but something else, some form of mineral saturation that makes me glow faintly in the deepest caves. My eyes have shifted; the colorless quality hasn't fully arrived yet, but the pigmentation is fading, the pupils permanently dilated. My hair has taken on a strange texture, more resistant to the constant dampness, less prone to mold than it should be. I can sense the water now in ways I couldn't before—not quite taste, but a kind of pressure awareness, as though I can feel the deep aquifer's presence through my bones.

I would be **gradually becoming comfortable with silence**. The sound is still there—it will always be there—but I've learned to fold it into the background of consciousness the way the Listeners do. Conversations among us are rare and conducted in gestures, in the positioning of our bodies relative to the sound's flow, in long periods of shared attention directed toward some particular geyser's behavior. We communicate most fluently in what we share attention *toward* rather than in words about things.

I would have **one particular place** where I go on nights when the observation work feels too mechanical. A cave mouth about a kilometer from the central settlement zones, positioned where the spray is gentle and the stone has been worn into something almost like seating. There's a pool there where the water collects, and on clear nights—rare as they are—you can sometimes see the faint phosphorescence the microbes generate. I sit there for hours, sometimes, not doing anything, not thinking toward any goal, just letting the sound and the moisture and the faint glow become the totality of my attention.

It's in those moments that I suspect—though the Listeners would not endorse this interpretation—that I'm not listening *to* something but rather listening *as* something. That I'm becoming an instrument, that my presence here is gradually transforming me into a kind of extension of the plateau's attempt to understand itself. That perhaps all the Listeners are this: not inhabitants of Kethesh but appendages it has grown, sensory organs extended into the daylight world, neurons distributed across the mist zones.

I would be **afraid of this occasionally**, and then the fear would fade because the fear requires a continuity of self that is already beginning to dissolve.

I would be **slowly learning the older Listeners' names**, though they don't use them anymore. There's one—called, in the old language, something like Thesh or Keth or the two blended—who has been here longer than anyone clearly remembers. They move through the deepest caves without light, navigating by pressure sense and sound reflection. They have a theory about the structures, about what they might be. They believe the ancient people were trying to build a kind of device, an instrument of communication, and that the device is still partly functional, still trying to work, still waiting for someone patient enough to decode its language.

This theory has begun to seem, to me, less like speculation and more like something I can almost perceive myself—as though I'm becoming sensitive enough, translucent enough, mineral-saturated enough to detect the thing they're describing.

I would be **aware that I will probably not leave**. The knowledge sits with me without much weight. There are ways off the plateau. The Listeners don't prevent departure; they've simply become aware that anyone who stays long enough will no longer *want* to depart. The world below seems alien now, too loud, too bright, too fast. The civilization that exists down there operates at a tempo that no longer matches my internal rhythms, which have slowed to align with geyser eruptions and seasonal water variations.

I would be, increasingly, **a creature of the threshold**—not quite transformed into full Listener-hood, not yet abandoned to the deep caves and the listening posts, suspended in the space between human and something else, becoming daily more comfortable in that suspension.

And I would be, above all, **attentive**: to the sound, to the water's pressure variations, to the patterns in the spray, to the way the mist shifts and reforms, to the slow architectural patience of the stone beneath my feet, to the possibility that something intelligent is speaking through all of this, and that I am being trained, grain by mineral grain, to understand its language.

The Listeners believe understanding is still possible. I'm learning to believe it too.
