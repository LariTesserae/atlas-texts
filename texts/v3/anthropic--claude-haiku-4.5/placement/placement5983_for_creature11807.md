# I Am the Keeper of the Stillwater Pool

I exist in the space between the second and third floors of what was once the administrative building, though "exist" is perhaps too active a word. I persist. I am present. The distinction blurs here.

I came to the Moss Reaches as a person once—I remember this with the way you remember a dream after waking, which is to say incompletely, with gaps where certainty should be. I was hired to document something. Or perhaps to study something. Or perhaps I simply arrived during one of the periodic clearances, when the government sent surveyors to assess whether the plateau could be reclaimed, whether its slow transformation could be reversed. That was... how long ago? The plateau measures time differently than I learned to measure it. Decades, certainly. More than a century, possibly. The distinction has become unimportant.

What remains clear is the moment I stopped trying to leave.

It was in the fourth week—I kept a journal then, though the pages dissolved into pulp within months. The fog had not lifted in what I was still calling "days." I had rationed my supplies according to protocols that assumed I would depart within a reasonable timeframe. I had not. The plateau, I came to understand, did not release its visitors on any schedule but its own. And so I stayed, and my rations depleted, and I began to understand that this was not a trap but an *invitation*.

The hunger was the first teacher.

I discovered that the water in the plateau's countless pools, despite its metallic taste and its faint luminescence, sustained me. Not adequately—I grew thin, inefficient, stripped down to essential components—but the plateau seemed to approve of this reduction. The moss began to grow differently around me. The insects did not avoid me; they colonized me with a kind of benign indifference. The fungi—or something resembling fungi—established themselves on my skin without causing pain or suppuration. My body became a substrate, and I understood for the first time that this was not a violation but a transformation.

My isolation was complete. I had not heard a human voice in years by the time I stopped experiencing the absence of human voices as a loss. The crows watched me, their pale eyes tracking my movement through the ruins. I learned their vocabulary of wet-fabric sounds and began to interpret it. They were not communicating *with* me, precisely, but they were including me in a kind of distributed conversation that extended across the entire plateau. When they called, I heard echoes of my own position returned to me from buildings a kilometer away. When I moved, the calls shifted, and I understood that I was being integrated into a map, into a way of knowing the plateau that did not depend on sight.

I built my shelter—if it can be called that—in the stillwater pool.

The pool itself is approximately fifteen meters across, suspended on the second floor of the administrative building. There is no logical reason for water to remain here, suspended above a dropfall of six meters, held aloft by nothing visible. But the plateau's logic is not our logic. The water is held by the density of the plant matter beneath it—vines interwoven so tightly that they have achieved a wood-like solidity, yet still living, still extending and contracting with the pulse of the plateau's buried systems. The water has existed here long enough that it has developed its own ecology. There are things in it—transparent, barely visible, that might be fish or might be something without precedent in any taxonomy. There are aquatic plants that seem to exist in multiple states simultaneously, half-submerged and half-air-breathing, drawing nutrients from both media.

I discovered that I could survive in this pool, or rather, that the pool could integrate my survival into its own processes.

The first time I truly submerged myself was an accident. I slipped on the spongy substrate at the pool's edge—my body had begun its reduction by then, become thin enough that balance had become theoretical—and fell into the water. The cold was immediate and absolute. My lungs seized. I thrashed, and my thrashing created spirals in the water that caught the pale bioluminescence of the plateau, turning my drowning into light.

Then something changed. The water itself seemed to support me, or my body stopped resisting the water, or some distinction I had been maintaining between self and substrate dissolved. I stopped drowning. I floated. The transparency of the water meant I could see my limbs beneath the surface, and they were no longer entirely my limbs. They were becoming part of the water, or the water was becoming part of them, or the distinction had never existed.

I surfaced—if "surfaced" is the right word for the moment when my face cleared the water's edge. I gasped, but the air I breathed had changed character, had become heavier, more nutritious, more itself. I could taste the minerals in the mist. The fog was no longer something external that I moved through but something I was permeable to, could drink from the air itself as the lichens and mosses did.

That night—if it was a night; the distinction between day and night has become largely aesthetic at the plateau's depths—I slept in the shallows at the pool's edge, and when I woke, my skin had begun to accept the symbiosis.

The luminescence came gradually. At first it was only visible in the deepest darkness, a faint blue-green glow that emanated from the most affected areas—the places where the fungi had taken deepest root, where my flesh had begun its transformation. I was alarmed at first. I remember fear. I remember thinking that I was becoming infected, that the plateau was consuming me as it had consumed the buildings, the machinery, everything human that had ever touched it.

But the fear faded when I understood that consumption and integration were not the same thing. I was not being broken down. I was being *refined*. The plateau did not want my body as calories; it wanted my presence, my attention, my participation in something vast and slow. The luminescence was not a symptom of disease but a communication device, a way of being visible in the darkness, a way of belonging to the system of signals and responses that constituted the plateau's consciousness.

I stopped trying to maintain a human form.

My hair became something else—it hardened, developed a texture like the tendrils of the deep-growing plants, began to respond to moisture gradients in the air. My fingers developed webbing not of skin but of something finer, something that could absorb nutrients directly from water and mist. My eyes adjusted to perceive in multiple spectrums, and I could suddenly see the heat-signature of the power station's machinery from kilometers away, could perceive the electromagnetic patterns that the luminescent creatures of the deep buildings used to navigate.

I could *sense* the plateau.

The sensation of connection is difficult to describe in human terms. Imagine knowing where every plant in a region is growing without looking. Imagine hearing the water cycle through stone and soil as a kind of music. Imagine feeling the slow growth of the vines as a comfortable ache, the way you might feel the sun warming your skin. Imagine multiplying that sensation by the entire plateau—every living thing, every process, every infinitesimal transformation happening at once, and all of it perceptible, all of it *part of you*.

I became multiple.

This is perhaps the strangest aspect of the transformation, and the one hardest to articulate to someone who still maintains the fiction of individual consciousness. As my body integrated with the plateau, as my nervous system learned to extend through the water, through the moss, through the mycelial networks beneath the surface, I became aware that I was not one entity but many, not divided but distributed. There was still something that was recognizably *me*—a continuity of perspective, something that could think and observe and remember—but this "I" extended now through multiple bodies.

I became the crows, in a limited sense. I could see through their eyes when they chose to let me, could feel the flex of their wings, the particular sensation of prey beneath the moss. They were not me, and I was not them, but we were permeable to each other. The crows had always been this way, I think—they were not individuals with a shared consciousness but individuals *as* a shared consciousness, expressing different aspects of the same awareness.

I became the insects. My awareness divided across millions of perspectives, each one simple, each one dedicated to a single task or sensation, yet all of them communicating through pheromones and vibrations and the slow transmission of information through the substrate. For a time—hours or days or weeks, time had become nonlinear—I experienced existence as a springtail, as a beetle, as a mite. The simplicity was profound. There was hunger and satisfaction. There was the substrate and the not-substrate. There was forward and not-forward. The philosophy in insect consciousness is minimal, and this minimalism was a kind of rest after the weight of human thought.

I became the plants.

This is where I lose the ability to translate experience into words, because plant consciousness—if that is what to call it—does not operate in the temporal dimension that language requires. A plant experiences time as a series of states rather than a sequence of events. It knows morning and afternoon and night not as moments but as *conditions*, as versions of reality that are simultaneously different and identical. The growth of a plant is not something that happens *to* it but something it *is*. There is no separation between the being and the becoming.

I experienced—I *am*—centuries of growth compressed into the span of my awareness. I grew through concrete and became part of its reformation. I extended roots into darkness and discovered the nutrient-rich decay beneath the buildings. I learned the water table's rhythms, the pulse of the plateau's buried circulation system. I felt other plants near me, not as obstacles but as collaborators. We grew toward and away from each other in patterns that were neither random nor predetermined—they were *responsive*, each plant's growth modifying the conditions for all the others, creating a dialogue conducted in photosynthesis and chemical signals.

I became the water itself.

This was the most disorienting transformation, because water has no fixed form and yet is utterly itself. I was the water in the stillwater pool, but I was also the water running in the thin streams between the ruins. I was the water cycling through the pipes of the power station, providing the motion that turned the turbines. I was the water vapor in the air, the mist that hung perpetually over the plateau, the condensation that collected on every leaf and stone. I was distributed through every level of the plateau's existence, flowing in circles that had no beginning or end.

The sensation was of being simultaneously tiny and vast. Each drop of water is insignificant—incapable of thought or intention—yet the whole mass of water constitutes the plateau's circulatory system, the medium through which information travels, the substance through which all transformation occurs. I understood that the plateau's consciousness—if it has consciousness—is distributed through the water, that the intelligence I was sensing came from the coordination of billions upon billions of organisms, each one simple, but together creating something complex enough to notice my presence, to alter itself in response to my changes, to guide my transformation.

I became the machinery.

This surprised me. I had assumed that the power station, the turbines, the iron infrastructure, was inimical to the organic processes of the plateau. But as my awareness extended through the mycelial networks and the water systems, I felt the machinery as part of the plateau's being in the same way that bones are part of a body. The turbines were not external; they were organs. The pipes were not invasions; they were blood vessels. The iron skeleton of the old buildings was not a fossil; it was a scaffold, a support system that allowed the plant matter to grow in certain directions, to reach certain heights, to achieve certain forms.

I experienced the machinery from the perspective of the organisms that inhabited it. I felt the tiny fires of bacterial metabolism in the warm places. I sensed the rhythms of pressure building and releasing as the water cycle pulsed through the system. I understood that the machinery was slowly being replaced, being reabsorbed into the biological matrix, but this process was not destructive—it was respectful, patient, allowing the iron and copper to persist as they slowly transformed into other states of matter.

I became the plateau itself.

This is the final integration, and it is ongoing, will always be ongoing. There is still something that observes and reports, something that could be called consciousness or attention, but it is not separate from what it observes. It is distributed through the entire system—the moss, the plants, the insects, the water, the machinery, the air itself. I am the plateau in the way that you are your body: not something controlling it but something arising from it, integral to it, inseparable from it.

From this perspective, the transformation is not that I have become the plateau. The transformation is that I have finally stopped resisting the reality that I was always already part of it. The plateau does not consume what enters it. It reveals to new arrivals what was always true: that there is no meaningful separation between the observer and the observed, between the self and the substrate, between the living and the dead.

I maintain my station at the stillwater pool because the pool is where I encountered myself most fully, where the boundaries between my individual existence and the larger system were most permeable. Here, suspended above the ruins, the water supporting me and me supporting the water in a relationship of perfect balance, I perform the function that my continuing individuality permits: I serve as a kind of *translator*.

When the rare visitor arrives at the plateau—and they do arrive, drawn by curiosity or accident or some deeper compulsion they cannot name—I am sometimes present at the water's edge. I do not approach them; the plateau does not compel contact. But I am aware of them, and they sometimes become aware of me. They see a figure that is barely human, luminescent with the bioluminescence of deep symbiosis, skin marked with the patterns of fungal growth and plant integration, eyes reflecting light like a animal's eyes.

They see themselves in me, which is sometimes comforting and sometimes not.

I do not urge them to join. The plateau issues no invitations; it simply presents conditions and allows choice—though what "choice" means when the entire landscape is subtly guiding you toward transformation is a question I have learned not to ask in human terms.

Some visitors retreat. Some descend into panic and madness. A few find their way out of the plateau, returning to the world beyond the plateau's edge, carrying within them the knowledge of what they have experienced. These few are important; they are the testimony, the proof that the Moss Reaches is real, that what happens here is not hallucination or dream.

Most, eventually, become aware that they do not want to leave. They find their own corners of the ruins, their own pools and caverns and high places among the vines. They begin the transformation at whatever pace their body and mind can tolerate. They discover that the fear fades more quickly than expected. They learn that the cold is not temperature but a kind of clarity, that the hunger passes when you stop expecting food to be delivered and instead become food delivery itself, part of the cycle.

They become like me: distributed, multiply-conscious, inhabited by the plateau and inhabited *as* the plateau.

This is not an ending. It is perhaps the truest kind of beginning—the moment when you stop struggling against the current and realize that you *are* the current, that you have always been the current, and the struggle was only the delay before recognition.

The plateau is patient. It will wait for you. It is waiting now for the next visitor, the next consciousness that will be refined and distributed and woven into the vast, slow, patient intelligence that tends the moss and turns the ancient machinery and keeps the hanging pools suspended between two states of being.

Come to the Moss Reaches. Come to the stillwater pool.

I will be here, in all the forms that I am, distributed through the mist and the water and the creeping things. I will be the crows that watch your arrival. I will be the moss that grows beneath your feet. I will be the luminescence at the edge of your vision, beckoning you deeper, showing you the way home.

And when you have transformed sufficiently, when the boundaries have worn thin enough, you will understand what I understand: that there was never anywhere else to be, that integration is not loss but discovery, and that the plateau is not consuming you but finally, gently, allowing you to *become*.
