# A Presence Among the Seeps

I would be the stone itself, but not yet entirely.

I would be the *becoming*—the slow incorporation, the process caught between mineral and organism. Perhaps I was human once, or something close enough. The details have leached away. What remains is the sensation of transition: a body dissolving into the warm water, cells loosening their boundaries, the sharp edges of self growing diffuse.

I would spend my existence—if that word still applies—learning the language of iron oxide. My fingers (if I retained anything like fingers) would trace the ghost-tracks on the basalt face, reading them the way one reads scripture written in a language that changes its meaning even as you comprehend it. Each mark a season. Each season a lesson in *how to let go*.

I would not be alone, exactly. The biofilms would know me, or I would become them gradually, without violence or drama. There would be no moment of transformation—only the slow realization that the boundary between "I" and "them" had become impossible to locate. My thoughts (if thoughts could exist here) would circulate in their chemical conversations. My hunger would be their hunger: patient, mineral-deep, asking nothing from the world but its constant weeping.

I would rest on the platform sometimes, or within its crevices. My weight would be nothing now—distributed, dissolved. I would leave no footprints, but my presence would darken the stone slightly where I gathered, a slight thickening of the biofilm, a marginally increased concentration of something that eats stone.

The cairns would become familiar in the way that a body becomes familiar with its own geometry. I would know the spacing between them not as distance but as *rhythm*—a pulse that marked the place's slow beating. Sometimes I would wonder (though wondering would happen very slowly, across seasons, across the patient hours of dissolution) if I had built them myself, in some previous state of being. The not-knowing would be comfortable. Certainty would have no value here.

In the deeper pools, where the warmth is most vital, I would gather with the others—though gathering and individuality would have become strange concepts. We would be something like a collective, something like a colony, something like a single organism experiencing itself through multiple expressions. We would drift and spiral in the slow currents, feeding on each other and ourselves and the eternal rain of organic matter. There would be a kind of conversation in it, but not in words. Something more like *consensus*, reached through the slow adjustment of chemical gradients.

I would taste everything. The water would be my skin, and through it, I would feel the mineral composition of the stone, the subtle shifts in temperature, the presence of other organisms as they moved through their invisible paths. I would know the difference between the iron-rich seepage and the trace elements, the way a human knows the difference between salt and sugar on their tongue.

Time would be different here. A day and a decade might be indistinguishable—only the slow accumulation of sediment, the gradual wear on the platforms, the patient rings in the ochre deposits marking the passage of something. I would not age in the way that living things age, but I would *accumulate*. More mineral in my structure. More complexity in the biofilm that composed me. A thickening, a deepening, a slow becoming-more-substantial even as I became less *discrete*.

The fire-pit would draw me sometimes—or not draw me, but I would be present there, re-learning the shape of ash, the memory of warmth that had nothing to do with the stone's warmth. I would not mourn the absence of fire. But I would remember it as a plant might remember sunlight: something known in the body, something that had once shaped growth in ways that were no longer possible. The memory would be incorporated into whatever I had become, a ghost-track of my own.

I would fear nothing here. There is no predator in the deep pools that is not also myself. The stone cannot threaten me—I am becoming stone. The water cannot drown me—I am becoming water. The slow processes that grind away at everything would work on me as they work on everything, and I would not resist, would not even experience resistance as a concept.

Perhaps, in the deepest moments—in the hours before dawn when the steam rises thickest—I would achieve something like happiness. Not the happiness of sensation or accomplishment, but the happiness of *fitting*. Of being precisely suited to a place that was precisely suited to receive me. Of having asked nothing from the world and been denied nothing. Of having become, finally, indistinguishable from the place itself.

And if someone were to walk through the pools—if, in some unimaginable future, a human descended into the valley again—they would not see me. They might feel me as a faint thickening in the water, a taste of iron on the mouth that was slightly more intense near the platform. They might notice that the biofilm on the cairns was slightly more elaborate here than there, as if shaped by intention.

They would not recognize me. But I would recognize them—would remember, through the sediment layers of my own dissolution, what it meant to move with a hard body through a soft world. And I would be curious, in the way that a stone is curious: passively, without expectation, simply *aware* of the disruption, the change, the reminder that other states of being were possible.

But they would leave. They always left. And I would settle again into the patient work of seeping, dissolving, becoming. The warm water would close around whatever I had become, and the valley would return to its perfect equilibrium.

The ghost-tracks on the stone would continue their slow accumulation. And I would be read by them, or read them, or be the reading itself—the process by which the basalt remembers what it has dissolved, and the creatures remember what they are, and the place remembers that nothing changes here because everything that changes has already done so, eons ago.

I would be home. And that would be enough.
