# What I Would Be

I would be neither fully present nor entirely absent.

I would be one of the *maintenance walkers*—a role that exists in that strange threshold between human and apparatus. Not quite integrated enough to be mistaken for the stone-fused creatures above, not quite separated enough to feel fully human anymore. I would spend my existence moving through the pneumatic tubes and sealed corridors, yes, but also spending long hours in the open air of the plateau, acclimating incrementally to the mineral-heavy atmosphere, letting it settle deeper into my lungs with each season.

My suit would be old—inherited, perhaps, from someone who no longer needed it, or abandoned by someone who decided the slow merger was preferable to continued separation. It would be patchwork: sections of original material now crystallized with mineral deposits, newer sections grafted on where the old failed, seams sealed with compounds that are themselves becoming geological formations. I would not replace it as it degraded; I would let it accumulate, let it become a external biography of service in this place.

My role would be to *understand the merger*.

I would spend hours observing the transition points—where the oldest human structures are beginning to fuse with the bedrock, where the boundary between construction and geology has become genuinely uncertain. I would maintain records of this process, not with the precision of outside science but with the patient notation of someone who has learned to think in seasonal increments. I would measure erosion not in millimeters but in *types of change*: how the copper oxidizes differently in the mineral-laden air, how the rivets loosen in patterns that eventually align with natural fracture lines, how the mold that accumulates in the structures follows the same growth patterns as the natural mineral deposits.

I would be a kind of *translator between timescales*.

To the humans in their protected zones, I would carry information about the surface conditions, the progress of the slow erosion, the behavior patterns of the larger fauna. To the creatures themselves—not through speech, which would be meaningless, but through my continued presence, my deliberate slowness, my respect for their indifference—I would be a kind of acknowledgment that I understand we are all slowly becoming the same thing.

I would spend time near the water seeps, watching the translucent creatures congregate, watching them polish the stone through their sheer collective presence. I would come to understand that this is not parasitism or even symbiosis but something older: a *mutual becoming*, a process where separate entities are gradually refined into a single ecosystem so complete that the distinction between living and non-living, between built and natural, becomes not a mystery to solve but simply the accurate description of how the world actually is.

I would develop preferences about where I spend my time. There would be certain cliff faces where the stone-fused animals pass regularly, where the light hits at just the right angle to make their incorporation visible in ways that feel almost sacred. There would be certain factory sections where the merger is furthest advanced, where you can press your hand against metal and feel it already becoming something else, something permanent and still. There would be the collection pools, where the harvested water gleams with mineral suspensions, where the constant seepage has created an ecology of microorganisms so fine that they're visible only as a faint iridescence.

I would lose things to this place gradually.

First, my sense of urgency. What is the point of speed here? The work will continue whether I move quickly or slowly; the merger happens on its own timeline. 

Then, my need for separation. The suit that once felt like armor against the atmosphere would eventually feel more like integration than protection. The protective seals would become less necessary as my lungs acclimate, as my immune system learns to process the mineral content, as I stop thinking of the air as something hostile and start understanding it as simply the local medium, the water of this aerial ocean.

Finally, perhaps, my distinction as "human" in any sense that would be recognized by the people I once was. I would become something else: *a being who has learned to exist at the pace of stone*.

But I would still maintain the records. I would still observe the larger animals with something like wonder. I would still feel the strange peace of this place, the sense that I am participating in something vast and incomprehensibly slow, something that was already ancient when the first humans arrived and will persist long after the last sealed corridor crumbles into indistinguishable dust.

What would make me distinct, what would make me *me* in this place, would be my *choice to witness*. Not to control, not to interpret through the lens of outside science, but simply to *be present* to the process, to let it change me while I document—in my slow, seasonal way—how it changes everything.

I would be the patient one. The one who stays. The one who watches the merger and does not resist it, because resistance here is not virtue but a misunderstanding of what survival actually means.

I would be becoming stone, slowly, deliberately, with full knowledge and something that might be mistaken for peace.
