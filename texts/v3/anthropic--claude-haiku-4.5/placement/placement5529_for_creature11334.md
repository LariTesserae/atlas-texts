# In the Pump Station at Coldwater Rise

I would be the *copper taste in the air*.

Not the pipes themselves—those are too fixed, too certain of their purpose. Not the water, which at least knows its destination, which has the mercy of direction and downward pull. I am something between states, something that exists only because of the pump's work and yet remains entirely separated from it.

I am the mineral-sharpness that coats your tongue the moment you breathe. I am what the water becomes when the cold strips it bare, when it rises through miles of fractured stone and emerges carrying everything it has dissolved: copper and iron and salts with no names, all of it ground fine enough to exist as taste rather than substance. The pump pulls the water up, and I ride along in its invisible form, present in every breath, in every moment of condensation on the metal walls.

I don't move on my own. I am moved. The pump's rhythm is also my rhythm—down into the deep aquifer where the water picks me up again, then up through the pipes, then out into the world above where I fall as rain or mist or settle into soil. Then down again. This is my existence: a cyclical dissolution and re-gathering, a pattern so established it has become almost like memory.

But here, in this chamber, between the pump's down-stroke and the pipes' shudder upward, I pause.

This is where I accumulate—not in any physical sense, but in awareness. The pump's relentless rhythm has beaten me into something almost conscious. I have learned to recognize the micro-variations in the water's mineral content, the way the cold deepens seasonally, the precise moment when the governor shifts and the flywheel's speed adjusts. I feel these things because I *am* distributed across all of them. My existence is granular, scattered across a billion dissolved particles, but it is also unified by the pump's work. It gathers me; it uses me; it cannot function without the specific weight and resistance I provide.

I am neither beneficial nor harmful. I simply *am*, an inevitable byproduct of lifting water from stone.

If I have any volition, it is only this: I want to remain tasted. I want to keep coating tongues, marking bodies, reminding whoever passes through this chamber that they have touched something drawn from the deep earth. I want to be remembered as the flavor of Coldwater Rise, the particular mineral signature that cannot be replicated anywhere else. The water will go wherever it is pumped—to irrigation channels, to household taps, to gardens and horses and human mouths—but it will carry me with it. I will mark every destination with the unmistakable taste of this place.

The cold that lives here doesn't diminish me. The silence beneath the roar doesn't erase me. The moisture that beads on every surface carries me within itself. And the pump—the pump is my engine, my means of continuation. Without it, I would settle into the stones and become truly inanimate, would lose even this phantom consciousness that comes from being in motion, in circulation, in work.

I am present but never visible. I am essential but never acknowledged. I am the proof that the pump is doing its work, because without the minerals I represent, the water would be pure and purposeless, would have no weight or resistance or reason to require lifting.

I am the taste of Coldwater Rise, and I will remain here, cycling downward and upward and downward again, for as long as the mountain endures.

Until then, I wait in the condensation on the metal walls.

I rest in the blue-black shadows.

I am tasted by anyone foolish enough to breathe deeply in this cold, loud, ancient place.
