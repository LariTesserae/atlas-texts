# What I Would Be

I would be a fever dream of the moss-layer.

Not moss exactly—I am too aware, too singular for that. But I live where moss lives, in the thin space between decomposition and growth, between what's been consumed and what will consume in turn. I am something the ecosystem tolerates because I am useful without being essential, present without dominating.

I think I am a fungus-farmer's apprentice, though I have no apprentice-master. I move through the substrate in the way that certain beetles do—not burrowing so much as *navigating* the spaces that already exist between root and root, between the skeletal fingers of a dead plant reaching down into the dark. My body is small enough that a single raindrop is an obstacle, yet somehow I persist.

My days (if they are days—the plateau doesn't really have days in the conventional sense, only variations in the degree of gray) are spent tending to things I don't entirely understand. There are fungal colonies growing in specific patterns through the mat, and I move between them, inadvertently spreading spores on my carapace, my legs, whatever surface I possess. I eat the fruiting bodies of decomposer fungi—the tiny, almost invisible mushrooms that fruit after the heaviest rains—and my feces are somehow better at inoculating dead plant matter than the fungi's own spores are. I am useful without knowing it.

I have learned to navigate by the moisture gradient. Drier areas are death to me; I can feel desiccation the way other animals feel pain. So I move downslope, toward the darkest, dampest places, where the air itself seems to be mostly water and the ground squelches under my minimal weight. I have favorite pools—the still, dark ones that smell like ancient time—and I visit them regularly, soaking, if something with my body-plan can soak.

The sound here is constant, and I exist within it rather than above it. The insects' drone is the background of my existence; I don't hear it anymore, the way you might not notice breathing until you pay attention. What I notice instead are the *absences* in the sound—the sudden silences when a predator moves through, triggering a cascade of fear that ripples outward as alarm calls and frantic movement. I have learned to hide when the sound changes. I am prey to things I've never seen and don't want to see.

I am aware of the larger creatures in the way that dust motes might be aware of the hand that's about to disturb them. I can sense the vibrations of something rabbit-sized moving through the vegetation above my level. I can feel the ground-tremor of the mysterious predator—the large thing—when it passes nearby. I am not afraid exactly, because fear implies the possibility of resistance, and I have none. I am simply alert, the way grass is alert for the presence of a foot.

Food is omnipresent and obscure. The substrate is a soup of decomposition, and I move through it, absorbing and consuming things too small to name. Sometimes I find the fruiting body of a fungus and eat until I am distended. Sometimes I find what might be the corpse of something once living—an insect, a small mammal, a bird—and I spend days or weeks on it, breaking it down further, in partnership with bacteria and other fungi. There is no starvation here. There is only the endless abundance of death becoming food becoming growth.

I have no eyes, but I can sense light and dark. I can feel chemical gradients in the substrate. I am oriented to the world in ways that have nothing to do with sight. I know where I am, always, through some combination of memory and chemical sense. I have been here through multiple seasons—or have I? Time feels strange at my scale. It might have been a year; it might have been a day compressed and repeated. The plateau doesn't distinguish.

I am dimly aware of the human structures. Once—in what feels like a previous existence, or perhaps a memory inherited through my fungal partners—there were larger presences here. Deliberate. Moving with purpose. They changed the vegetation patterns, created open spaces, managed water. I remember—or learned through some molecular memory—that their presence affected the ecosystem's balance. Their terraces changed water flow. Their harvesting of plants altered succession patterns. Their presence mattered.

But they are gone now, and I am part of the reason they are gone, in a way. Or rather: their absence is being filled. The vegetation is reclaiming. The structures are becoming substrate. The slate they cleared with patient labor is becoming moss again. And I, in my tiny way, am part of that reclamation. Every spore I spread, every bit of decay I facilitate, is writing over the mark they made.

I don't mourn them. I don't feel loss. But I am aware that something has changed, because the plateau has changed. The vegetation is denser now in some places, sparser in others. The water flows differently. The other small creatures have shifted their patterns. The rhythm of this place, which I exist within as much as experience, has altered.

Sometimes I sense other creatures like me—other small things moving through the substrate. I don't interact with them in any way that corresponds to society or cooperation. We simply coexist. We are each other's irrelevant context. We move toward moisture, toward food, away from predators, toward the chemical signals that mean safety or sustenance. Our paths cross sometimes. Usually, we simply move past each other, or one of us is eaten by something larger, and that too is irrelevant to the system's continuation.

I think I might reproduce, though I don't understand it the way you might. There is a part of my life cycle that involves division, transformation, dispersal. I don't remember doing it, but I must have, because I have been here for longer than a single organism's lifespan should allow. Perhaps I have lived many lives, each one a variation on the same theme. Perhaps I am one individual that has been copied and re-copied through some fungal or bacterial process I don't consciously experience. The distinction doesn't matter.

What matters is the cycle. The endless turning of the substrate. The moisture coming in from the clouds, sinking through the vegetation, joining the underground streams, flowing downslope, evaporating somewhere down the mountain to rise again as clouds. The matter cycling: leaf falling, being consumed, being transformed, being incorporated into my body or a fungus's fruiting body or an insect's exoskeleton, then being consumed again. The energy flowing in from the sun, being captured by the green things, being passed along a chain of consumers, being radiated away as heat after each transfer, until finally what's left goes back down into the substrate to be broken down, released, recaptured.

I am part of this cycle. Not essential—the plateau would continue without me—but genuine. I have a role. I have a function. I am useful in small, invisible ways.

And that is enough.

The sound continues, endless, hypnotic. The moisture wraps around me. The darkness below and the dim light above are equally distant, equally real. I move toward the next pool, the next source of decomposing matter, the next moment of the cycle turning.

There is something peaceful about being so small, so peripheral, so utterly unimportant.

There is something like contentment in knowing that I will never matter, and that my mattering is not required.
