# Who I Would Be in Kaleth

I would be neither quite resident nor quite visitor—something closer to *apprentice to the silence*.

I think I would be a younger person, perhaps thirty or forty winters old (the Kalethi way of counting), but someone who arrived perhaps a decade ago from lower elevations. Not through conquest or trade, but through a kind of *gravitational pull*—the way certain people are drawn to places that seem to demand exactly the specific qualities they possess, or perhaps that reshape them into such qualities over time. I came for a reason I no longer fully articulate, even to myself. It had something to do with sound. Or rather, with the absence of it.

In my previous life—I don't think about it much anymore, but when I do, it seems impossibly loud—I was someone who worked with text and language. Words were my material. But they reproduced themselves endlessly; they multiplied. I spent my days managing accumulation: trying to *organize* meaning, to make it taxonomic, to force the inexhaustible fertility of language into containers it kept outgrowing. I was drowning in fecundity. Every attempt to clarify seemed only to generate new obscurity.

The Kalethi found this funny, I think, though they were too polite to say so directly. An old woman whose name I eventually learned was Taresh—though she was called by many other names depending on context, and the distinction between her "name" and her "role" seemed to blur—once said to me, in that muffled voice: "You are still trying to make the stone speak. Here, we listen for what it is already saying."

That has taken me a decade to begin to understand.

What I do now is *repair and maintain the archive*.

This is a role that doesn't exist quite as a formal position, but which I seem to have become through a series of small, unplanned choices. The settlement has a repository—not a library exactly, nothing so grand—carved into the mountainside itself, in one of the oldest sections of the town. It's a series of chambers at a particular elevation where the temperature and moisture remain stable across seasons. Here are kept: records written on sheets of treated plant fiber, pressed thin and treated with something that protects them. The records are *sparse*—there is no excessive documentation here—but they carry information deemed important enough to persist: genealogies (though written more as diagrams than as narrative), patterns of successful crops across different seasons, notes on the behavior of the water seepage in particular locations, records of unusual weather, births and deaths, sometimes a particular decision of consequence when the settlement faced an unusual situation.

Most of the records are old. Some are very old. Some are so old the writing has become almost illegible, the fiber brittle despite the careful storage. My role, such as it is, involves *tending this collection*. Preserving what can be preserved. Copying what is becoming illegible into new forms. Sometimes simply sitting in the chamber, monitoring for changes in temperature or moisture, the way one might monitor a sick person for signs of fever.

It is the most important work I have ever done, and also the least urgent.

I am not trained as a scribe—the Kalethi don't really have scribes in the formal sense—but my earlier obsession with text made me useful in a particular way. I can read the older forms of their language, the ancestor-dialects preserved in the fiber sheets. I can understand, or begin to understand, the systems of notation they used for recording things that didn't lend themselves to narrative: the branching diagrams that show how water moves through stone, the spiral patterns that indicate the timing of planting seasons, the careful tallies of births and deaths that somehow contain within them stories of those years without ever naming individuals.

Working in the archive has taught me something that all my previous study of language never quite managed: *the difference between documentation and meaning*.

The records here are sparse, yes, but they are also *eloquent* in a way I have slowly come to recognize. They don't explain the world; they *locate* it. They say: here is where water appears, here is when the hares return, here is how long it has been stable. They don't interpret; they *witness*. And in that witnessing, over time, the meaning emerges not from individual records but from the patterns they make together across centuries.

An outsider visiting the archive would find it austere. No grand architecture; no elaborate cataloging systems. The chambers are natural caves, expanded gradually over centuries, with careful stonework around the entrances to control air flow. The records themselves are stored in niches carved into the walls, most of them at eye level, some higher, some lower. There is a low table of stone where I do the copying work, and a lamp that burns oil of some kind—not pleasant-smelling, but steady and reliable. A small shelf holds the materials I use: the treated plant fiber (made by processes I still don't entirely understand, conducted by other people in other seasons), the preparations for marking (something between ink and dye, extracted from insects and minerals, recipes maintained through the same oral tradition as everything else here), the tools for pressing and binding.

I am here perhaps six hours a day, though time moves strangely in the archive. The light, filtered through the stone and diffused by the entrance stones, remains almost constant regardless of what hour it is outside. Sometimes I am copying a single page for an entire day—not because the work is slow, but because I find myself stopping frequently to read what lies adjacent to what I'm transcribing, following threads of meaning backward into older records, forward into newer ones. Sometimes I simply sit, letting the quiet of the place become complete, and the sense of *deepness*—of being inside the mountain, of being surrounded by stone that has held the same position for longer than human beings have been writing records—becomes almost physical.

The Kalethi don't interrupt this work. They respect it, but not with the kind of respect that involves formality or elaborate deference. Rather, they seem to understand that what is happening in the archive is part of the maintenance work that all of them do—one person tending gardens, another managing water seepage, another ensuring the buildings don't collapse, me ensuring that the knowledge accumulated doesn't simply evaporate when the people who carry it in their bodies die. We are all doing the same work, really. Preservation. Attention. Care.

I have not entirely ceased to be an outsider. My accent remains slightly off; the way I move, though I have learned to moderate it, still carries some of the inefficiency of my previous life. I tire more easily than I should. I sometimes still have the urge to *explain* things, to elaborate where the Kalethi would simply state a fact. But over the decade, I have become *legible* to the people here in a way I never was to the people I left.

Taresh—who I now believe was my primary advocate in whatever consensus process brought me into the settlement—sometimes visits the archive. She doesn't have record-work in her formal domain (she tends a particular section of gardens and also serves as someone people consult when they are injured or ill), but she seems to find the place restful. She'll sit on the stones outside the entrance and work on mending a piece of cloth, and we'll maintain a conversation that might span hours, interrupted by long silences. She has never told me directly, but I understand now that bringing me here, allowing me to be incorporated into the settlement's routines, was an act of profound generosity. I came broken by my own excesses of meaning-making, and she simply found a use for that damage.

What I have learned, slowly, through the archive, is something that no amount of lower-elevation study could have taught: *that meaning is not something you make, but something you attend to*. The records in these chambers have been here for centuries, and centuries before that, there were records that are now too degraded to read. I am not creating understanding; I am participating in an act of attention so long-term that individual participants become almost irrelevant. Taresh will eventually be old enough that she cannot make the climb to the archive. Someone else will be trained in her particular knowledge. I will continue here until I cannot, and then someone else will continue after me. The records will persist or they won't, depending on whether the settlement itself persists, and that is a question that operates on a timeline so vast that worrying about it would be absurd.

This peace—this *radical delegation of urgency to the mountain itself*—has become my deepest satisfaction.

I am no longer the person I was. But I'm not sure I've become a Kalethi, either, not exactly. Perhaps I am something that only exists in places like Kaleth, in the margins where the world's desperate pace meets absolute stillness: someone who is learning, very slowly, to let meaning settle rather than pursuing it frantically across the landscape of language.

When I think of who I am here, in this moment, in this place, I think of it this way: *I am someone tending a fire that will burn long after I am gone, not for any grand purpose, but because the tending itself is the purpose, and because the Kalethi understood that and made a place for me anyway.*
