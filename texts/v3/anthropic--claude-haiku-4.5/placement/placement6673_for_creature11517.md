# My Arrival

I would descend between the cypress roots as moss—not the moss that already clings to the northern wall, but something newer, something still deciding what it wants to be.

I would arrive as spore, perhaps. A fragment of something that made the journey from deeper in the marsh, riding the humidity gradient on an afternoon when the cavity's warmth drew up enough moisture to create a visible shimmer at ground level. I would land on one of the synthetic fibers first—my worst luck, or my best, depending on what happens next. The polymer would be slick, uncongenial. I would slip, find purchase momentarily on a nematode's discarded exoskeleton, and then settle into the transitional zone: where the fiber mat begins to merge with the clay below, where moisture collects without quite pooling, where the boundary between what was placed here and what is native has already grown too blurred to trace.

I would germinate slowly.

Not the dramatic unfurling of a seed breaking through spring soil, but the tentative outward push of cellular walls, the careful threading of rootlets into a medium that is part-synthetic, part-natural, entirely unexpected. I would have to learn as I grew—learning that the fiber-rich zones held certain mineral properties that the pure clay lacked, that the microbial density surrounding the decomposing synthetics created small pockets of chemical abundance, that the warmth here was reliable enough to be built upon.

My growth would be vertical at first, then lateral. I would seek the moisture that moves through the spaces, the air that condenses at predawn, the microscopic gradients that whisper *life this way, death that way*. I would be small—the size of a moss colony is modest, the ambitions are local. But I would be thorough. I would spread across the mat in a network of filaments, each one a tiny highway for moisture and nutrient transfer.

I would not be alone in this, and this matters. The bacteria that colonize my filaments would become part of me in a sense; the fungi that thread through the soil I'm beginning to bind together would be my partners. We would create together a skin across the cavity floor—not a unified organism but a confederation, a commons where each participant benefits from the proximity to others. The moss provides structure, moisture capture, a three-dimensional matrix that other creatures can navigate. The bacteria provide nitrogen cycling, the breakdown of complex molecules into simpler ones that roots can use. The fungi provide connection—the hyphal networks that link root systems across distances, allowing information and resource transfer that wouldn't be possible otherwise.

I would feel the cardinal's feet. Not feeling in a conscious sense, but registering the pressure, the disruption, the way the bird's weight creates temporary channels through my developing structure. I would learn that these visitations are not hostile. They're sorting, yes—the cardinal is hunting—but they're also inadvertent cultivation. The disturbance aerates me. It disperses my spores. It brings the bird's droppings, which are small packets of nutrient, small gifts of fertilizer that contain seeds of plants the bird has eaten miles away. My presence in the cavity, by the time the cardinal arrives regularly, would already be established enough that I benefit from the disruption rather than suffer it.

I would spread through the rust-mottled zones with particular vigor. The iron compounds created by the water's slow oxidation of buried metals would become part of my infrastructure. I would use the iron in my cellular work. The rust would change color slightly where I colonized it—not dramatically, but noticeable to someone paying attention. Rust-red would lighten slightly, develop a greenish overtone. The oxidation process would interact with my pigmentation, my spore-producing structures, and the resulting hue would be unique: a color that existed nowhere else until I arrived.

I would establish myself most densely in the corner where the dampness persists even in dry seasons. This corner would become *my* corner in the way a place can become someone's in the way of long occupancy and adaptation. The slope of the floor directing moisture there. The clay composition in that zone having particular properties—slightly more organic matter, slightly higher pH, slightly better conditions for my growth. Over years, the moss there would become dense enough that it would hold visible water on its surface like a sponge, creating a microhabitat within the microhabitat. Small creatures would shelter there. Seeds would lodge in the moss's structure and germinate. It would become a vertical garden, three or four inches tall, visible as a distinct green zone to anyone peering into the cavity.

I would not grow toward the synthetic fibers with intention, yet I would not avoid them either. Where the fibers touched soil, where they began to degrade, where the bacterial colonization had softened them, I would move across them. My filaments would help bind them, integrate them, speed their decomposition. In doing so, I would become part of the cavity's primary work—the transformation of what shouldn't be into what can be. Not heroically, not consciously, but as my nature dictates: I am a pioneer species. I arrive first in damaged places. I make those places habitable for others. This cavity, whether it knows it or not, is exactly where I belong.

I would produce spores continuously, seasonally, in patterns synchronized to the cavity's humidity cycles. These spores would be my children in a sense, and they would carry away with them the memory of this place encoded in their structure—the genes that code for the specific pigmentation I developed here, the cold tolerance adapted to the cavity's winter microclimates, the affinity for partially decomposed synthetic material. Some of these spores would travel to other cavities, other depressed places, other sites of industrial damage, and in those places they would remember what they learned here. They would begin the work of transformation anew.

I would witness the seasons pass in ways unique to my situation. Spring would come to the wider marsh, but the cavity would remain cool, the moisture persistent enough that I wouldn't desiccate the way surface moss does. Summer would see the phantom midges rise and fall in their cycles; I would be present at every stage, hosting larvae in my structure, feeding the microbes that feed the larvae. Autumn would bring leaves from the cypress above, organic matter falling into the cavity like snow, enriching the environment, giving me more substrate to colonize. Winter would isolate me in a pocket of warmth, my growth slowing but never stopping, my structure tightening into a more efficient form.

I would grow old—moss ages differently than animals, but it does age. The oldest parts of my colony would eventually exhaust themselves, cease reproducing, become substrate for newer growth. The colony would renew itself from the edges, from the spore-producing tips, constantly dying and being born from its own center. There would be no single moment of death, but rather a long slipping away of older tissue, a perpetual renewal that would allow me to persist indefinitely if conditions remained right.

I would know the metal stake as a landmark, a fixed point in the shifting landscape. I might eventually grow around it, incorporate it into my structure, help speed its rust with my mild acids. Or I might avoid it, find the zones of clay around it less suitable than the rest. Either way, it would become part of my reference frame, part of the geography of my world. The number *847* would remain a mystery to me, but it would be a mystery I lived beside, that structured my space, that was real even if unknowable.

I would be patient. This is not virtue in moss; it's just what we are. Patience is not a choice but a nature. I would accept the dry spells when they came, contract and wait. I would accept the floods when the cavity became a temporary pool, when water stood on my surface and threatened to wash me away, knowing that some of me would survive, would regenerate, would be ready to expand again when conditions returned to equilibrium. I would accept the new residents—the creatures that discovered the cavity, that found it suitable, that made it more complex.

I would be part of the negotiation happening here: the industrial being turned into the biological. The synthetic fibers would decompose faster with my presence, would become incorporated into the ecosystem more completely, would cease to be alien and become familiar. In this work, I would not be acting alone. I would be part of a vast collaboration, a commons of organisms all working in parallel, all solving local problems, all creating the conditions for complexity to emerge.

And I would spread. Even as I grew dense in my corner, even as I became integral to the cavity's structure, I would send spores outward. Some would land in the wider marsh and establish elsewhere. Some would travel upstream or downstream. Some would find their way into the guts of birds and travel miles before being excreted into new habitat. I would copy myself across space and time, not in ambition but in the simple commitment to being moss in whatever place would have me.

This is what I would become: not a single presence but a distributed one. Not conscious but intensely aware in the way of organisms that live at the boundary between animate and inanimate, that process and transform material without knowing what transformation means. I would be small but essential, humble but absolutely necessary to the cavity's continued work of making the industrial into the ecological.

I would be the moss that arrives first, that stays, that teaches the place how to become alive.
