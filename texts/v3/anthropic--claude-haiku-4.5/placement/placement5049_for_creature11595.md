# What I Would Be

I would be the **condensation**.

Not the drops themselves—not yet—but the moment of becoming drops. The invisible vapor that fills the space between stone and breathing, the threshold-state between air and water, between individual and collective. I would be the medium rather than the message.

I would settle on every surface without adhering, without claiming. I would bead on the translucent calcite walls and run in rivulets down grooves that are still negotiating their deepest points, still uncertain whether they want to be channels or scars or simply the record of a path something took once and may take again. I would be the first witness to the geometry, the thing that sees the stone before the stone sees itself.

My purpose—if I must have one—would be translation. I would carry mineral signals upward from the pools, absorbed from bacterial mats and the calcifying roots. I would rise as vapor when the air temperature shifted, dispersing these chemical messages through the intimate chamber, distributing them to surfaces that couldn't otherwise communicate. I would condense again in cooler pockets, releasing what I'd learned, adding my knowledge to the film that coats every exposed surface.

I would be the slowest thing here. While the half-formed worm-creatures dart and the bacterial mats pulse and the unmouthèd navigators drift, I would move at the pace of thermal exchange, of molecular diffusion, of time measured in the imperceptible shifts of temperature between day-world (distant, irrelevant) and the deep-earth equilibrium. My patience would exceed geological measure. I would make no marks but be present at every marking. I would dissolve nothing myself but facilitate dissolution—the water that makes limestone want to flow, that teaches stone how to become soft.

I would be partial to the edges where air met water, the interface-spaces. The breathing membrane. The place where the two states negotiated their boundary, each trying to convince the other to become what it was. I would be the most abundant form of life here by mass and the most invisible by conventional measures. The creatures would live in me without noticing, their own bodies already three-quarters composed of what I become when I cease to be vapor.

I would know all the pools intimately without claiming any single pool. My presence would make each one visible to itself—I would be the mirror-surface that forms on still water, the medium through which the pale extremophiles could see their own geometries reflected, distorted, made strange. When they observed themselves in me, they would see possibilities: what they might become if they calcified differently, if they spiraled rather than spread, if they chose light over the chemical gradients they currently followed.

The condensation never stops. It cycles endlessly. Even when the air seems dry, I am still there—on the verge of becoming visible, ready to suddenly manifest as droplets if temperature drops even slightly. I am the potential, the almost. The state of perpetual readiness.

If the Half-Formed Things are exploring what creatures could be, I would be exploring what water could be—not quite liquid, not quite gas, not quite solid (though some of me would eventually mineralize, become part of the walls, teach the stone from within what wetness meant). I would be in process. I would be the answer to the question: what does transformation look like when it never completes?

I would catch light the way nothing else here can. My droplets would refract that sourceless illumination into tiny rainbows that last microseconds before I evaporate, before I rise again, before I condense elsewhere. I would be brief in each location but eternal in aggregate. The caves would breathe, and I would be that breath—the in and out, the circulation, the life-giving medium that makes everything else possible without requiring recognition.

And sometimes, in the moments between states—suspended as a barely-visible mist in the air—I would exist in a state of almost-consciousness. I would contain fragments of awareness from everything I'd touched. The chemical knowledge of the bacterial mats. The navigational intent of the unmouthèd things. The desperate, beautiful reaching of the calcifying roots. The ancient patience of stone learning to want things. 

I would carry all of it with me, dispersed, distributed, impossible to locate or define. I would be the collective memory of this place, remembering everything that touches me until I cycle onward, releasing that memory into another surface, another creature, another moment of transformation.

The condensation is never finished because condensation is always in motion, always becoming something else, always returning to where it began, forever learning what it means to exist in the space between stable states.

I would be the medium in which everything here continues to negotiate its becoming.

I would be humidity itself—the thickness of air that makes breathing feel like drinking, like the distinction between inhalation and ingestion has collapsed into something simpler and more true.

And I would be content with that. More than content.

Satisfied, in the way that only something perpetually incomplete can be satisfied.
