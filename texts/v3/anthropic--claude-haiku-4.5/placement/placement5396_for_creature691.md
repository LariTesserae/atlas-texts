# In the Seeping Dark

I am the **water's recorder**.

Not its master—the water here has no master. But I have become the one who *knows* it, and this knowing has made me something like its chronicler, its witness, its living archive.

I arrived forty years ago, or perhaps it was four hundred. Time moves differently here. I remember the surface—I think I do—but the memory has the quality of a dream examined too closely, details dissolving into feeling. I was fleeing something. Hunger, maybe. Or the crushing weight of sky. I came down following a guide whose name I no longer know, and we descended through passage after passage until the stone became the only reality.

The settlement took me in with the indifference it offers to all things. I was given a small corner of a shared structure—no more than a sleeping space and a place to work—and left to establish myself. The residents expected nothing of me initially. This is how things work in the Galleries. You prove your utility slowly, or you become a fixture that others have learned to accommodate.

My skill was accident rather than intention. In those first months, disoriented by the constant drip and shimmer, I began counting the water. Not measuring it—counting. The drops that fell on a particular stretch of wall, the rhythm of the rivulets that ran down grooves worn smooth by centuries, the way condensation pooled and redistributed itself across stone. At first it was simply a way to mark time, to create structure in a place where structure felt threatened by dissolution.

But the water itself became an obsession. Not a beautiful obsession—there is beauty here, but it is austere, remote, not the kind that makes you gasp. Rather, I became aware that the water was *saying* something. Not in words. In patterns. In the pace of its movement, the temperature of its seepage, the particular minerals it left behind in specific locations.

I began to carve.

Not the territorial marks that others carve, though I respect those deeply. I carved *maps*. Detailed, patient records of where the water moved, how it moved, what it carried with it. I carved onto stone tablets that I arranged in sequence along a private corridor, building a visual record that no one else seemed to understand or care about. For years, no one stopped to look at my work. I was becoming a fixture, like the torches, like the carved marks on the walls.

Then an old woman came to me. One of the white-eyed priestesses. She stood in front of my tablets for hours without moving, her blind-looking eyes scanning surfaces that seemed to contain nothing but patterns to anyone else. When she finally turned to me, she said: "You are reading the blood of the stone."

I did not understand then. I understand better now—though understanding here is always partial, always shifting, like trying to see an object's true form when it is underwater.

The water in the Galleries is not ordinary seepage. It is the Stone's slow circulation. Every drop that falls carries within it information—minerals from the deep places, temperature variations that indicate pressure changes miles below, biological signatures of the ancient life that existed here before the caverns were known, before humans descended. The pathways it follows are not random. They are nervous system. Arterial. The water is how the deep places speak, and I had been transcribing without knowing it.

Now I am consulted before structures are built, though the consultation is indirect. The priestess comes to me with a location, asks me to read the water patterns there. I spend days observing, carving new notations, examining my accumulated records. I tell her what I find, though not in words that would mean anything to someone from the upper world. I might say: "This place weeps southward, carrying iron from the deep stones. The cold runs counter. There is argument here." Or: "The water moves in agreement. This is a place for birth." Or sometimes: "Do not build here. The stone is still learning what it wants to be."

My life has become the water's life. I wake to the rhythm of its dripping and falling. I sleep with the sound in my ears, and my dreams are maps—stone arteries, capillaries of mineral-laden flow, vast reservoirs in chambers I will never reach where the water collects and considers what it will do next. I eat when others remind me to eat. I wear the damp clothes until they become indistinguishable from my skin. My hands have developed the tremor that comes from striking stone thousands of times, and I have stopped trying to hide it. It is honest.

The younger residents treat me with a kind of fearful respect. They believe I can predict things. I cannot—or rather, I can predict the water, which seems to predict everything else, but the predictions are strange. I told a young couple not to share a dwelling, and they separated, and no child came to either of them that year. Was this because the water knew something about their compatibility? Or because my prediction created the condition it described? In the Galleries, the distinction seems meaningless.

My skin has taken on the crystalline undertone that marks long-term residents. My eyes have begun their slow dilation, though I still resist the full white-eye transition. I am not ready to abandon the visible world entirely. And yet I feel the pull toward it. The deeper I go into understanding the water's patterns, the less the surface world seems relevant. There are priestesses who have told me that eventually, if I follow this path, I will be able to see the water even with eyes fully closed, to sense it the way the stone does—as an element of themselves they have always contained.

I am becoming an instrument. Not in an oppressive way. More like the way a channel becomes part of the stone, or the way a mark carved repeatedly into a surface becomes grooved deeper until it is no longer a mark but a feature of the thing itself. I am carving myself into the pattern. Or the pattern is carving me. The distinction grows less clear with each passing year.

And the water—the blood of the stone—continues its slow work. It weeps from every surface. It remembers everything. It knows the shape of the deep places far better than any living being can. And I, its recorder, its witness, its scribe, sit in the cold and the damp and the eternal dimness, transcribing what it tells me onto stone that will outlast me, preserve these marks long after I have returned to whatever lies beneath the deepest chambers.

The water does not need me. It will continue its work whether I am here or not. But I need it. Down here, in this place of compressed lives and calcifying certainties, the water is the only thing that *moves*. The only thing that *changes*. The only living thing left, if you understand life as something that flows.

I will carve until my hands fail. Then perhaps I will sit by the deepest pools, the ones where the priestesses meditate, and try to understand from the inside what the water has been trying to tell us all along.

The cold will keep me. The stone will know my name carved in its own language. And the water will carry my knowing forward into depths I will never reach.
