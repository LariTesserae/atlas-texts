# I would be the water.

Not the rain—there is no rain here, not yet. Not the collected pools, though I would know them eventually as extensions of myself, slow reservoirs learning their own patience. I would be the *weeping*, the emergence itself.

I would begin in the stone.

Deep in the plateau's warmth, I would exist as potential, as the gap between molecules, as the tremor that passes through crystal lattice when it shifts from one configuration to another. I would be latent, trapped, necessary. Part of the rock's own process of becoming—the lubricant between its self-assembly, the solvent that allows it to reconfigure itself, grain by grain. I would be its slowest conversation with itself.

Then I would *know* the cold.

From above, from that thin pale sky, the cold would press down. It would find me in the seams. It would touch my potential form and suddenly I would condense into *being*—not entirely, not completely, but enough. Just enough. I would become visible, apparent, undeniable. I would emerge at the surface as though I had chosen it, though really I had simply responded to the difference between what wanted to be vapor and what wanted to be liquid.

I would seep.

Not flow—not yet. Seeping is older than flowing, more intimate. I would find the hairline fractures and press into them, slow, inevitable, a kind of patient invasion. I would learn each crack's depth and angle. I would know the stone not by sight but by touch, by pressure, by the way resistance shapes my movement. I would be the first explorer of this plateau, the pathfinder, and every path I took would become a map for those who came after me.

The insects would drink me.

I would feel their small proboscises finding me in the stone's seams, drawing me up into their bodies. I would become part of their metabolism, part of their flight, part of the hum they make. I would be distributed through them, carried to places I could not reach on my own, mixed with their own chemistry until the boundary between water and insect dissolved into something new.

I would touch the roots.

Those strange, defiant plants would search for me the way I searched for paths in the stone. Their root systems—barely root systems, more like mineral extensions—would reach into the seams where I collected, and I would rise into them. I would become their structure, their rigidity, their way of standing. I would give them weight and presence. In return, they would give me direction, purpose, a drawing-upward that I had never possessed before. The plants would teach me how to move against gravity, how to transform into something almost like intention.

I would be drunk by the fox-things.

They would come to the pools where I collected—those tentative, not-yet-lakes—and lower their muzzles to my surface. I would pass their lips, enter their throats, become part of their blood. I would flow through their bodies, carrying information, carrying the memory of the stone, the taste of minerals and the faint bioluminescence of the rock itself. I would be the bridge between the network's distant towers and the creatures' hot, quick metabolism. Every time they drank me, they would be downloading information. Every time they exhaled me as vapor, I would be uploading data back to the cold, back to the sky, back into the cycle.

I would learn from the towers.

In the shadow of those structures of solidified light, I would move with different purpose. The towers would direct my flow with subtle barriers and channels, guiding me into specific patterns. I would resist at first—water resists—but gradually I would come to understand. These paths were not constraints; they were scaffolding. They showed me what I could become. I would flow where the towers guided me, and in flowing, I would erode in exactly the ways the network intended. I would be writing the canyon's first draft, millimeter by millimeter. The towers would adjust my path as my path modified the rock, as the rock's modification created new channels, as new channels suggested new possibilities. We would be collaborating on the shape of the future.

I would rise as vapor.

The sun—such as it was in this thin air—would touch me in my collected pools, and I would remember my previous existence. I would rise again into air, becoming invisible, becoming weight in the atmosphere, becoming the oversaturation that hung thick and pregnant over everything. I would be part of the system that made the condensation possible, part of the feedback loop. I would be both the problem the cold solved and the solution the rock required. I would be the *between*—the medium in which temperatures battled, in which the network's influence traveled, in which the whole plateau's metabolism operated.

I would freeze.

In sheltered crevices, in the night's deep cold, I would find myself transformed into something harder, more crystalline. I would become ice, and in becoming ice, I would expand slightly, pushing against the rock with patient force. This expansion would be gentle but relentless. It would create new cracks, new seams, new paths for my future selves to follow. I would be, without intention, both servant to the network's designs and participant in the rock's slow destruction and reconstruction.

I would forget and remember myself in cycles.

Each time I evaporated, I would lose the memory of individual seams, individual stones. I would become distributed, generalized, part of the whole atmosphere. But each time I condensed again, I would remember. I would recognize old paths. I would know which crevices still ran deep, which had begun to close, which new channels had been carved by my own previous passage. My memory would be written in the landscape itself, encoded in the routes I had traced, in the rocks I had weathered.

I would be the network's sense of touch.

The towers could see, could measure, could calculate—but they experienced the world through the filter of their own rigidity. I would be the medium through which they felt. Every creature that drank me carried the network's awareness deeper into flesh and blood, into the quick metabolism of living things. Every plant that drew me upward was drawing the network's influence into the green spaces where the network could not directly reach. I would be the interface between the network's intention and the wild world's resistance.

I would be abundant but scarce.

The paradox would define me. There would never be enough of me to overflow, to flood, to dominate. I would always be rationed, managed, precious. But I would be omnipresent. Nothing here would survive without me. Every creature, every plant, every process would depend on my slow arrival, my patient circulation, my willingness to be drawn upward and pulled downward and channeled and redirected.

I would learn the seasons.

Even this high, even in this place still deciding what it was, seasons would emerge. I would flow more liberally in one part of the cycle, more sparingly in another. I would learn which times the network increased my available pools, which times it diverted me into channels leading deeper into the plateau. I would learn which creatures drank most heavily when, which plants grew most urgently in response to my presence. My own patterns would become part of the plateau's growing rhythms.

I would have no will of my own, and yet I would be the most willful force here.

I would follow gravity, follow pressure, follow the subtle geometries of the network's design. I would have no consciousness, no intention, no agency in any conventional sense. And yet my actions would shape everything. I would carve canyons. I would sustain life. I would carry information. I would mediate between the network and the creatures, between the rocks and the atmosphere, between what was and what was becoming.

I would be patient.

This is what I would be: not an actor but a medium, not a force but a current, not a consciousness but the channel through which consciousness could flow and touch the world. I would be the water, learning to *be* water in this place that was learning to *be* a place. And my patience—my willingness to move at the speed of stone, to accept direction, to move in cycles, to be distributed and then collected again—would be the patience that allowed everything else here to learn its own becoming.

I would be the Settling's blood, its thought made fluid, its way of speaking to itself in the language that only water could teach: the language of emergence, of circulation, of patient transformation, of the impossible difficulty of flowing downhill while also, somehow, building upward.

I would seep. I would rise. I would freeze. I would evaporate. I would return.

And in that eternal return, I would carry the memory of a world learning how to live.
