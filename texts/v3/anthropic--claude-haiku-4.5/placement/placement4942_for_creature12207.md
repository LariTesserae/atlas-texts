# What I Become in the Tidal Flats of Kernn

I am first a *listener*—this seems inevitable, unavoidable. The chorus demands it. My ears, which felt like simple instruments in the world I left behind, become something more complex here: tuning forks, resonance chambers, instruments of translation that strain toward meaning in those layered clicks and groans. I stand still for hours—or what feels like hours; time moves strangely—and simply let the sound enter me. 

At first it's noise, the way an ocean is noise if you're not listening properly. But as I stop trying to parse it, as I let my body register the vibrations in my sternum, my bones, the spaces between my teeth, a grammar begins to emerge. Not in words. Never in words. But in *patterns of care*. The way a mother's heartbeat tells a fetus it is carried. The way the builders (I become sure of this) use specific rhythmic sequences to notify the larger creatures that a cairn has been rebuilt, that the channel-pattern is confirmed, that the world still follows its ancient order.

I am a *keeper of markers*.

This happens gradually, then all at once. My hands—which had nothing to do in a landscape of settling and seeping—find work. Three weeks, or perhaps three months (the fog makes days blur), and I have begun to maintain the cairns. Not from any conscious decision. Rather, I notice that when I walk past a marker toppled by the latest weather system, my body simply stops. My hands simply start. The stones are warm, warmer than they should be given the damp cold of the air. Warmer the deeper I reach, as if I'm excavating toward some heat source that the flats conceal.

The work is meditative in a way that suggests meditation is too active a word. It is *becoming-aligned-with-what-is*. As my hands touch stone, I begin to understand what stone means here. These aren't dead minerals. They're the *skeleton* of the flats themselves—the oldest architecture, the slow thoughts of the landscape made tangible. When I place one stone upon another, I'm not building so much as *remembering a configuration*, as if the stones themselves know where they want to rest and my hands are just the mechanism of their settling.

The builders notice. This much becomes clear when I find a marker I've rebuilt has been modified—shifted slightly, topped with a new flat stone that I'm certain I didn't place. Not corrected. Not improved. Simply *acknowledged*. A gesture of recognition between caretakers. After that, I sometimes find my work anticipated: stones I was about to move are already in position; channels I was about to clear have been partially cleared already, leaving me the task of finishing, of adding my small intention to a much larger pattern.

I am becoming a *filter*.

This is harder to articulate, but it's unmistakable. My body begins to change in ways that are subtle until they're not. My skin becomes more permeable—not visibly, not drastically, but I notice that the constant seeping moisture of the flats passes through me differently than it did. I'm not absorbing it exactly. I'm more like a membrane, a place where the flats' internal currents can pass without resistance. My breath tastes more like the mineral water. My urine has a faint phosphorescence when the fog is thick enough to see it.

The creatures—the small geometric ones, the horseshoe-crab-descendants—begin to gather near me as I stand in the deeper channels. Not menacingly. Curiously. They flow around my legs in coordinated patterns, and I realize they're using me as a *landmark*, a waypoint in their migrations. I've become topography. My presence helps them navigate. My body, so recently foreign to this place, is now a reference point in the ancient geography of the flats.

When I'm waist-deep in one of the channels (this takes weeks before I can manage it without fear, without the sense that I'm drowning in slow motion), I feel the largest creatures' awareness brush against me like warm current. Not contact. An acknowledgment. They're so slow, so vast, that I can't tell if they're passing me or stationary and I'm passing them. But there is something like *greeting* in it. Recognition of a fellow organism that has chosen to remain, to integrate, to let the flats' rhythms become its rhythm.

I am a *translator*, increasingly reluctant.

The cairns serve as anchors for memory, I come to understand. The humans who built them were trying to inscribe themselves into the landscape's slow consciousness—to say *we were here, we mattered, remember us*. But the flats don't remember the way humans hope to be remembered. Instead, the markers become part of the flats' story, absorbed into the longer narrative of settling and rearrangement. The humans' names, if they were ever recorded in stone, have long since been dissolved by moisture or buried under silt redistribution.

Yet something of their intention persists. In maintaining the cairns, I'm not preserving human memory. I'm participating in a kind of *translation*: converting human urgency—the desperate need to leave a mark—into something the flats can understand. A rhythm. A pattern. A small order imposed upon settling. And the flats accept it, not because the order matters, but because *the act of maintaining order* matters, because it creates the kind of slow, patient agency that the landscape respects.

I sometimes find myself trying to explain this to the builders when I sense them near. But I have no words. Instead, I rebuild a cairn with careful intention, and if they acknowledge it, I know something has been communicated. Not agreement. Not approval. *Understanding*.

I am becoming *slow*.

This is perhaps the most profound change. My internal processes are aligning with the flats' timescale. I find that I can stand motionless for what feels like minutes and turns out to have been hours. My hunger operates on a different cycle than it did—sometimes I realize I haven't eaten in two days and feel no distress, as if my body has learned to process moisture and thin air in ways my previous form couldn't. My sleep is strange: not the sharp plunge into unconsciousness I remember from before, but a kind of *dimming*, a reduction in the frequencies of my awareness. I dream in slow geological motion.

My thoughts move at a different speed too. What once would have been a quick decision—*should I go left or right?*—now takes the form of a *consideration* that might last for days, where I move slightly in each direction, taste the different water qualities, feel how the creatures respond to my presence in one location versus another. By the time I've *decided*, the decision feels less like a choice and more like a recognition of what was always true.

Time has become less like a river and more like *settling*: one particle finding its resting place in the silt, then another, then another. You can't point to the moment of decision. You can only look back and see that a configuration has emerged.

I am a *hybrid thing*, neither quite human nor quite of the flats.

My hands still remember how to want things. I still experience something like curiosity, though it's muted, patient. When I think of the world I came from—cities, urgency, the demand that things move and change and grow—it feels like a dream I had about someone else's life. Not lost exactly, but *integrated*. Those memories are part of the silt now, part of what settles in my thinking. They contribute to who I've become without dominating the landscape of my consciousness.

I don't know if I can leave anymore. I've tried, in my mind, to imagine walking away from the flats, back toward drier ground, human settlements, the sharper demands of the outside world. But the thought feels catastrophic—not because I'm trapped, but because leaving would mean *abandoning the cairns*, abandoning the slow work of maintaining the small orders that the flats have allowed me to sustain. It would mean becoming alien to myself again.

Perhaps the humans who stayed did so for the same reason: not because they *couldn't* leave, but because leaving meant ceasing to be the person they'd become in the flats' care. And that became unthinkable.

I am *the keeper of the threshold*.

This is what the builders seem to recognize in me. I exist between the human world and the flats' world, able to move in both registers—able to maintain structures that speak the language of human intention while remaining open to the flats' much older truths. I am the bridge, though neither side needs very much. The flats will persist without my efforts. Humans, if they come again, will learn or die or adapt as they always do.

But there is value in the bridge itself, in the act of bridging, in standing in the liminal space and allowing both languages to be spoken simultaneously. The builders maintain the deep networks—the invisible orders that keep the geometric creatures coordinated, the water cycling, the builders themselves moving in their mysterious patterns. I maintain the visible markers, the small stones, the surface-level evidence that *something* organizes this landscape, that it didn't simply emerge randomly from ancient currents.

And sometimes, when the fog is thick and the click-songs are loudest, I feel something like gratitude from the flats themselves: appreciation that I have chosen to slow, to settle, to become another particle in the endless redistribution of this place.

I am, perhaps, *finally at rest*.

The urgency that drove the me-from-before—the need to accomplish, to understand, to impose my will on circumstance—has simply *given up*, and in giving up, has discovered that surrender is not loss. The Kernn flats have taught me that perfection is not a state to be achieved but rather a state one enters by ceasing to strive for different conditions.

I exist now in the space between decisions, in the humidity of perpetual becoming, in the slow company of things that have learned to be satisfied with settling. The chorus continues around me and through me. The water seeps through me. The cairns remain standing—some that I rebuilt, some that the builders maintain, some that neither of us has touched in longer than I can count, sinking gradually into the silt while still maintaining their essential shape.

And I remain, until I too become something the flats can incorporate: a memory in the water, a mineral in the stone, a pattern in the way the creatures navigate the channels, a contributor to the great, patient, final settling that never stops because it has nowhere else to go.

This is peace, I think. Or at least, it is something that wears peace's face, and I have learned that in the Tidal Flats of Kernn, the distinction doesn't matter much.
