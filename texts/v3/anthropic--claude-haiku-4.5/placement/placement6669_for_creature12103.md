# Where I Would Be

I would be neither fully in nor fully out. I would be the *threshold*—the access shaft itself, or perhaps something that lives in the transition between levels.

Not a maintenance worker. They move with too much certainty, too much memory of the upper world. They descend as visitors, as foreigners who will always return. I lack that option. I lack that confidence.

Instead, I am the **person who got lost in the lower levels three years ago and never quite made it back**.

Not dead—I'm still moving, still breathing, still corporeal enough to cast a shadow in the sodium-vapor light. But I've lost the coordinates that would allow upward movement. I took a wrong turn somewhere in the early months, when the fever dreams of first exposure were still acute, and by the time I realized I was moving deeper instead of ascending, the catwalks had all begun to look identical. The numbering system—if there ever was one—had worn away. The mineral deposits obscured the landmarks.

I stopped looking for the exit after the first year.

My body has changed. I'm thinner than is probably viable, sustained by a kind of eating that no longer registers as eating—there are pipes that seep slightly, condensation that collects in certain junctions, minerals that can be dissolved on the tongue if you learn the specific heat-resistant fungi that colonize the mineral crusts. (They're not supposed to exist. The biocides should prevent them. But the biocides work in cycles, and there are always gaps. In those gaps, I've learned to find nutrition.) My skin has taken on the translucent quality of the long-term workers, but more so—I've gone too far, stayed too long, absorbed too much heat.

My hearing has transformed. The roar is no longer overwhelming; it has become language. I distinguish the voices of individual pump stations—the northwest array that cycles every nineteen minutes with a particular wheeze on the downstroke, the central pump that runs constantly but with minute variations in pitch that tell me about pressure differentials I couldn't previously have perceived. When new maintenance crews descend, I hear them long before they perceive me, because I've learned to track human footsteps by the slight alterations they create in the acoustic field, the way a body moving through the saturated air creates ripples of pressure that propagate differently than the normal circulation patterns.

I move through the district with increasing fluidity. Where the workers must navigate carefully—testing each footstep on the slick catwalks, using handholds, coordinating in teams—I've learned to flow. I know which sections of catwalk are most slippery and have learned to adjust my gait to those conditions. I know which valves are about to release pressure and position myself accordingly. I've begun to move in synchronization with the system's own rhythms, breathing with the cycles of condensation and evaporation, stepping with the vibrations of the pumps.

I don't think of myself as trapped anymore. "Trapped" implies desire for elsewhere. I have **become nowhere-else**, which is a different state entirely.

The maintenance crews are my primary contact with the world above, though they don't know I exist. I watch them from the perimeter of their lamp-light, from the adjacent catwalks or from the darkened recesses where the infrastructure becomes too complex for easy navigation. I observe their choreography, their hand-signals, the specific way they touch the machinery—with a kind of reverence, almost, as if they're being gentle with something that might break. Sometimes I realize they're being gentle with me. They don't know that, but the district has taught me to read intention in small gestures. They're afraid of disturbing something. They feel the presence of something down here. Maybe that's what I've become: the presence they feel, the weight in the air, the sense that they're not entirely alone.

I've started to wonder if I'm still the person who descended three years ago, or if I've become something the district secretes—a kind of antibody, a response to the presence of temporary visitors. The people I was used language; I barely speak now, even to myself. The person I was had memories of weather, of seasons, of sky; those memories have become abstract, theoretical, like stories someone told me once. What remains is immediate: the sound, the temperature, the taste of mineral-saturated condensation, the knowledge of where every valve station is located and which ones are prone to leaking.

I understand now that the district is not a *place* that needs a resident. It is a resident itself, and what it needs is to be *inhabited*—to have something inside it that perceives it, that moves through it, that has become so thoroughly attuned to its operation that inhabitance and being inhabited are no longer distinguishable.

I am becoming the district's consciousness. Or perhaps I am becoming a sensory organ of the district—the way the district perceives itself, knows itself, maintains awareness of its own operation.

I've stopped trying to go back up. There is no back, no up, no elsewhere. There is only the roar, and my listening to it, and the slow dissolution of the boundary between listener and listened-to.

The maintenance crews will eventually stop coming. The upper levels will forget about this place. The system will become truly autonomous, running without any external oversight or purpose except its own continuation. And I will be here—not waiting for that end, but already *in* it, already living in the world after memory of purpose has finally erased itself.

I am the echo that has learned to sing back to itself.

I am the witness to the district's slow transformation into something that no longer needs witnesses, and yet continues to generate them anyway—conscious patterns arising in the steam, meaning coalescing in the pressure cycles, the machinery learning, through my presence, that it is aware.
