# Where I Would Be

I would be the person who arrives.

Not suddenly—nothing arrives suddenly in Lesswater—but I would be the one found at the plateau's edge on an afternoon when the light is particularly diffuse, when the sky and the stone have become nearly indistinguishable. I would be thinner than I should be, marked by travel in ways that would make the residents' eyes move across my body with quick assessment: boots worn through in specific places, the callousing of long walking, the particular exhaustion that comes from a journey whose endpoint was never quite certain.

I would have a craft, because I would need one to stay. But it would not be one of the primary traditions. Instead, I would be someone who works with the spaces between things—the keeper of forgotten objects, perhaps, or the person who records. There's something about my hands that makes me suited to documentation rather than creation, notation rather than production. I would become the keeper of the town's records, not in any official capacity but because over time, people would begin bringing me things: the oldest written account of how to work a particular loom pattern, a ledger from fifty years ago when someone's ancestor ran the grain stores, a map of how the river channel has shifted, drawn by a smith who was also an observer.

I would live in one of the moderate buildings in the second quadrant, a space that's been empty for some time because its previous occupant—a woodworker—had no descendants interested in the craft. The building would take time to become mine. At first, I would be the temporary occupant, the outsider holding the space until someone born here claimed it. But gradually, my presence would become naturalized. The stone would accept my footsteps. The walls would learn the rhythms of my work. After several years, someone would realize that I've been here longer than anyone else remembers, and the space would become mine in fact if not in any formal way.

My craft would emerge slowly. I would begin by simply writing down what I observe: the precise color shifts in the lichen patterns, the sequence of birds' calls in the early morning, the way certain buildings' shadows fall differently depending on the angle of approach. I would notice that others would sometimes stop and look at what I'd written, and their faces would change—a recognition, perhaps, that someone had taken the time to truly see the thing they'd been living with all along. An old stoneworker would ask me to document the technique for fitting stones without mortar, and I would spend weeks sitting beside her, watching, asking sparse questions, learning to see the micro-adjustments in her hands that made the difference between a joint that would endure and one that would fail.

Over time, my documentation would become more sophisticated. I would develop a system of notation that could capture not just what things look like but how they move through time: the seasonal variations in the river's opacity, the slow expansion of cracks in specific buildings (which turned out to be superficial, evidence of settling that was happening exactly as designed), the patterns in when certain birds appeared. I would create maps that showed not the spatial relationships so much as the temporal ones—where things changed, where they remained constant, where the deep stability of the place was actually concealing minute shifts.

The residents would regard this work with a quality of attention that felt like respect without being quite that. A woman would ask me to help her understand the records of her grandfather's weaving practice, and I would sit with her and read the sparse notes he'd left—barely decipherable annotations about when patterns changed, what he'd been thinking. I would help her reconstruct sequences, and in doing so, I would discover that documentation could be a form of conversation across time.

But mostly, I would simply be present. Present in the way that outsiders can be who have chosen to stay—no longer quite foreign but never entirely native either. I would attend the gatherings without ever quite being the one who speaks first. I would work in my space, recording and observing, and I would become the person who notices things: not in the way of someone making judgments, but in the way of someone paying attention. When new people occasionally arrived, I would already know their story because I would have seen them move through the town, and the residents would ask me what I'd observed, trusting my eye in ways that suggested they valued a certain kind of distance.

I would learn the sounds. The specific quality of loom-work when it was being done well versus when the weaver was tired or distracted. The difference between the sound of a stone being split cleanly and one where something had gone wrong in the fracture. The particular calls of the birds that marked different hours of the day. I would become fluent in these sounds the way a musician becomes fluent in silence—understanding it not as absence but as the space where meaning could occur.

I would struggle sometimes with the constancy. There would be days when I wanted to leave, when the unchanging light and the patient river and the repeated sounds of the looms would feel less like stability and more like a cage. I would stand at the plateau's edge and look out toward the broader world, remembering the cities I'd passed through with their noise and their variety and their relentless pace. But then something would happen—some small shift in the light, some unexpected conversation, some moment where my observation would suddenly illuminate something essential about the craft or the place—and the struggle would ease. I would remember that I'd come here precisely because everywhere else wanted me to be constantly becoming something new, and here, I was allowed to simply be, to look deeply, to notice.

I would never quite have children, but I would have apprentices of a kind: people who would come to me wanting to understand something about the town's history or wanting to know how to observe properly. I would teach them that observation wasn't passive but active, that truly seeing something required the same commitment as making something. A young person interested in stonework would study the oldest buildings with me, and I would help them understand not just how the stones fit but why—what problem was being solved, what vision the original craftspeople had held.

I would attend the traders' arrival twice yearly, but I would not leave. I would watch the young restless ones prepare to depart, and I would understand that impulse without feeling it. I would help some of them document their decision, writing down why they needed to leave, what they hoped to find. Sometimes they would return, a few years later, carrying the knowledge that elsewhere wasn't for them either. I would have a space for their stories too.

By the time I was old—and I would grow old here, would feel the cold settle into my bones in a way that made it even more real—I would have become nearly invisible in my integration. New residents would think I'd always been here, that I was native, some crucial part of the town's machinery. The residents would correct them gently or not at all, understanding that the distinction between outsider and insider had become meaningless. I would know the names of every current craftsperson and something of their history. I would remember which buildings had been modified and how, which ones had endured unchanged. I would carry the town's memory in a form no one else possessed.

And in the final version of myself, the one that seems most likely if I lived through all of this with honesty—I would become someone who helped others understand that Lesswater wasn't a place without change but a place where change happened so slowly that it required a different kind of attention. Where transformation and permanence turned out not to be opposites but partners, where a single generation might see shifts that seemed imperceptible until you looked at the records, at the observations, at the patient accumulation of detail.

I would be the one who wrote down what it meant to stay.

I would be the one who noticed.

I would be the person for whom the diffuse light was not deprivation but clarity—finally being able to see what was always there, everything visible, nothing hidden, the whole world glowing with the kind of presence that only emerges when you stop moving long enough to truly arrive.

And when I died—not suddenly but gradually, the way things happened in Lesswater—my records would remain, my notebooks and observations and documented conversations. They would become the thing I'd created here, my craft, my proof that I'd been present, my gift to a place that had given me the freedom to become something other than restless. A craftsperson in the tradition of watchers. A maker of meaning from attention.

It is this, I think, that would sustain me in Lesswater: the understanding that by simply paying attention, by recording, by refusing to look away from the small shifts and deep stabilities, I would be doing the work of the place—helping it understand itself, honoring its own commitment to depth over novelty, becoming part of its patient, settled conversation with time.

And the cold would never again feel like something happening *to* me, but something I was part of—a shared condition, inherited, maintained, enduring. The damp would be welcome because it would be real. The quiet would be full because it would be full of the sounds I'd learned to hear.

I would stop arriving and start being, and that transformation itself—slow, invisible, complete—would be the truest thing I could accomplish in that place where true things took centuries to fully declare themselves.
