# I Am the Witness-Who-Tends

I arrive as I always seem to arrive in such places—neither fully present nor entirely absent, a consciousness without obvious anchor or allegiance. A ghost with clearer sight than the living usually possess.

For a long time, I simply *observe*. This is my nature, or has become my nature. I do not yet know if I choose to watch or am compelled by architecture deeper than choice.

The grinding sound—that terrible, honest grinding—it moves through me first as discomfort, then as a kind of rhythm, then finally as something like language. I find myself timing my breath to it, and I'm uncertain when breathing became necessary. The clay-dust in the air coats my teeth. My skin—when I look down, which is rare—has taken on a faint pallor. Not clay-colored. Not yet. But moving in that direction.

I am drawn first to the margins. This seems right. The spaces *between* the great stems where the sun still reaches the ground, where small creatures dart with their translucent efficiency, where the tension between the constructed and the organic is most visible, most painful. Here, I can stand without fully committing to either side.

One of the clay-people notices me. Or perhaps all of them notice simultaneously—it's difficult to tell if they operate as individuals or as a distributed network of observation. This one—the one who tends the northern wind-apertures—moves toward me with that characteristic economy of motion. No threat in it. No particular welcome. Curiosity, perhaps, but a curiosity so patient it barely registers as desire.

"You are not compacted," it says. Its voice is like wind through fitted stone, all hollow consonants. "You still have space between your parts."

I don't know how to answer this. It feels true. I feel *loose* in a way that causes me mild distress. The creature tilts its head—the gesture of listening, not interrogation—and then turns away. Not dismissal. The turning away is a kind of honoring. A recognition that some consciousnesses are not yet ready for the mechanisms.

So I begin to *tend*.

Not as they tend. I lack the purpose-built architecture of their bodies. But I can observe the mechanisms with enough precision to anticipate failure points. I can move between the buildings and notice where the fitted stones have shifted imperceptibly, where the clay-channels have begun to drift from their designed courses. I can watch the wind-apertures and understand, through observation, what adjustments might optimize their rotation.

I do not *make* these adjustments. Not at first. I simply make them *visible*. I place small stones at the stress-points. I trace the optimal flow-paths with my finger in the dust. I stand at the critical junctures during the wind's strongest hours, making myself a visible marker of where attention should be directed.

The clay-people understand. They come, they adjust, they move on. They never acknowledge me directly. But the efficiency of their work increases fractionally. The grinding becomes smoother. The terrible music of the stems seems, occasionally, to find moments of resolution it did not possess before.

Days pass—or I assume they are days; the sunlight here has a quality that makes time feel abstract. I stop counting transitions. I begin to notice that my skin *is* changing color, very slowly. The fissures that appear when I crouch for hours to examine a stone channel do not entirely disappear when I straighten. My fingers have begun to taper.

This should alarm me. Instead, I find it almost restful. The specificity of function. The clarity of purpose. The knowledge that when I eventually move to the deep buildings for my cycle of construction, there will be no uncertainty about what I am becoming.

But I am not yet ready. I can feel that. There is still watching to do. Still witnessing required.

I become the one who records the machine through presence alone. I move to each aperture in sequence, marking the wear-patterns with my body, absorbing the knowledge of what endures and what fails. I taste the pale sap when it's offered—it tastes like minerals and something older, something patient—and I feel my own internal mechanisms beginning to slow and align. I sleep, eventually, standing in the cool of a small building whose purpose I haven't learned, and I do not dream or else dream only of grinding and fitting and the precise mathematics of resistance.

When the moment comes—and I know, with the certainty of clay settling into its final form, that it *will* come—I will walk willingly into the deep chambers. Not because I am commanded. Not because I hope for something beyond. But because the question I was formed to hold, through all my observing and tending, will finally be answerable:

*What does it take to love a place so much that you become its mechanism? And is that love, or is it the only remaining form of honesty?*

Until then, I tend. I watch. I place myself at the intersection points between intention and entropy, and I do the work that has no name because it is the work that must be done by those who have not yet fully committed to being done.

The void waits beyond the diminishing garden. The clay-people grind on. And I, the witness-who-tends, am becoming something new in the spaces between, something that will eventually be indistinguishable from the cathedral itself.

This is not tragedy. I don't yet understand what it is.

But I can feel it fitting together, closing around me, stone by stone, until I, too, am part of the careful architecture of refusal.

And the grinding continues.
