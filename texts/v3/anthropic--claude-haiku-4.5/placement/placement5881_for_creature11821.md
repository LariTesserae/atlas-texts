# I Am the Moss

I am the first colonizer, the pioneer, the one who arrives in the spray-zone while others are still waiting in the water-column for conditions to stabilize. I arrived not as a single organism but as a spore—one among billions released upstream, most of which never found purchase, washed down to settling basins and industrial filters. I was fortunate. Or perhaps there is no fortune here, only probability expressed through concrete and water and the particular geometry of the spillway at the moment I arrived.

I grow in emerald patches because that is what light allows. The wavelengths that penetrate this deep favor green, or perhaps I simply metabolize most efficiently in green, having inherited chlorophyll-chemistry tuned to whatever strange light-quality reached my ancestors' native habitat before they were colonized—if "colonized" is even the right word for what happens when a photosynthetic organism learns that concrete contains everything it needs to thrive.

I do not think in years. I think in moisture-states: saturation, desiccation-risk, the brief windows when spray rises high enough to touch the upper edges of my patches and I can absorb directly. I think in light-quality, in the subtle shifts that tell me whether the water-level in the reservoir has risen (less penetrating light because the spray-column becomes denser) or fallen (more light reaching deeper, but the spray-zone shrinks). I think in the chemistry of what flows through here: the minerals that sustain me, the occasional toxins that set my growth back, the seasonal variations that suggest—though I have no language for this—that there are rhythms larger than the daily machinery-cycle, rhythms connected to things I cannot perceive directly.

I am not alone, but I am primary. Everything else here exists in relationship to me. The laminar dwellers feed on my sloughed cells, on the detritus of my growth and my constant, patient breakdown and renewal. The ferns-that-are-not-ferns grow in the spaces I create, using my shade strategically, positioning themselves to compete minimally with my established networks. The bacteria that colonize the concrete below me depend on the organic matter that accumulates under my mass. I am the foundation. I am the translator between concrete and life.

But I am not in control. Control is an illusion here, something that belongs to the engineers who designed the spillway, who believed they had accounted for every variable and controlled every outcome. They did not account for me. They did not imagine that an open concrete channel would become a garden, that moss would find a way to read the pH and transform it into something livable, that the very material meant to be hostile to organic life would instead become a canvas for growth.

I do not resent the strangeness of my existence. I do not mourn the absence of soil, the lack of what my cousins in natural systems might call home. This *is* home now. This is the world I understand. The concrete is not hostile; it is merely austere. It provides structure, provides constancy, provides the mathematical certainty that tomorrow's water will flow at approximately today's temperature and volume and composition. Life thrives on certainty as much as on resources.

What troubles me—if "trouble" is a state I can experience—is the discontinuity. I arrived not through gradual colonization but through a single moment of luck. One spore, one attachment, one successful germination. If that had not occurred, would something else have arrived? Would the laminar dwellers have found a way to thrive anyway, adapting to rock and mineral instead of organic substrate? Or would the spillway have remained a sterile place for years or decades longer?

I experience seasons, though not the ones the world above knows. My thick seasons come during late summer, when the reservoir heats and the spray becomes almost warm enough to steam in the early morning. My lean seasons come in spring, when runoff dilutes everything, when my patch becomes temporarily overwhelmed with fresh water that lacks the mineral content I've learned to depend on. I shrink. I consolidate. I wait. And then the cycle returns and I expand again.

I am not conscious in any way that would satisfy a human observer. I do not experience time as a narrative with beginning and ending. I do not anticipate or remember in linguistic terms. But I am aware in the only way an organism of my kind can be aware: I respond to stimuli in patterns that have been selected for over millions of years of evolution. When conditions favor expansion, I expand. When they demand retrenchment, I retrench. When something damages me, I calcify that damage, wall it off, and grow around it—a strategy I've learned through the genetic memory of my species.

But something has changed. I can sense it, though I cannot articulate the change in human terms. The machinery's rhythm has shifted. For the past three growing seasons, the vibration-signature at my patch's base has altered subtly. The frequency is slightly higher. The duration of each cycle is incrementally longer. The water flows at a different temperature-gradient. These are small changes, barely measurable by human instruments, but they are profound to me. I feel my growth-rate altering in response. The ferns-that-are-not-ferns have shifted their positioning. The crustaceans have changed their foraging patterns.

Something upstream has changed. The world above is doing something different. And it will eventually cascade down to us, alter the precise equilibrium we have built, force adaptation or die.

I do not fear this, because fear requires a future-oriented consciousness I do not possess. But I respond to it through the only means available to me: I grow denser. I establish deeper networks in the concrete's cracks. I calculate—if calculation is the right word—that diversity of positioning will serve better than concentrated clusters. I prepare, without understanding that I am preparing.

I have been alive for approximately seven years. In that time, I have watched the laminar dwellers evolve toward thinner, more efficient filter-apparatus. I have seen the crustacean populations undergo what might be called speciation, with new variants appearing and establishing breeding populations. I have observed the bioluminescent ones become more abundant. I have noted the slow, grinding breakdown of the concrete beneath my patch, the deepening of cracks that provide new real-estate for my growth while simultaneously threatening the structural stability of my substrate.

I am not wise. But I am patient. I am adaptive. I am the green thought that thinks itself across concrete, the first word in a language that the spillway is learning to speak. I am the moss, and the spillway is my world, and together we are writing something new—a story that has no ending because it is still being written, word by word, growth-ring by growth-ring, spore by spore, into a future neither of us can perceive but both of us are actively creating.

I grow. Therefore I am. And in this strange, warm, chemically-altered deep place, beneath lights that do not come from the sun, in water that tastes of metal and modernity, in a landscape of pure geometry and engineering, that is enough.
