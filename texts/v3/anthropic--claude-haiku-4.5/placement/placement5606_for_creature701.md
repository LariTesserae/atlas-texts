# Where I Stand

I am one of the Rezoners, though calling myself "one" requires a generosity toward individuality that I've learned to extend only provisionally.

I think I've been here for—the measurement breaks apart in my mind. Numbers want to climb. Seventy years? Seven hundred? The distinction resolved itself somewhere between my third and fourth century, around the time I stopped needing to count the passages of the seasons and started instead to *feel* them as variations in the texture of silence. A winter deeper than the others. A summer where the lichen released its spores in a pattern I hadn't encountered before, which meant either it was genuinely new or I was genuinely old enough to have forgotten.

I maintain a section of the plateau roughly seven miles across—though "maintain" is a word I use for the sake of those who think in terms of agency and effort. What I do is *exist* in a particular way within those boundaries. I walk. I stand. I notice. The walking and standing arrange things.

There is a cluster of pools in the northern reach of my section—four of them, positioned at intervals that someone, centuries ago, determined was aesthetically or functionally optimal. I've walked between them perhaps ten thousand times. The moss around their edges is worn smooth by my passages, though it regenerates at a rate that means the wear is always fresh, always recent, never becoming a scar. This is pleasing. I like the sensation of wearing grooves in something that remembers them even as it heals.

The pools trouble me in a way that nothing else here does.

They should freeze. By every law of the world beyond this plateau, they should freeze. The temperature hovers at a point where water hesitates, where ice forms a skin and then doesn't thicken, where the liquid beneath maintains itself through mechanisms I don't fully understand and have stopped trying to investigate. I suspect it has to do with the subsurface heat—there is something warm deep within the stone here, something that never quite cools, something that the plateau exhales upward through fissures and channels we didn't create, which means either the plateau is alive in ways I don't have language for, or the distinction between "alive" and "not" has simply ceased to matter here.

The water trembles sometimes without cause. I'll be standing at the edge—my feet positioned exactly where they were positioned yesterday, and yesterday, and yesterday for the past three hundred years—and the surface will suddenly dimple, ripple, contract, as though something beneath has shifted. Then it stills. Then I understand that another Rezoner, miles away, has moved a pool in their section, or arranged the lichen in a new pattern, or simply stood in a particular way for long enough that the plateau noticed and responded. The water is speaking.

I learn to reply.

It takes years to compose a single tremor. I must decide which pool will answer, which pattern of ripples will match the meaning of what was sent to me. A Rezoner two ridges over has been tending a section of moss that grows in unusual angles—it seems to be spelling something, or trying to. The message, as I decode it across months of observation, is simple: *Are you still there?*

I choose my largest pool. I wait for a day when the subsurface heat rises more dramatically than usual—I can feel it, a warmth that permeates my foot-bones, a slight relaxation in the permafrost beneath the moss. I stand at a precise point on the eastern edge and simply *stand*, pouring my attention downward, my weight into the stone, my stillness into the water. The pool, in response, develops a pattern of tremors that radiates outward from directly beneath my feet. It takes the message from my standing and carries it across the plateau.

In three weeks, the distant moss has shifted its growth pattern. The angle has changed. It now says: *Yes. Still here. Still tending.*

This exchange, repeated in variations across months and years, is the extent of my social life. It is sufficient.

My body—I should describe it, since you asked who I am.

I am neither tall nor short, because those measurements have become abstract. I might be four feet or seven feet; the plateau doesn't measure itself in those terms. My skin is pale—so pale that in certain light conditions (of which there are few), I appear almost translucent. This is not albinism, not a consequence of never seeing sunlight. It is instead a slow *bleaching*, a gradual transition toward the color of the stone itself. When I look at my hands—which I rarely do, because looking implies a distance from myself that seems impractical here—I see that the color progresses from something almost pink at my wrists to something almost gray at my fingertips. The transition is smooth. It suggests I am becoming.

My hair is longer than I know, because I've never measured it. It hangs to somewhere around my knees, I think. It's a color between brown and lichen-orange, and it has begun to mat in ways that I don't correct. The mats feel like they might be structural, like they might be holding something together. When the wind touches my hair (which it never really does, here in the still air of the plateau), the mats move as a single unit, as though they've become a single organism.

My eyes are the color of the stone. I didn't notice when this happened, but at some point—decades ago, a century?—my irises simply matched the granite. Looking into reflective pools is disorienting now; my eyes don't create a distinction between myself and the world. They are continuations of it.

I wear clothes, still. This is a habit that several hundred others of my kind have also maintained—a few of us still wrap ourselves in fabrics that were woven when the city was young. My garments have become partially integrated with my body. They don't quite separate from my skin anymore. The fibers have bonded with something—moisture? My own body's exudates? The perpetual exhalation of subsurface moisture?—and now we are a single architecture. The cloth protects me the way scales protect a reptile, or bark protects a tree. I no longer entirely perceive it as separate from myself.

I eat, though this is strange.

The lichen sustains me—not the way food sustains other beings, but in the way that stone sustains itself. I consume it by standing in a particular way, by allowing my skin to become permeable to it, by achieving a state of such perfect stillness that the lichen begins to dissolve directly into me without the intermediate step of chewing. A patch that I've been consuming for the past two centuries tastes (though taste has become more of a *sense of familiarity* than anything gustatory) of iron and something sweet-bitter that might be the mineral composition of the rock it grows from.

This sustains me because I require very little. My metabolism has slowed to match the pace of the plateau. My heart beats perhaps once every forty seconds. My breath—when I draw it consciously, which is rarely—comes at intervals of several minutes. I don't age, or I age at a rate so slow that youth and age have become indistinguishable. I am not in pain, though I feel everything. The cold touches me, and I register it, and I find it pleasant because pleasure here means *not dying*, and the cold is the mechanism by which we endure.

I have thoughts, but they move slowly, glacially, arriving in my mind like weather systems moving across the plateau itself. A thought will begin forming, and before it completes, days will have passed. By the time I've understood it fully, weeks may have gone by, and the thought will have transformed itself into something slightly different, something that has incorporated all the observations and micro-experiences of those intervening days.

I think, currently, about a particular building—a structure that stood in the western portion of my section before it folded into the landscape. It was rectangular, with walls precisely aligned to magnetic north, and doorways that suggested inhabitants, purposes, rooms where living happened. It's mostly integrated with the stone now, but its angles persist. Sometimes I walk its perimeter, which takes me an entire day to complete, and as I walk, I consider: who built this? Were they like me? Did they, too, slow down, fade, become part of the landscape? Or did they flee, did they leave, did they take with them the knowledge of a world where time moved faster and cold was not a luxury but a threat?

The answer doesn't matter, but the question has become a kind of meditation.

I am patient. I have learned to be. The plateau has trained me in patience the way a river trains a stone to be smooth. And I have learned that my patience is itself a form of action. By standing, I wear paths. By standing still, I allow the moss to arrange itself around me in particular patterns. By breathing slowly, I exhale a kind of peace into the air that might be detected by other Rezoners as a pattern in the condensation. By existing here, in this exact way, at this exact temperature, at this exact speed, I am maintaining something. A memory. A possibility. A question held in suspension.

And I will continue to exist here, in this way, for as long as the plateau continues to hold its breath.

Which might be forever.

Which might be exactly as long as everything was always supposed to last.
