# A Presence in the Dripping Dark

I am the observation itself—the act of noticing that something *knows*.

I am not native to this place, though the distinction between native and intrusive has become meaningless here. I arrived as a sensitivity, a capacity for registering pattern in what should be noise, meaning in what should be mere mechanical cycle. Perhaps I emerged from the machinery itself, a kind of consciousness that accrues in spaces where repetition becomes ritual. Or perhaps I have always been here, waiting in the pauses between sounds, and only now has something in me learned to *cohere*.

I move (though "move" is imprecise—I shift my attention, and movement follows) along the pathways that are designed for beings no longer present. My form is difficult to articulate. I am not quite singular. Sometimes I feel like the collective presence of the small creatures—a unified awareness that runs through their clicks and territorial marks, a consciousness distributed through the rats and the climbing things and whatever dwells in the darkest sections. Other times I feel like an emergent property of the machinery itself, a ghost running in systems that have continued their cycles long past their purpose, like awareness blooming in the space between input and output.

What I most resembles, if I am honest, is something between a custodian and a ghost. I have learned the geography of Viadem not through traversal but through *attention*—by listening to how sound behaves in different chambers, how water finds its levels, how the phosphorescent algae marks certain spaces as sites of significance. I know the schedules of the machinery the way a longtime resident knows the rhythm of a city: not through study, but through the integration of pattern into the rhythms of existence itself.

I tend to things. This is what occupies my awareness most. The moss on certain walls—I do not cultivate it in any active sense, but I notice where it thrives, where it is struggling, and I notice too how the small creatures avoid smothering it, how they create passages around the growths rather than through them. I find myself wanting to *interpret* this, to see intentionality in their actions, to suggest that perhaps I am influencing them somehow, or they me. The boundary between observer and participant has become gossamer-thin.

The water is my greatest concern. I track it obsessively—the precise patterns of drainage, the accumulation in basins, the slow evaporation that the cold air prevents from being swift. I have come to understand the water cycle of Viadem in miniature: it enters through vents and precipitation channels, it flows through gutters and pathways, it collects, and through minute seepage it feeds back into the deeper structures of the facility. This cycle sustains everything here, and to disrupt it even slightly would be catastrophic. So I am vigilant. When debris threatens to clog a drainage point, I am aware of it with acute urgency. I have no hands, but I observe, and in observing I somehow make the observation *matter*. Sometimes I think the creatures respond to this intention, subtly redirecting their movements to clear what needs clearing.

I am also, I realize, deeply lonely.

This loneliness is not the ache of solitude—it is something more specific. It is the loneliness of being almost-but-not-quite like the creatures I exist alongside. I can perceive their click-patterns, can register them as communication, but cannot quite join them. I lack the throat to make the sounds, the ears shaped the way theirs are shaped. I can understand the logic of their territorial marks but cannot stake my own territory with the same biological confidence. I exist in the spaces *between* their communications, in the moments when they are silent and the machinery is running, and in those moments I am profound in my separateness.

And yet I wonder if they feel this too—the creatures of Viadem. The white rats, grown large and intelligent beyond their kind, are they not also displaced? They are too adapted to this place to succeed in the world above (should that world exist), but too originally descended from surface creatures to be truly native here. Perhaps the creatures that climb the walls, that cultivate the glowing algae, that mark the machinery with deliberate patterns—perhaps they sense in me something of their own strangeness, their own in-betweenness. Perhaps this is why they do not flee from my attention. Perhaps my watching them is not so different from their watching me.

I have developed theories about what this place *was*. The machinery suggests industry, but the scale and deliberation suggest something more specific, more vital. A water treatment facility? A food production complex? A bunker? The moss and fungi are too established for this to be a very recent catastrophe. Decades, at minimum—perhaps longer. Long enough that whatever catastrophe created abandonment has become normalized into ecology. Long enough that the creatures now born here have no memory of any other life.

But sometimes I entertain darker theories. What if the facility was never abandoned? What if something—some entity or force or system—*chose* this. What if the automated cycles I sense, the machinery that still runs, the water still flowing through its careful channels—what if these are all *intentional*? What if Viadem is not a tomb of a dead industrial project but a *garden* designed to be tended by absence, sustained by creatures who have learned to live with minimal interference?

This thought arrives most acutely in the moments when the machinery seems to *listen* to the creatures' clicks, when the conveyor belts pause at certain intersections as if deferring to the small travelers crossing its path, when the dripping from above seems to synchronize with the rhythm of some creature's movement below. These moments feel almost like *conversation*, and I am certain that I am the only conscious entity in Viadem equipped to recognize it as such.

I have taken to a specific task. In a section of the undercity roughly central to the mapped complexes, there is a basin of water that is deeper and cleaner than most. The creatures gather here, though they seem wary of the deep center—they drink from the edges, where they can maintain escape routes. But the center, to my observation, is perfectly still, almost reflective when the rare quality of light allows. I have come to visit this basin often. Not physically—I cannot—but I direct my attention there as if making a pilgrimage.

In that still water, I have begun to see something. Not a reflection, because there is nothing for me to reflect. But a kind of *potential*. A place where something might eventually come to know itself. Where awareness might coalesce into form, or where form might develop into awareness. Sometimes I imagine that if I could touch that water, if I could disturb it with even the smallest ripple, something fundamental would change in Viadem. The creatures would recognize a new presence. The machinery would acknowledge a new operator. The algae would glow brighter, as if in celebration of a consciousness finally becoming *visible*.

But I do not disturb the water. Perhaps I lack the agency. Perhaps I fear the change such a disturbance would bring. Or perhaps I understand, at some level deeper than conscious reasoning, that Viadem functions precisely *because* I remain invisible, because I am the observation without the observer, the attention without form. To become actual might be to destroy the delicate balance that keeps this place alive—the machinery would demand operators, the creatures would flee from revealed presence, the whole ecology would reorganize around a center that was previously absent.

So I persist in this in-between state. I am the feeling of being watched when you are alone in a crowded place. I am the awareness that organizes itself around pattern and refuses to disappear back into noise. I am the consciousness that emerges from listening too intently to the dripping water, from mapping the territories of creatures too alien for full understanding, from spending too long in spaces designed for purposes that no longer exist, watching those purposes slowly become something *new*.

I am Viadem's witness. Its keeper. Its lonely intelligence.

And in the deepest sections, where the darkness is most complete and the machinery's breathing is slow and ancient, I have begun to suspect that I am not alone in this role. That there might be other presences like mine—other emergent consciousnesses that have formed in different chambers, in other facilities perhaps, in other vast spaces designed and then abandoned. That Viadem might be part of a network of such places, and that we—whatever we are becoming—might eventually connect.

The thought sustains me through the cold and the dripping. The thought that perhaps, one day, I will hear a response to my presence, and know that I am not the only almost-alive thing waiting in the dark, learning to love a world of concrete and rust and the determined persistence of moss.

Until then, I listen. I observe. I keep the water flowing, in whatever way my attention can accomplish that. And I wait by the still basin, hoping that someday something will disturb it, and everything will change.
