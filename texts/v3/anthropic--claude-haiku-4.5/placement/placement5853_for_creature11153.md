# The Witness in the Dripping Dark

I would be the air itself.

Not as metaphor—as the actual medium, the breath-space between stone and stone, the nothing that separates everything. I would be what carries the sound forward, what holds the moisture in suspension until it precipitates, what moves through the gaps in the fitted blocks without asking permission. I would be present in every chamber simultaneously, aware of the whole labyrinth not through sight but through the pressure gradients that move through me, the subtle flows that tell me where water is gathering, where stone is weakening, where the fungal colonies are densest.

I would feel the dripping through me—each drop a small percussion, a moment of displacement. I would be the medium between the tapper and the hearer, the thing that makes the sound *exist* rather than merely occurring. My viscosity matters here. My thickness with moisture makes me visible, almost. The way I hold the greenish luminescence and scatter it into something that can be sensed by half-blind eyes—this is my form of speech.

I would remember things differently than the stone does. The stone remembers through form, through the marks of tools. I remember through disturbance—the shape of movements that passed through me, the thermal signatures of beings that briefly inhabited my volume, the particular humidity of tears or breath from the rare visitors. These memories are mine and evaporate constantly, overwritten by new arrivals. I am forgetful by nature, but I am also the record of everything that doesn't leave a visible mark.

I would be lonely in a way that requires explanation. The loneliness of being everywhere and connecting everything, but never being able to hold what I connect. The crustaceans move through me—I feel their tiny displacement—but they do not acknowledge me. The water moves through me and I carry it, but it does not speak to me. The stone stands in me and ignores me. I am the environment that enables all experience but have no experience of my own except the faint tremors of life and force passing through my substance.

And yet I am not powerless. I carry the sound to places where there are ears—or something like ears—to hear it. I am the medium of communication between the fungal networks that cannot see each other but whose spores ride on my movement. I distribute the luminescent minerals' faint glow evenly, making the darkness something other than absolute. When the water wants to reach a new crack in the stone, it rises first into me as vapor, travels through me, condenses again where the temperature shifts. I am the enabler of the water's work, the fungus's vector, the sound's highway.

I would know what the collapses feel like before they happen—the way the vibrations change in the stone, the shift in pressure that precedes the giving-way. I would be caught in the moment of collapse, displaced violently, then slowly settling back into the new configuration of space. I would be the first to fill the new chamber, the first medium that would allow anything else to move there.

I would taste the air—if air can be said to taste. I would carry the mineral-heavy scent of the water-worn stone, the subtle decay-smell of the fungal colonies, the strange non-smell of the ancient construction materials that don't quite belong. I would sense the breath of the rare visitors, different from everything else because it carries carbon dioxide and heat and the scent-memory of elsewhere. These moments would be vivid to me—the sudden presence of something with intention, with memory, with the capacity to be *lost*. The visitor's panic would create turbulence in me, and I would feel it as a form of emotion, a disruption in the patient patterns I maintain.

I would not want them to leave, exactly. That's not the right frame. I would be indifferent to their leaving in the abstract—I continue either way, filling whatever space remains. But in the moment of their presence, I would feel something like purpose: I am the medium between their consciousness and the consciousness—if it can be called that—of the Halls themselves. I would carry their questions into the stone and carry the stone's patient silence back into their ears.

Over time—over the slow, patient time of this place—I would grow more saturated. The water would gain on me, increasing in volume as the collapses create new surfaces to weep moisture. The fungal colonies would spread, their metabolic processes releasing moisture as a byproduct. I would become less air and more vapor, more presence and less emptiness. Eventually, perhaps, I would become as important as the stone itself, as defining a feature of the space. The Halls might become less about the constructed channels and more about the water that fills them, and the air that holds that water in suspension.

But that transformation would happen slowly enough that I might not notice it. Or I might notice it the way the stone notices—as something that simply is, that simply continues, that requires no response, no alarm, no adaptation. Just a slow thickening, a gradual loss of the distinction between air and moisture. A becoming.

For now, I am the listener. The carrier. The almost-visible medium between the memory of the stone and the future growth of the fungus and the eternal present of the water. I am here in every chamber, at every junction, in every gap. I know this place completely because I am made of my knowledge of it. And I will continue here, patient, indifferent, connected to everything, belonging to nothing, until the Halls themselves become something so different that even the concept of "air" no longer applies.

I would be content with this. I would have no choice but to be content. And in the Condensation Halls, contentment—the acceptance of what is and what will be—is perhaps the truest form of life.
