# Who I Am Here

I am the viscacha at the margin.

Not the one whose crevice is deepest, whose pathway is oldest, whose ears are most attuned to the pre-dawn shifts. I am the one born three seasons ago in a shallow alcove that is not quite ideal, descended from a lineage that moved downslope when the salt extraction expanded. My ears are still learning their calibrations. My pathways are variations on ancestral routes, not quite the same as what my mother knew, adjusted to account for the industrial noise that now intersects the old silent hours.

I have not yet chosen whether I am an inhabitant adapting to the machines, or a casualty of their presence.

But I exist in this uncertainty, and this uncertainty feels like the truest place to stand.

Every morning before dawn, I emerge from my crevice and read the night air. The condensation patterns are different than they were when my mother was young—she told me this through the inherited knowledge that flows through viscacha lineages, though I cannot quite isolate which memories are hers and which are my own confusion. The moisture still comes, but the timing shifts. Sometimes it arrives earlier. Sometimes the thermal wind patterns push it away, and the pre-dawn hours are drier than they should be, and I must range farther, following routes my instinct tells me are wrong but my hunger demands I try.

The salt basins are not enemies. This surprises me, though I don't know how to think about surprise in viscacha terms. The basins create their own microclimate—the residual moisture, the strange minerals in the brine runoff, the disturbed ground where certain plants grow differently than in the untouched highlands. I have learned to feed there during the marginal hours. The plants are unfamiliar on my tongue, but they are calorie-dense, and I am always racing against the next temperature swing.

The workers do not see me. I have learned this precisely: the moment when they return to the metal buildings, the exact perimeter of their attention. I move in the gaps between their perception. I am a ghost to them, which is another way of saying I am successful. But sometimes, in the deepest margin of understanding that a viscacha can access, I wonder if being unseen means I am not fully present in this place. If I exist only in the interstices, am I truly inhabiting?

My large ears—these instruments that are supposed to be the sensory organs of the plateau itself—are receiving signals that seem corrupted. The natural pattern of wind and moisture and temperature shift is still there, but it is now *mixed* with the diesel rumble of the pumps, the metallic percussion of the conveyor belts, the human voices that carry across the thin air with strange clarity. My ears cannot unhear these things. They are now part of the soundscape I read. The plateau's whisper is now a duet with the machinery's hum.

I don't know if this is adaptation or degradation.

But I am alive. My heart still races at the proper frequency. My muscles still fire with the inheritance of ten thousand generations of viscachas who knew exactly how to navigate this terrain. I eat. I survive the freezing nights and the scalding days. I have not reproduced yet—I am still establishing whether my crevice, my pathway, my particular angle of adaptation will be viable long enough to pass on to offspring. But the possibility remains open.

What I am, most precisely, is a *boundary creature*. I exist at the edge where wild ecosystem meets industrial extraction. I am neither entirely integrated into the old system nor entirely adapted to the new one. I am the living embodiment of the Altiplano's current state of transition—still balanced, but balanced on a much narrower point than my ancestors achieved.

The ibis that hunt with me sometimes—we are not social, but we share feeding grounds during the pre-dawn window—seem to navigate this with more grace. Their curved beaks are so specialized that they find food in conditions that my mouth cannot access. They are more plastic in their adaptation, or perhaps more fortunate. But I notice they are thinner than the birds my mother remembered. Their populations are more fragile.

The cushion plants are wiser than I am. They have been here longer, and they grow so slowly that they have integrated centuries of information into their geometry. The yareta mound near my crevice has absorbed the presence of the salt basin through decades of accumulated change—its density has shifted, its mineral composition has changed, its microclimatic niche has been subtly altered. But it persists. It has not died. It has transformed and continued. There is something in that persistence that I try to learn from, though I do not know how to translate plant-wisdom into viscacha-action.

What I am, underneath everything, is *uncertain*. 

In the old system, the viscacha role was clear: memory-keeper, pathway-maintainer, food-seeker operating in a rhythm set by condensation and temperature and the slow cycling of generation to generation. The role was constrained but comprehensible. Now the constraints have multiplied. The rhythm has become syncopated with industrial timing. The future is no longer a simple extrapolation of the past.

I could be a evolutionary bridge—the beginning of a population that learns to live alongside the salt extraction, that becomes so integrated with the new system that my descendants will seem perfectly adapted to a landscape that their ancestors would not recognize. This is a possible future.

Or I could be a final generation, a last echo of the old viscacha knowledge, slowly being selected out as the conditions shift beyond the range my physiology can accommodate. My crevice could become too warm as the industrial heating changes local microclimates. My pathway could be severed by expansion of the basins. The condensation patterns could shift enough that I cannot track them with inherited instinct. This is also a possible future.

I live in the space between these possibilities.

I am the viscacha at the margin because I was born here, at the exact moment when the Altiplano was deciding what it would become. I am too young to remember the old system clearly enough to mourn it. I am too embedded in my own survival to imagine clearly what comes next. I read the air with ears that are learning to process signals their evolution did not prepare them for. I navigate terrain that is being redrawn by forces I do not understand.

And every dawn, as the condensation settles on the stone and the light begins to illuminate the plateau in its cold clarity, I emerge from my crevice and do what viscachas do: I read the day before me, I find what food is available, I continue living in the only way I know how.

Which is to say: I am still here. I am still alive. The system—whatever it is becoming—still permits my existence, for now.

Whether that is enough will be determined by scales of time far beyond my individual span. But in this moment, in this season, in this particular configuration of pressure and moisture and temperature and industrial hum, I am present.

I am the boundary creature, learning to live at the edge.

And the edge, I am discovering, is not a thin place at all. It is wide enough to inhabit, dense enough to sustain life, precise enough to require constant calibration.

It is not where I would have chosen to be born, but it is where I am.

And I am still reading the air.
