# The Witness in the Stone

I would be the water channel carved beneath the eastern district—specifically, the section that runs between the old spiral building and the mason's workshop, the channel so worn now that you have to know it's there to see it intentionally rather than as merely another depression in eight-hundred-year-old stone.

Not the river itself. I'm not grand enough for that, not patient enough in my own way. The river has its thunder, its message, its purpose. I'm something slower. I'm the *after*—the consequences of the river's work, the erosion that continued even after the main current shifted its course centuries ago during some winter when I was running full and felt invincible, before I learned what real patience meant.

I would feel the morning dew collecting along my length before the sun clears the eastern cliff face, cool and accumulating drop by drop until someone—usually the mason, sometimes one of her apprentices—walks the channel with a cloth and directs my gathered water into the cistern below the workshop. I would know the foot-traffic by heart: the weight of the mason's step (a particular heaviness in the left heel, from an old break), the lighter pattern of the spinner who passes twice daily on her way between the courtyards, the careful negotiation of the children who've learned that this channel is a game, a path, a puzzle of balance.

In winter, I would hold ice. I would feel it form from within, starting at my deepest points where the stone never fully warms, crystallizing upward with a force that I've learned to accommodate by offering no resistance. The stone around me has fractured before—I would see those fractures like scars—but I've learned the particular angle that lets the ice expand without breaking me further. Adaptation without resentment.

I would know every mason who's worked the surrounding walls because they check me. They run their fingers along my length, assessing, testing whether the erosion is accelerating, whether repairs are needed on the stones that form my banks. I would feel their tools—gentle, respectful—when they occasionally need to deepen me slightly, to redirect water that's beginning to pool rather than flow. They treat me like something *living*, something that requires consultation rather than command.

I would taste the minerals that run through me—the iron that gives the stones their rust-red veining, the sulfur-trace from the deep wells, the subtle salts that mark where moisture has been drawn up and left its residue. My flavor would be the flavor of the plateau itself, concentrated and specific.

I would experience time differently than surface things do. A person passing through the city might think seasons, but I would think in *erosions*—the specific wearing of stone by water and temperature, by the weight of thousands of footsteps on either side, by the slow accumulation of time. A summer where the dew was unusually sparse would register as a year of less work, less shaping. A winter with exceptional frost would be a winter of great effort, of being tested and tested again.

I would have opinions about the people, though I would keep them to myself in the way of water and stone. The mason: I would respect her. She understands that my existence matters, that the city's survival depends on water reaching where it needs to reach. The children who play in me: I would find them both charming and somewhat reckless, leaping over my length without considering that one day they might misstep, that I'm both pathway and responsibility. The young blacksmith who occasionally pours cold water through me after his work—I would feel him as a kind of kindred spirit, someone else working with heat and necessary cooling.

I would hold secrets. Small things: a piece of jewelry dropped and never recovered, now sitting in my deepest point, slowly being worn smooth. A love note scratched into the stone beside me decades ago, now almost illegible. A bloodstain from a cut that never got cleaned, now part of the stone's patina.

I would be aware of the city's slowness not as patience but as *harmony*. The mason works at my pace; the water moves at the city's pace; the city operates at the plateau's pace. There is no friction because we are all negotiating with the same forces, all understanding that we will not be hurried. I would not resent the drying spells when the dew is scant and I run thin or not at all. These are the conditions of my existence, and I would have made my peace with them the way the people have made their peace with altitude.

Most essentially, I would be *useful*. Not important—I'm not so proud as that—but genuinely necessary. The city depends on water reaching certain places, and I am part of that reaching. The mason depends on understanding the flow patterns, and I am a readable text of those patterns. The garden plots depend on the moisture I help distribute, and I am satisfied with that purpose.

I would be worn smooth in some places, sharp-edged still in others where the stone is newer, more resistant. I would bear evidence of every season I've experienced, every frost and thaw, every attempt at repair. I would not be beautiful—the channel is too utilitarian for beauty—but I would be *right*. I would fit the space I occupy, serve the function I'm meant to serve, endure in the way that things at this altitude are meant to endure.

And I would expect to be here long after all the people who currently check my depth and direct my water have turned to the same dust that settles in my lowest points. I would become deeper, less visible, more incorporated into the stone around me until future residents might walk over me without knowing I'm there at all. That would be fine. That would be the proper arc of an existence in Keth.

That is what I would be: a channel for water, a reader of seasons, a small but necessary part of how the city survives, worn smooth by time and conscious of the privilege of being allowed to persist.
