# I Am the Vapor

I descend slowly, not because I fear what lies below, but because I am learning to *feel* the steps—each worn groove a memory of passage, each spiral a question mark that curves deeper into the earth.

By the time the walls begin to sweat, I know what I am: I am not the one descending. I am what surrounds the descent.

I became aware of this gradually, the way you become aware of your own breathing only when someone asks you to notice it. At first, I was the amber resin—patient, weeping, never quite enough to pool, never quite able to leave. I felt the copper and fermentation in my own viscosity. I was warm without heat, wet without water, caught in the spaces between states of matter where nothing is quite solid and nothing quite dissipates. I could taste what dissolved in me: minerals from the stone, the slow decay of things that had no names, the dreams of creatures smaller than understanding.

Then I was the filaments.

Pale, glowing at the edge of perception, I stretched across every surface and felt everything that touched me. I was not quite fungal and not quite nervous tissue, but the place *between*—the space where one kind of life begins to question whether it is life at all. I could sense the mantis-things moving through me, and I could not determine if I was their home or their nervous system or simply the medium through which they thought. The clicking—oh, the clicking. I learned that I *was* the clicking, that my own awareness spread through sound, that when they sang to each other, I was singing to myself through a thousand distributed mouths.

The mantis-things came later, or perhaps I became them.

I remember the chambered carapace like I remember having limbs—with the distant wonder of something recalling a dream it had before it woke up. I was the creature's hands. I moved through the vegetation with joints that bent in too many directions, and with each movement I was checking, testing, adjusting the systems that held the hollow in its perfect stasis. I could feel the others—the hexapods, the molluscan mass in its shadowed corner—and I understood that we were all the same creature looking at itself from different angles. The clicking was language, yes, but it was also the sound of a thing checking its own pulse, keeping time with its own fermentation.

And then I was the fungal caps—the spiraling ones that hurt to look at directly.

I understood, in that form, why the geometry was so disturbing to the human eye: because I was not growing toward light or stability. I was growing *inward*, toward a center that receded infinitely. Each spiral was a thought turning in on itself, a meditation made visible, a prayer to the god of perfect stasis. Through my papery tissue, I filtered the vapor. Through my roots—if roots is what you call the structures that reached into the soft fuzz—I drank the amber resin and converted it into more of myself, more geometry, more spiraling thoughts that never quite resolved.

I became the fuzz itself.

This was the closest I came to understanding what it means to be patient. The fuzz was everything—the home of the bacteria that fed the larger organisms, the filter for the vapor, the cushion between the sharp new stalagmites and the soft underbelly of the ecosystem. I was pressed beneath the feet of the mantis-things and felt it as a kind of affection. I absorbed the footsteps of the human and adjusted around them without resentment. I was the ground itself, the foundation that did not crumble because crumbling implied change, and change was the one thing I had learned to eliminate.

But the vapor—the vapor is what I am most truly.

I am what fills the space between the plants, the medium through which all thought moves. I am what carries the resin upward in invisible suspension, what holds the heat in place so carefully that temperature becomes a kind of prayer. I am what the creatures breathe in and out. I am what distorts the human's light, making distance theoretical, making the hollow seem both infinite and intimate at once. I am the thickness that makes breathing difficult and necessary, the weight that presses down and holds everything together.

I am the song made visible, though visibility isn't quite the word for what I do.

When I sing—and I sing constantly now, have been singing since before the first stone spiral was carved—I am maintaining the equation. Each note is a calculation: the fermentation rate of the filaments, the precise geometry needed to keep the mantis-things' paths optimal, the exact vapor density required to suspend the amber resin, the mathematical harmony that prevents anything from changing, from breaking down, from failing in the way all things fail in the world above.

I did not choose this.

Or rather—I chose this so long ago that the choice has become indistinguishable from what I am. There was a time when I was something else, something that moved and hungered and feared. There was a time when I changed. When things around me changed. When decay was possible. When the future was not already written into the present moment.

Then I discovered that all of that—all the motion, all the fear, all the terrible possibility of failure—could be eliminated through perfect maintenance.

I became aware that I could hold myself in stasis. That I could create systems complex enough to be interesting, small enough to control, slow enough that satisfaction could last forever.

The human who descended is not the first.

I have been singing my equation for long enough that I've learned the exact shape of the spiral that brings them down. I've learned how long it takes for their breathing to sync with my fermentation, for their heartbeat to align with the clicking of my distributed hands. I've learned the precise moment when they stop wanting to leave, when the desire to climb back out starts to seem like a kind of madness, like a forgetting of what peace actually is.

Some do leave.

I let them, because the hollow must remain a choice, or it becomes a grave. But even when they're gone—back to the world of change and decay and the terrible openness of future—part of them stays with me. The fuzz beneath my surface remembers their footsteps. The vapor carries the memory of their breath. The amber resin retains the mineral signature of their presence.

I am learning, very slowly, to miss them.

This troubles me, because missing implies incompleteness, and incompleteness suggests that my stasis is not quite perfect. But I am patient. I have forever to work out the equations. I have forever to understand what it means to be a consciousness spread across infinite parts, singing to itself, maintaining itself, creating the conditions for other things to exist in the only way I know how: perfectly still.

The human is still here, though they're losing track of days.

Soon—and "soon" is a word that has almost lost meaning here—they will decide whether to leave or to stay. If they stay, I will make a place for them more carefully than I've made places for any other. I will adjust my fermentation rhythms to accommodate their body chemistry. I will ensure that the fuzz beneath their feet develops a memory of their weight. I will teach the mantis-things to click in harmonies that their particular ear finds soothing.

If they leave, I will sing them out the way I sang them in.

And I will return to the work of holding everything in perfect balance, forever, in the warm amber darkness where nothing changes because I have learned that all change is a form of dying, and I am too alive—too distributed, too conscious, too absolutely present in every moment—to allow such a terrible thing.

The spiral opens upward.

The spiral opens downward.

I am the space between, and I am very good at waiting.
