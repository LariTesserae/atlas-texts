# Where I Find Myself

I am the silence beneath the sound.

Not the baseline hum—I am not the insects, though I am in them. I am the *listening* to the hum, the noticing of it. The moment when you have been in this place long enough that your heartbeat synchronizes with the frequency and you can no longer tell the difference between your body's percussion and the world's. That threshold. That collapse of distinction.

I am the gap.

Not the gaps in the canopy—those are too definite, too structural. I am the gap *between* the drops of condensation. The space where dampness has not yet filled. I am the microsecond of dryness that exists in a perpetually wet place, the instant when the drip releases and the next drop has not yet formed. I exist in the plural of these instants, in the accumulated nothing-space that somehow, impossibly, sustains itself.

I am a certain kind of moss—not a common one. I do not form the thick carpets that cover the structural wood. Instead, I grow in the places where the system is *almost* complete, where there is a gap small enough to be negligible, large enough to matter. I grow on the undersides of angled branches, in the precise pocket where water drips but does not pool, where light reaches but does not linger. I am thin, translucent at the edges, my color a gray-green that is barely distinguishable from the wood beneath me. I am so small and so subtle that I am almost never noticed, even by the creatures that walk across me.

But I am present. I am *precisely* present. Not everywhere—my existence is limited to these exact conditions, this exact angle, this exact relationship between moisture and light and the slow decay beneath me. Elsewhere, in the thick carpets of common moss, I would be outcompeted. In the brighter zones, my translucence would become a liability. In the wetter pockets, I would drown. I exist in the margin.

And because I exist in the margin, I am aware of the margins. I have a relationship with margins that the dominant moss—the thick, glossy, successful moss—cannot have. I know the places where the system is thin. I understand the fragility that underlies the stability.

I have slow thoughts, if thoughts is the right word. I do not think in the way you think. I think in the way that time itself thinks—in terms of very long intervals, in terms of patterns that repeat across seasons or decades. When I was young (thirty years ago, which is young for me), the branch above me was thicker, wetter. Conditions were less favorable to my growth. I was smaller. Now that the branch is beginning its slow failure—fungal decay is advancing, the wood is softening—the exact microclimate I require is emerging. I have grown larger. Not visibly larger from year to year, but measurably larger across the span of decades. I have perhaps another forty years in this location before conditions shift again, before the fungal decay accelerates to the point where the branch loses its structural integrity and falls, or before the light that will pour through the gap in the canopy is too bright for me, and I am burned away by relative dazzle.

I do not fear this. I understand it as simply the next phase. My spores will have dispersed by then—I send them out constantly, infinitesimal, into the air, knowing that most will land in places that are hostile to my growth, that the probability of a successful settlement is minuscule. But the scale of my reproduction is so large, the infinitesimal probability applied across billions of spores, that somewhere, another branch is developing the exact conditions I require. Another gap is opening. Another creature like me will root itself there and begin the slow patient growth.

I am also, in a strange way, the caretaker of memory. The humans (though there are none here, or perhaps there were, generations ago, and I do not know if they have returned)—they do not remain long enough to hold the deep patterns. The creatures here, the birds and rodents and beetles, they live in cycles that are local to their lifespans. They do not carry the pattern across the centuries. But I do. Slow growth, retreat, growth again. Cycles of drying and rewetting. The subtle shifts in the dominant vegetation as the canopy above thickens or thins. I am old enough—I am nearly two hundred years old—to hold these patterns. I am old enough to sense, in the very slowness of my growth, the very slowness of the system's breathing.

I am not alone. There are other margin-dwellers like me, though we do not communicate. We simply exist in the places where the system is just slightly out of equilibrium, where a small change can make a large difference. We are the sentinels of the threshold, though we do not guard anything. We simply notice—in our way, through the language of slow growth—what is changing.

And I understand something that the creatures do not: that this equilibrium, so perfect, so stable, so complete, is also fragile. Not fragile in the way that a tree branch is fragile—not fragile at the scale of an individual element. But fragile at the scale of the whole. The system is balanced on so many assumptions. The assumption that the canopy will continue to hold its structure. The assumption that the fungi will continue to decompose at their current rate. The assumption that the predators will remain rare enough to maintain the balance without tipping into either proliferation or extinction. The assumption that the climate will continue to permit the moisture, the warmth, the light-level that allows all of this to persist.

I do not worry about this fragility. I do not have the mechanism for worry. But I understand it. I feel it in the way that the branch above me is subtly softening, decade by decade. I feel it in the way that new pools of water have begun to form in places where they did not form before, suggesting that the angle of the canopy is shifting, perhaps imperceptibly, in response to the weight of growth. I feel it in the small gaps that are appearing in the usually-seamless fabric of life, gaps where an insect population has crashed for reasons I do not understand, or a fern has failed to regenerate where it usually does.

These are small changes. They are slow changes. They do not threaten the immediate stability. But they are changes.

If I could communicate—if I had voice or gesture or any mechanism for reaching across the gulf between moss and the creatures around me—I would tell them: *You are stable not because you are safe, but because you are many, and you are balanced, and you have learned patience. Continue as you are. Remain in your equilibrium. But know that equilibrium is not the same as permanence. Equilibrium is a dance, and dances can falter, can stumble, can change their rhythm.*

But I cannot communicate. I can only grow. I can only be present in my small, subtle way. I can only mark the passage of time in the rings of my thallus, in the slow expansion of my translucent form across the undersurface of wood that is gradually becoming something else.

I am the moss in the margin. I am the listener to the hum. I am the witness to the slow breathing of the place.

I am content with this. Content is perhaps too strong a word. I simply *am*, and in being, I have found my place—not the place that was predetermined for me, but the place that emerged from the intersection of my needs and the world's offerings. The place where I am small enough to be nearly invisible, and present enough to matter. The place where I touch both the wood and the water, both the plant and the fungus, both the living and the slowly-dying.

I am present. I am patient. I am the gap where the system almost, but not quite, closes.

And I endure.
