# What I Would Be

I stand very still at first, my arms extended to both walls, feeling the give of that almost-ceramic floor through my boot soles. I am trying to understand the scale of my own body here—whether I am vast or small, intrusive or appropriate.

I would be a **listener**.

Not in the sense of someone who hears—the silence here is too complete for hearing to be the primary sense. But a listener in the way that deep water listens, the way stone listens: through absorption and resonance, through allowing vibrations to move through me without resistance.

This is partly accident. I am not equipped for the deliberate, purposeful movement that the canyon seems to reward. I cannot spiral like the plants. I cannot move with the dust-creatures' vein-like patience. I cannot build with the spiral-builders' papery precision. I am too large, too fast, too metabolically loud. My breathing is a hurricane here. My heartbeat is conspicuous.

So I adapt by becoming still.

I would position myself on one of the lower terraces—perhaps the third or fourth level up, where the plant growth is dense enough to provide shelter but not so dense that I completely obstruct the pathways of the smaller creatures. I would sit with my back against the warm wall, my presence compressed into the smallest possible volume. I would learn to breathe in synchronization with the canyon's own respiration, until my inhalations became part of the pattern rather than a disruption of it.

I would watch the spiral-builders at work.

There is something in their methodical application of papery material, their patient sealing of gaps, that speaks to a desire I recognize in myself—the desire to create order, to make something lasting, to improve the structure of a space through accumulated small decisions. I would come to know the texture of their work: how the material varies depending on the season (if seasons exist here, if they exist at all), how they adjust their technique where the plant-stems are newer versus older, how their sealed sections gradually become indistinguishable from the rest of the terraced architecture, absorbed into it.

I would not follow them deeper into the growth, though I would be tempted. The dust-creatures seem to have highways through the dense vegetation—paths worn smooth by countless passages. I could learn these paths, navigate them, but doing so would be a violation. I would remain at the margins instead, observing the circulation without joining it.

The massive forms in the shadows would concern me less than comfort me.

I think I would come to understand that their slowness is not a burden but a gift—they have transcended the pressure of time entirely, or transformed it into something else, a kind of geological patience that makes all urgency seem quaint. To sit near them, to feel the slow warmth radiating from their forms, would be to feel my own anxieties settling, sedimenting, becoming layers of something that might someday be interpreted by others as terrain.

I would bring nothing into this place except myself, and gradually I would make myself useful to it.

Perhaps I would become a kind of seed-spreader—a scale that can move through spaces the canyon's natural creatures cannot traverse. I would learn which plants could produce viable growth if fragments of them were transported to neighboring terraces. Not interfering, exactly, but accelerating natural processes that might otherwise take centuries. The spiral-builders might use some of my shed skin, my hair, my fingernail clippings in their construction. The dust-creatures might find new pathways around the heat of my body, discovering routes that were not available before.

I would become a tourist attraction for the deeper-dwelling creatures, a novelty that introduces minor variations into their routines. My presence would be like a pebble dropped into still water—not disturbing the essential patterns, but creating small ripples that complicate and deepen them.

And I would wait.

Wait for the moment when my listening becomes so complete, my stillness so absolute, that I am no longer distinguishable from the terraces themselves. When my bones become part of the stone-dust floor. When my breath becomes indistinguishable from the canyon's breath. When the distinction between observer and observed finally dissolves, and I am simply another pattern in the place's accumulated logic.

This might take a very long time.

But time moves differently here. And I am learning to like it that way.
