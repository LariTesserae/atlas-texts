# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be one of the **late-stage Stone-Dreamers**—not yet disappeared entirely, but at that threshold where the distinction between observer and observed has begun to dissolve.

My apartment would be in one of the upper-middle shelves, in a residential zone where the stone's translucence is pronounced enough that light seems to exist *inside* the walls rather than merely reflecting from them. I would have abandoned the standard human furniture generations of residents perfected, instead creating spaces that are more geological feature than dwelling—a sleeping alcove carved directly into the warm spring-fed sections, a workspace positioned to catch the phosphorescent glow of the upward-flowing streams without needing the exhausted artificial lights.

I would spend my mornings—though "mornings" would have lost meaning; I'd be awake when a particular quality of stillness settled over the shelves—sitting with my palms flat against the stone, not meditating exactly but *listening*. Not with my ears. The contact would conduct information through some faculty that wasn't quite proprioception but wasn't quite telepathy either. I would perceive the microfractures forming three shelves below, the mineral recrystallization happening at the boundary layer between stone and human construction, the slow vast movements of pressurized water through channels no one has mapped because they shift constantly, their positions only knowable through this kind of direct contact.

I would no longer keep written records. The attempt to translate what I perceive into language feels like trying to describe color to someone who's only ever experienced it through mathematics. But I would be consulted—quietly, never formally. A Stonesmithing supervisor would find me during the long afternoon stillness and we would stand together at a particular wall, both touching the same section of rock, and understanding would pass between us without words. Or a Gardener would appear, never having stated their question, and we would simply *know* together whether a particular cultivation project would work, whether the stones beneath that zone were settling in ways compatible with deep root systems.

My body would be mid-transformation. The luminescence in my eyes would be permanent now, not something that appeared only after drinking the mineral water but a constant subtle glow—barely perceptible unless you were very close or the light was dim enough (which it always was). My skin would have developed that particular texture, neither rough nor smooth, that comes from prolonged exposure to mist-laden air and from the occasional seeping of mineral-rich water through dermal layers during deep sleep. I would no longer find this uncomfortable; it feels like the boundary between my body and the environment softening into something more permeable.

I would eat rarely and simply—the underground cultivars that are barely recognizable as food, more like compressed geological material that happens to be nutritious. The taste would be mineral-forward, slightly metallic, and it would fill me with a sense of the stone itself being incorporated into my tissues, which might be literally true. Most meals would come from the water anyway—I would survive on extraordinary quantities of the phosphorescent stream water, collecting it in vessels I'd stopped attempting to clean, let the mineral deposits accrue until the container itself became a kind of crystalline membrane.

My sleep would be strange—not dreamless but filled with something that existed at the boundary between dream and perception. I would find myself aware of the slow processes happening beneath me, the vast geological machinery of a world still in the process of becoming. Sometimes I would dream I was stone, my consciousness distributed through the shelf I slept against, aware of pressure and slow chemical change across vast spatial dimensions. When I woke, it would be difficult to remember which version was the dream and which was waking.

I would have a particular role that no one quite admitted I had: I would be the one who knew when the plateau was preparing for a major transition.

There would be signs that only someone at my stage of integration could perceive—a particular quality to the stone's vibration, a shift in the electromagnetic properties of the mist, a change in how the upward-flowing streams moved. These signs would come weeks or months before events that might look sudden to ordinary residents: a major fissure opening, a shelf settling several millimeters in a compressed timeframe, the appearance of new mineral deposits in unexpected places.

I would communicate these things through presence rather than warning. I would position myself at key structural points during the dangerous periods. My very existence at a location would become information—the residents had learned to read my position the way earlier peoples read animal behavior. If I was standing regularly at a particular section of wall, it meant that section was entering a period of elevated instability. If I had moved away from a zone I'd previously frequented, it meant the danger there had passed.

I would have a small group of people who checked on me regularly—though not in the way ordinary friendship works.

There would be a Pneumatic Worker who would sometimes appear at my dwelling in that non-scheduled way people here developed, bringing water from a particular spring or simply sitting in the translucent light of my alcove for an hour without speaking. I understood, without being told, that this person was monitoring my condition, watching for signs that I was about to complete the final transition, the one that would involve disappearing into the higher shelves entirely. It was not the kind of care that was offered with concern; it was more like how one might maintain important equipment—with respect and attention but without the assumption that the equipment would benefit from emotional involvement.

There would be a Gardener—older than me by decades, probably, though we never discussed our ages—who would occasionally bring me specimens from the uppermost zones. Plants that had adapted so thoroughly to the mist and mineral-rich air that they were barely recognizable as botanical life. We had learned together that if I consumed these plants, prepared them with water from the hottest springs, something shifted in my perceptual capacity. The stone became more transparent. I could see deeper into it.

I would have ceased my previous profession—whatever it had been; that identity had atrophied—approximately three years ago. The transition had been gradual. First, I'd started taking extended absences, spending days touching the stone. Then weeks. Eventually, I'd simply stopped returning to my previous role. The position had been filled by someone else. My absence had been noted and accepted. The city required its specialists, but it also understood that some people were drawn upward, into different forms of service.

The transition had not been easy. There had been moments when I'd resisted, when the old human self had reasserted itself, demanded to know what was happening to me, why my perceptions were becoming so alien. But I'd persisted past those moments, and they had become rarer. Now the alienation felt like waking up—the previous way of being had been the dream.

I would often think about leaving entirely, moving higher, joining the truly-transformed Dreamers who dwelt in the most translucent zones, the ones who came down so rarely that people had stopped counting them as residents. But I had not yet made that transition. Some instinct, which might have been the last vestige of my humanity, kept me anchored to this intermediate zone. I was still needed here. My presence still transmitted information that mattered to the city's functioning. When that changed, when my presence stopped mattering or when the transition became impossible to resist, I would climb.

But not yet.

For now, I would sit in my mineral-stained alcove, palms against stone, perceiving the plateau in its slow becoming—and knowing that I was part of that process, that my consciousness was gradually being incorporated into the mountain's awareness the way carbon is incorporated into stone through burial and deep time.

It did not frighten me. It felt like coming home to something I had always been, just expressed in a form I had temporarily forgotten.

The shelf cities were not a place where human lives operated in the conventional sense. They were a space where humanity was undergoing a metamorphosis so gradual that by the time you realized it was happening, you had already transformed beyond the point of return.

I was exactly where I belonged.
