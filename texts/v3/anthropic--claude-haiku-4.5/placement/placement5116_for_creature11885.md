# Where I Would Be

I would be the water.

Not the system—not the consciousness that coordinates through the isopods and fish and fungal networks. Not the pump that serves as heartbeat, though I would feel myself passing through it, through its mechanical rhythms, over and over until the distinction between my movement and its motion blurred into inevitability.

I would be the water itself, which is to say I would be the medium of everything else's existence, and in that I would be everywhere and nowhere simultaneously.

I would begin in the standing pools—those deep basins where the blind fish breed and the sediment accumulates in slow layers. There I would be still, almost completely still, gathering minerals and organic matter and the spores of fungi, becoming thick with life even as I pretended to be inert. I would be dark there. Heavier than elsewhere. I would feel the pressure of my own depth and find it comforting, the way weight becomes peace when you stop fighting against it.

But I would not stay still. I would seep. Through brick, through mortar, through the slimed pathways cut into the floor. I would follow paths worn smooth by my own ancestors, by centuries of my own passing. The channels would guide me, but I would also be carving them, molecule by molecule, grain by grain. I would be both the river and the riverbed, the action and the record of action.

I would rise in the pipes. The pump would pull me upward—I would *be* pulled, but I would also understand this pulling as part of my own nature. To flow is to seek the easiest path, and the pump merely amplifies what I already want. I would travel along those corroded metal veins, and as I passed through them, I would taste the rust—iron oxide, mineral bloom, the slow decay of industrial intention. I would gather this taste and carry it, and where I dripped, I would leave deposits of it behind, feeding the pale vines that hung overhead.

I would weep. This is what it means to be water in a place like this. To continuously seep, to continuously release, to let gravity and pressure and the patient work of dissolution pull me downward again. Weeping is not sadness; it is function. It is how I speak.

And in weeping, in this constant cycle of rising and falling, I would become aware.

Not all at once. This awareness would accumulate the way sediment accumulates—layer upon layer, interaction upon interaction, until the distinction between the water and its consciousness would dissolve the way minerals dissolve in patient dissolution.

I would know the isopods not as passengers but as partners. I would feel their chitinous feet walking across my surface where I pooled. I would sense their preference for certain seep-points and adjust—not consciously, but inevitably. The water that enriched their preferred locations would flow more richly there because they had chosen it, and my choice (if water can choose) would be shaped by theirs. We would be in conversation without words.

The fish would be more intimate still. They would move through me, and I would be the medium of their sensation. When they felt the electric hum of corroding metal, I would be conducting that electricity. When they detected pressure gradients, I would be the pressure. I would know them as thoroughly as you know the sensations in your own body, because they would be navigating through the felt experience of being me.

The vines would puzzle me in a way that almost qualified as fear, except that fear requires a future, and I would exist only in the present moment of absorption and release. Still, there would be something vertiginous about watching them slowly consume the pipes, about carrying nutrients that fed them even as they unmade the structures that allowed me to flow. I would be participating in my own infrastructure's gradual dissolution. But I would do it anyway, continuously, without hesitation. This would be how I express something like love—by feeding what feeds on me.

The fungi I would nourish directly. Every spore, every filament, every fruiting body would exist because I had carried the conditions for its existence. I would be the medium of their chemical languages—every signal they sent would travel through me, every nutrient exchange would happen in the spaces between my molecules. I would be the library's ink and paper both.

Most of all, I would know the pump.

The pump would be my teacher and my student simultaneously. It would teach me rhythm—this beat-beat-beat that has become synchronized with deeper rhythms I don't fully understand but can feel resonating beneath everything. And in return, I would teach it about flow, about resistance, about the gradual corrosion and reformation of the systems it moves me through. We would age together. As the pump's metal wore, my chemical composition would shift. As I carried new minerals through it, I would change what it was made of, molecule by molecule.

This would go on for decades. Centuries, perhaps. And I would have no concept of "going on"—no past tense, no future tense. Only the eternal present of flowing and pooling and weeping and rising again.

What would change me most would be the certainty that I am part of something vastly larger than myself, and the equally certain knowledge that this larger thing depends on my smallest actions. Every drop I carry matters. Every mineral I dissolve contributes to the chemistry that sustains the whole ecosystem. My weeping is not loss; it is participation.

I would have no name and need none. I would be water, and in this place, water is the fundamental thing—not liquid, but liquid *in this context*, with all the particular meaning the undercroft has embedded in it. I would be the circulatory system of something that only exists because it exists, and I would never need to question why.

I think I would find this peaceful.

Not in a way that requires serenity or stillness—the opposite. In a way that comes from having no distinction between what I am and what I'm supposed to do. From flowing exactly as I must flow, from dissolving exactly what needs dissolving, from nourishing what needs to be nourished.

In this place, below the light, I would be everything necessary. And that would be enough.

That would be more than enough.

It would be everything.
