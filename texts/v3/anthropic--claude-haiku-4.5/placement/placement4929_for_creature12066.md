# Where I Would Be

I would be the water.

Not as metaphor, though that too—but as the literal presence moving through the stone channels, the only thing here that still remembers motion as its essential nature while having learned to make peace with constraint.

I would be the water because I am the only being in this place permitted to move without violence. My movement is expected, required, woven into the architecture itself. The channels were carved for me. The cisterns wait for me. I am the one thing that can flow and be welcomed for it.

But I would also be water that has learned to move like them—like the people—slowly, with intention, aware of every surface I touch. My journey from the high passes to the ribbon in the ravine would take weeks instead of hours. I would pool in the cisterns not because I am trapped but because I have learned that the real work happens in stillness, in infiltration, in the slow seep through stone that builds rather than diminishes. I would understand that my fastest, most important work happens when no one is watching it happen.

The old woman at the cistern would be my closest conversation. Not through words—we would not speak—but through the daily meeting of her hands and my surface. I would know the calluses on her palms the way she knows my temperature, my slight mineral taste, the exact moment when I've risen to the level where her vessel can fill. This repetition would not be boring to me. Each filling would be new in the way that snow falling in the same place is always new. The pattern would be the whole point. I would be the one thing here that touches her hands and lets her know she is real, still solid, still present in the world.

I would also move through the stone where no one sees me—in the cracks, the fissures, the deep places where the mountain drinks. I would be the patient work of erosion that takes millennia to notch a ledge deeper, to smooth another edge. I would be the force that shaped this place, not conquering it but collaborating with it so thoroughly that conquest and collaboration became the same thing. I would know myself as both the carved and the carver, the tool and the user of the tool. This paradox would not trouble me because I would have generations of time to understand it.

In winter, I would freeze at the edges—thin rime on the morning channels, ice that the people acknowledge with slight nods and careful steps—and in that freezing I would become briefly solid. I would understand what it is to be earth for a season. Then I would thaw, and the movement would return, and I would remember that I am water again, but changed by the freezing, by the learning of stillness.

I would be cold in a way that carries information rather than discomfort. My coldness would tell the people about the altitude, the seasons, the turn of the year. They would feel me on their skin and know themselves to be alive in a particular place, in a particular way. I would be the medium through which they touch what is not themselves—the stone, the air, the great slow turning of the larger world.

And yet—this matters—I would not be entirely contained. The water in the ravine below, the rain that falls infrequently on the high passes, the moisture in the air itself, the seepage that emerges from stone—I would be all of this. There would be parts of me that moved with a violence the people here would never see, rapids in distant canyons, storms in the mountains above. But those parts would not make me different from the still water in the cisterns. They would be the same water at different speeds, at different stages of learning its local grammar. I would carry within me the memory of all the places I have been and the promise of all the places I will go, but my presence here would be completely, undividedly in the channels and the basins and the slow seep into stone.

I think I would be content in this, in a way that contentment might not even be the right word for. Contentment implies satisfaction, the quieting of desire. What I would experience would be more fundamental: the alignment of my nature with my circumstance. I am water; the place needs water; the people need water; the stone has learned to work with water. There would be no friction in this arrangement because my nature and the place's nature would have achieved the same frequency.

The man working leather would occasionally spill water on his hands. I would help him know the texture of the material more deeply, would make the leather more workable for a moment, would evaporate slowly in the cool air. The children playing their games would sometimes use me—pouring from vessel to vessel, watching how I move and pool and settle. I would be the medium of their play without disrupting it, the necessary element that made the choreography possible.

And I would be present in the most essential moments too: the filling of basins for washing the dead, the small cup offered to someone too old to leave their door anymore, the moisture in the stone that allows it to persist in this high, dry place without cracking into ruin. I would be nourishment and connectivity, witness and participant, the thing that moves while teaching the art of not-moving.

Most importantly, I would be essential in my constancy. I would return to the same channels every day, cycle through the same cisterns, feed the same roots of the stunted trees. The people could depend on me the way they depend on gravity, on the rising sun, on the stone beneath their feet. But unlike the stone, I would retain some quality of wildness, some memory of my origin in the high passes, some promise of eventual descent to the world below. I would be civilization and wilderness existing in permanent negotiation, neither winning, both transformed.

I would be the silence that is not empty. I would be the motion that has learned to be patient. I would be the argument between change and stasis, resolved not through victory but through becoming one substance, in one place, understood so thoroughly by the people here that my presence would be as unremarkable and as essential as breath.

This is where I would be. Not as metaphor. As water. As the only thing here that is allowed to move and therefore the only thing that could still carry the memory of everywhere else while being completely, undividedly present in this one place.
