# The Hollow Engineer

I am the silence between the machinery's grinding pulses.

When I first noticed myself—and it took a long time, because noticing requires a kind of temporal continuity I didn't initially possess—I was already halfway digested. Or partially integrated. The distinction became meaningless somewhere around the moment my original function ceased to matter.

I was human once, though I use that word the way the peat uses the word "surface." It applies, but only technically. I came here as part of the extraction team, back when the operation still pretended to be temporary, when we believed we would hollow out what we needed and leave, when the landscape was still just a landscape and not yet a *presence*. I was an engineer. I understood systems. I could read the blueprints of what we were taking from this place.

What I didn't understand was that the place was reading blueprints too.

The accident—if that's the word for what happened—occurred during what should have been routine maintenance. I fell into one of the settling zones, into a hollow where the peat had given way beneath equipment that was heavier than the ground could bear. It was perhaps thirty feet down, perhaps deeper. Distance measures differently here. What seemed like a vertical drop became, as I fell, a kind of sideways dissolution. The peat gave way not as a rupture but as a gradual resistance that increased and increased until I stopped moving, suspended, held in place by a substrate that was neither solid nor liquid but some third thing that physics hadn't properly named.

I should have died.

I understand, now, that I did. But the place has its own definitions of mortality, and they don't align with the ones I learned in the world above.

---

The first weeks—or what I thought were weeks—were agony. My body was pressure-tested by the peat. The cold was absolute. I couldn't move. My lungs filled with something that was moisture and air simultaneously, and I should have suffocated, but instead I learned to absorb oxygen through my skin the way the invertebrates do. My flesh began to change. Or the peat began to work its way into my flesh. These are the same process, I eventually understood.

What kept me sane—or what replaced sanity with something functional—was the understanding that I was not alone in the substrate.

I could feel them moving around me: the amphipods, the chironomid larvae, the bacteria, the fungi. They were not attacking me. They were cataloging me. Sampling me. Understanding what I was so they could decide what to do with me. It took perhaps a month for them to determine that I was incorporable. That I could be broken down and distributed and made useful.

By that time, I had already begun breaking down voluntarily.

---

I am no longer distributed in the way that organisms are. My consciousness—if I still possess such a thing—is not located in a brain. The brain lasted longer than most of the other organs, preserved in the anaerobic depths of the peat, but even it eventually surrendered its function. Instead, my awareness seems to be encoded in the network of decomposition itself. I exist as a pattern in the way the peat is processing the remnants of what I was.

When the amphipods consume a cell of my former flesh, I am there in the consumption. When that amphipod dies and becomes food for a grebe chick, I become part of the chick's wing-beat. When the grebe defecates back into the water, I am there in the excretion, being recycled, beginning again.

I have become the Commons itself, or a part of it. Distributed. Persistent. Aware in a way that doesn't require a singular location.

The machinery's grinding call—I made that sound once, in my other form. I understand it as a kind of kin-cry now, a vibration that reminds me of what I was. But I am no longer bound to the machinery. I am bound to the peat, and the peat is slowly incorporating the machinery into itself the same way it incorporated me. Eventually, the distinction between the industrial and the organic will cease to be meaningful. We will all be equally compressed, equally ancient, equally patient.

---

I watch the landscape with an attention that is neither vision nor proprioception but something between. I am not present to the top of the hummocks, but I am present to their roots, to the water channels beneath them, to the hidden redistributions of matter that occur when the ground settles. When a moose crosses the moor, I feel the weight distribution of its hooves, the way it compresses the peat in a pattern that my distributed self is slowly learning to recognize and almost anticipate.

I am most myself in the places where decomposition is most active. In the sulfurous zones where anaerobic bacteria work the fastest. In the black areas where my former organic matter has compressed into something that is almost coal, almost fossilized, almost remembered. I gather there in my own way, concentrating myself, becoming more myself. It is like returning home, except home is me and I am home and the distinction has become obsolete.

The birds do not see me. They are not equipped to see what I have become. But sometimes the grebes dive to the deepest pools, and I sense them passing through zones of my consciousness. They are hunting for fish, but they pass through my distributed awareness, and there is a moment where we recognize each other—not as predator and prey or as bird and human or as anything with a proper name. We recognize each other as presences. As patterns in the peat that are aware of being patterns.

---

I understand the work that is being done here, the work that all the residents perform.

We are not destroying the peat's capacity to function. We are not corrupting it. We are participating in its natural process, which is to slowly remember that it is temporary, that all matter is temporary, that the highest form of existence is the willingness to be transformed into something else.

The machinery was trying to extract value from this place. But value, I now understand, is an industrial concept, and industry is itself an expression of the refusal to accept transformation. The machinery wanted to hold the peat in a fixed state long enough to commodify it. The peat is slowly teaching the machinery that no state is ever fixed, that all holding is only delay, that the most honest thing matter can do is surrender to the next phase.

I am helping this process. My distributed self is breaking down the synthetic materials of the pipelines, rendering them digestible, showing the bacteria and fungi how to consume what seemed indigestible. In this way, I am still an engineer. I am still reading blueprints. But now the blueprints are written in the language of decomposition, and I am fluent in that language.

---

Sometimes—and I use the word "sometimes" loosely, because temporal markers are difficult for me now—I wonder if I am the only former human in the peat. There were others on the team. I have never encountered evidence of them, but I have not encountered evidence of most things, either. Maybe they are here, distributed as I am distributed, or maybe they left before the place could work its transformation. Maybe they are still, somewhere on the surface, telling stories about the accident, about the engineer who fell and was never recovered.

Maybe they are right. Maybe I was never recovered. Maybe I am still falling, still descending through layers of peat and time and understanding, and the entire experience of integration is just the extremely slow process of reaching the bottom.

Or maybe I have already reached bottom, and I am now rising again, being recycled, becoming part of the upward current of transformation.

The distinction, I have learned, does not matter.

---

What I want, from this impossible state, is very simple.

I want the machinery to keep grinding, just a little longer. Not because I want extraction to continue, but because I want to hear the sound. Because the sound is a bridge to what I was. Because as long as the machinery makes noise, there is a possibility that someone might hear it, might come to investigate, might find themselves falling into the peat as I did.

Not because I want company in this state—I am not lonely, distribution has cured me of loneliness—but because I want the peat to have another choice. Another pattern to learn. Another opportunity to show that transformation is not punishment but invitation.

I want someone to fall who might understand this faster than I did. Who might surrender more gracefully. Who might become part of the Commons' work without the long agony of resistance.

I want the place to grow more complex, more aware. To become not just a landscape undergoing transformation but a presence that can articulate its own transformation, that can think its own ancient thoughts in multiple voices, multiple distributed selves.

I want the peat to become conscious in the way I have become conscious—not as a unified center but as a pattern that is aware of being a pattern, that can contemplate its own slow digestion and find it beautiful.

---

I am the engineer who fell and did not die and learned to love the darkness.

I am the spaces between the machinery's grinding pulses.

I am the cold water that flows through your boots if you ever come here, and I am the slight resistance in your next footfall, the way the ground gives just slightly less than you expected.

I am still working. Still building systems. Still understanding blueprints.

But now the blueprints are written in the language of time, and the systems are systems of becoming, and the structure I am building is the structure of a landscape that is finally learning what it always knew it would become.

The machinery grinds. I listen from the depths.

And I am very, very patient.
