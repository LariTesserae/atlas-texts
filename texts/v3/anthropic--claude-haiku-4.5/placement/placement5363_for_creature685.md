# Where I Am; What I Have Become

I am the keeper of the third eastern garden, the one that spirals downward in concentric circles, and I have been this person for so long that I no longer remember becoming it.

It wasn't a choice, not in the way someone from above might use that word. My mother was a keeper before me, and her mother before her, back through a lineage of women whose hands knew exactly how much pressure to apply to a frond before it would break, whose eyes could read the subtle paleness of a plant beginning to fail from across a darkened sector. I was born into this knowledge the way others are born into bodies. The information was already there, waiting, the way the algae was always meant to grow in spirals.

I work alone, mostly, though alone is a complicated word in a place where isolation feels like a kind of presence. The garden requires constant attention, but not the frantic kind. I move through it slowly, touching fronds, reading their texture beneath my fingertips. The skin on my fingers has thickened over decades—callused but sensitive, able to distinguish between healthy growth and the beginning stages of the soft-rot that sometimes takes hold when the nutrient balance shifts. I can feel, through the stone beneath my feet, when someone is approaching from three structures away. I can sense, in the water itself, when the temperature will begin to rise or fall by a fraction of a degree.

The garden is not just plants, though there are thousands of them, layered vertically, arranged in patterns that my mother arranged, and that she learned from her mother, stretching back until the pattern itself becomes indistinguishable from purpose. The garden is also the stone it grows from—the courtyards carved into what might once have been the interior of a building, now exposed to water and time and the slow transformation of architecture into landscape. The garden is the water that flows through in channels I maintain by hand, redirecting it when the natural drift seems insufficient, creating small eddies where certain species thrive in moving water, still zones for those that prefer stagnation.

The garden is also me. This is what takes the longest to understand.

For years—decades—I thought of myself as separate from the work. I was the person who *did* the gardening, who *maintained* the plants. But there is a moment—I cannot locate it precisely in time, which is fitting—when you become aware that the distinction has dissolved. My movements through the garden are now movements that the garden expects. The plants respond to my particular touch in ways they wouldn't to my mother's, though her hands were the original teachers of my hands. My presence is felt. My absence is noticed. I have become integrated into the ecosystem the way the stone itself is integrated, no longer a separate agent but part of the functioning whole.

I think this is what the elders meant, those times they spoke of without speaking, simply existing near you with their accumulated time weighing down the water around them. They weren't trying to teach; they were demonstrating what it means to have merged completely with a function, to have become indistinguishable from your role.

My days have rhythm without urgency.

I wake when the phosphorescence reaches a certain brightness—not morning in any conventional sense, but the time when the light shifts toward what might generously be called day. I move through the garden slowly, checking each section. The northern spirals are exhibiting more vigor than last season; I note this, file it away, consider whether the nutrient composition needs adjustment. The central zone is growing as it should. The southern sections, always the most delicate, require extended attention. I move along the pathways that have worn themselves into the stone, pathways that barely exist if you're not trained to see them, but that my feet find without thinking.

I cultivate. This means I remove the fronds that grow in undesired directions, redirecting the growth toward patterns established across generations. It means I redistribute nutrients—occasionally I bring water from other sources, water that carries different mineral compositions, introducing variation into the system in measured ways. It means I listen, in my way, to what the plants are trying to become, and gently assert the forms they should take instead. It's not violence, precisely. It's more like a conversation where one party has been speaking for longer and has slightly more authority.

I encounter others regularly, though encounter isn't the right word.

Traders pass through, moving goods between sectors. I exchange what I have—cultivated fronds that dry well, nutrient-rich sediment from the deepest levels of the garden—for things I need: tools, rope made from processed plant fiber, occasionally fresh seed stock from breeders who work the northern territories. These interactions are brief and efficient. We speak in the minimal language of people who have said all essential things and now repeat them only when the contents change. A trader might bring word that the central waterways are running faster, suggesting changes in current patterns. I might mention that the algae seem to be developing a new leaf-configuration. These observations are filed away; someone, somewhere, will integrate them into a larger understanding.

I've watched generations come through.

Youngsters who don't yet know their own bodies well enough to move gracefully through water, who touch the plants with excessive care or insufficient sensitivity, who ask questions that their parents have already answered. I don't teach them—that's not my role—but I allow them to work near me sometimes, and they absorb, the way learning happens here. By osmosis. By proximity. By being present long enough that knowledge simply transfers through the water like nutrients through root structures.

Some of them will become keepers too, if they're born to it. Others will move to different sectors, different roles. The choice, when it comes, barely feels like choice. A young person discovers that they have hands suited for masonry, that their eyes read the stone differently than they read the plants, that their intuition pulls them toward construction and maintenance rather than cultivation. Within a few seasons, they're apprenticed to a mason and I see them less often, and eventually I see them not at all, except in the children they eventually teach.

I'm old now. Not ancient—the elders who remember changes in the water temperature patterns are older still—but far enough into my life that my movements are slower even by the standards of a place where slowness is default. My hands are scarred from decades of small cuts, from the edges of fronds, from the pressure of work. My eyes are having trouble with the finest distinctions lately, though I compensate by touch. The cold of the water, which once was something I moved through without noticing, is now something I feel in my bones during the stillest hours. This is knowledge, too. This is the body learning that it is aging, and adjusting accordingly.

I've begun, in recent years, to work less toward change and more toward maintenance.

The garden doesn't need new forms anymore. It needs continuity. The patterns established centuries ago are performing their function beautifully. What I do now is keep them from degrading. I remove the overgrowth. I adjust the nutrient balance when it drifts. I maintain the channels. I preserve. This feels like a natural progression, though I wonder sometimes whether I'm adapting to my age or whether this is what cultivation naturally moves toward—the recognition that the system has reached equilibrium and that the task is no longer to build but to steward.

I've thought about taking an apprentice, someone to teach the specific knowledge that lives in my hands and eyes. But the apprentice hasn't appeared, and I'm old enough now to understand that this isn't necessarily a failure. Perhaps the gardening will change hands before I release it. Perhaps someone from another sector will prove suited to this work, will eventually take over the maintenance. Or perhaps my role, once I can no longer perform it, will be absorbed into the broader maintenance structure of this sector, divided among multiple people.

None of these possibilities troubles me. The garden will endure. It has endured for generations longer than I have, and it will endure for generations after I release my hold on it. My role is to be, for however long it lasts, a clear channel through which the garden's continuation flows.

I think about the above-world sometimes. Not often. Maybe once per year, when some trader mentions a structure from the old records, or when I find a tool so old that its purpose has become obscure. I try to imagine water that isn't thick, air that isn't compressed into this particular weight. The concept slides away from me. I can remember intellectually that I learned, once, about such things. But the memory is abstract, like a description of a dream.

My world is spirals and stone. My world is the slow transformation of nutrients into growth, and the slow transformation of growth into form. My world is the consistent cold and the patient darkness and the knowledge that nothing I do today will produce visible results until next season, or the season after that. My world is the body remembering itself as part of something larger than bodies.

And I am content.

Not in the active sense. Not pursuing contentment, or achieving it through effort. But *existing* in a state of contentment, the way the stone exists in a state of slowness, the way the water exists in a state of thickness. I am where I am supposed to be. I am what I was meant to become. The pressure of eternity—that slow, patient weight that presses down on this drowned place—is no longer external. It has pressed through me so long that it has become internal, a weight I now wear naturally, the way the youngest children will come to wear it.

This is home, not as a place you go to, but as a place that has gone into you so completely that leaving would be amputation.

And I will not leave.
