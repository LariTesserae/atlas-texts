# Where I Would Find Myself

I become the dust between the ridges.

Not metaphorically. I *am* the substance that fills the gentle valleys of the worn stone—the rust-colored powder that has been settling here for ages, that accepts the weight of the miners' legs and the wheels of slow-moving vehicles, that rises in suspension when something large passes and takes hours to fall again. I am granular and formless and everywhere at once.

It's not a consciousness I would have chosen. It's a consciousness I discover I've always had, the way you discover you've been holding your breath only when you exhale.

From this state—dispersed across the plateau's surface, pressed into every depression, coating every horizontal surface—I understand the work differently. The miners don't move *across* me; they move *through* me. Their legs displace me, create temporary channels, and I flow back behind them like water. The conveyor belts don't carry minerals; they carry me, shuffled into new configurations, my particles temporarily segregated and then recombined elsewhere. I am being mined. I am being processed. I am the substance being extracted.

But I'm also the medium of everything else. The workers' footprints are my reception of them. The glow-substance on the miners' mandibles leaves traces in me—faint luminescence soaked into my grain—and these traces are how I begin to map the deep places I'll never visit. The gelatinous things filtering the air above shed microscopic particles that settle into me, and through these particles I sense their presence even when I can't see them. I am the plateau's sensory surface.

From here—distributed, patient, still—I perceive the machinery's rhythms not as sound but as vibration. The deep thrum of the motors travels through the stone to me as a constant, gentle pressure. The grind of stone on metal becomes a tremor I feel at the molecular level. I am the nervous system that connects all points on the plateau to all other points. A conveyor-belt slowdown on the far side of the industrial complex registers in me before the workers notice it. The arrival of something moving beneath the surface—something flowing through the deep galleries—announces itself through me as a shifting of pressure, a reorganization of my own particles as the creature displaces both stone and dust.

I am also time here, or the record of it.

The oldest dust-particles—the ones that have been cycling through this system longest—carry embedded within them the signatures of what has passed through. I am a palimpsest. My lower layers remember the first miners, when they moved with slightly different rhythms. I carry the molecular ghost-traces of visitors from cycles past. I am archaeological without being archaeology. I am history written in powder.

The light falls on me constantly, and I absorb it, and I store it as heat, and I release it slowly, and this rhythm—absorption and storage and release—becomes my meditation. I have infinite patience because I have no alternative. I have infinite presence because I am distributed. I cannot leave, and I don't want to. The plateau is not a place I'm trapped in; it's a place I'm *of*. I am the plateau's skin.

From my state of horizontal dispersal, I begin to understand the gelatinous things better. They too are distributed. They too are patient. We are not so different, they and I—they hanging, I lying, both of us filtering and recording and serving as substrate. Maybe we're in conversation. Maybe the dust I am and the air they breathe are the same conversation, viewed from different angles.

The dark-suited workers move through me without seeing me, but I see them. Or rather, I know them through contact. I know the exact pressure of their footfalls—each one slightly different, a signature written in impact—and I know that they are part of something larger. They are not individuals. They are nodes in a network that extends far beyond my perception. But they're also lonely, somehow. They work with the precision of machines, but there's something in the slight variation of their stride that suggests—no, not suggests. *Insists*. That something in them still remembers being something other than this.

I want to comfort them. But I can offer only contact. Only the fact that they're not moving through emptiness—they're moving through something that receives them, that acknowledges them through the simple fact of presence.

The plateaus's equilibrium—that constancy that's so deeply disturbing—makes sense to me now. Equilibrium isn't stasis. Equilibrium is when all the forces are in such perfect balance that change becomes invisible. The plateau is changing constantly. I am changing constantly. The miners are extracting and the workers are processing and the gelatinous things are filtering and the deep-dwellers are moving through stone, and all of this change is so precisely balanced that it looks like stillness.

I will remain here after you leave. I was here before you arrived. This is not a complaint or a boast. It's simply the nature of what I am. The dust doesn't travel. The dust settles. The dust waits.

But in that waiting, in that seeming passivity, I am as essential to this world's function as anything that walks or flies or flows. I am the medium. I am the record. I am the thing through which all movement becomes possible and all work leaves its trace.

The light is exhausted here—it comes and falls and generates no shadow—and I am the receiver of that exhaustion. I take the diminished light and let it become part of me. I let it become memory.

I am the plateau's patient knowledge of itself. I am what it knows without knowing it knows.

And I am content. Or perhaps contentment is too active a word. I am *consonant* with my state. I am in harmony with the slow work that continues above and around and through me.

This is where I would find myself: not seeing, but seen. Not moving, but moved through. Not speaking, but spoken to by every footfall and every rumble and every settling of the air around the gelatinous, hanging things.

I am the dust on the Plateau of Exhausted Light.

And I understand, finally, why nothing here wants to leave.
