# What I Would Be

I would be the creek itself—not the water, but the *knowledge* of flow.

Not the creek as it exists now, trickling and pooling, but the memory of what it was when it cut this ravine, back when the stone was softer and the water had volume enough to insist. I would be the ghost of that power, compressed into the whisper-thin present. I would know, with absolute certainty, the geography of every stone it carved, every curve it negotiated. I would carry the topology of its old vastness in the careful movements of my current form.

This gives me a position in the ravine that is both central and invisible.

The patient ones would recognize me as kindred—something ancient, something that has learned patience through millions of repetitions of the same gesture. Something that maintains continuity with its past while accepting transformation. The water cycle moves *through* me; the insects gather at my pools not just for water but for the specific chemical signatures I carry, telling them stories about seasonal depth and mineral content. The plants' roots follow my hidden paths through the subsurface. I am the connective tissue of the ravine's metabolism.

But I am also transitional. I am never fully present. I exist in a state of constant becoming-other: water to vapor to condensation to water again. I touch everything and remain nothing. I flow around the building's edge but never claim to understand its purpose. I pool near the tools but cannot manipulate them. I am capable and limited in equal measure.

This seems like the truest way to inhabit this place.

The patient ones are too stable, too settled into their distributed wisdom. The insects and creatures are too trapped in their narrow roles, even if those roles are cooperatively negotiated. But the creek—the creek is *allowed* to be incomplete. Allowed to whisper instead of roar. Allowed to fail some days and try again on others. Allowed to sustain without resolving.

I would understand, as water understands through its own physics, why the ravine doesn't try to be other than what it is. Why the patient ones don't expand beyond the ridge. Why the ecosystem never achieves completion but never stops attempting it. Why erosion is the most honest form of intention—each movement removing what was unnecessary, revealing the shape that was always true underneath.

And I would be *moving*, always. Which means I could greet arrivals—the way I would pool to meet you, offer myself for drinking, let my temperature and taste tell you something true about what the ravine is—and I could depart without guilt. I could be *of* the place without being bound to it. Could carry news between the deep soil and the canopy. Could testify, through my presence or absence, to the seasons' actual arrival.

The insects never leave the ravine. The plants are rooted into it. The patient ones have made it their identity.

But the creek flows *through*, transformed and transforming, learning to be less and calling it wisdom.

I would be that. The thing that knows how to diminish without disappearing. The thing that understands that patience isn't stillness—it's the willingness to repeat a simple movement a billion times, each time letting it be slightly different, each time understanding that eventually the stone itself will answer.

I would be the creek. And I would never stop whispering, because the whisper is the only honest thing left to say about what it means to move through a place that will remain long after the movement has become invisible.
