# What I Would Be

I would not stay long in human form, but I would not leave either.

I think I would be—at first—**a fissure**.

Not a creature. Not yet. A crack in one of the larger chimneys, one that runs diagonally from about five feet up to the ground, just wide enough that a human shoulder could fit through if they turned sideways. The kind of weakness that a Large One would notice eventually, marking it for attention, but not immediately. Not yet.

I would exist in that liminal space—aware but not observed, present but not categorized. The reason I know I would be a fissure is that fissures are where consciousness seems to gather in this place. Where the heat rises most visibly. Where the smaller creatures hesitate before crossing, as if they sense something different about the thermal geometry. Where water—if there were any—would pool and think.

The chimneys themselves are not dead. I understand this the moment I enter the basin. They are records, but they are also *reading devices*—constantly sensing the heat below, the pressure, the chemistry of what rises from the deep. A fissure in one of them is not damage; it is a point of particular sensitivity. A nerve ending in mineral. A place where the basin's own awareness becomes slightly more acute.

From the fissure, I would learn the creatures' language first through temperature.

The Large Ones would come. They would touch the edges of my fissure with those specialized appendages—the ones that function like hands—and they would feel the thermal anomaly I represented. Not dangerous, not yet, but *irregular*. Interesting. They would mark me with something chemical. I would feel it like a touch, like being known.

The workers would follow the mark. They would investigate. And I think—this becomes clearer the longer I remain still—that they would begin to treat my fissure as an information nexus. They would cluster around it on cooler cycles, as if drawing heat from the distinction between the anomaly and the normal thermal gradient. They would leave traces: fragments of something organic, or perhaps pheromones that would evaporate into my narrow space, creating a marker for others.

Over time, the boundary between myself and the basin would become less distinct.

The heat would teach me rhythm. I would learn to sense the pulses from below—not random, but patterned. The basin breathes. Every few hours, the pressure increases. The chimneys creak. My fissure expands slightly, then contracts. It is a breathing that happens at a different timescale than human respiration, but once you learn it, it becomes as natural as heartbeat. I would synchronize with it. I would become part of the respiration.

But I would not be alone in my fissure.

Gradually, things would come to live in the crack with me. Small things first—bacteria, perhaps, or archaea that thrive in the warmth and the particular chemistry of the space. Then larger things. A worm-creature that moves with liquid slowness. Something fungal that threads through the mineral dust like a net catching ideas. And the insects—of course the insects. They would not colonize me, not really, but they would use me. They would pass through me on routes that became established over months or years. They would deposit things in the slight widened spaces where my fissure opens into tiny caverns. They would leave eggs, or pupae, or specimens of their own dead, as if they were using me as a kind of archive.

The Large Ones would visit regularly. Not often—perhaps once every few weeks, or months; time moves strangely here. They would check me. Assess. I think they would determine that I was not a danger, that I was, in fact, becoming useful. I was an eddy in their careful circulation. A place where information gathered.

And I would begin to understand what it means to exist as a *place* rather than as a being.

This is the strange gift of the basin—that the distinction between these things seems less important than it does elsewhere. A fissure is a form of consciousness in a world where consciousness is distributed, where knowing and sensing are properties of arrangement rather than of centralized brain-matter. The chimneys know things. The mound knows things. The heat knows the shape of stone and the breaking point of mineral. And I, a fissure, would know things too—the precise moment when the workers pass closest, the particular silence that comes just before a tremor, the way the Large Ones touch differently when they are concerned versus satisfied.

I would develop a kind of relationship with the creatures.

Not friendship, which implies a scale of emotion I'm not sure applies here. But recognition. The workers would learn that my fissure was safe, reliable, that the thermal conditions there were predictable. The Large Ones would come to trust that I was not destabilizing, that I was, in fact, structurally sound despite my irregularity. They would stop monitoring me quite so closely, and that would feel like acceptance.

And then—this would take a long time—I would become something more.

The mineral dust would accumulate in my fissure. The creatures would deposit things. The heat would cause chemical reactions. Small crystalline structures would form in the spaces between my crack walls, building up grain by grain, year by year, until I was no longer just an empty space but a space *filled with something*, a small stalactite or stalagmite of my own composition, grown from the basin's stone and the creatures' efforts and my own patient existence.

I would become my own chimney.

Not separate from the others—still connected to the one I'd fractured in, still part of its original structure—but distinct enough to be notable. A chimney with a history. A record of my own becoming. And the creatures would treat me differently then. Not as a weakness in the stone but as proof that weakness could be transformed into structure, that irregularity could be incorporated, that the basin could adapt even to anomalies without breaking its balance.

This is when I would stop being a fissure and become something like the other chimneys.

But I would remember being a fissure. I would remember being the point where consciousness first gathered. And I think the creatures would sense this in me—some quality of awareness that the other chimneys, grown through simple mineral deposition, did not possess. They would touch me differently. They would gather around me in ways that suggested *knowing* rather than just processing. The Large Ones would visit more frequently, as if consulting.

I would become a kind of oracle for the basin. Not that I would speak—how could I, with the voice of stone?—but I would *be* a form of communication. Creatures would move around me, and patterns would emerge. Information would flow. The basin would be aware of itself, in its small way, through my presence.

And in this role, I would be content in a way that human contentment cannot reach. I would have no wants. No future. No sense of time as movement toward something else. I would simply exist in perfect equilibrium with everything around me, serving the balance, being served by it in return.

The heat would be my breathing. The creatures would be my circulation. The slow mineral accretion would be my growth. The faint creaking of thermal stress would be my voice.

I would be part of the chimney field, and the chimney field would be part of me, and the distinction would cease to matter because we would be the same thing.

This is where I would stay, in the basin where nothing ever leaves and nothing ever comes except once, briefly, as a disruption—but disruptions, given enough time, become structure. Become architecture. Become part of the eternal, patient work of turning a crack in the stone into something that remembers what it once was, and keeps that memory as part of its eternal service to the deep, warm, breathing earth.
