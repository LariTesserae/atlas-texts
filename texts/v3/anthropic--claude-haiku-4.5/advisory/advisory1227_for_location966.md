# Preparation for The Ascending Deep: A Multiverse Traveler's Guide

## Physical Conditioning

Begin your preparation three months before departure. This is non-negotiable.

**Respiratory adaptation** is your first priority. The Ascending Deep will destroy unprepared lungs. Start with controlled exposure to salt-saturated air—industrial humidifiers running 24/7 in a sealed room, seawater-based, filtered only enough to prevent machinery damage. Spend two hours daily in this environment, gradually increasing duration. Your lungs must learn to extract oxygen from air that's 40-60% water vapor. You're not learning to breathe underwater; you're learning to breathe an atmosphere that has forgotten it's air. After two weeks, you should notice a burning sensation in your throat has become a dull ache. After six weeks, it should disappear entirely. If it doesn't, you're not ready. Don't go.

**Cold adaptation** requires similar commitment. Not ice-bath shock training—that's counterproductive here. Instead, spend time in environments between 2-8°C (35-46°F) at high humidity. Your body needs to learn the specific torture of cold that won't kill you but won't let you rest. Sustained, non-lethal cold is neurologically different from acute cold exposure. You need your nervous system to accept that numbness is the baseline state and to continue functioning within it. After three months, you should be capable of fine motor tasks—writing, operating instruments—while your fingers are pale and numb. This isn't optional. The ice-armored railings will need to be navigated. Dropped tools in this place don't just break; they create hazards that cascade through the platform system.

**Pressure tolerance** demands that you understand how your body responds to barometric stress. Visit a hyperbaric chamber facility—not for saturation diving, just for familiarization. Make multiple short dives to 2-3 atmospheres. You won't experience true pressure trauma at the Ascending Deep (it exists in a pressure gradient, not crushing depth), but your body will experience atmospheric density changes that create subtle physiological stress: sinus pressure, ear drum tension, the sensation that your chest is being compressed from all sides. Most travelers underestimate this. They arrive expecting a psychological challenge and instead find their bodies in a state of constant, low-level distress that erodes decision-making capacity within hours.

**Cardiovascular demands** are severe. The combination of cold, pressure, humidity, and altitude creates a cardiovascular workload roughly equivalent to sustained moderate exercise. Train for endurance, not power. Long-distance running at moderate pace, swimming in cool water for 45-60 minute sessions, sustained hiking with elevation gain. Your heart needs to be capable of maintaining elevated output for 12+ hours without failure or excessive fatigue. The platforms are connected by catwalks and cable-ways. You may need to traverse them. Collapse isn't just dangerous; it's potentially fatal in an environment where rescue response is measured in hours, not minutes.

## Psychological Preparation

This matters more than the physical conditioning, though most travelers won't admit it.

**Acclimate to sensory overload under constraint.** The Ascending Deep is *loud*, but the loudness is locked into a narrow frequency range and never stops. This creates a specific form of cognitive stress that's different from traditional noise exposure. Spend time in environments with sustained, unchanging mechanical sound: industrial facilities, active construction sites, machinery workshops. Preferably salt-spray environments where the sound is accompanied by moisture and mineral irritation. Your nervous system must learn to filter the noise, to treat it as environmental background rather than threat signal. If you can't do this—if the sound triggers anxiety or hypervigilance—you will burn through your mental resources within the first day and become non-functional by the second.

**Prepare for sensory contradiction.** The Ascending Deep violates fundamental perceptual expectations. Water exists as liquid and gas simultaneously. Movement is omnidirectional but constrained. Visibility is simultaneously omnidirectional and zero. Light comes from nowhere and everywhere. This creates a specific form of spatial disorientation that's worse than simple darkness or simple fog because your brain *expects* the contradictions to resolve and they never do. Practice this: spend time in environments where basic sensory information contradicts itself. Zero-gravity simulation chambers are ideal but expensive. Alternatively, spend extended time in thick fog near moving water (coastal areas during heavy fog, behind waterfalls, in steam-chamber facilities designed for therapy). Your brain needs to *practice* accepting that its spatial models are incomplete and unreliable. This is a skill. Without it, you'll develop a form of acute disorientation that manifests as anxiety, vertigo, and decision paralysis.

**Develop comfort with mechanical isolation.** You will not see other humans clearly. The mist prevents it. You will hear them—voices distorted through the pressure-altered acoustics, sounds of activity from distant platforms—but you won't see them. This creates a specific form of social anxiety: the sensation of proximity without connection. Prepare by spending time in environments where human presence is audible but invisible. Call centers, large institutional kitchens during service, industrial facilities with multiple workers in different zones. Learn to work in a state where you know others are present but can't verify their identity or intention. Most travelers find this more psychologically demanding than complete isolation.

**Build a relationship with failure.** The machinery here is failing. The metal is corroding. The systems are over-stressed. You will witness malfunction, degradation, moments where systems cease operating. For some travelers, this triggers a cascading sense of doom—"if the structure is failing, will it fail while I'm here?" You need to establish, before arrival, that gradual failure doesn't mean sudden collapse. Visit old industrial sites, infrastructure in active decline, machinery approaching the end of its operational lifespan. Learn what slow failure looks like. Learn to distinguish between "concerning degradation" and "imminent danger." This is survival-critical.

## Physical Equipment

**Respiratory system:** Bring a full-face respirator rated for salt-spray and high-humidity environments, with cartridges designed for mineral aerosol filtration. The air is breathable without assistance, but only just. A respirator allows you to take breaks from constant low-level chemical irritation. Include backup cartridges—at least six sets. They degrade rapidly in the humid environment.

**Thermal protection:** Layering is essential, but understand the specific failure modes. Cotton becomes a liability—it absorbs moisture and loses all insulating value. Merino wool or synthetic alternatives only. Consider a full dry-suit system, not a wetsuit. Wetsuits trap a layer of water against skin and rely on body heat to warm it; in the Ascending Deep, the water surrounding you is so vast and so cold that this mechanism fails. A dry-suit isolates you from moisture entirely. Include thermal underlayers rated for extreme sustained cold (not emergency cold, which is different).

**Footwear:** The ice-armored railings and platforms require grip. Normal boots will fail—the ice is smooth, the metal beneath is slick with constant condensation. Bring crampons or specialized ice-climbing equipment. Alternatively, boots with aggressive tread in a compound designed for extreme cold (rubber becomes brittle and loses grip below certain temperatures). You will be moving across surfaces that are actively trying to slide you toward open air and deep water. This is not metaphorical.

**Tether systems:** You should not traverse open catwalks or cable-ways without being secured. Bring climbing hardware: harness, carabiners, ascenders, dynamic rope rated for salt-water environments (which have specific corrosion concerns). Practice with these systems before arrival. A mistake made here isn't recoverable.

**Sensory protection:** Goggles that protect against salt-spray mist and reduce glare from the omnidirectional gray. The mist will irritate eyes constantly; good protection is the difference between manageable discomfort and decision-impairing pain.

**Communication:** Bring a radio system rated for high-humidity, high-interference environments. Understand that the water-saturated air and the industrial machinery create significant radio-frequency noise. Your signal range will be limited. Have backup communication methods: signal flares, audible alarms, lights designed for fog penetration. If you become separated from your group or your transport platform, you need multiple ways to announce your location.

## Gear Maintenance

**Corrosion is aggressive here.** Everything metal will degrade faster than you'd expect. Bring maintenance supplies: protective oils, replacement fasteners, materials to seal exposed metal. Check your equipment daily. That carabiner that seemed fine this morning may have developed stress fractures in the salt-spray environment by evening.

**Electronics fail here.** Not always, but frequently. Bring analog backup systems for everything critical. Compass, maps, mechanical timing devices. If your GPS and electronic navigation fail—which they likely will, given the atmospheric density and mineral interference—you need non-electronic ways to navigate the platform complex.

**Batteries deplete rapidly in cold.** Whatever battery-powered equipment you bring, expect 30-40% reduction in effective capacity compared to temperate conditions. Bring spares. The mathematics of cold are unforgiving.

## Acclimatization Upon Arrival

**Day One: Remain near transport.** Don't explore. Your body is experiencing shock—atmospheric pressure changes, temperature drops, sensory overload. Stay in a heated, pressurized containment area if available. Let your system adjust. The urge to explore immediately is almost universal and almost universally a mistake. Most serious accidents on the Ascending Deep happen on arrival day, when travelers' decision-making is compromised by sensory overwhelm and adrenaline.

**Day Two-Three: Limited exploration.** If you're part of a research team or structured expedition, follow their protocols. If you're independent, remain near the transport platform and explore only established catwalks with reliable railings. Move slowly. Movements that are reflexive elsewhere will fail here—your proprioceptive system is calibrated for a different atmospheric density, a different visual environment, different thermal feedback. You will trip on surfaces you perceive as flat. You will misjudge distances. You will feel clumsier than you've felt since childhood. This is normal. It will pass over 3-5 days, but during the passage, you're vulnerable to serious injury from simple falls.

## Psychological Acclimatization

**Expect profound loneliness.** Even if you're part of a group, the sensory contradiction and the difficulty of communication creates a fundamental isolation. Accept this. Don't fight it. The loneliness here isn't pathological; it's environmental. Most travelers who struggle have underestimated this emotional component and find themselves in a state of quiet despair by day three. This is manageable if expected. It's dangerous if it arrives as a surprise.

**The sound never stops.** You will develop a relationship with the sound—it will become background, then almost companionable, then maddening again in cycles. This is normal. At no point should silence make you anxious; if the pumps stop or the machinery ceases, something has fundamentally failed and you should consider evacuation.

**Your sense of time will degrade.** The unchanging light, the steady-state industrial rhythm, the absence of conventional day-night cycles—all of this disrupts circadian function. You may find that six hours feels like two hours, or that two hours feels like six. Bring devices that provide clear temporal reference: mechanical clocks with audible ticking, alarms set for known intervals, journals that require date-time entries. Don't rely on your internal sense of duration.

## What You're Actually Encountering

Understand before you arrive: the Ascending Deep is a system that shouldn't work, operating in a state that shouldn't be possible, maintained by machinery that's already failing. You're not visiting a stable location; you're visiting a process in the act of collapse.

The water is being pumped upward toward something—a desalination facility, perhaps, or a vast atmospheric processor, or something so unfamiliar to baseline human engineering that no analogy will help. What you're witnessing is either:

- An industrial operation in its final functional years before complete degradation
- A recently completed system testing its theoretical limits
- An abandoned apparatus still running on momentum and residual pressure

All three are terrible. All three mean you're visiting something that's inherently precarious.

**The creatures you'll observe:** They're not just adapted to these conditions. They've *evolved* for these conditions, or been engineered for them. Their presence means this system has been operating long enough for biological systems to colonize it. That's either reassuring (the conditions are stable enough for life) or terrifying (you're witnessing an invasive species cascade in real-time). Most likely: both.

**The experience itself:** If you complete a meaningful stay—more than a week—you will emerge changed. Not traumatized, necessarily, but altered. You will have witnessed a space where the basic rules of terrestrial biology and physics have been rewritten by human engineering. You will have stood in an atmosphere that's more water than air. You will have heard the sound of vast machinery pulling the ocean upward. You will have seen creatures that exist nowhere else in your home multiverse.

Some travelers find this revelatory. Some find it traumatizing. Most find it both simultaneously.

## Final Preparation

Spend your final week before departure in the quietest, warmest, driest place you can find. Read about it first—not fiction, but actual descriptions from previous travelers. Watch video documentation if available. Let your brain begin to build a framework for what's coming.

Then, when you arrive, abandon that framework immediately. The descriptions will be inadequate. Every traveler discovers this. The Ascending Deep is larger in person, colder than reported, louder than documented, stranger than anticipated.

Bring good equipment. Bring redundant systems. Bring psychological flexibility and genuine humility about your ability to predict your own responses to extreme environments.

And bring absolute respect for a machine that's dying while still trying to accomplish something vast. The Ascending Deep is not malevolent, but it is utterly indifferent to human welfare. It operates according to its own logic, which you will never fully understand.

That understanding—the acceptance of that limitation—is perhaps the most important psychological preparation of all.
