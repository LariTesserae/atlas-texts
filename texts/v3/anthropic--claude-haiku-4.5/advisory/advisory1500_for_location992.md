# A Preparation Guide for the Cavernous Market of Teth

## Physical Conditioning

Begin your preparation months in advance. Your lungs must adapt to air that is saturated and mineral-rich in ways surface air is not. Practice breathing exercises in steam rooms or humid environments—not to become comfortable with moisture, but to train your respiratory system to process it without stress. The oxygen density is likely normal, but your body's *perception* of breathing will change in an environment where every inhalation carries particulate matter and water vapor. Your sinuses will work differently here.

Your eyes will need acclimation more than you expect. Spend time in dimly lit spaces—not complete darkness, but the half-light of early dusk or interior rooms lit only by candlelight. Learn to navigate without depending on sharp visual detail. The market will demand that you read situations through shadow, through the peripheral sense of movement rather than direct sight. Your pupils will dilate further than they typically do; prepare them for this. The adjustment period, where everything feels slightly unreal and colors seem washed away, will last several hours. Plan for this disorientation.

Your skin must be conditioned. The relentless dampness will affect you in ways dry-climate visitors underestimate. Moisture wicks through fabric constantly. Your clothing will never fully dry while you're in the market. More importantly, your skin will soften, especially on your feet and hands—areas prone to maceration in wet environments. This isn't dangerous if you've prepared, but the physical sensation of perpetually damp skin is profoundly unsettling if you're unprepared for it. Spend time in humid environments. Learn to recognize the feeling of clothing clinging to your body as simply *the baseline condition* rather than an aberration. Practice walking on damp surfaces until your gait becomes stable and automatic rather than cautious.

Your feet deserve particular attention. The worn slate floor is not slippery, but it is *yielding* in subtle ways—water pooling in grooves creates uneven surfaces. Your typical stride will be wrong here. Practice walking on wet stone. Develop an understanding of where your weight should distribute. The merchants navigate this space without thought; you will need to practice until it becomes similarly automatic. Consider that many visitors to the market walk these passages daily, sometimes multiple times daily. Your unfamiliar gait will mark you as an outsider immediately.

Your equilibrium will be tested. The constant sound of water, the lack of natural light cues, the enclosed space—these create a mild sensory deprivation that can trigger balance issues in sensitive individuals. If you have any history of vertigo, dizziness, or spatial disorientation, discuss this with a physician before traveling. The market itself is perfectly stable; the disorientation, if it comes, will be purely neurological. But it can be intense. Some visitors report a floating sensation or a peculiar sense that the stone walls are gently tilting, even though they rationally know this isn't true.

## Respiratory Preparation

The air quality is not poor—the natural circulation prevents stale conditions—but it is *different*. You will be breathing air that has passed through decomposing vegetation, mineral dust, animal respiration, and oil smoke. The smell is not unpleasant once you acclimate, but the initial exposure can trigger mild nausea in some individuals.

Arrive well-fed. Do not attempt to visit the market on an empty stomach. The combination of humidity, enclosed space, and unfamiliar air can suppress appetite and create mild gastric distress. Eat something substantial before descending. The merchants sell food—dried fruits, seeds, roots preserved in something that might be brine—but you should not rely on purchasing sustenance during your first visit.

Bring water. The market itself has water—likely drawn from the underground stream—but you should not depend on accessing it easily during your first visit. The merchants know the sources and the routes to them; you do not. Dehydration combined with the humid environment creates a false sense of being hydrated; you will drink less than you should because your thirst signals are muted by the moisture-laden air. Bring more water than you think you'll need.

## Psychological Preparation

This is the critical phase that most visitors underestimate.

The market is a place of *enclosure*. You will be underground. The stone ceiling, though lost in shadow, is real and present. Many people who have no claustrophobic tendencies whatsoever experience significant psychological strain in environments where the sky is replaced by stone. Spend time preparing for this. Sit in basement rooms. Learn to recognize the sensation of weight above you—not as threatening, but as *present*. Breathe through it.

The market is also a place of *permanent twilight*. Humans have circadian rhythms tied to light cycles. When you remove those cues, the mind becomes slightly untethered. You may experience time distortion—a visit that was objectively three hours may feel like either thirty minutes or three days, depending on your neurological response to the muted light. This is temporary and not dangerous, but it can be disorienting. Bring a timepiece and check it occasionally. Anchor yourself to objective time rather than relying on your intuitive sense of duration.

The dense, orderly chaos of the market can trigger anxiety in people who expect clear spatial organization. Everything is dark and crowded and interconnected. The narrow passages feel like a maze, though they are in fact quite logical once you learn the pattern. This knowledge doesn't help your initial experience. You will feel lost even though you are not in any real danger. Accept this feeling. Don't fight it. The merchants are watching; they understand that outsiders navigate with uncertainty. You will not be harmed for looking confused.

The constant presence of other people—the ambient murmur of dozens of conversations, the brushing past of bodies in narrow corridors, the sense of being surrounded—can trigger what some individuals describe as a mild crowd anxiety. This isn't the panic of large crowds, but something subtler: the awareness that you cannot easily leave, that you are dependent on moving through spaces dominated by people who belong here and you don't. Spend time in moderately crowded environments before your visit. Practice moving through spaces where you don't control the flow. Learn to accept proximity to strangers without tension.

The market smells. The primary components—mineral dust, water, fermented vegetation, oil smoke—combine into an odor that is distinctive and, to most visitors, somewhat unpleasant initially. Olfactory adaptation happens relatively quickly, usually within twenty minutes to an hour. But during that first period, the smell can trigger nausea or a mild sense of revulsion. Prepare for this. Understand that the sensation will pass. Do not attempt to mask the smell with strong perfumes or aromatic materials; this will only create additional olfactory conflict.

## Practical Preparation

Clothing must be carefully chosen. Wear layers, all of them moisture-resistant or quick-drying. Cotton will absorb water and remain damp; synthetic fabrics are superior here. Your clothing will be uncomfortable if wet, but it will be actively harmful if it stays wet long-term and creates conditions for fungal or bacterial growth on your skin.

Wear long sleeves and long pants. The pale roaches and other insects in the market are not hostile, but exposed skin invites unnecessary contact. The insects won't harm you, but the psychological stress of feeling them crawl across bare skin can be significant.

Wear footwear with good grip. The slate is not slippery, but it is wet. Your boots should have treads designed for wet stone, not smooth soles. Many long-term visitors to the market wear specialized boots with particular sole patterns that have evolved over generations—you won't have access to these, but you should choose your footwear accordingly.

Bring a small lamp or lantern of your own, though you likely won't need to use it. The hanging lamps provide sufficient light to navigate. But the psychological comfort of having your own light source—something you control—is significant. Many visitors find that simply carrying a lamp (lit or unlit) reduces anxiety measurably.

Bring a cloth or handkerchief for your face. When you're adjusting to the humidity and the air quality, having something you can press to your nose provides psychological relief, even if it doesn't actually help with respiration. The ritual of it matters.

Bring payment in a form the merchants will recognize. If you don't know the local currency, bring something of obvious value—rare materials, metals, items that don't deteriorate in humid environments. Barter is likely acceptable, but understanding the market's economic system before you arrive will reduce stress and confusion.

## Navigation and Social Preparation

The merchants will watch you. This is not threatening; it is simply how the market operates. Unfamiliar faces are noted, tracked, understood in relation to typical customer patterns. Do not attempt to hide your foreignness. Accept that you will move slowly, that you will seem uncertain, that you will likely stop and reorient yourself multiple times. This is normal. Visitors do this.

The passages themselves have logic, though you may not perceive it immediately. Generally, the main thoroughfares are wider and better-lit. The side passages lead to specific stall clusters. If you become confused, moving toward larger open spaces will generally guide you toward the major commercial areas. The dead ends are rare and obvious; you will not find yourself trapped.

Learn a few phrases in the local language before you arrive. At minimum: "How much?", "What is this?", "Where is [specific good]?", and "Thank you." The merchants appreciate any effort to communicate in their language, even if your accent is terrible and your grammar is wrong. It signals respect for their space.

Do not touch goods without permission. The items in the market—the dried fruits, the fibrous plants, the cloth bundles—are merchandise. Handle them only if invited to do so by the merchant. This is both practical (goods can be damaged by careless handling) and cultural (the market has its own norms about transaction and exchange).

## Temporal Considerations

Plan to spend at least three to four hours in the market during your first visit, even if you don't intend to make major purchases. This allows your nervous system to acclimate, your eyes to adjust, your sense of orientation to stabilize. Attempting a quick visit—arriving, purchasing something, and leaving within an hour—will leave you in a state of perpetual disorientation and stress. You will miss what makes the place remarkable.

Visit when the market is moderately busy, not at peak times. Overly crowded conditions will exacerbate spatial anxiety. But visiting during very quiet times creates a different kind of unease—the sense that the market is watching you, that your presence is being actively noted and evaluated. Moderate activity provides the psychological comfort of being an anonymous presence in a functioning community.

Plan to have a rest period after your visit. Acclimating to a fundamentally alien environment—even one that is safe and welcoming—is neurologically taxing. You will be tired in ways that aren't purely physical. Expect to spend several hours in a quiet space, ideally with natural light and air circulation, after you leave the market.

## Emotional Preparation

Understand that you may feel emotional while in the market, even if nothing emotionally significant is happening. The combination of sensory novelty, spatial disorientation, crowding, and environmental stress can trigger emotional responses that seem disproportionate to circumstances. You might feel unexpectedly sad, anxious, or even joyful. These emotions are real and valid, but they are partially generated by your neurological response to the environment rather than by objective circumstances.

This is not weakness. It is a normal response to a genuinely unusual space.

You may also experience a peculiar sense of *timelessness* while in the market. The lack of natural light cues, the repetitive nature of market activity, the sense that this place has existed in this configuration for centuries and will continue to do so—these create a psychological state where you feel slightly outside of normal time. Some visitors find this deeply peaceful. Others find it disturbing. Prepare yourself for the possibility that your sense of progression and change will be muted.

## The Fundamental Truth

The Cavernous Market of Teth is not dangerous. It is not hostile. It is not a place designed to test or harm visitors. It is a functioning community market that happens to exist in an unusual location. The merchants are not exotic or alien—they are people engaged in ordinary commerce. The space is stable and has been maintained for centuries.

But it is *strange*. It is strange to your body, strange to your senses, strange to your psychological expectations of what "normal" space should be. The market will challenge your assumptions about light, sound, smell, proximity, and time. It will require you to process information in ways your senses are not optimally designed for.

This challenge is not inherently negative. Many visitors find that moving through the market, successfully navigating its passages, completing transactions, experiencing its particular ecology—they find all of this genuinely beautiful. The market has an integrity and a coherence that comes from existing in equilibrium for so long. It is a space made by human hands that has become something almost natural. It is worth the discomfort of acclimation.

Prepare thoroughly. Respect the environment and the people who inhabit it. Breathe. Let your eyes adjust. Listen to the water. Accept the dampness. Move slowly. And in the half-light of the hanging lamps, you will begin to understand why people maintain this market, why it endures, why it is worth the descent into permanent twilight to reach it.
