# Preparing for the Ascending Dry

## Physical Preparation

**Respiratory Conditioning**
Begin training weeks in advance. Spend time at genuine high altitude—if accessible, 12,000+ feet—or use altitude simulation chambers. Your lungs need to develop compensatory breathing patterns before arrival. The thinness here is *continuous*, not something your body will easily adapt to even with time. Practice slow, deliberate breathing; shallow panic-breathing will exhaust you dangerously fast. Learn to recognize early signs of altitude sickness: the disorientation you'll experience here might be medical, might be environmental strangeness, and you need to distinguish between them. Bring supplemental oxygen bottles, though understand that breathing supplemented air in a place this alien might be its own kind of disorienting.

**Skin and Mucous Membrane Protection**
The desiccation is aggressive. Industrial-grade moisturizing creams won't cut it—bring medical-grade barrier creams typically used for severe dermatitis, the kind that create actual waterproof seals. Apply them obsessively: hands, face, lips, any exposed skin. The mineral mist will work into cracks in your skin and won't leave easily. Bring a humidifier designed for personal use, even though electricity sources are unclear. Lip balm with SPF 50+. Saline nasal rinse solutions to prevent your nasal passages from drying to the point of bleeding. The air will pull moisture from mucous membranes and you'll feel it like a low-level burn after hours of exposure.

**Adaptation Timeline**
Plan for a 7-10 day acclimation period where you do almost nothing. Simply exist. Let your body calibrate to the altitude, the mineral composition of the air, the light cycle (however strange it is). Physical capability drops sharply the first few days—expect to move slowly, to tire quickly, to feel a constant low-level malaise. This is normal. Don't fight it. The people here move without haste because rapid movement in this environment is metabolically wasteful.

**Clothing and Footwear**
Bring layers despite the apparent lack of temperature extremes. The place feels thermally stable but your body's heat regulation will be confused by the altitude and desiccation. Loose, light-colored fabrics that reflect the strange light. Multiple pairs of high-quality hiking boots with excellent ankle support—the terrain is unforgiving, the stone is sharper than it looks, and an ankle injury here means you're functionally immobilized. Bring backup footwear. The crystalline surfaces are harder than normal rock and will wear through standard soles faster.

Bring a wide-brimmed hat. The light is sourceless but the UV exposure seems high despite the filtered quality of the illumination. Your eyes will hurt if you don't protect them.

**Hydration and Nutrition**
Pack water far in excess of what you think you'll need. 6-8 liters per day minimum for the first weeks. The dehydration isn't just thirst-based—it's happening at a cellular level. Electrolyte supplements are essential. Your body will be working harder than you realize at altitude to perform basic functions.

For food: bring high-calorie, mineral-dense provisions. Nuts, dried fruits, protein bars, whole grains. Avoid anything that requires elaborate preparation. The settlements might provide something (this is genuinely unclear from the description), but you cannot rely on this. Do not attempt to derive nutrition from the mineral environment until and unless you've observed the residents doing so or been explicitly instructed.

## Psychological Preparation

**Reality Calibration**
This place will confuse your sense of ground-truth. The light behaves strangely. The horizon suggests artificiality. Time doesn't work normally. Prepare yourself mentally for cognitive dissonance at a constant low hum. This isn't paranoia—the description itself admits that reality feels thin here. 

Before you go, spend weeks contemplating the idea that you might not understand the basic rules of what you're experiencing. What if the people aren't actually people? What if the settlements have purposes you can't recognize? What if the "stone" is something else entirely? Don't go in looking for answers. Go in prepared to suspend your need for them.

**Silence Tolerance**
The quiet here will be profound in ways that might trigger anxiety or unreality. Spend time in genuine silence at home—not meditation silence, but the specific kind of silence where your own bodily sounds become overwhelming. Sound-proof rooms, very early mornings in forests, underwater with a snorkel. Get comfortable with the sound of your own heartbeat, your own breathing, your own swallowing. In the Ascending Dry, these internal sounds will be your primary acoustic reference point.

**Pattern Recognition Management**
You will see patterns that might not exist. The human brain is built to find meaning, especially in alien environments. The "pulsing" sky, the "arranged" settlements, the suspicion that reality is being "rendered"—some of this is real observation, and some of this will be your pattern-recognition systems running on overdrive in a place that doesn't match your internal models.

Prepare a cognitive discipline: when you catch yourself inferring meaning, pause. Write it down. Observe whether the pattern repeats independently or only when you're looking for it. Learn the difference between genuine strangeness and your own interpretive artifacts.

**The Feeling of Maintenance**
You will develop a conviction that something is being maintained here, that you're part of a system with purposes you don't understand, that your presence might be required or might be disruption. This feeling will be almost certainly grounded in real observation—something *is* being maintained. But that doesn't mean you understand what it is or what your role is.

Go in with explicit permission to be useless. You don't need to understand the system to exist within it. You don't need to know your function to be present without disruption.

## Practical Preparation

**Communication Difficulty**
The residents here are sparse and they speak softly, to each other and presumably to themselves. Language might not be the barrier you expect—they might understand you fine. But they operate on time scales you don't. Questions that require immediate answers won't work here. Instead, prepare to ask things that can be answered through demonstration, through gesture, through shared observation.

Bring a journal. Written communication might be clearer than verbal, and the act of writing forces you to organize thoughts that the place itself seems designed to dissolve.

**Observation Protocol**
Establish a routine of documentation without intrusion. Pick a settlement node and observe it for 3-4 hour blocks, several times daily (accounting for the unclear light cycle). Record: what people do, when they do it, whether it repeats, what objects they interact with, how they move through space. Don't interview them yet. Don't interact. Document until patterns emerge.

**Ethics Boundary**
Decide in advance what you will and won't do. Will you accept food or water from the residents? Will you enter their structures? Will you photograph or record? Will you touch the stone formations? These decisions need to be made with a clear head before you arrive, because the place will erode your certainty about what's acceptable or safe.

The residents aren't hostile, but they aren't welcoming either. They're patient. That patience is different from hospitality, and you need to understand the distinction.

## The Deeper Preparation

**Metaphysical Grounding**
Consider what the Ascending Dry might represent. A system in stasis? A maintenance state? An ecosystem that operates on principles you don't have vocabulary for? Don't go looking for metaphor—but do go prepared to understand that ordinary physics, ordinary biology, ordinary time might not fully apply here.

Read about extremophile organisms, about crystalline matrices and lattice structures, about how minerals can hold energy over geological time scales. This won't prepare you for what you'll encounter, but it will expand your reference frame for "possible."

**Accept Impermanence of Understanding**
You will not fully understand the Ascending Dry. You might develop working models, useful heuristics, local knowledge. But the fundamental *why*—why it exists, how it maintains itself, what process generates the subtle wrongness in the light and the horizon—this might remain opaque.

Go prepared to be comfortable with mystery. Go prepared to observe without requiring explanation. This might be the most important preparation of all.

## On Arrival

When you first arrive and feel the thin air enter your lungs, when you see the horizon pulse slightly at the edge of your vision, when the silence presses down like weight—this is when you'll know whether you're actually ready.

You probably won't be. No one is, for a place like this.

But the settlement will be there, patient and arranged and ancient, waiting to maintain its equilibrium around your presence. Step carefully. Breathe slowly. Watch more than you speak.

Time here is long. You have it.
