# Preparing for the Trellis Gardens: A Traveler's Guide

## Physical Conditioning & Health

**Begin months before departure.** Your respiratory system will be your primary vulnerability. The air is not hostile, but it is *thick*—saturated with moisture, spore-loaded, dense with the byproducts of active decomposition and growth. If you have any history of respiratory sensitivity, asthma, or compromised lungs, reconsider this journey. For others: practice breathing exercises in humid environments. Take hot showers with the bathroom sealed and breathe deeply. Gradually acclimate.

Your skin will be perpetually damp. The dampness is neither cold nor warm; it simply *is*, constant as gravity. Bring antifungal treatments—not because the environment is hostile, but because extended exposure to this saturation invites fungal colonization on skin folds. Your feet especially: they will weep. Dry socks exist in theory only here. Accept this before you arrive.

Your sinuses will feel pressurized. The moisture saturation affects inner ear equilibrium slightly. Plan for three days of mild disorientation. Bring decongestants, but recognize they're palliative only—you're adapting to a different air density, and chemistry can only mediate so much.

Strengthen your legs. The lattice flooring is deceptively treacherous—warped, soft, with gaps that will catch boot heels. You'll be walking slowly, carefully, testing each step. The exertion is low-intensity but prolonged. Expect to move through the space at perhaps a quarter of normal walking speed, constantly adjusting balance on uneven boards.

## Clothing & Protective Equipment

**Cotton is wrong.** Cotton holds moisture like a sponge and never dries. Bring moisture-wicking base layers, the kind designed for perpetual-sweat environments (athletic or diving undergarments work). Wear them. Accept that you will not feel dry again during your visit.

**Bring two complete outfits.** You cannot rotate clothing here; anything you wear will become waterlogged. Plan to change at the mid-point of your stay. Pack both sets sealed in waterproof bags until needed.

**Footwear is critical.** Not hiking boots—they'll rot. Bring specialized footwear designed for wet environments: either neoprene diving booties (excellent traction on slick surfaces, quick-drying) or purpose-built water shoes. Your feet will be perpetually damp. Accept this. Bring at least four pairs of thick wool socks; they'll feel wet, but wool maintains thermal regulation even when saturated.

**A light respirator.** Not a gas mask—you're not protecting against toxins. A simple NIOSH-rated dust mask with activated charcoal and organic vapor cartridges will ease the transition. The spore load is not dangerous in short-term exposure, but the respiratory load is real. Many travelers report breathing easier with this minimal filtration during the first 24 hours.

**Waterproof outer layer.** Not for rain—the polycarbonate roof largely functions, and what moisture reaches you comes from below and around, not above. Wear it for the constant drip and seep-splash when you disturb foliage. A lightweight rain cape or poncho works better than a jacket; jackets create pockets of stagnant air.

**Head covering.** Not for weather, but to prevent constant water drip from the vines and moss directly into your eyes and ears as you navigate the dense zones. A wide-brimmed hat collects water but sheds it; baseball caps are worse than useless. Consider a lightweight hood.

## Psychological Preparation

**This is the most important preparation.**

The Trellis Gardens will disorient you—not physically, but conceptually. You will be inside something that is simultaneously a machine and an organism, simultaneously built and wild, simultaneously dead (the infrastructure) and explosively alive (the plants). Your mind will resist this non-binary state.

Spend time before departure meditating on processes. Not the *results* of processes, but the *process itself*—the slow, patient, grinding iteration of systems toward equilibrium. Read about succession ecology, forest development, the patience of decomposition. Understand that what you're entering is not a place but a *verb*—growth and decay happening at the same scale, simultaneously, indefinitely.

Expect the loss of linear time perception. The light here erases the circadian cues your brain uses to mark hours. Plan on feeling displaced in time—you may experience a single hour as three, or six hours as one. Bring a watch. Check it frequently, but do not be surprised when it contradicts your body's sense of duration.

Accept that you will not see "the whole" of this place. The density prevents panoramic perception. You will experience it as a series of intimate chambers, green grottos, micro-environments. The *entirety* will remain partially hidden, even after hours of exploration. This is intentional. The place resists knowing.

Prepare for a profound *silence* that is not actually silence. It is a silence *full* of sound—the drip, the occasional shift, the whisper of moisture. But there is no human sound here, no machinery noise, no indication of human intention. This absence can be deeply unsettling. Some travelers experience it as peace; others as profound loneliness. Know which category you fall into before you go.

## Navigation & Orientation

**Bring a GPS unit.** This will not provide you with good data—the dense vegetation may obscure satellite signals—but having a backup to your sense of direction is psychologically necessary. It will frequently read "error" or "signal lost." This is normal. Accept it.

**Bring a compass and mark your entry point clearly.** The space is only 30 by 15 meters, but you can become locally disoriented in the dense zones. You will not be truly lost—you cannot get lost in a bounded space of 450 square meters—but you can experience being lost, which is psychologically equivalent.

**Move slowly.** Do not rush. This place rewards patience. Set yourself a wandering pace of perhaps 0.25 miles per hour. You are not trying to "see" the Trellis Gardens; you are trying to *be* in it.

**Mark branching points in your memory.** The vegetation is dense enough that you may enter a zone and genuinely not remember which direction you came from. The space is not maze-like—it's too small for that—but it is disorienting. Periodically stop and orient yourself by remembering the light patterns, the moisture intensity, the specific vines.

## Biological Encounters

**Expect minimal visible wildlife.** The grey-backed lizards (species varies by dimensional variant) are shy and harmless. Insects are present but rarely visible; you'll sense them more than see them. Spiders are abundant; they are not aggressive and will flee from vibration.

**Fungal and microbial life is everywhere.** This is not inherently dangerous for brief exposure (24-48 hours), but be aware: you are breathing spores constantly, touching mycelium, walking on decomposing wood colonized by countless microorganisms. Your immune system will respond. Plan for a post-visit period of low-grade fatigue as your body processes the exposure. This typically resolves within a week. Bring antihistamines for any allergic response.

**Do not consume anything.** The berries, fruits, and mushrooms here are genuine, and some may be edible, but the hybrid ecology means nothing here has a predictable origin or genetic history. The passionflower vines bear fruit that *looks* edible and may even taste pleasant, but the crossing of pollination pathways means the nutritional and potentially toxic content is unknown. Assume all plant matter is potentially toxic, even if visually identical to familiar species.

**The irrigation system:** You'll encounter evidence of it everywhere—the seep-paths, the valves, the capillaries. Do not attempt to interfere with it. Do not drink from it. The water is recirculating, possibly for decades, and the microbial load is incalculable. It sustains the ecosystem; you should not.

## Sensory & Emotional Experiences to Anticipate

**Claustrophobia without walls.** You are indoors, bounded by a visible structure, yet the density of vegetation creates a sense of enclosure comparable to forest understorey. Some travelers experience this as liberating; others as suffocating. Both responses are normal.

**Loss of the outside.** The polycarbonate roof admits light but no view. You will not see the sky, the horizon, anything beyond the structure. This severing from external reference can trigger unexpected emotional responses—not fear, typically, but a kind of existential vertigo. You are in a place that is complete unto itself.

**The pressure of growth.** This is difficult to articulate, but many travelers report it: the sense that the plants are actively *growing*, that the space is slowly becoming denser, that you can almost perceive the expansion happening. This is not imaginary—the place genuinely is in active succession—but the perception of it is amplified by the moisture saturation and the ambient humidity-temperature effects on your proprioception. Your body is sensitive to microchanges in air density; the constant shifts register as an almost tactile pressure.

**The absence of intent.** There is no *planning* in the Trellis Gardens. The irrigation system operates, but to what end? The vines grow, but toward no visible goal. The decomposition proceeds, but not in service to any external purpose. Everything happens because of the logic of the system itself. This indifference can be profoundly grounding or profoundly destabilizing, depending on your psychological bent.

## Practical Logistics

**Duration:** Plan for 12-36 hours maximum. The sensory load accumulates. By hour 40-48, most travelers experience fatigue that exceeds what their physical exertion warrants. The damp, the spore load, the sensory saturation—it accumulates.

**Hydration:** Bring more water than you think necessary. You will not feel thirsty—the moisture saturation suppresses thirst signals—but you are sweating constantly. Dehydration is a genuine risk. Plan to drink 1.5 liters minimum for every 8 hours of exploration.

**Nutrition:** Bring high-calorie, shelf-stable foods. Your appetite will be suppressed. Eat anyway. The environment is doing metabolic work on your system; you need fuel. Nuts, nut butters, dried fruit, chocolate—things that don't require preparation and won't be spoiled by dampness.

**Exit strategy:** The Gardens can be exited via two routes: the service corridor at the north end, or by finding the brick-walled terminus at the south. The service corridor connects to what may be wider Keswick Station infrastructure (dimensional variant dependent). The south terminus opens to outdoors. Have both routes mentally mapped before entry.

**Post-visit:** Plan for a full day of rest after exit. Your body will feel exhausted even if you've moved minimally. Sleep will come easily and will be deep. Some travelers experience vivid dreams incorporating the moisture-sounds and the green-darkness. This is normal. The cognitive load of processing a non-standard ecosystem is significant; sleep is your brain consolidating this novel information.

## Final Guidance

The Trellis Gardens are not dangerous in the conventional sense. There is no collapse risk, no toxic atmosphere, no hostile organisms. But they are *demanding* in a subtle way—demanding on proprioception, on circadian rhythm, on the part of your brain that insists that designed spaces should have clean boundaries between construction and nature.

Go because the experience of being inside an active process—watching a building slowly become a garden, watching infrastructure and wild life find an equilibrium—is not available anywhere else. Go because the absence of human intention is itself a form of instruction.

But go prepared for strangeness. Go prepared to have your sense of boundary, of time, of purpose, gently dissolved. Go understanding that some part of you will remain there—not lost, but *diffused*, like moisture into saturation, like intent into process.

The Trellis Gardens do not give themselves up easily. But they will change you in the attempt.
