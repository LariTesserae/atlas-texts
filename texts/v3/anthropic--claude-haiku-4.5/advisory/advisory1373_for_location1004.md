# A Preparation Guide for the Drowned Expanse

## Before You Descend

**Mental Preparation is Your First Task**

You need to understand that you are not visiting a disaster. Do not pack your psychology for apocalypse. This is not a sunken city waiting to be salvaged or mourned—it is a working civilization that achieved stasis before your great-great-ancestors were born. Go in expecting ruins and you will misread everything. The resignation in a resident's face is not tragedy; it is the peace of someone who has never known otherwise.

Prepare yourself to see beauty in slowness. You have trained your eye, your ear, your sense of time in a world of change. Here, change happens in increments so small they read as permanence. A stone becoming smooth is not deterioration—it is maturation. The same cultivated algae you see on your first day will look identical on your last, yet residents will speak of variations you cannot perceive, shifts that have accumulated across your lifetime but manifest across centuries. You must retrain your intuition for what counts as *different*. Learn to read the faint language of gradual transformation, or everything will seem frozen.

**Accept the Pressure**

Not water pressure—you will be given equipment for that, or something close to it. The pressure you need to accept is *psychological*. 

The weight of depth will speak to you in your bones. Your body knows that water this deep is wrong for you, and it will tell you so constantly. The cold will whisper to you that you are dying slowly, and technically, you are—every moment in the Expanse accelerates your entropy, no matter how well-prepared you are. The darkness will press against your eyes. The silence will make you aware of the sound of your own blood in a way you've never been before.

This is normal. This does not mean you should leave. The residents live with this pressure constantly, and they are not suffering. They are simply *here*, in a way that has become complete in itself. You must learn the difference between discomfort and genuine danger, and accept that some discomfort is the price of witnessing what this place is.

## Physical Preparation

**Your Body Will Betray You**

The cold will be your first battle. Not the cold of winter—you know that cold, it passes. This cold is *ambient*, inescapable, patient. It will steal heat from your core with mathematical inevitability. You need:

- Layered garments that insulate when wet. The residents wear wrappings of treated fiber—plant-based, locally produced, designed across centuries to maximize heat retention. You will be tempted to bring modern insulation. Bring it, yes, but understand that it will not perform as expected. Water this deep seems to phase through conventional materials as if they were merely suggestions. Acquire local wrappings. Wear them the way residents wear them: overlapped, loose enough to allow water circulation, tight enough to trap dead water against your skin—paradoxically, this warm-up layer is essential.

- A mechanism for core temperature maintenance. Small chemical heat sources exist in your home multiverse—things that activate on contact with water. Rig a system to keep these against your chest. They will not keep you warm. They will slow your cooling. That is all you can ask for.

- Accept that you will be cold. This is not a problem to solve, but a condition to inhabit. The residents do not fight it; they *accommodate* it through constant gentle movement. Plan to never be still. This sounds exhausting. It becomes, after a time, meditative.

**Movement Is Survival**

Your ability to navigate water will determine the quality of your entire experience. The residents move through it with liquid grace, using techniques that would look like swimming to an untrained eye except that they never break the surface (there is no surface, not truly). There is no current to aid you. The water is nearly static, which means every movement is pure effort translated into displacement.

Before arrival, learn to move in salt-water tanks if possible. Not to prepare for speed—speed is irrelevant—but to understand the resistance. Water is denser than air; your body will require 800 times the effort per movement. Learn to make gestures economical. The residents move like they are choreographed, every motion purposeful, no wasted energy. A single gesture might take three times as long as it would in air. Adapt to this. Stop thinking of it as slowness. Start thinking of it as intentionality.

Your legs will tire. The residents' legs are phenomenally strong, honed across lifetimes. If you are a normal human, you will achieve a state of burning muscle exhaustion within hours. Plan for this. Take frequent rests on the flat stone surfaces—the courtyards, the platforms, the wide areas between structures. Sitting still will make you cold faster, but the alternative is collapse. Find the rhythm: movement until exhaustion, rest to maintain warmth through muscle tension and deliberate shivering, movement again.

**Respiration is Adaptation**

You will be given breathing apparatus. Whatever apparatus you bring from elsewhere, it will need modification. The air here is not depleted (the Expanse residents have long since evolved or engineered solutions to atmospheric exchange—air rises from below in slow convection cycles), but it *feels* wrong to breath. Your lungs expect a gaseous composition your evolution prepared you for. Here, the air holds moisture, tastes of mineral deposits, seems to have texture.

Breathe slowly. The residents' respiratory rates have adapted downward across generations—their oxygen consumption is lower than sunlit-world humans. Your body will demand faster breathing as compensation for the cold and the exertion. Resist this. Hyperventilation will make you light-headed, and light-headedness in a place where mistakes can trap you under tons of ancient stone is a luxury you cannot afford.

There will be moments where you panic. The cold will spike in your mind to *freezing*. The darkness will become total in your perception. Your lungs will feel insufficient. Prepare for this now: these moments pass. You don't need to overcome them—just endure them. Residents do this their entire lives. The panic is your body's evolutionary warning system misfiring. Acknowledge it. Do not obey it.

## Sensory Recalibration

**The Light Will Teach You How to See**

You have evolved in daylight. Your eyes contain cells that have never needed to function in near-total darkness. The bioluminescence of the Expanse will be insufficient for you initially. You will see shapes, suggestions, implications of form rather than form itself.

Bring some light source—chemical luminescence is best, something that won't suddenly fail. For the first days, use it freely. Then, deliberately, stop using it except in emergencies. Your eyes will adapt more slowly than you'd like, but they will adapt. Within a week, you will perceive details you initially missed entirely. The frost-pale algae will develop color variations. Stone will show texture. Architectural details will emerge from suggestion into clarity.

But understand: you will never see this place the way residents see it. Their eyes have adapted across generations. What reads to you as dim twilight is, to them, bright daylight. The distinction matters. They navigate by detail you cannot perceive. Watch them move, and you will see them reading the environment in ways you cannot consciously mimic. This is not a problem. It is simply the reality of being an outsider in a place built for different eyes.

The absence of color will strike you most deeply. Everything is gradient: grey stone, white-pale vegetation, dark water, darker sky. No red, no yellow, no blue except in the water itself—and even that blue is so deeply saturated it reads as black. Your eyes will crave color. They will not find it. Accept this deprivation. It changes how you think. The world becomes about *tone* instead of hue, about subtle distinctions instead of bold categories.

**Sound Is Something You Lose Gradually**

The silence here is not the absence of sound; it is a *kind* of sound—the sound of water, barely moving, muffling everything. Your ears will strain. They will mishear. A distant resident's voice will sound like sighing. Your own footsteps will seem deafening, then distant, then like they're coming from somewhere else entirely. The echo inside your own head from the water pressure will create phantom sounds—voices, tones, rhythms that exist only in your inner ear.

Speak, and your words will not travel. Shout, and the water will laugh at you, distorting your voice into something you don't recognize. This will be deeply disorienting. Most travelers report a kind of psychological claustrophobia from this—not fear of enclosed spaces, but fear of being unable to communicate, unable to be heard.

The residents have solved this through a combination of gesture and proximity. They conduct business face-to-face, close enough that voices reach. They have developed a sign language that is not complete but sufficient for essentials. They seem comfortable with the silence in a way you will not achieve. But you can approximate it: accept that long-distance communication is essentially impossible. Make peace with moving closer to people, with leaning in, with the intimacy that auditory isolation enforces.

There is a peculiar benefit: in the silence, you become aware of your own internal sounds in ways that feel like meditation. Your heartbeat, your respiration, the subtle sound of your own movement through water—these become louder as external sound diminishes. Many travelers report this as unexpectedly centering, even peaceful, after several days of adjustment.

**Taste and Smell Are Alien**

The water itself tastes of minerals, age, sediment. It has a flavor that persists even if you manage to keep it from your mouth—it flavors the air, the insides of your breathing apparatus, eventually your perception. There is no fresh water here, not as you know it. Residents have learned to extract and filter, but what they drink is not something you would recognize as pure. It will have mineral content that affects your electrolyte balance. Bring supplementary minerals. Bring salts. Your body will demand them.

The smell is impossible to avoid and impossible to fully describe. It is not foul—it is not spoiled or corrupted—but it is *deep*. Ancient. A scent of stone and layered sediment, of microorganisms that have evolved in darkness for generations, of the algae cultivations which smell sweet but in a way that seems to originate from somewhere besides your olfactory nerves. Some travelers report it as comforting; others as nauseating. Both reactions are valid.

Your sense of smell will become unreliable over time. The scent-receptors will adapt to the constant presence of these deep-water molecules, and you will transition from finding it overwhelming to barely noticing it. This is normal. Appreciate this adaptation—it's your nervous system being intelligent, filtering out constant background stimulus to allow you to notice things that change.

## Navigation and Orientation

**Cartography Here Is Impossible**

Maps of the Drowned Expanse exist. They are simultaneously accurate and useless. The structures are mapped. The major pathways are marked. The sectors are delineated. But these maps were made by people who could navigate using landmarks you cannot perceive—subtle shifts in water temperature, variations in the algae cultivations that indicate region, acoustic signatures of different sectors.

To you, all the stone looks the same. All the pathways seem to duplicate themselves. The darkness makes the grand scale of the place utterly invisible. You can walk for what feels like hours and cover a distance that, according to residents, is only a few structure-blocks. Your sense of space will be fundamentally broken.

Here is the solution: do not navigate alone. Not because it is dangerous in the traditional sense (it is not; the residents maintain the pathways, and structural collapse is rare and usually localized), but because you will become lost with absolute certainty, and lost in this environment means wandering until you collapse. Hire a guide. Pay them very well. A good guide is worth more than all your equipment.

The guides are not natives necessarily. Some are, but many are other travelers, previous visitors who have stayed long enough to learn the patterns. They often speak languages you recognize. They can interpret not just the physical layout but the social geography—where to go for certain goods, which courtyards are open to travelers, which are private residences, where the main cultivation efforts happen, where the oldest structures stand.

**The Vertical Dimension Is Real**

The Drowned Expanse extends downward. This is not metaphor. The structures have stories beneath stories. Most traveler traffic remains on what might be called the upper levels—perhaps fifty to a hundred meters below the abstract surface, though the concept of meters is meaningless when you cannot see the true vertical. But the residents live in all directions: deep gardens in the lower levels where the pressure increases noticeably, higher structures (relatively speaking) where more light filters through, sideways structures built into what amounts to the sides of the larger formations.

Do not attempt to explore downward without a guide who knows the weight of water at different depths and understands your body's limits. The pressure increases gradually, and at certain thresholds, your equipment will fail or your physiology will revolt. Residents have adapted to this through lifetime of exposure. You have not.

There are upper levels—structures that rise toward the absent surface. These require more equipment and more skill, as the water is shallower and the pressure less absolute, but the cold increases and the darkness... changes. Sunlight reaches here, barely, in colors that are not blue but something older and more complex. Some residents live here, but most are adapted to the depths. Travelers often report that the upper levels are more disorienting than the lower ones, because they are almost familiar—they almost make sense—but not quite.

**The Scale of Time Is Disorienting**

You can walk for what your body experiences as a full day and arrive at a location you recognize. Confusion: did you go in a circle? No. The structure is simply vast beyond your perceptual capacity. Distances that seem reasonable—"just walk past thirty archways"—translate to journeys that require multiple rests, multiple temperature-management interventions. 

The residents move through this space at a pace that seems glacial but is, by their understanding, normal navigation speed. If you try to move faster, you will exhaust yourself catastrophically. Match their pace, even though it will seem impossibly slow. You will arrive.

## Social Preparation

**The Residents Are Not Suffering**

This is crucial. Do not bring pity. Do not bring the perspective of someone looking at disaster and trying to help. The people of the Drowned Expanse are not trapped; they are *home*. Their culture, their science, their arts, their philosophies—all of it has evolved to match this environment perfectly.

Yes, many travelers from elsewhere find it grim. Yes, it is cold, dark, and slow. But to the residents, this is simply life. They have never known otherwise. Many, when given the opportunity to leave, choose to stay. Some who have left have returned. The place has a way of becoming essential to those who spend time here.

Approach the residents with genuine curiosity. They will sense condescension and respond coolly. They will sense authentic interest and become remarkably generous with their time and knowledge. Understand that their comfort with darkness and cold is not something they've overcome—it is something they are native to. There is a difference.

**Communication Is Layered**

Language is one layer. You will need to learn basic phrases—greetings, requests for direction, expressions of thanks. The primary language of the Expanse is ancient, its roots traceable to the pre-drowning era, but it has evolved significantly. Vowel sounds have become more nasal. Consonants have softened. It will sound like you are listening to someone speak through sleep.

But language is secondary to the communication that happens through presence and gesture. Physical kindness matters enormously here. Sharing body heat through proximity is not uncomfortable—it is bonding. Helping someone navigate a difficult passage is remembered and reciprocated. Working together in the cultivation gardens—even if you're inefficient at it—demonstrates respect and builds relationship.

The residents also communicate through knowledge-sharing and story. Sitting with an elder resident, hearing the history of a particular structure, understanding the genealogy of a specific algae strain—this is how information travels and bonds form. Your willingness to listen—truly listen, with patience for the slow speech patterns and the frequent pauses—will determine how much of the deeper culture you access.

**Expect to Develop Attachment**

Many travelers report that after two weeks, they begin to think of the Expanse as home. After a month, the idea of leaving becomes genuinely difficult. The slowness, which initially feels like stagnation, begins to feel like peace. The cold, which was agony, becomes something you can practically ignore. The darkness transforms from threatening to comforting.

Be aware of this. It is normal. It is also potentially dangerous, because it can lead to the decision to stay—and that decision, made in the altered mental state that deep submersion in this place creates, might not be the decision you would make with sunlight in your eyes and choices that feel truly free.

Plan an exit strategy before arrival. Decide on a departure date. Tell your guide. Make arrangements with the port authority (such as it is—basically, one aging resident who maintains the equipment that allows transit between the Expanse and elsewhere). When your date arrives, *leave*, even if you don't want to. You can always return. What you cannot easily do is extract yourself from a decision made under the influence of months of sensory and psychological adaptation to an environment fundamentally foreign to your biology.

## What to Bring

**Physical Equipment**

- Breathing apparatus, as designed for your origin multiverse, plus backup apparatus, plus the local variants you'll acquire upon arrival
- Multiple layers of insulating material (plant-based fiber is standard, but bring synthetic backup)
- Chemical heat sources
- A light source (chemical luminescence, mechanical, something reliable and not dependent on electricity)
- A map (useless, but psychological comfort)
- First aid equipment adapted for cold-water injuries
- A journal and writing materials made from materials that function when wet
- Trade goods: items from your home multiverse are valuable. The rarer they are here, the more useful as currency. Technology from elsewhere is less useful (it fails in the water, lacks context) but can be interesting to residents for the conceptual novelty.

**Mental Equipment**

- Genuine patience
- The ability to sit with discomfort without trying to fix it
- An openness to being slower than you've ever been
- Respect for knowledge that has evolved over centuries
- Acceptance that some questions will not be answered quickly or directly
- The willingness to be incompetent at basic tasks (navigation, cultivation, even movement) and to learn from residents without defensiveness

## What to Expect at Different Stages

**Days 1-3: Acute Disorientation**

Everything will feel wrong. The cold will be all-consuming. You will want to leave immediately. The darkness will seem absolute. Your body will hurt. You will not understand how the residents move so easily when movement is so difficult. You will question every decision that brought you here.

This is normal. Virtually every traveler reports this exact sequence of thoughts. It passes.

**Days 4-10: Adaptation Begins**

Your body will be in pain, but the pain will become more like discomfort—chronic, manageable, part of the background. Your eyes will begin to perceive details that were invisible before. You will move slightly faster. You will stop thinking quite so much about the surface world.

The Expanse will begin to reveal patterns. You will start to recognize sections of pathways, to understand the logic of the structures, to begin to perceive what residents mean when they speak of the beauty of certain sectors. The fear will diminish, though it will not disappear entirely.

**Weeks 2-4: Belonging Begins**

This is the dangerous and wonderful period. You will start to think of the Expanse as home. You will understand jokes residents make. You will begin to anticipate the rhythm of daily movement. You will care about things: whether a particular cultivation is thriving, what news arrives from other sectors, the health of residents you've begun to know.

The place will seem less like a catastrophe and more like a solution—a place where humans have adapted to impossible circumstances and created something functional and even beautiful. You will begin to understand why residents stay. You will begin to understand why some travelers never leave.

**Month 2 and Beyond: Integration or Integration-Resistance**

You will either find your place here, or you will consciously resist it. The key is to remain aware that the resistance itself is a sign of integration—you have enough of a relationship with this place to feel its pull and to consciously decide whether to yield to it.

If you leave at this point, you will leave changed. The way you perceive time, light, temperature, and human connection will have been fundamentally altered. The sunlit world will feel loud, bright, and excessively fast. Some travelers are deeply disturbed by this transition. Others find it refreshing. Most find it bittersweet.

## A Final Note

The Drowned Expanse is not a tourist destination. It is not a place to visit briefly and return to tell stories. It is a place that *changes* those who visit it, because it is fundamentally incompatible with the evolutionary pressures that shaped the human form. To come here is to voluntarily enter an environment where your body is wrong, your senses are confused, and your psychology is placed under duress by conditions that the residents have long since stopped recognizing as difficult.

And yet, somehow, you will survive it. You will adapt, at least partially. You will come to understand something about human resilience and capability and the strange beauty of cultural evolution in impossible circumstances.

You will also, very likely, think about returning every day for the rest of your life.

Prepare accordingly.
