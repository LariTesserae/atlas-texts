# Preparation for the Scarp of Hollow Mirrors

## Physical Conditioning

Begin your preparation six months before departure. This altitude—somewhere between 4,500 and 5,500 meters above sea level based on the atmospheric descriptions—demands respiratory adaptation that cannot be rushed. Start with sustained cardiovascular training: long-distance running, stair climbing with weighted packs, anything that teaches your lungs to extract maximum oxygen from increasingly thin air. Sleep at elevation if possible. Spend weeks at 2,500 meters minimum, then progress. Your body must learn to produce red blood cells proactively, not reactively. You will arrive at the Scarp still somewhat oxygen-deprived—this is unavoidable—but proper conditioning reduces the chance that this deprivation becomes dangerous rather than merely unpleasant.

Your legs will bear more strain than you anticipate. The approach to the ledge is likely technical, possibly treacherous. The scree field behind the Scarp will test balance and ankle strength. Train specifically for unstable surfaces. Strengthen your ankles, knees, and hips in ways that modern life typically doesn't. Consider months of trail running on loose ground.

## Cold-Weather Gear Architecture

Standard alpine gear is insufficient. The Scarp presents a specific thermal challenge: perpetual dampness combined with below-freezing temperatures. Wet cold is more dangerous than dry cold; it conducts heat away from your body three times faster than air alone. Your layering system must prioritize wicking and insulation *through* moisture.

**Base layers:** Merino wool exclusively. Not synthetic. Not cotton. The Scarp's moisture will find any synthetic and convert it into a conductor of your own body heat. Merino breathes while maintaining insulating properties even when saturated. Two sets minimum.

**Mid layers:** Fleece (not down—down becomes useless when wet). High-loft synthetic insulation as backup. You need air pockets that trap warmth regardless of saturation. A heavy wool sweater might sound antiquated; it's essential.

**Outer shell:** A hardshell jacket designed for ice climbing, not general mountaineering. The Scarp's constant glaze and the adhesive quality of the stone suggest you may need to move carefully across surfaces with millimeters of refreezing condensate. Gore-Tex or equivalent is non-negotiable. Bring two: one for active climbing, one for layering during immobile periods.

**Footwear:** This is critical. The slick, adhesive stone requires boots with exceptional grip. Ice climbing boots with specialized soles (Vibram XS Trek or equivalent) are ideal. Standard hiking boots will betray you. The constant moisture means waterproofing matters less than drainage and drying capability. Bring insulated wool socks—at least four pairs—and accept that they will be damp. Damp socks are survivable; frozen feet are not. Plan for frequent sock changes. Your boot insulation must be rated for −15°C minimum; the Scarp hovers around −8°C, but wind chill and the particular wet cold will feel colder than the thermometer suggests.

**Specialized cold-weather gear:** Balaclava (essential; the dry cold will burn exposed skin rapidly). Goggles designed for snow and high-altitude use—the glare from the limestone basins can cause snow blindness even without snow. The reflected light here is intense enough to damage unprotected eyes within hours. Bring two pairs; one will inevitably fog or crack.

Insulated gloves rated for −20°C, with a grippy palm treatment. You'll be handling stone constantly. Regular mountaineering gloves will be inadequate. Plan for glove rotation and drying—carry at least three pairs, ideally four.

A face mask designed for wind and extreme cold, separate from your balaclava. The hum you'll hear suggests significant air movement through the alcoves; this wind, though apparently constant rather than gusty, will be relentless.

## Respiratory and Physiological Preparation

Bring supplemental oxygen if your expedition budget and multiverse-travel regulations allow. Two bottles minimum. You may not need them, but the Scarp is high enough that the decision to use oxygen is better made from a position of having it available. The damp cold will compound respiratory stress—your lungs will work harder to warm incoming air, and that air is already thin.

Learn the symptoms of acute mountain sickness thoroughly. Headache, nausea, difficulty sleeping, shortness of breath beyond what conditioning suggests you should experience. If these symptoms appear, you must descend immediately. The Scarp is not a place where you can push through.

Bring diamox (acetazolamide) if legally obtainable in your origin universe. Start taking it two days before arrival. It increases your body's respiratory drive and helps prevent altitude sickness. Consult a high-altitude medicine specialist before departure; individual responses vary.

Hydration is counterintuitive here. The moisture in the air will make your body *feel* hydrated even as it's actually dehydrating. High altitude increases fluid loss through respiration. You will urinate more frequently in cold environments as your body attempts to shed excess water. Drink obsessively. Carry thermoses of warm liquid—electrolyte-supplemented water, not plain water. Your digestive system at altitude processes calories poorly; focus on high-fat, nutrient-dense foods. Nuts, chocolate, cheese, dehydrated meat. Avoid anything your stomach won't immediately recognize.

## Psychological Preparation

The Scarp will be psychologically demanding in ways you should anticipate rather than discover in situ. You are ascending to a place where human presence is archaeological, where the landscape is actively erasing evidence of inhabitation, where the silence is profound enough to hear. This is not a comfortable location.

Prepare yourself for isolation. The nearest other humans may be days of travel away. You will have no cell service, no external communication, no rescue infrastructure. This is extreme wilderness. Some people find silence and isolation transcendent. Others find it destabilizing. Honest self-assessment here is essential. If you have claustrophobia or agoraphobia, reconsider. The Scarp offers both: the tight confines of the alcoves and the absolute void of the drop-off.

The constant hum and the periodic cracks from deep within the mountain will trigger your threat-detection systems. Your brain will interpret these sounds as danger—avalanche, rock fall, structural failure. Intellectually, you understand the Scarp has stood for centuries. Emotionally, in the moment, you may struggle. Meditation or mindfulness training conducted at altitude will help. Spend time before departure practicing staying calm in environments that feel unstable.

The light and the mirrors are disorienting. The constant reconfiguration of the reflection landscape will strain your spatial awareness. Some visitors report mild perceptual distortions: the sense that the alcoves are moving, that distance is uncertain, that the far end of the ledge is simultaneously near and infinitely distant. This is normal. Don't interpret it as altitude sickness or cerebral edema unless accompanied by other symptoms. The Scarp's optical environment is genuinely confusing—your confusion reflects accurate perception, not malfunctioning neurology.

Plan to spend at least two days acclimatizing before undertaking any investigation of the structures themselves. The temptation to move immediately to the alcoves, to search for meaning or artifacts or explanation, will be strong. Resist it. Your body and mind need time to adjust. Use the first two days for short walks, exploration of the immediate ledge area, observation. This has the secondary benefit of allowing you to understand the Scarp's rhythms: how the light changes, how the mist moves, when the hum intensifies, where the safest footing is.

## Practical Equipment Checklist

**Navigation and safety:**
- High-altitude GPS unit (magnetic compasses behave oddly at extreme elevations)
- Detailed topographic maps of the Scarp region and the surrounding mountain range
- Rope (100+ meters), harness, carabiners, belay device—assume you may need to rope-anchor yourself to stable points
- Ice axe appropriate for mixed terrain
- Crampons (the glaze on the stone will require them during certain times of day)
- Headlamp (for the alcoves; the interior darkness is absolute)
- Backup lighting (a second headlamp; a hand torch; chemical light sticks)
- First aid kit specifically designed for high-altitude/cold injuries: blister prevention, frostbite treatment, severe dehydration management

**Shelter and insulation:**
- A tent rated for extreme cold (−25°C minimum). Standard mountaineering tents are inadequate.
- A bivy sack rated for the same temperature range (backup shelter)
- Sleeping bag rated for −15°C minimum (−20°C preferable)
- Foam sleeping pad (at least 2 inches thick; the stone will conduct heat away from you with remarkable efficiency)
- Emergency bivouac shelter or tarp system

**Documentation:**
- Notebook (paper; pens/pencils will freeze but will work if kept inside your clothing)
- Camera (test cold-weather operation beforehand; many cameras fail at extreme altitude and cold)
- Recording device for the hum (you may want to document its frequency and rhythm)

**Supplies and food:**
- High-calorie food for 10 days minimum
- Water purification system (the basins may be safe to collect from, but verify before consuming)
- Thermos bottles for maintaining warm liquid (crucial for both hydration and morale)
- Stove rated for extreme cold (liquid fuel or canister designs that function below −10°C)
- Fuel in quantity (you'll cook more at altitude; do not underestimate consumption)
- Salt, if you intend to investigate the theory that the alcoves were mining salt

**Specialized investigation tools** (if researching the site's history):
- Small excavation tools (brush, trowel, small pry bar)
- Specimen bags
- pH testing strips (to assess mineral composition of the basin water/residue)
- Cloth for cleaning oxidized metal
- Notebook for sketching architectural details

## Arrival Protocol

You will arrive at the ledge already altitude-stressed. The transition from normal atmospheric pressure to the Scarp's rarefied air will be jarring. Your first actions:

1. **Sit immediately.** Don't stand. Don't assess. Don't admire the view. Sit on the stone, control your breathing, allow your body to register the altitude change. Five minutes minimum.

2. **Hydrate.** Drink warm liquid. Your electrolyte balance is already disrupted.

3. **Assess the immediate area for shelter.** Identify a location near an alcove—you'll want the shelter it provides—that is flat and protected from wind.

4. **Set up camp.** Do not explore yet. Do not investigate the structures. Establish your shelter, your sleeping system, your water source. This takes hours and should consume the rest of your first day.

5. **Document first impressions.** Write them down while the novelty is acute, before the Scarp becomes normal. These first-day observations are valuable for understanding how your perception adapts.

## The Investigation Itself

The alcoves are where the meaning is, and this is where preparation meets improvisation. Bring tools appropriate for careful investigation of archaeological structures—light excavation tools, brushes, documentation systems. But understand: these structures have been here for centuries to millennia. Another few days of careful exposure will not significantly damage them. Move slowly. Photograph everything before disturbing anything. If you find artifacts—that oxidized metal, mineral deposits, tools—document their location precisely before removal.

The basins themselves may be sampling sites. Collect water or residue from basins of different sizes and ages. The mineral composition might explain the Scarp's history. The hum might correlate with specific environmental conditions; document when you hear it, at what intensity, in what weather conditions.

Most importantly: respect the silence. The Scarp is not a place that rewards haste or loud activity. Move carefully. Speak quietly. Listen. The mountain will reveal its patterns if you give it time.

## Psychological Anchors

Bring something of comfort: a photograph, a book you've read multiple times, an object with personal significance. The Scarp is profound and beautiful, but it is not comforting. In the evening, in the cold, in the humming silence, you will be grateful for something that reminds you of warmth and inhabited places.

## Final Warning

The Scarp is high, cold, isolated, and potentially hostile to human presence. It is not dangerous in the sense of obvious threats—there are no predators, no unstable geology, no avalanche risk. Rather, it is dangerous in the way that the high places are dangerous: through indifference. The mountain does not care whether you live or die. The cold does not acknowledge your suffering. The silence is indifferent to your loneliness.

This is not meant to dissuade you. Rather, it is meant to focus your preparation on the accurate threats: cold injury, altitude sickness, isolation stress, and the subtle errors of judgment that occur when humans are pushed beyond their normal operating parameters.

Go prepared. Go respectfully. Go knowing that you're entering a place that has been patiently unmade for centuries, and that your time there is measured in days while its time is measured in millennia.

The Scarp has survived everything. The question is whether you will too.
