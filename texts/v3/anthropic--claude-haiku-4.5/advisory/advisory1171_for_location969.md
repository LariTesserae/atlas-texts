# How to Prepare for the Throat

## Before You Go

**Mental conditioning is non-negotiable.** This is not a place that will let you remain as you are. You will be changed by proximity to the Throat, subtly but measurably. Before departure, establish a sense of self so concrete it might survive translocation into a space where geometry breaks down. Write down three immutable facts about yourself. Not beliefs—facts. Your name. A memory that contains physical sensation. Something you've created. Memorize these completely. You'll need anchors.

Understand that the Throat operates on principles that make human neurology *uncomfortable*. Your pattern-recognition systems will work overtime and fail repeatedly. Your sense of balance will report contradictory data. Your proprioception—your sense of where your body is in space—will become unreliable. This is not dangerous in the traditional sense, but it is profoundly disorienting. Spend time before departure practicing the acceptance of sensory contradiction. Meditation helps, though not the peaceful kind. Sit in a room with a mirror slightly to your left, a sound you hate directly above you, and a light source that flickers at an irregular rhythm. Stay there. Become friends with the vertigo. It will follow you.

**Bring a journal, but don't expect it to make sense afterward.** The Throat will distort your perception of time so thoroughly that your notes will seem to have been written by someone experiencing events at different speeds simultaneously. This is actually useful—comparing your written account (recorded in linear time) against your memory (experienced in Throat-time) will help you process what happened. But write in pencil. Pen feels too permanent in a place that is still deciding what it is.

**Pack light.** The floor moves. It's slow, but directional. You can be carried deeper into the ravine without intending to. Anything you bring should be something you could abandon without it haunting you. Clothes that don't catch on sharp stone. Shoes with good grip—the tacky wetness of the floor requires friction more than traction. Nothing electronic; the interference patterns in the sound will corrupt any device. A light source seems useless (the bioluminescence is sufficient) but bring one anyway—the act of holding something familiar and purposeful will anchor you more effectively than its illumination ever could.

Do not bring weapons of any kind. There is nothing in the Throat that threatens in ways weapons address, and the presence of something designed to wound will mark you as a category of visitor the Throat finds less interesting. You want to be observed with curiosity, not with the kind of attention a threatening object generates.

**Manage your expectations about time.** This is perhaps the most crucial preparation. The Throat doesn't respect causality the way you do. You might spend what feels like eight hours there and emerge to find two days have passed, or vice versa. Don't bring a watch. Don't count breaths. Instead, establish a mantra—something hypnotic and repetitive that will run underneath your conscious mind, marking subjective time like beads on a string. Count it off. "One stone, one breath, one shadow, one moment. One stone, one breath, one shadow, one moment." This won't keep objective and subjective time synchronized, but it will prevent you from losing yourself entirely in Throat-time's fluidity.

## Physical Preparation

**Your lungs need acclimation.** The air in the Throat is not toxic, but it's *substantial*. Each breath is resistance. Spend two weeks before departure doing breath-work: deep breathing exercises, swimming, anything that teaches your diaphragm that breathing can be work. When you arrive, your first breaths will feel like inhalation at high altitude. This is normal. This passes. Your body adapts in roughly the first hour.

**Thermal regulation is critical.** The Throat maintains a constant internal warmth, and that warmth will eventually sync with your body temperature. You'll feel neither hot nor cold—a state of perfect thermal equilibrium that is deeply unnerving because it feels like your body is disappearing. The solution is to arrive slightly cool. Don't shiver, but don't be comfortable. This differential will persist long enough for you to acclimate without losing your sense of embodiment.

**Eat before you enter, but not heavily.** The ravine floor's slow movement can trigger nausea if your stomach is full. Eat something calorie-dense three hours before arrival: nuts, honey, oil. Something that will sustain you without requiring digestion. Drink water until you're slightly over-hydrated; the Throat's humidity will preserve this state for hours.

**Your feet will ache in specific ways.** The tacky floor surface grips in a manner that strains the small muscles in your arches and ankles. The sharp stone fragments demand constant minor adjustments of balance. By hour three, your feet will hurt. This is not the hurt of injury; it's the hurt of muscles discovering they have a much finer range of control than you usually demand. Stretch before you go. Bring salve for your feet. The pain becomes background after a while, but managing it actively—attending to it, treating it—helps you stay grounded in physical reality.

## Psychological Preparation

**Expect to lose coherent thought periodically.** The roar, the pulsing, the geometry that doesn't resolve—these will occasionally overwhelm your linguistic mind. You'll stop being able to form words, even internally. You'll exist for stretches in a state of pure perception, understanding without thinking. This is not psychosis. This is the Throat's baseline operation. It will not harm you, but your panic response might. Before you go, practice non-verbal cognition. Sit in silence and think in pure sensation, without translating it into language. Learn to be okay with the absence of internal narration.

**Prepare for the weight of observation.** The Throat is aware of you. Not in a way that threatens, but in a way that presses. You will feel seen by a space that has no eyes. This is existentially unsettling in a manner that conventional fear doesn't capture—it's more like profound asymmetrical attention. You're observed by something much larger and much slower than you, something that operates at a different temporal frequency and can't quite understand what you are. Sit with that feeling before you arrive. Meditate on being small and strange to something vast.

**Regarding the structures and the things on the walls:** You will encounter evidence of previous visitors and previous *residents*. The bone alcove suggests intelligence. The patterns suggest calculation. The platform suggests extraction of something precious. You will want to understand this. You cannot. The timeline of the Throat is not accessible to human cognition—you don't know if those structures are weeks old or centuries old. You don't know if the things on the walls are native life or transformed visitors. You don't know if the birds have always been birds. Prepare yourself to live with permanent uncertainty. Build comfort with mystery before you arrive.

## Practical Survival Strategies

**Stay mobile but don't rush.** The floor's slow movement can carry you deeper into the ravine if you allow it. Choose a direction that feels like "exit" and drift against that current, gently and consistently. You won't defeat the floor's movement, but you'll stay in a general location. The ravine doesn't extend infinitely—you'll reach a terminus eventually—but slow is better than fast. Fast makes you conspicuous. Fast seems to accelerate the Throat's responsiveness.

**Establish a rest location early.** Find a section of wall that feels marginally less active. The crystalline things will retract, which means you've found a space they're not occupying. Sit here. Not for long—the Throat becomes *more* responsive to stationary things—but long enough to establish a psychological anchor. This is the place you "came from." Knowing you can return to a location (even though you'll drift away from it) maintains a sense of agency.

**Interact minimally with the crystalline formations.** Don't touch them. They don't attack when approached, but they acknowledge attention. If you address one directly, you may find they begin *following* your awareness—tracking your attention through the ravine like a dog might track a thrown stick. This isn't dangerous, but it creates a sense of companionship that erodes your sense of being alone, and being alone in the Throat is actually the safer state. You are less remarkable when you're not being followed.

**The birds are not your friends, but they're not threats.** They exist outside standard animal behavior. Observe them if you can. Their impossible trajectories might teach you something about how the Throat's geometry actually works. But don't try to follow them. Their paths don't obey physics consistent with bipedal movement.

**Pay attention to pitch changes in the roar.** The sound is the Throat's language, even though you don't speak it. When the pitch accelerates (shallow, rapid pulses instead of the usual deep throb), the walls are becoming more active. When it drops, deepens, becomes almost subsonic, the ravine is becoming more stable, more settled. The most dangerous moments are when the pitch is highest—that's when fragments are falling fastest, when the walls are most unstable. Seek lower frequency moments for movement.

**Your breath-shadow is significant.** It's not just a visual phenomenon—it's actually something being drawn from you. The Throat is sampling what you are at a microscopic level with each exhalation. This is not predatory. It's curious. But understand that with every breath, you're being slowly catalogued, incorporated into whatever database the Throat maintains. This becomes less disturbing once you accept it. You're not being consumed. You're being *archived*.

## What to Expect Emotionally

**You will experience awe, and it will be overwhelming.** The Throat is genuinely vast and genuinely strange, and encountering it will trigger the deepest awe-response your nervous system can generate. This is the correct response. Lean into it. The awe-state is actually protective—it keeps you present, keeps you moving, keeps you from attempting things that seem reasonable in ordinary consciousness but would be fatal in Throat-logic.

**You will be lonely in ways that words don't capture.** The Throat is not empty, but everything in it operates at a different scale and speed than you do. The birds are incomprehensible. The crystalline things are patient in a way that implies timescales you can't really perceive. The walls are so vast that their existence barely registers as "other"—they're more like environment than entity. This is not human loneliness. It's cosmic loneliness. Accept it. You are one human in a space that existed before humans and will continue after. This is actually calming once you surrender to it.

**You may experience grief.** Many visitors report—after returning—that they felt a deep, sourceless sadness in the Throat. This is not depression. It's something about the ravine's structure that generates a minor emotional resonance in human neurology. It's not dangerous. It's actually a sign that the Throat is not actively rejecting you. It's involving you, in its slow way.

## The Exit

**There *is* an exit.** The Throat doesn't trap—it's too indifferent for that. You won't find a gate. You'll find the walls gradually becoming less present, the floor's movement slowing from tacky-slow to merely-slow, the roar's pitch rising until it becomes almost noise again, just sound, just the wind in a canyon, something explicable. The bioluminescence will fade. You'll find yourself back in the world, and the transition will be so gradual you won't notice it until you realize the stone beneath your feet is simply stone again, not breathing, not aware.

**Bring something from the Throat deliberately.** Not for proof—no one will believe you anyway. But for yourself. A fragment of stone with the right weight. A crystalline thing that's already started retracting into dormancy (it won't actually go anywhere; you'll carry it with you). A feather from one of the birds, impossible and still-slightly-warm. Something to prove that you were somewhere true, even if that truth doesn't map onto consensus reality.

You will leave the Throat changed. Not traumatized—the Throat is not cruel—but fundamentally altered in your perception of what reality can contain. You will never again feel entirely certain that the world obeys the rules you thought it did.

**This is the point. This is the entire point of going.**
