# Preparing for the High Thermals: A Traveler's Guide

## Mental Preparation: The Psychology of Void and Clarity

Before you pack anything, understand what you're actually visiting: a place that has transcended hospitality. The High Thermals don't welcome you. They simply permit you to exist within them, provided you accept their terms completely.

Expect psychological strain you may not have anticipated. The combination of absolute clarity and vast emptiness creates a peculiar form of exposure—there is nowhere to hide from the landscape, and nowhere to hide from yourself. The mind struggles here because it evolved in forests and savannas where complexity masked existence. Here, complexity has been stripped away to reveal the fundamental mechanics: wind, stone, scarcity, and time. Some people find this clarifying and profound. Others find it suffocating.

The constant sound is not background noise; it's a presence that will occupy part of your consciousness for the entire stay. It doesn't allow for silence, which means it doesn't allow for the mental rest that silence provides. You will be aware of the wind's pitch changing throughout the day. You will start to distinguish the pikas' territorial screams from their alarm calls. You will hear the difference between wind moving through rock fissures and wind moving through juniper wood. This is not optional—your nervous system will attune itself whether you want it to or not. The question is whether you can accept this attunement as meditative rather than maddening. Some travelers find the eternal sound deeply peaceful; it becomes a kind of moving meditation. Others experience it as low-level torture. Spend a week in a consistently windy place before you go. Get accustomed to acoustic environments you cannot control.

Plan for existential disorientation. The spatial scale here is designed to make human concerns seem very small. This is occasionally enlightening and occasionally terrifying. If you have anxiety that manifests as existential dread, the High Thermals will amplify it. If you have a meditative practice or philosophical framework for understanding your place in a vast and indifferent cosmos, bring it. You will need it.

Expect your sense of time to distort. Because the landscape doesn't change significantly, and because the quality of light remains nearly constant throughout the day, your brain may struggle to track temporal progression. Distances that you expected to take one hour take three because there are no visual markers to help your intuition calibrate distance. You'll check your watch and discover the day has moved differently than you perceived it. This is disorienting but not dangerous if you accept it.

Bring a frame of reference for finding beauty in extremity. The High Thermals are beautiful, but not in the soft, inviting way of a temperate meadow. The beauty here is austere and mineral and honest—the beauty of mathematics and engineering and things stripped to their essence. If you cannot find this beautiful, you will find this place merely barren and hostile.

## Physical Preparation: Your Body as a Refugee from Kindness

**Acclimatization to Altitude**: Fourteen thousand feet is genuinely high. Unless you live at significant elevation, you need a staged approach. Spend at least 3-5 days at 8,000-10,000 feet before attempting this plateau. Your body needs time to increase red blood cell production, adjust kidney function, and adapt your breathing pattern. Ascending too quickly causes acute mountain sickness (AMS)—headache, nausea, fatigue, cognitive impairment. On the High Thermals, where mistakes cascade dangerously, arriving with AMS is a critical liability. Some people acclimatize in 2-3 days; others need 7-10. There's no predicting which you'll be. Plan for the longer timeline.

**Cardiovascular conditioning**: The altitude means every physical activity—walking, climbing, even standing around—requires more from your cardiovascular system. You don't need to be an athlete, but you should be able to hike for 2-3 hours at moderate intensity without stopping. The terrain here is uneven and often technical; you might walk two miles and consume the energy of walking five on flat ground. If you're currently sedentary, spend two months doing progressive hill training before you go.

**Lung capacity and breathing**: The thin air means your breathing pattern will be wrong for several days. You'll either hyperventilate (which causes dizziness and numbness in extremities) or underventilate (which causes headaches and fatigue). The adaptive breathing technique is slow, deep diaphragmatic breathing—fill your lungs completely, hold for a count, exhale fully. Practice this daily for a month before you go. When you arrive, this will be your meditation and your survival mechanism simultaneously.

**Your skin will crack**: The desiccation here is severe. Even with constant hydration and skin care, the moisture content of your skin will drop noticeably. Bring heavy-duty lip balm with SPF (the UV at this altitude is intense—your lips will burn and then crack). Bring a thick hand cream or balm; your hands will become raw. Bring body lotion; your shins and forearms will develop the texture of sandpaper. Any cuts or abrasions will heal slowly and tend to become infected more easily due to the dry conditions and the mineral particulates in the air. Treat any wound immediately with antibiotic ointment and keep it covered.

**Your lips will bleed**: This isn't metaphorical. The mucous membranes in your mouth will dry out severely. You'll find blood on your toothbrush. Your mouth will become painful. Breathe through your nose as much as possible (this warms and humidifies incoming air slightly). Carry lip balm at all times—in your pocket, in your pack, on your wrist. Apply it obsessively. Staying hydrated helps, but won't completely prevent this.

**Dehydration will be your constant companion**: The desiccating air pulls moisture from you at a rate that surprises people. You will need to drink more water than you think is reasonable, and you'll still be thirsty. The catch: your thirst mechanism is not reliable at altitude. You can be severely dehydrated while not feeling particularly thirsty. The solution is to drink on a schedule, not on sensation. Drink 250-500ml of water every 20-30 minutes of activity. This sounds like a lot—it is. Your urine should be clear or nearly clear; if it's yellow, you're dehydrated. Electrolyte supplementation (sodium and potassium) helps; water alone is insufficient because the minerals are lost through sweat.

**Calorie requirements increase substantially**: Your metabolism will run at a higher rate to maintain body heat and to cope with the altitude. You may need 4,000-5,500 calories per day even if you're not doing strenuous hiking. Under-eating leads to faster fatigue, poor decision-making, and difficulty maintaining body temperature. Bring calorie-dense foods: nuts, nut butters, fatty dried meat, chocolate, olive oil to add to meals. Eat regularly even if you're not hungry; hunger is not a reliable signal at this altitude.

**Sleep will be difficult**: The altitude, the constant cold, the psychological intensity, and the inability to silence external noise all make sleep challenging. Your first 2-3 nights may be nearly sleepless. This is normal but dangerous because sleep deprivation impairs judgment precisely when you need judgment most. Bring a good sleeping bag rated to at least -10°F; the cold will wake you if you're under-insulated. Use earplugs to muffle the wind noise, if you can tolerate them. A sleep mask may help signal your brain that it's time to rest, even though darkness is only temporary. Accept that you may only manage 4-5 hours of actual sleep per night. Plan your activities accordingly.

**Your immune system will be suppressed**: The altitude and cold stress your immune system. You'll be more susceptible to minor infections. Keep your hands clean. Don't touch your face without washing your hands first. If you develop a cough or feel illness coming on, take it seriously immediately. Respiratory infections develop faster and become more severe at altitude.

## Gear and Equipment: Your Interface with Hostility

**Clothing**: This requires more thought than you expect.

*Base layers*: Merino wool, not cotton. Cotton holds moisture and loses insulation when wet. Merino regulates temperature and wicks moisture effectively. Bring 2-3 sets so you always have a dry layer. Include a merino wool balaclava; the constant wind will chap your face severely, and the balaclava protects while allowing better breathing than a traditional scarf.

*Mid-layers*: Bring multiple options: a thin fleece for moderate cold, a heavier insulated jacket for severe cold. These should be synthetic or down, not cotton. Layering is your survival strategy because the temperature can shift 20-30 degrees in an hour when weather changes.

*Outer layer*: A wind-resistant shell jacket and pants rated for high wind (not just rain). The wind here is relentless, and even a light shell provides substantial temperature reduction through wind-chill. It should be fully waterproof because when rain comes, it comes hard, and the cold + wet combination is dangerous. Bring a hooded design; the hood protects your face and neck.

*Extremities*: Insulated gloves in merino or synthetic material (not leather—leather becomes brittle and ineffective in cold). Bring two pairs so one can dry while you wear the other. Insulated socks—bring at least four pairs. Your feet will be cold regardless, but good insulation helps. A hat or balaclava that covers your ears; you lose substantial heat through your head, and ear exposure leads to frostbite risk.

*Footwear*: Waterproof insulated hiking boots with excellent ankle support. The terrain is rocky and uneven; a twisted ankle here is a genuine emergency. The boots should be rated for alpine conditions (at least -20°F insulation). Break them in completely before you go; new boots cause blisters, and blisters at altitude and in cold become infected more easily.

*Extras*: A lightweight gaiters system to keep rocks and ice out of your boots. A neck gaiter or buff to provide additional wind and cold protection to your neck and face. A lightweight emergency bivy sack in case you end up out unexpectedly after dark.

**Hydration and water management**: 

Bring a hydration system that won't freeze or break in the cold. Soft plastic bladder systems (like CamelBak) will fail; the water freezes and the plastic becomes brittle. Metal bottles (like Nalgene) work but conduct cold. The best solution is an insulated water bottle with a wide mouth that allows you to refill easily and see how much water remains. Bring 2-3 bottles; you'll need to carry 2-3 liters of water at any time. On a day hike, this is manageable weight. On multi-day expeditions, you'll need to plan water sources carefully.

Water sources exist here (the hidden streams and seeps), but they're not always where you expect them, and the water quality is questionable. Bring a water filter that works in cold temperatures (some filters clog when the water is very cold). Alternatively, plan to boil water or treat it with chemical purification. The seep water is mineral-rich and may have pathogens; don't drink it untreated.

Insulate your water bottles by wrapping them in insulated sleeves or neoprene. Even then, water will warm slower than it should. Drinking cold water at altitude can cause GI distress. If you can, keep one bottle against your body where it stays slightly warmer.

**Navigation equipment**:

This is critical. The vast empty spaces and consistent rock formations make visual navigation difficult. Bring:
- A detailed topographic map of the area
- A compass (know how to use it)
- A GPS unit (bring a second battery; cold drains batteries much faster)
- A personal locator beacon (PLB) or satellite communicator—somewhere this remote, cell service is nonexistent. If something goes wrong, you need a way to call for help. These aren't optional on the High Thermals.

The landscape is stable and won't change, which helps, but vast and confusing, which doesn't. Take compass bearings frequently. Mark your route on the map as you go.

**Sun protection**:

The UV at this altitude is severe—approximately 20-25% more intense than at sea level, and the thin air provides less protection. Bring:
- Sunscreen rated SPF 50+, applied every two hours and after water contact
- Sunglasses that block UVA and UVB completely, with side protection to prevent sun reaching your eyes
- A wide-brimmed hat (not a baseball cap; the High Thermals don't allow for compromise)
- Lip balm with SPF

The clarity of the light means you won't realize you're being sunburned until it's too late. Fair-skinned travelers can develop severe sunburn in 20-30 minutes. Plan for this.

**Shelter and sleeping equipment**:

If you're staying overnight (and you should for your first visit—day trips don't allow proper acclimatization), you'll need shelter. A tent designed for high-altitude use provides wind protection and insulation. Standard three-season tents fail here; the wind will stress them, and the cold will condense moisture inside, which then freezes. Look for a four-season tent with a low profile and strong wind resistance. Set it up in a location protected from the wind if possible (a leeward side of a boulder formation works). Guy lines will need to be very secure; the wind will test them.

Your sleeping system needs to be rated for -10°F or colder. This means a sleeping bag rated to those temperatures plus an insulated sleeping pad. The ground here is cold and steals heat rapidly. A standard foam pad is insufficient; use an insulated air mattress or a closed-cell foam pad rated for extreme cold. Combining two pads increases insulation. You'll be cold regardless; the question is how cold is tolerable.

Bring a sleeping cap; you lose substantial heat through your head, and sleeping cold leads to very poor rest.

**Nutrition and food management**:

The caloric demands are high, so bring more food than you think you need. Focus on calorie density:
- Nuts and nut butters (peanut butter, almond butter)
- Fatty dried meats (salami, jerky, cured ham)
- Chocolate and other sweets
- Dried fruits
- Trail mix with high fat content
- Olive oil or butter (sounds odd, but a spoonful provides serious calories)
- High-fat instant meals (instant mac and cheese with added oil, instant rice and beans)
- Energy bars and protein bars

Avoid foods that are filling but low-calorie (salad, vegetables, lean proteins without fat). Eating enough here requires focus and intention—you must consume calories even if you're not particularly hungry.

At these temperatures, food freezes solid. Bring a thermos or insulated bag for meals you want to eat warm. Eating cold food at altitude is unpleasant and slightly more challenging for digestion. If you can, warm water for tea or instant soup in the morning and evening; the warmth is comforting and increases morale.

**First aid and medical supplies**:

This is remote. Help is not nearby. Bring a comprehensive first aid kit:
- Blister treatment supplies (blister pads, tape, antibiotic ointment)
- Antibiotic cream and bandages for cuts
- Pain relievers (ibuprofen for altitude headache and general pain)
- Altitude sickness medication (consider bringing acetazolamide/Diamox if you're prone to AMS; discuss with a doctor)
- Anti-diarrheal medication (the mineral-rich water and altitude can upset digestion)
- Anti-nausea medication
- Electrolyte replacement packets
- Tweezers (for cactus spines and splinters)
- Scissors and tape
- Elastic bandages for sprains
- Medications for any chronic conditions you have, plus extras
- Sunburn treatment

Any injury here is serious because evacuation is slow. A twisted ankle means you may need to be carried out. A severe cut that won't stop bleeding is a genuine emergency. Treat injuries immediately and conservatively.

**Navigation and emergency gear**:

- Headlamp (it gets dark, and you may be navigating in dim light)
- Extra batteries
- A emergency shelter (bivy sack)
- A whistle (sound travels in the thin air; this is your signaling device)
- A mirror for signaling aircraft
- A rope or cordage
- Emergency food (in addition to your normal food)
- A lighter and waterproof matches

## Logistical Preparation: Getting There and Getting Back

The High Thermals are remote. There's no lodge, no ranger station, no infrastructure. You need to arrange:

**Access**: How you reach the plateau depends on the specific location, but generally you'll drive to a trailhead and hike in. The final approach is often steep and technical. Allow extra time. What looks like 5 miles on the map may take 3-4 hours of actual hiking.

**Exit strategy**: Always have a clear plan for how you'll leave. Know the trailhead location. Know your route down. The descent is often harder on the body than the ascent, especially when tired and dehydrated. Plan to take it slowly.

**Parking and permits**: Determine if permits are required (they often are in protected areas). Ensure your vehicle is parked securely at the trailhead. The High Thermals are remote, but not so remote that theft from parked cars doesn't happen.

**Weather monitoring**: Check forecasts daily before you go. Weather at 14,000 feet changes rapidly and unpredictably. A clear forecast can change to a lightning storm in 30 minutes. Watch the clouds. If weather is deteriorating, descend immediately. This is not a place to wait out a storm.

**Timeline**: Plan for more time than you think you'll need. Assume you'll hike at 1-1.5 miles per hour on this terrain, not the normal 3+ mph. Assume acclimatization will take 2-3 days. Assume weather will cost you a day. Plan a visit of at least 4-5 days if possible, more if you can.

## The Actual Experience: What It Feels Like

When you first arrive, you'll likely feel a combination of wonder and mild dread. The landscape is beautiful in a way that bypasses comfort—it's austere and honest and stripped to essentials. The wind will probably surprise you; it's a constant presence that's hard to describe until you experience it. The sound won't stop. You'll find it either meditative or maddening depending on your temperament; there's no middle ground.

The first night is almost always difficult. The altitude will make you slightly nauseated. The cold will keep you from getting comfortable. The wind will keep the tent vibrating. The sound will prevent sleep. This is normal and will improve by night three or four as your body acclimates. The key is to accept this and not panic about it.

The first full day, you'll find that distances are deceptive. What looks like a two-hour walk becomes a four-hour walk. The terrain that looks manageable becomes technical and challenging. Your legs will ache in ways you're not accustomed to. You'll feel the altitude in your chest and in your head. This is also normal and improves as you acclimatize.

Food will taste different than it does at sea level—often slightly metallic or off. Eat it anyway. Your appetite will be suppressed by the altitude, but you need the calories. Push food in regularly.

The light here is extraordinary. Sunrise and sunset are brief and intense, with colors that seem exaggerated. Midday is so bright you'll need sunglasses even on cool days. The shadows are sharp and black and beautiful. The night sky is dark enough that the Milky Way casts actual shadows. If you've never been far enough from light pollution to see the stars properly, this will astound you.

The solitude is real and profound. You may not see another human for your entire visit. This is either healing or disturbing depending on your temperament. Many people find the solitude clarifying and meditative. Some find it isolating and difficult. Know which applies to you.

The animals are remarkable if you move slowly and quietly. The pikas will scream at you in territorial anger. The sheep will watch you from impossible distances, their silhouettes sharp against the blue. The birds are more active than you'd expect. At night, you might hear the sound of small creatures moving across the rocks.

The sensation of being at the edge of the world is real. You are on a plateau that stretches forever in most directions. The sky is enormous. The stars don't twinkle—the thin atmosphere is so clear they're steady points of light. The silence (despite the wind) is profound in ways that are hard to describe. It's the absence of human sound, of engines and voices and civilization. It's extraordinary.

## Final Practical Advice

1. **Go with someone if possible**: The High Thermals are manageable solo, but infinitely safer with a partner. If something happens to you, a partner can get help or provide assistance. If you go alone, absolutely must have a satellite communicator.

2. **Tell people where you're going and when you'll return**: Leave an itinerary with someone reliable. If you don't check in by a specific date, they should contact authorities.

3. **Start small**: If this is your first high-altitude experience, do a one-night trip first. Assess your tolerance. Come back for longer visits.

4. **Listen to your body**: If you feel seriously ill (not just uncomfortable, but genuinely ill), descend. Altitude sickness can progress to serious conditions. If you feel significantly worse on day two than on day one, it's time to go down.

5. **Respect the environment**: This is a fragile ecosystem. Stay on established paths where possible. Pack out everything you pack in, plus any trash you find. Leave no trace.

6. **Enjoy the strangeness**: You are visiting a place that is genuinely alien within the world you know. The strangeness is the point. The hostility is part of the beauty. The difficulty is part of the value.

The High Thermals will change how you understand your place in the world. Whether that change is comfortable or uncomfortable depends on you, but it will happen. Prepare accordingly, approach with humility, and plan to return home with fewer certainties than you left with.

This is a place that teaches through exposure and demands your full presence. Give it that, and it will reward you with beauty and clarity that you won't find anywhere else on Earth.
