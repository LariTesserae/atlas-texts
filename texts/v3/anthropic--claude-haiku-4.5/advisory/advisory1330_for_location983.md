# Preparing to Visit the Sunken Cities of the Plateau

## Physical Preparation

**Clothing is your first consideration.** This isn't a place where inadequate layers will teach you a lesson—it's a place where inadequate layers become permanent decisions. You need a base layer system designed for constant moisture: merino wool is superior to synthetics here because the damp seems to penetrate in ways that break the usual logic of technical fabrics. You'll sweat despite the cold, and that sweat will have nowhere to go. Plan for it. Your outer layer must be breathable but also water-resistant, something that accepts that it will be perpetually damp rather than fighting the condition. The stone weeps constantly. The moss releases moisture like slow breath. You will be wet.

Footwear requires serious thought. Regular hiking boots will become dead weight—heavy, water-logged, impossible to truly dry. What you want is something closer to amphibious shoes: good grip (the moss is slippery, and the smooth stone offers no friction), drainage that's actually effective, and breathability that prevents the fungal infections that love these conditions. The moss cushions your step, which is pleasant until you realize you're sinking slightly, which means the substrate is softer and less stable than firm ground. Your feet need to be protected *and* able to sense what they're standing on. This is a difficult balance.

Bring more socks than seems rational. Then bring more than that. Moisture-wicking wool, not cotton. Assume you'll change them twice daily and still end the day with wet feet.

**Hydration is counterintuitive but critical.** The constant visible moisture creates an illusion of availability. The stone pools are real and tempting, but drinking from them is a calculated risk. The microorganisms that create that faint shimmer above the water—they're alive, and your digestive system may register their existence catastrophically. The water is also heavy with minerals in concentrations that can overwhelm kidneys not accustomed to them. You must carry potable water, and you must carry more than you think you'll need, because the plateau's climate will dehydrate you in ways you won't immediately notice. The cool air, the thin atmosphere at this altitude, the constant evaporation—your body is losing water even as it's surrounded by moisture. You'll breathe shallowly in an attempt to adapt to the altitude, your mucous membranes will dry despite the humidity, and you'll become dehydrated without quite realizing it. By the time you notice, you'll be in a condition where recovery takes days.

**Respiratory adaptation takes time.** The air up here is thin. If you're coming from lower elevations, give yourself at least three days of acclimatization before attempting any serious exploration. Your body will rebel mildly: headaches that feel like your skull is being gently compressed, a shortness of breath that persists even when you're standing still, occasional lightheadedness. These symptoms pass, but not if you ignore them or push through too aggressively. Spend the first days in less demanding areas. Walk slowly. Sit for long periods. Let your blood chemistry adjust to the altitude.

The thinness of the air also means UV exposure is more severe than the cool temperature suggests. Even though the light is filtered through constant cloud, the UV still penetrates. Your skin will burn in ways that feel impossible in such a cool climate. Sunscreen is mandatory, and the high-SPF kind.

**Sleep will be difficult.** The cold keeps you from sleeping deeply. Your body never fully relaxes because it's never fully warm. You'll find yourself in a half-conscious state for hours at a time. Plan for this. Bring sleeping gear rated for temperatures lower than you expect—your body's natural warmth can't quite overcome the plateau's cold, and your unconscious mind will spend the night calculating temperature differentials. A sleeping bag is essential, but it will not feel entirely sufficient. Consider bringing a sleeping pad as well, and understand that even then, you may sleep only four or five genuinely restorative hours per night. The rest will be in a state of alert rest, your body aware of every shift in air temperature, every distant drip of water echoing through the stone.

## Medical and Psychological Preparation

**The silence is not nothing.** Prepare your mind for it as you prepare your body for the cold. Most people experience genuine disorientation in the first 24-48 hours. The quiet of this place is so complete that it creates a kind of sensory deprivation. Your brain, expecting a baseline level of auditory input, begins to generate it internally. You may hear things—distant voices, music, the sound of machinery—that aren't present. This is not hallucination in the clinical sense. It's your perceptual system attempting to maintain equilibrium. Understanding this in advance makes it manageable. You can recognize the experience as a normal response to abnormal quiet rather than a sign that something is wrong with you.

By the third or fourth day, most people acclimatize. The silence becomes less oppressive and more oddly comforting. You'll notice yourself listening to things that were always there but required the absence of competing sound to perceive: the subsurface murmur of moving water, the nearly-inaudible patter of mineral-rich moisture dripping from stone, the soft percussion of moss settling. Your nervous system recalibrates. Some people find this transition pleasant. Others struggle with it throughout their stay.

**Bring journals and writing materials, even if you don't consider yourself a writer.** The silence and the stillness of this place create a psychological need to externalize thought. Writing—even simple, repetitive journaling—provides an anchor. It's a way of confirming that you exist, that time is passing, that your thoughts are real. People who don't bring writing materials sometimes struggle. They become too internal. They start over-interpreting the subtle shifts in the landscape, finding significance in random variations, developing relationships with individual moss colonies.

**Prepare for profound boredom mixed with profound unease.** There is very little to *do* on the plateau. You can explore the ruins, but you'll exhaust the immediately accessible areas quickly. You can sit in silence and observe, but observation only fills so many hours. The unease comes from the sense that you're being watched—not by anything present, but by the absence of anything. The ruins are empty, but they were built for habitation. The emptiness is emphatic. You will experience moments of acute awareness that you're in a place designed for beings you'll never meet, exploring artifacts of a civilization that has receded into deep time. This awareness can be disorienting or profound, depending on your temperament. Prepare for both possibilities.

**Altitude sickness is a real possibility.** Even if you acclimate successfully, you might develop High Altitude Cerebral Edema (HACE) or High Altitude Pulmonary Edema (HAPE). The symptoms start subtly: persistent headache, unusual fatigue, difficulty concentrating. If they progress, you experience confusion, loss of coordination, difficulty breathing even at rest. This is a medical emergency. You must have a plan for descent before you ascend. You must know how many hours it takes to reach lower altitude from your exploration points. You must be honest about your symptoms and willing to abort your mission if they appear.

Carry acetazolamide (Diamox) if you can obtain it. Start taking it 24 hours before ascending. It's not a perfect solution, but it significantly reduces the likelihood of altitude sickness.

## Practical Preparation

**Navigation will be difficult.** GPS functions at this altitude, but the plateau's vast scale and gentle slopes make it easy to become genuinely lost. A specific location might be 50 kilometers away or 5 kilometers away, and the visual perspective won't reliably tell you which. The mist creates additional disorientation—distances seem to compress and expand unpredictably. Bring high-quality navigation tools: a detailed topographic map (if one exists), a GPS unit with batteries, a compass. Better yet, bring a redundant system. And understand that even with tools, you may find yourself unable to locate specific ruins you've already visited.

Mark your paths clearly. Use brightly colored biodegradable markers (cloth strips, not plastic). The markers will weather, but they'll persist long enough to guide you back. This feels primitive in an age of GPS, but when mist rolls in and visibility drops to 20 meters, you'll understand why.

**Plan for equipment failure in ways you normally wouldn't.** The constant moisture attacks electronics. What would survive in normal conditions might fail here. Bring backup power supplies. Keep electronics in waterproof containers. Test everything before you arrive. Have physical backups—paper maps, a compass, a non-electronic watch. The plateau is old and indifferent to human technology. Your instruments are borrowed tools, not guarantees.

**Learn to read water as navigation.** The entire plateau is a hydrological system designed to move water downslope. If you follow water, you follow the grain of the land. Water always moves downslope. If you become lost, finding any stream and following it downward will eventually take you to the edge of the plateau and the possibility of descent. This isn't reliable for reaching a specific destination, but it's reliable for finding your way out.

## Emotional and Philosophical Preparation

**Prepare for a profound encounter with absence.** The Sunken Cities are not ruins in the sense of destroyed grandeur. They're not tragic or dramatically fallen. They're simply *gone*. The structures remain, but the beings who made them—their purposes, their thoughts, their reasons for building in this harsh place—have receded beyond recovery. Walking among the ruins, you'll feel this absence acutely. It's not a feeling of haunting, exactly. It's more like understanding, in a visceral way, that you are temporary. Everything you know will be temporary. Ten thousand years is not that long geologically. Your entire civilization could fit into the time scale that this plateau operates on.

Some people find this clarifying and beautiful. Others find it destabilizing. Both responses are appropriate.

**Prepare for silence to become a form of companionship rather than isolation.** After the initial disorientation, many visitors report that the quiet becomes almost social. Not in any mystical sense, but in the practical sense that silence can be a shared experience. You become aware of yourself as a small element in a vast system. The plateau doesn't care if you're present. But your presence in this landscape creates a minor perturbation—you displace air, you create sound, you change the moisture distribution where you walk. You become part of the system. This awareness is grounding. The silence is no longer empty. It's full of this strange comradeship with a place that precedes you and will continue long after you leave.

**Plan for re-entry difficulty.** Returning to normal civilization after several days on the plateau can be unexpectedly jarring. The noise of ordinary life—traffic, conversation, machinery—will seem aggressive and intrusive. You may experience a period of depression or dissociation. This passes, usually within a few days, but it's worth anticipating. Don't immediately return to stimulating environments. Give yourself transition time. Sit in quiet spaces before resuming your normal life.

## Practical Logistics

**Arrive via the established routes.** There are ways to reach the plateau, though they vary depending on the specific multiverse iteration. They're never quick. Most approaches involve multiple days of climbing or specialized equipment. Budget considerably more time for access and egress than you expect. The plateau doesn't give access easily.

**Bring more food than seems necessary.** Your caloric requirements increase at altitude and in cold, even when you're sedentary. You'll eat more than you plan. Bring calorie-dense foods that don't require significant preparation: nuts, dried fruit, high-fat protein bars, chocolate. Prepare meals that require minimal cooking—the cold and the moisture make fire-building difficult, and you'll have limited energy for complex food preparation.

**Establish a baseline for your own state before you arrive.** Know your resting heart rate, your normal blood pressure, your typical cognitive state. This gives you a reference point for detecting changes. The altitude and the cold will change both. Knowing the baseline helps you distinguish between normal adaptation and dangerous changes.

**If possible, go with a companion.** The plateau is navigable solo, but you're more vulnerable to error—missed markers, navigation mistakes, medical emergencies with no witnesses. A companion who can watch for your deterioration, confirm that you're seeing what you think you're seeing, provide a secondary map-reader and navigator. If something goes wrong, a companion might mean the difference between recovery and tragedy. At the very minimum, ensure someone knows where you're going and when you should return.

## The Essential Understanding

The Sunken Cities of the Plateau are not a destination for tourism. They're not a challenge to overcome or a curiosity to satisfy. They're a place where human presence is incidental. You'll visit them because you're capable of reaching them, and because you're drawn to them for reasons you may not fully understand. But the plateau itself doesn't care if you come or go. The ruins will continue weeping stone-water into moss for thousands of years whether you're there or not.

Prepare with respect for this indifference. Prepare with respect for the scale and the cold and the silence. Prepare by accepting that you're a temporary visitor to something permanent. Prepare by being ready for physical discomfort, sensory oddity, and profound perspective shifts.

And prepare by understanding that you'll likely want to return. People do. Not because the plateau is beautiful—though it is, in its austere way—but because it contains a kind of truth about the universe's scale and age and lack of concern for individual human narratives. Once you've experienced that truth directly, lesser places seem somehow incomplete.
