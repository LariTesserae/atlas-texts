# Preparation for the Terraced Reservoir: A Traveler's Guide

## Before You Go

**Mental Conditioning is Non-Negotiable**

You must prepare yourself for sensory deprivation disguised as abundance. The Reservoir will overwhelm you with stimuli while simultaneously removing everything your nervous system evolved to process: day/night cycles, animal sounds, wind, the sight of sky. Your brain will interpret this as both luxury and imprisonment. Practice meditation. Spend time in sealed greenhouses or conservatories. Learn to find the rhythm in mechanical humming. Listen to recordings of continuous humidity-controlled environments for 2-3 hours daily for a month before your jump. Your anxiety about being touched will need to be addressed—the vegetation here is *relentless* in its presence. You cannot escape it. You can only learn to stop flinching.

**Acclimatization Timeline**

- Days 1-3: Disorientation. Your inner ear will struggle with the stillness and the weight of the water above. Expect mild vertigo, especially when looking up. Some travelers report a creeping claustrophobia that emerges on day 2 and peaks on day 3. This passes.
- Days 4-7: Acceptance. You'll start to hear the system—the true system beneath the surface sounds. The way the pumps cycle in thirds. The rhythm of the dripping sap. Time will begin to feel negotiable.
- Days 8+: Danger zone. This is when some travelers stop wanting to leave.

**Physical Preparation**

Wear layers of lightweight, moisture-wicking synthetic clothing. The humidity will make cotton a mistake—it will cling, rot slowly, and eventually support its own micro-ecosystem. Bring extra clothes, more than you think you'll need. Moisture degrades, not destroys, but you'll want the option of dry fabric against skin.

Wear shoes with good traction and soles that won't conduct the platform heat directly into your feet for hours. Broken-in hiking boots are traditional. Sandals are a mistake; the sap is sticky, and the vegetation will try to entangle your exposed skin. This isn't painful, but it's invasive.

Bring a wide-brimmed hat or hood. Condensation drips from the leaves above the walkways, and it's constant, and it's cold by the time it reaches you. Most travelers report that having a dry head significantly improves psychological resilience.

**Medical and Practical Gear**

- Water purification: The water you'll drink comes from the drip-system—it's pure, but the Reservoir is not a place where you should trust ambient water sources without processing. Bring a high-quality filter and iodine tablets as backup.
- High-SPF sunscreen: The water-ceiling diffuses light in strange ways. You will not burn as you would outdoors, but the light-geometry shifts every two hours, and certain angles will expose patches of skin to rays that feel sharper than they should. SPF 50+.
- Antifungal cream: The humidity supports fungal growth on human skin. This is not inevitable—many travelers pass through without incident—but bring it. Toenails are at particular risk. Treat early.
- Electrolyte packets: The heat and humidity will dehydrate you faster than you expect. The drip-water is clean but mineral-poor. Your electrolyte balance matters more here than in most places.
- A sealed container for specimens: If you want to bring anything back, you'll need it. The Reservoir doesn't release its materials easily. Even a leaf will begin to wilt immediately upon separation from the system.
- Notebook and pen (bring waterproof versions): You'll want to track the cycling times, the bloom sequences, the pump rhythms. Not for documentation, necessarily—for sanity. The human mind needs to count things.

**Psychological Preparation**

The Reservoir has a subtle but insistent effect: it makes you feel *tended to*. The temperature is always perfect. The humidity is always right. The vegetation is always in some phase of bloom. Everything has been optimized for human habitation by something (someone? you'll never be entirely sure) with patience measured in decades.

This feeling is seductive. Prepare yourself to want to stay. Prepare yourself to resist that want.

The isolation here is profound. You are not isolated from people—there are no other people to isolate from, so that particular flavor doesn't apply. You are isolated from *change*. The system does not surprise. It does not fail. It does not grow in unexpected directions. After a week, this constancy will feel either like safety or like a very comfortable coffin. Many travelers experience both feelings simultaneously.

**Sensory Groundings**

Bring objects that reference the outside world, but don't bring too many:
- A recording of ocean waves (genuine waves, not synthesized): Play it occasionally in your rest-chamber. The randomness will help you remember that entropy exists outside this place.
- Something textured that isn't plant: Smooth river stone, piece of bark, piece of rope. Hold it. Remember that textures vary.
- A photograph of yourself in sunlight: Not for nostalgia—for evidence that you exist in places where light falls at angles.

**Communication Protocol**

Most stable jumpers report that external communication becomes difficult after day 10. Your sense of time drifts. Your letters home may describe six hours as if they were three hours, or describe a full day as a quick afternoon. This isn't delirium—it's adaptation. The Reservoir doesn't recognize clock-time. It recognizes cycles.

If you need to maintain contact with your home timeline, establish a strict communication schedule *before* you arrive and set alarms. The alarms will feel jarring—hold onto that. The jarring is you touching reality.

**Upon Arrival: The First Hour**

You'll materialize on the mesh platform. Likely near the center, though exact arrival points vary. Your first sensation will be the heat through your soles and the weight of the water-ceiling through your eyes. Both are real; neither will hurt you.

Spend the first twenty minutes simply standing still. Do not immediately explore. Do not immediately investigate the vegetation. Just breathe. The air is rich—richer than your lungs expect—and breathing it is an adjustment. Let your system acclimate. Notice how your heart rate gradually drops.

After twenty minutes, move slowly toward the nearest edge. Orient yourself. Understand that the platform is smaller than you think—50 meters isn't far. You can walk its perimeter in about five minutes. Do this. Mark it mentally as your boundary. This helps.

**Navigation and Exploration**

The platform is organized, though not in ways that are immediately obvious:
- The eastern edge holds the three pumps and the mechanism for viewing the heat-exchange systems below.
- The northern edge is densest with fern-growth; the air here is slightly cooler and feels more private.
- The western edge has wider open spaces between vegetation clusters—better for moving through without constant contact.
- The southern edge faces the main walls and offers the clearest view of the chamber's scale. Come here when you need to remember how large the system is.

The vegetation is negotiable. It will not hurt you. It will touch you—constantly. It will tangle your clothing. It will deposit sap on your skin. This is not attack; it's just what happens when you move through a place entirely colonized by growth. Lean into it. Stop resisting contact.

The walls can be reached, but the journey takes time. The vegetation grows denser as you move toward them. Most travelers report that reaching the walls feels like entering a different chamber, even though you've only walked a hundred meters. The light changes. The humidity increases. The sense of enclosure intensifies. Some travelers find this peaceful. Some find it overwhelming. Plan your first wall-approach for when you're mentally fresh.

**Time Management**

The two-hour rotation of light—this is your only external clock. Use it. Plan meals, rest, exploration in two-hour blocks. Your body will naturally shift to this rhythm. After about a week, you'll find yourself hungry and tired on the rhythm whether you mean to or not.

The pumps cycle in thirds—roughly every six hours, they pulse with slightly increased intensity. This is worth noting. It breaks up the monotony. Some travelers synchronize their sleep to avoid pump-pulses; others find sleeping through them grounding.

**Risk Mitigation: The Real Dangers**

The Reservoir is not immediately dangerous. No creature hunts here. No toxins lurk. But there are subtle hazards:

- **Fungal and microbial growth on your skin**: The humidity is so high that your natural skin microbiome can become aggressive. You will need to establish a cleaning routine that feels excessive—full-body washdowns with soap at least once daily, more if you're particularly sensitive. The water from the drip-system is fine for this.

- **Nutritional imbalance**: The Reservoir provides food, but limited variety. Some jumpers bring protein supplements, dried fruits, shelf-stable foods from home. Not because you'll starve—the Reservoir offers edible plant matter—but because psychological stability comes from familiar tastes and nutritional variety. Bring protein bars. Bring salt. Bring the flavors that keep you feeling like yourself.

- **Psychological drift**: The subtle, constant optimization of the system will make you forget what it feels like to want something, struggle with something, fail at something. This isn't depression. It's a more insidious state—contentment so complete it erases ambition. Build in deliberate dissatisfaction. Practice wanting small things you won't get. Read the hard books. Remember what striving feels like.

- **The water above**: It's stable. It won't fall. The membrane won't rupture. But the psychological weight of knowing there are three meters of water suspended above you, held by forces you don't understand, will accumulate. On difficult days, don't look up. On harder days, do look up and remember that it's held this way for however long this system has run, and it will hold for however long you're here.

## What to Expect: Daily Life

Your days will acquire rhythm—not necessarily the rhythm you expect.

**Morning** (as defined by light-cycle, not internal clock): You'll wake hungry. The air is slightly cooler now. Eat from the food stores you brought or from the edible plants the system grows—the purple orchids have edible nectar, though it's faintly bitter. The vegetation feels slightly damper from condensation. This is the best time for exploration, when the system feels least alien and most habitable.

**Midday**: The light intensifies as the rotation brings the brightest rays through the water. The platform heats noticeably. The vegetation seems to lean toward the light. This is meditation time—sit still, observe the growth, watch how the flowers track the rays. Your brain will begin to integrate into the system's time-sense.

**Evening**: As light shifts again, the air somehow feels richer. Shadows emerge from the vegetation in places where you didn't notice vegetation before. This is when the jasmine blooms, and the scent is almost hallucinogenic in its sweetness. Eat again. The food matters less than the routine.

**Night**: The darkness isn't total—the water-ceiling holds a faint bioluminescence, or the system itself glows faintly, or your eyes adjust to near-nothing and it feels like glow. Sleep comes easily. The platform beneath you radiates gentle heat. Your dreams will be vivid and will often feature outside places—storms, crowds, open skies. This is normal. Your sleeping mind is processing the absence of these things.

## Exit Strategy

This is crucial. Establish your departure date *before* you arrive. Write it down. Give it to your home-timeline contact. Set an alarm for the day before.

The Reservoir will make you want to stay. Every single jump-log reports this. Some describe it as attraction, some as inertia, some as a deep absence of reason to leave. You will feel it between days 8-14, most acutely. You will think about how the system has no chaos, no failure, no one who will disappoint you.

You must leave anyway.

Leave before you stop wanting to.

## Final Notes

The Terraced Reservoir is not hostile. It's not even indifferent. It's a place that has been optimized for human flourishing by an intelligence (human or otherwise, alien or domestic, recent or ancient) that understood something about human needs: we want to be tended to, we want stability, we want our environment to acknowledge and support us.

But we also need departure. We need the possibility of leaving. We need the reminder that not everything is optimized for us, that the world extends beyond chambers and cycles.

The Reservoir doesn't need you to stay. It's self-sustaining. But it will make you want to. That wanting is part of the system's function, perhaps unintended, perhaps not.

Go there. Let it tend to you. Let the cycles calm your nervous system. But don't forget the date you wrote down.

Come back.

There are other places to visit. The multiverse is full of thresholds, and most of them demand something from you—effort, endurance, adaptation. The Reservoir demands almost nothing.

That's the thing to remember when you're deciding whether to leave.

Demand something of yourself anyway.
