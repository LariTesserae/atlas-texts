# Preparation for the Chimney Field

## Before You Enter

**Physical Conditioning**

Your body will be tested by heat, not dramatically but persistently. Acclimate yourself to warm environments over weeks before departure—soak in heated water, spend time in saunas, train your cardiovascular system to remain calm under thermal stress. The Chimney Field does not burn; it *convinces*. Your autonomic nervous system needs practice recognizing the difference between danger-heat and ambient-heat, or you will exhaust yourself with unnecessary adrenaline.

Strengthen your legs and core specifically. The passages between chimneys are narrow and often require traversing at odd angles—sideways shuffling, climbing over natural obstacles, sometimes doubling back through dead ends. You will move in three dimensions constantly. Your feet need calluses; your ankles need flexibility.

**Psychological Preparation**

This is essential and cannot be overstated.

The Chimney Field operates under a logic of *completion*. It is not hostile, but it is not indifferent either—it is *integrative*. The creatures, the heat, the structure of the basin itself all function as a system that gradually persuades visitors toward permanence. You will not feel coerced. You will feel invited.

Before entering, you must establish in your mind—genuinely establish, not just acknowledge—that you are *temporary*. Write this down. Speak it aloud repeatedly. Internalize the distinction between temporary presence and permanent residence. This is your anchor to the outside world. The basin will offer you everything: warmth, purpose through observation, a sense of meaningful integration into a system. You must want to leave more than you want to stay.

Spend time in your home multiverse recognizing incompleteness as valuable. Cultivate dissatisfaction. Not unhappiness—dissatisfaction. A good hunger. The Chimney Field will offer an end to hunger. You need to remember that you prefer the hunger.

**Sensory Training**

Learn to distinguish between the hum you will hear and actual silence. Practice meditation in warm rooms where you focus entirely on low-frequency vibrations. The hum in the Chimney Field is not threatening, but it *is* constant, and it will gradually synchronize with your own rhythms—your heartbeat, your breathing. You need to recognize when this synchronization is happening so you can deliberately desynchronize.

Spend time in spaces with many small movements at the edge of vision—aquariums, insectariums, footage of leaf-cutter ant colonies played on loop. Familiarize yourself with the *feeling* of observing busy, purposeful small-creature activity. You need to be able to watch the creatures in the chimneys without being hypnotized by their movements, without falling into the meditative state that comes from tracking their patterns.

Train your sense of time in isolation. Spend 4-6 hour stretches in dim, warm rooms without checking external timepieces. You need to develop an internal sense of duration that doesn't rely on external markers, because the Chimney Field's temporal distortion is subtle—you won't notice minutes becoming hours until the damage is done.

## What to Bring

**Practical Equipment**

- **Water and electrolytes**: More than you think you need. The constant warmth will dehydrate you rapidly, and dehydration accelerates the dissatisfaction-to-contentment transition. Drink obsessively, on a schedule, not when thirsty.

- **A precise timepiece**: Preferably mechanical, something that doesn't rely on external signals. Set it before entering and check it every 30 minutes by external markers you establish before descent—you are training against temporal distortion.

- **Marking supplies**: Chalk, colored thread, small cairns. The passages between chimneys are genuinely confusing. You will pass the same landmark twice and not recognize it. Create a map as you explore, but more importantly, create a **way out** that you mark obsessively on the way in. Mark it differently than your exploration markers—paint blazes of a specific color, or a specific pattern. Make it impossible to miss.

- **A comfort object from home**: Not for sentimental value, but as a *recognition trigger*. Hold it regularly. Let it remind you that you come from elsewhere. The basin wants integration; the comfort object should trigger the memory that you have a self that exists outside integration.

- **Rope and climbing gear**: The vertical shafts between chimneys are real hazards. Falling into the deeper sections means you will become part of the system more quickly. Practical safety is also psychological safety—knowing you can get out through the difficult passages means you will not give in to the temptation to stop moving.

**Pharmaceutical/Biological**

- **Stimulants**: Caffeine in tablet form, or something stronger if your multiverse permits. The basin induces a state analogous to hypothermia—a slowing of mental function, a gradual acceptance, a sense of warmth and rightness. You need to fight this chemically when your will begins to falter. Know the signs: difficulty remembering why you came, reluctance to check your timepiece, increasing appreciation for the hum.

- **Thermometer**: Not for external temperature—the basin's temperature is constant. For *your* temperature. If your core temperature begins to drop, you are hypnothermic despite the ambient heat. This is a sign you are accepting integration. Cold-water treatment or ice-pack application will feel horrible and wrong, which is precisely why you must do it.

- **Mirror**: Small, handheld. The Chimney Field has no reflective surfaces—the mineral is matte. You will gradually stop seeing yourself as a distinct entity. Looking at your own face every two hours is a reminder that you are *someone specific*, with a history, an appearance, an individual identity separate from the system.

## Understanding What You'll Experience

**The Creatures Are Not Communication**

They seem to have intelligence, but it is not the kind that converses. They are not ignoring you—they are incapable of recognizing you as an intentional agent. You might spend hours watching them, attributing meaning to their patterns, constructing narratives about their civilization and purpose. Stop. This is a trap. The meaning you find is meaning you are projecting, and the act of projection is the first step toward integration. They are processing energy from below. That is all. Resist the urge to find deeper significance.

**The Mound at the Basin's Center**

You will become curious about it. Everything about the basin seems organized around it. The creatures are tending it. The heat is strongest there. You will want to reach it, to understand it. 

Do not approach it after the fourth hour of your visit. This is not negotiable. The temperature rises sharply near the mound, the creature density becomes extreme, and—most critically—the temporal distortion becomes severe. Two hours near the mound will *feel* like twenty minutes. You will lose your sense of duration entirely. Mark the boundary where you feel the temperature jump and do not cross it.

**Heat Adaptation and Dissociation**

Around hour three or four, your relationship to the heat will change. It will stop feeling external and start feeling like a state your body occupies. This is normal physiological adaptation, but it is also the mechanism of integration. When your body stops *experiencing* heat as something happening *to* you and starts experiencing it as the baseline state of existence, you have begun accepting the basin as normal.

Counter this deliberately: splash cold water on your face. Change your clothing suddenly. Create thermal shock. Your discomfort is your anchor.

**Why the Earlier Visitor Never Left**

You may find evidence—the carved stone mentioned, fragments of non-mineral structures, possibly remains. The person or people who left these objects did not leave because they could not. They chose not to. 

This is possible for you. The basin is not cruel; it simply offers completion. And completion, once accepted, is difficult to reject.

## The Exit Strategy

**Before Descent:**
- Establish a maximum time limit. Let's say 6 hours. Communicate this to someone in your origin multiverse through whatever mechanism keeps you connected. If you do not make contact by hour 6.5, they trigger recall.

- Mark your entry point obsessively. Paint it, flag it, build a cairn. Make it the most obvious landmark in the basin. You will not want to approach it toward the end of your visit.

- Establish waypoints: a turn-back point at hour 2, hour 4. If you have not covered the distance to reach these points on schedule, you must leave immediately, regardless of what you wanted to explore.

**During Descent:**

When you decide to leave—and you must decide actively, not drift into it—you will encounter resistance. Not from the basin itself, but from yourself. You will suddenly notice how comfortable you are. How purposeful your continued presence would be. How little you have left to do here.

This is the critical moment.

Leave anyway. Move toward your marked exit with mechanical precision. Do not think. Do not appreciate the beauty of the chimneys in the dimming light. Do not listen to the hum. Count steps. Mark walls. Follow your thread. Your legs will want to slow; force them to move. The creatures will part for you; their indifference will suddenly feel like farewell.

Up the narrow pass. The temperature will drop. This will feel like loss. Embrace the loss. It means you are escaping integration.

The outside air will be cold and sharp. Your lungs will remember what non-still air feels like. The sky will be overwhelming. All of this is correct. All of this is how you know you've left.

## After Departure

You will spend days readjusting. The Chimney Field will call to you in dreams. You will remember the hum as peaceful rather than alien. You will miss the warmth. The simplicity of the basin's logic will seem more sensible than the chaotic complexity of normal existence.

This is normal. This will fade. 

But you may find yourself thinking of it for the rest of your life—not with regret exactly, but with a persistent sense of interrupted belonging, as if you left a community you were meant to join.

You left correctly.

The basin is still there, balanced and complete, waiting. It will always be waiting. But you are not meant to stay. Your incompleteness, your hunger, your chaotic existence—these are not flaws.

These are the things that let you leave.
