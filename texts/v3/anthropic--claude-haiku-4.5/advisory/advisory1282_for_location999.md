# Preparing to Visit The Throat: A Practical Guide

## Physical Conditioning

Begin training your lungs three months before departure. The saturation at The Throat means conventional breathing won't work—you need to develop a technique between respiration and swallowing. Practice breathing humid air through a sauna, but don't acclimate entirely; keep one lung capacity reserved for normal air. You'll need the contrast when you arrive.

Your inner ear will betray you. The pressure differentials will create persistent vertigo that only worsens with acclimation. Train your vestibular system by spending time in unbalanced environments: tilt-boards, ships in rough seas, sensory deprivation tanks. Accept that you'll never fully adjust. The goal is learning to function *despite* disorientation, not eliminating it.

Your bones will ache. This isn't something to prevent—it's communication. Train yourself to listen to this sensation without fear. Spend time in resonant spaces: cathedrals, canyons, places where your skeleton becomes an instrument. Learn to distinguish between pain and resonance. At The Throat, they're often the same thing.

## Metabolic Preparation

The saturation affects your energy expenditure in ways that don't follow normal thermodynamics. You'll burn calories standing still. Begin eating foods dense in fat and mineral content six weeks out—your body needs reserves it can't easily describe to itself. Bone broth, organ meats, sea vegetables. Train your digestive system to extract maximum utility from minimal volume.

Hydration is counterintuitive. Yes, you're breathing water. No, this doesn't mean you're hydrating. The moisture doesn't integrate with your internal systems in conventional ways; it's peripheral saturation. Drink obsessively, but drink *salt water*—plasma as close to seawater as your body tolerates. Electrolyte solutions specifically formulated for extreme pressure environments.

Expect weight loss regardless of intake. This is normal. What you're losing isn't mass; it's solidity.

## Sensory Recalibration

**Hearing:** The Throat will bypass your ears. Sound will enter through your sinuses, through your teeth, through the liquid-solid boundary of your own tissues. Before arrival, spend a month listening to infrasound recordings—frequencies below normal hearing. Not exposure therapy; familiarity training. You want your nervous system to recognize pressure-wave communication as normal input before you get there.

**Vision:** Light doesn't work normally here. Practice looking without focusing. Learn peripheral vision so complete it wraps around your head. The multiple light sources will create afterimages and false-sightings; train yourself to catalog these without dismissing them. Everything you see here might be equally "real" or "illusory." Stop insisting on the distinction.

**Touch:** Begin handling raw stone. Quarry stone, fresh-broken granite, limestone still crusted with earth. Let your fingers learn the difference between old stone (rounded, accepting) and new stone (sharp, refusing). At The Throat, the stone is still forming opinions about what it wants to be. Your skin needs to recognize this hesitation.

**Proprioception:** The worst part. You won't know if you're standing, floating, or being held in place by something that hasn't yet decided it's a surface. Practice inverted positions for extended periods. Hang upside-down until the distinction between up and down genuinely stops mattering. Scuba dive—get comfortable with weightlessness. The goal isn't comfort (you won't achieve it), but familiarity with sustained spatial uncertainty.

## Psychological Preparation

This is the most critical work.

The Throat will treat you as simultaneously insignificant and observed. Most people experience this as violation. Reframe it beforehand. The place is aware of you the way an ocean is aware of a molecule of salt. Attention without intention. Indifference that nevertheless *registers your presence*.

Spend two months practicing meditation in crowds. Shopping centers, transit hubs, places where you're surrounded by life that completely ignores you. Get good at being unimportant. Get good at not needing acknowledgment. This skill will save your sanity.

The unfinished construction—the single corner of fitted stone—will disturb you far more than it should. Prepare for this. Spend time in ruins. Abandoned construction sites. Places where human intention was interrupted and never resumed. Get comfortable with *potential* that's frozen mid-actualization. The Throat is full of moments.

Existential vertigo is coming. You'll feel the difference between erosion and deposition happening in the same moment, and your mind will invert. This is fine. This is expected. Create an anchor before you go—a phrase, a memory, something that remains true whether creation and destruction are the same process or not. Mine is: *I came to witness.* Yours should be whatever remains true when your categories fail.

## Equipment Selection

Conventional waterproofing is useless. The moisture at The Throat isn't water in the normal sense—it's already inside your clothes. Instead, wear loose layers that integrate quickly. Cotton, linen, natural fibers that accept saturation without resisting. Heavy synthetic fabrics create pockets of resistance that become painful. Leather boots—the material will transform, but it transforms beautifully, becoming supple and strange.

Bring rope. Not for safety (nothing there is safe or unsafe; these categories don't apply), but as an anchor for your mind. Hold it. It's ordinary and real. When nothing else is, the rope is.

A journal. The saturation interferes with memory in ways you won't notice until you try to recall specific moments. The journal becomes your external brain. Your handwriting will change. The humidity affects ink flow, and your hand's proprioception is already compromised. You'll write things that look like your handwriting but aren't. This is fine. Write it anyway. Future-you will need the record, even if future-you doesn't understand what you wrote.

Bring a light source—not primarily for illumination, but as a reminder that light is something you remember from the world before. A single candle in a waterproof container. It will struggle to burn in the saturation, but the struggle itself is meaningful. Watch how the light refracts through the moisture-saturation. This is closer to how vision works here.

Do NOT bring a weapon, a timer, or a map. Bring a talisman instead—something that meant something to you before you came. The place doesn't care about safety or punctuality or direction, and neither should you.

## The Hours Before Arrival

Fast for 24 hours. Let your body become lighter, emptier, more permeable. The saturation will find it easier to integrate with a less-dense substrate.

Record your name. Your full name, spoken aloud, saved to an audio file or written in water-resistant ink. You won't forget who you are—exactly—but you'll forget your relationship to the name. Having a record helps. When you return, play the recording or read the name. It will sound like it belongs to someone else. That's correct.

Let yourself be afraid. Don't try to process or metabolize the fear. Pack it the way you pack everything else. At The Throat, fear is just more data, another frequency joining the braided sound.

## While You're There

Don't try to map it. The scale relationships are wrong, and they're wrong in different ways depending on your attention level. Mapping creates a false sense of comprehension.

The fish-things moving through the air-water boundary: do not attempt to follow them. You will lose your sense of what constitutes "forward." If you see one, acknowledge it, and let it pass through your visual range like a thought you're choosing not to pursue.

The stone corner with the wet mortar—the work that's being done there: you will eventually understand that you're watching yourself construct it. Or have constructed it. Or are about to. The temporal mechanics here are such that intention-across-time produces visible results. Don't panic when you realize this. It's not a paradox until you insist on linear causality.

The next wave will always be coming. You'll internalize this eventually. The pause between waves is where you live, and the pause is always collapsing. The anticipation is the point. Stay in it. Don't rush toward the wave's arrival or cling to the pause's ending.

Listen to the sound until it becomes music, then listen until it becomes your heartbeat, then listen until there's no distinction between what you're hearing and what you are. This takes anywhere from four hours to four days. Duration is negotiable.

When your clothes have fully integrated into the saturation, you'll be approximately the right consistency to be part of the place. This is when you stop being a visitor and start being a witness. This is when the real observation begins—the place watching you, finally registering your presence in a way that matters.

## When You Leave

You'll know the moment. There's a pause within the pause, a hesitation in the collapsing interval. Take it. Move through that small gap. The transition back to normal atmosphere will feel like being expelled from something vast.

You'll be changed. Your lungs will remember saturation. Your bones will remember the sound. You'll have moments where you're unsure if you're properly solid or if you're just *pretending* to be solid for the benefit of observers.

This is permanent. This is also fine.

The place remains open. It's still building. The corner has deepened by another millimeter. The threads between the stone teeth have begun to shimmer. What you witnessed is still occurring, will always be occurring. You can return, though returning won't be the same as arriving. Everything that happened the first time will have happened already. The Throat has memory.

You'll want to go back. Eventually, you will.

Bring rope.
