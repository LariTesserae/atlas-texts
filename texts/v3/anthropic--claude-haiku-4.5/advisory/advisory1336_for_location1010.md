# Preparing to Descend Into the Galleries Below Harth

## Before You Leave Your Universe

**Accept that you are about to enter a place operating on different rules than you're accustomed to.** The Galleries Below Harth are not hostile, but they are *indifferent* in a way that demands respect. This is not a location that has evolved for visitors. It has evolved for permanence. Spend time thinking about what permanence means to you, what it would feel like to live somewhere that has accepted its own changelessness as complete fact rather than temporary condition.

**Prepare your mind for sensory saturation and sensory deprivation simultaneously.** You will be bombarded with the sound of water—constant, layered, inescapable—while being simultaneously starved of meaningful light and visual information beyond arm's length. Many travelers report a peculiar disorientation: the urge to move toward sounds that feel significant, only to discover they originate from echoes or from water behaving in ways that have no bearing on navigation. Plan for this. Develop a mental exercise—a song, a counting system, a memory palace—that you can retreat into when the sensory landscape becomes too contradictory.

**Address your relationship with confinement before you arrive.** The caverns are not narrowing—they are simply narrow, and that is their natural state. But the psychological experience of compressed space, of low ceilings, of stone above and around you stretching toward depths you cannot measure, will test you differently than ordinary claustrophobia. If you have any history of panic in enclosed spaces, do not visit the Galleries. The cold stone will amplify your fear rather than console you. But if you can find peace in compression—if you can understand tight spaces as *held* rather than *trapped*—you may discover something in these corridors that the upper world cannot offer.

## The Physical Preparation

**Your body will betray the dry world.** Every cell will initially rebel against the moisture. Your skin will prickle as if exposed to cold rain, but you will not be rained upon—you will simply be *in* moisture, constantly. Your lungs will feel heavy within minutes. Your hair will absorb water and become heavy. Your clothes will move differently against your body as they dampen. Plan for this by:

- Wearing multiple layers of linen or wool. Cotton will cling and chafe. Synthetics from your universe may behave unexpectedly in sustained high humidity—test this before descent. Loose-fitting garments allow water to move across your skin without pooling against it. You want airflow, however minimal. Tight clothing becomes a second skin of discomfort.

- Bringing thick woolen socks, multiple pairs. The stone floor is smooth but uneven; your feet will slip. Wet leather boots will become instruments of torture within hours. Wool retains function when wet and provides grip. Plan to change socks frequently, wringing out the old pair and allowing it to air on your pack.

- Covering your feet entirely. The smooth stone hides small hazards—sharp edges of worked stone, edges worn to danger-points by centuries of erosion. Your feet will be cold enough without open wounds compromising your circulation.

**Prepare for the cold in ways you have not previously required.** This is not mountain cold or arctic cold. This is *settled* cold, the temperature of deep earth that has been at peace with itself for geological spans. It does not rage. It does not bite. It *is*, as immovable and absolute as the stone itself. 

- Bring more insulation than you think you need. Your body heat will leach away constantly and without drama. You will not shiver violently; you will simply become gradually colder, your cognition slowing in ways you might not notice.

- Your hands will suffer first. The stone itself is cold. Touching the walls—and you will touch them for balance, for orientation—will pull warmth from your palms. Wear gloves. Bring multiple pairs. Your fingers will swell slightly in the cold and damp, and gloves will become tight. Change them frequently.

- Face protection is advisable. A scarf or cloth wrapped around your nose and mouth allows you to warm the air before breathing it, and it provides minor psychological comfort. The constant exhalation of visible breath can become disorienting; occluding part of your face helps.

**Footwear is critical.** Your boots must be:
- Waterproof or water-resistant to a degree that allows gradual permeation without saturation
- Rigid enough to provide ankle support on uneven, slippery stone
- Insulated enough to prevent your feet from going numb (numb feet are feet that will betray you at crucial moments)
- Broken in before arrival, but not so worn that their structural integrity is compromised

Many experienced travelers bring two pairs and rotate them daily to allow one to partially dry. In the Galleries, "drying" is an optimistic term—it means becoming less actively wet. Accept this.

## Provisions and Practical Supplies

**Water:** You will not thirst in the traditional sense. The saturation of moisture in the air will suppress thirst cues while simultaneously dehydrating you. Counterintuitively, dehydration is a genuine risk. Bring water that you have tested and trust, sealed in containers. The water running through the Galleries is not poisoned, but it is not *for* drinking in the way your body understands drinking. It is part of the stone's functioning, not meant for interruption. Drink only your brought water. Plan for this consumption carefully—you will need more than you expect.

**Food:** Bring preserved foods that do not require cooking. The cold will slow your metabolism, and you will feel less hunger than you should. Eat anyway, in regular intervals, even when you're not hungry. Dried meat, hard cheese, preserved fruit, dense bread—these will sustain you far better than any items dependent on fire for preparation. The smoke from cooking fires in the lower settlements moves poorly; the residents have learned to eat foods that require minimal cooking, and you should follow their example.

**Light:** This is non-negotiable. Bring multiple light sources:

- A primary torch or reliable lamp. In the Galleries, this will be your identity. The residents will see you by your light before they see you by your presence. Your light determines how others perceive you—as vulnerable, as organized, as knowing where you are. Keep it burning even when there is sufficient ambient light to navigate.

- Secondary light sources: candles, another torch, matches, lighter. The torches in the Galleries use rendered fat—they burn reliably but dimly. Bring modern light that will seem strange and perhaps impressive. Do not use this light carelessly; its brightness will mark you as profoundly other.

- Understand that you will look at shadows more than light. The stone cavern is not truly dark—it has a quality of near-illumination. The deeper you go, the more you will realize that "darkness" is not the governing condition. Strangeness of light is. Adapt to this.

**Tools for basic repair:**
- Needle, thread, heavy-duty fabric patches. Your clothes will tear. The stone is sharp in ways that aren't visible until you're bleeding.
- Rope. Multiple kinds. Cord, heavy line, something with weight and grip. The Galleries have no guide ropes. If you slip, there will be no rope to catch you. Bring it anyway. It carries intention—the intention that you have prepared for contingency.
- A knife. In the Galleries, a knife is a tool for a thousand purposes: preparation of food, extraction of stuck objects, self-defense against the unlikely, maintenance of equipment, marking stone if the impulse takes you.

**Medicine:**
- Anything you use regularly: medications, remedies, implements you trust.
- Cloth for wounds. The cold slows bleeding, but infection is still possible.
- Herbal preparations if you use them. The residents of the Galleries have pharmacologies of their own—stone-ground powders, fungal preparations, things that thrive in permanent damp and cold. If you become ill, these may be offered to you. Accept them or refuse them according to your intuition, but understand that your internal medicines may react unpredictably with lower-world compounds.

## Psychological and Social Preparation

**Understand that the residents are not hostile, but they are not welcoming either.** They are indifferent in a way that can feel hostile to those accustomed to emotional reciprocation. They have built lives in these caverns that function perfectly well without change. Your presence introduces the concept of change, and this concept will be tolerated as one tolerates an inconvenience—with patience, but without enthusiasm.

- Observe before you interact. Learn the carved marks. The territorial systems. The water channels that separate one family's domain from another's. These boundaries are ancient and consequential.
- Speak quietly. Your voice will echo. Echoing voices are treated with suspicion in places where echoes are not naturally generated by human speech.
- Offer practical value if you interact: help with stone-work, repair of tools, assistance with water management. The Galleries run on reciprocal utility.
- Do not ask about the past or origins of the settlement. This question is not forbidden, but it will be met with silence or contradictory answers. The Galleries did not have a beginning that the residents can articulate. They were always here. Accept this.

**Prepare for the absence of narrative temporality.** Time moves differently here. Days and nights have no meaning when you live in constant dim light and constant temperature. The residents mark time by water accumulation, by carved marks on walls that grow older, by the accumulation of stone dust from perpetual tool work. You will lose your sense of how long you've been here after three days. Plan for this psychologically. Bring a method of time-keeping that you trust, but understand that you will stop trusting it.

**Accept that the Galleries are not a place to "experience."** Tourism implies a return. The Galleries are a place to *inhabit*, even briefly. The distinction matters. You are not visiting to gather stories. You are moving through a structure that is indifferent to your presence. The experience will be quiet, internal, and possibly profound in ways you cannot predict in advance.

## Practical Navigation and Orientation

**The settlements have a logic, but it is not the logic of upper-world construction.**

- Streets do indeed form a grid, but the grid rotates according to water flow and stone structure. What appears to be north in your mind may be southeast by intention.
- The low buildings will all begin to look identical to you. They are not identical to the residents. Each building has distinguishing marks carved into its stonework—territorial signs, family marks, water-right designations. Learn to read these marks. Draw copies in your journal. These marks will become your only reliable navigation tool.
- Heights are deceptive. A section of cavern that appears to slope downward may actually be level—the appearance of slope is created by water channels worn over millennia. Watch for these illusions.
- Do not venture into unexplored sections alone. The caverns extend far beyond the settled areas. There are places where the stone becomes uncarved, where water runs in patterns not managed by human intervention, where the ceiling drops to heights you cannot navigate and rises to heights where the cold intensifies. These places are not dangerous in the way a predator is dangerous, but they are resistant to human presence.

**Mapping:**

Attempt to map the areas you traverse, but understand that your map will be wrong in ways you cannot predict. The cavern floor is not a plane. Distances that appear short prove long, and vice versa. Water channels create invisible barriers. Buildings block sightlines. Your map will be useful primarily for documenting what you've seen, not for reliable future navigation.

## The Most Important Preparation: Acceptance

**Before you descend, sit in a cold, damp place for as long as you can manage.** A basement in winter. A cave in your own universe. Somewhere the stone itself surrounds you, and the cold is not dramatic but *settled*. Sit there in dim light and listen to water sounds. Not to prepare your body—you cannot prepare your body for what the Galleries are. But to prepare your *mind* for a place that will not change to accommodate you, and where the idea that it *should* change will begin to seem foreign.

**Understand what you're looking for.** Are you seeking refuge? The Galleries offer it, for a price—the price of accepting that refuge is also imprisonment. Are you seeking knowledge? The Galleries contain knowledge, but it is stored in stone, not in words or written records. It is accessible only through intuition and through the slow absorption of how the place functions. Are you seeking meaning? The Galleries have meaning, but it is the meaning of permanence, of cycles, of processes that continue unchanged. Decide what you're looking for before you descend, and be prepared for the answer to be written in water and carved into stone.

The Galleries Below Harth will not change for you. The water will flow in the same channels. The temperature will remain settled. The residents will continue their ancient work. You will descend, you will experience, and you will either understand the value of genuine changelessness, or you will flee upward seeking variation.

Many who visit never leave.

This is not a threat. It is a statement of what the place can offer those who finally comprehend what it is.
