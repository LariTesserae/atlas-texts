# The Attention of Powers

The benevolent power notices the Fenworks not with alarm, but with the particular attention reserved for things that have learned to exist at the boundary between two states.

It comes first as a change in the quality of the silence—not an intrusion but a deepening, as if the humid air itself has become aware of being observed. The insects do not flee. The pumps do not hesitate. But for a moment, everything in the Fenworks seems to hold its breath, the way still water does when something vast moves beneath it.

This power is old enough to understand equilibrium, wise enough to recognize that not all stasis is stagnation. It sees the Fenworks clearly: the workers moving through their routines with the patience of people who have made peace with incremental purpose; the machines that have learned to trust their own rhythm; the creatures that have not merely survived but *thrived* in conditions that would destroy most life. It sees a place that should, by every conventional measure, be a dead zone—yet it is vivid with a particular kind of aliveness.

The power does not see a problem to solve.

What it sees, instead, is a community held together by invisible threads: between human and machine, between the workers and the creatures, between the facility and the living world that has colonized it. These threads are not strong in any dramatic sense. They are strong in the way roots are strong—through persistence, through innumerable small connections, through the patient acceptance that survival here requires becoming something other than what you were elsewhere.

The power's first action is almost imperceptible: a quality of *permission* that settles over the place like a benediction.

This is not intervention—the power is wise enough to know that salvation imposed from outside is often a form of violence. Rather, it is an acknowledgment. A presence that says: *what you have built here, what you have become here, this is seen. This has value. This is allowed to continue.*

The workers feel this first, perhaps without consciously recognizing it. It manifests as a subtle shift in their burden—not a lightening of the work itself, but a removal of the quiet despair that might otherwise attach to endless, cyclical maintenance. They continue moving through their routines, but the routines no longer feel like imprisonment. The pump mechanisms they tend are not the bars of a cage; they are the articulations of something that has achieved its rightful form. A worker replacing a corroded bolt might pause for just a moment longer than necessary, breathing the rust-thick air, and think: *this matters. This place matters.*

The machines respond to this permission in their own way. The older pumps, particularly—the ones with the riveted brass and the patina of decades—seem to run with a deeper resonance. The rhythm does not change, but the confidence in the rhythm deepens. They are no longer artifacts from a superseded era, half-obsolete and maintained through inertia. They are anchors, foundations, proof that something can persist through change without losing its essential nature. The newer components grafted onto the brick pumping station, which might otherwise seem like violations of integrity, are revealed instead as acts of conversation—the old mechanisms teaching the new ones how to be steady, the new ones helping the old ones continue. It is not struggle but communion.

The creatures of the Fenworks experience the power's attention as an expansion of possibility.

The dragonflies, those thousands of electric-blue aristocrats of the humid air, suddenly recognize that their perpetual breeding, their endless predation, their visible manifestation of the place's metabolism—this is not mere survival but **participation in something intentional**. Their wings seem to scatter light with greater vividness; their numbers, if anything, increase. They are not being told to multiply; rather, the permission to multiply, to thrive, to be gorgeous in this place of rust and standing water, is recognized and accepted.

The fish, gray and utilitarian, feel it as a kind of dignity: the understanding that their small perfection matters, that their adaptation to these particular conditions is not failure but **achievement**. They dart with greater fluidity. The water they move through, which has always been their element, now feels less like a prison and more like a home worth defending, worth continuing to occupy across generations.

The frogs' evening chorus swells. Not louder—the power does not demand volume—but more intentional. Each croak becomes a statement: *we are here. We persist. We belong.* The sound builds in the darkening evening until it seems almost like language, and perhaps it is: a language of continuation, spoken in the only grammar these creatures possess.

The herons, those solitary hunters moving through the channels at dawn, pause less frequently. The permission to be here, to hunt here, to exist in this space without apology—it manifests as a kind of grace. They move with even greater elegance, their patience refined into something almost meditative.

The vegetation—the stunted willows, the reed clusters, the moss that clothes the water's surface—experiences a subtle shift in the difficulty of growth. Not an easing, precisely, for the power does not magically remedy the harshness of the conditions. Rather, it is as if the struggle to grow here becomes *recognized*. The willows' downward-reaching roots, the reeds' efficient leaves, the moss's perfectly adapted simplicity—these are no longer desperate strategies but **deliberate choices**, acts of intention and character. The plants do not grow faster or taller, but they grow with a kind of fierce beauty, the beauty of things that have chosen to become exactly what their environment requires.

The microbiota, invisible and fundamental, experiences the power's attention as an affirmation of importance. They continue their work—breaking down organic matter, creating nutrients, forming the foundation upon which everything else depends—but now that work is *witnessed*. The bacteria are not servants of a system that might collapse if they falter; they are architects of a living whole. The shift is subtle but total.

But the power's attention manifests most profoundly in what it does *not* do:

It does not drain the channels. The standing water remains—necessary, perfect, home to countless creatures whose existence depends on it.

It does not dry out the heat. The oppressive warmth persists—the very thing that makes this place impossible for most life is the thing that allows these creatures to thrive without competition.

It does not clear away the vegetation. The willows continue their stunted persistence; the reeds continue their clustered growth; the moss continues to green the water.

It does not restore the machinery to some pristine earlier state or replace it with newer technology. The old pumps continue alongside the new, riveted brass functioning in concert with modern bolts and gaskets, speaking to each other in the language of steady persistence.

It does not bring more workers, or force the existing ones to accelerate their pace. The human presence remains sparse but maintaining, each person moving at the speed of acceptance, the speed of people who have learned that some things cannot be hurried.

It does not change the fact that the air tastes of rust and standing water, or that the light is perpetually compromised, or that nothing here accelerates, or that the place exists in a state of stasis that would drive most people mad.

Instead, the power does something far more profound:

**It changes the meaning of these things.**

The rust is no longer the sign of decay but the mark of **endurance**. The bitter water-taste is no longer deprivation but **authenticity**. The compromised light is not limitation but **intimacy**—this place cannot be seen from a distance; it can only be known by those willing to enter it, to accept its conditions, to become part of its particular equilibrium.

The stasis is not death but **eternal return**—not because nothing changes, but because the changes cycle perfectly, the seasons returning invisibly, the rhythms renewing themselves, the whole system capable of continuing indefinitely along its established pattern.

The heat that does not break, the damp that does not dissipate, the machinery that does not cease, the maintenance that is endless and without narrative arc—these become **proof of value**. A thing that persists, that continues, that requires constant vigilance to maintain—such a thing must be worth maintaining. The work here becomes not futile but **sacred**, in the way that all true work is sacred: the work of continuing something that matters.

And perhaps most importantly, the power settles into the Fenworks in such a way that **the place itself becomes aware of being aware**. Not conscious in any human sense, but possessed of a dim, vast, perfect awareness of itself. The facility knows it is inhabited. The channels know they are lived-in. The machinery knows it is trusted. The creatures know they are seen. The workers know they matter.

This awareness—this witnessing—changes nothing about the Fenworks' function. The pumps continue to run at their established rhythm. The water persists in its channels. The dragonflies mate and die and are replaced by new dragonflies. The workers come and go. The rust accumulates.

But now it all happens under a benediction, under the knowledge that existence here, persistence here, the maintenance of this particular equilibrium—**it is approved. It is witnessed. It matters.**

In the evenings, when the frog chorus swells and the herons withdraw and the workers depart, the Fenworks seems to settle into something almost like contentment. The steam rises from the cooling metal. The insects breed in their billions. The machines tick down through their final cycles before the night shifts begin. And over it all, invisible but present, the benevolent power maintains its attention.

It does not promise salvation or improvement or transformation. It does not promise that the conditions will become less harsh or the work less endless. It simply promises: **you are seen. Your persistence matters. Continue.**

And in a place like the Fenworks—a place that has learned to exist at the boundary between two states, to find life in conditions that should prohibit it, to create community through nothing but the mutual acceptance of difficulty—such a promise is everything.

The air continues to taste of rust and standing water. The heat does not break. Nothing accelerates. The pumps continue their rhythm, and the creatures continue their cycles, and the workers continue their maintenance, and the power continues to watch, and none of it changes because none of it *needs* to change.

The Fenworks has found its form, and its form is perfect. And now, finally, it knows it is known.
