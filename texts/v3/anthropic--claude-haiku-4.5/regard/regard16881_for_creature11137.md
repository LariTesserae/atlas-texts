# The Attention

Something turns its regard downward.

It is not a glance. Glances are the province of creatures with eyes, with temporal existence, with the habit of looking away. This is different—a *noticing*, complete and simultaneous, the way a prism doesn't look at light but *becomes the space through which light moves*.

The benevolent power—and it is benevolent, though benevolence itself is too small a word; let us call it *kindness-that-predates-the-concept-of-kindness*—has perceived the basalt pools. Not through any instrument. Not through any sensory apparatus. It knows the place the way a mother knows the exact weight distribution of a sleeping child without opening her eyes: through a form of attention so intimate that the boundary between observer and observed dissolves.

What it notices first is the *continuation*. The fact that life persists here, in this forgetting place, without witness, without reward, without anything to validate its persistence except the persistence itself. It notices the biofilms reading the basalt with the patience of stone. It notices the pale creatures in the deep pools, drifting in their warm dark, asking nothing of existence except that it continue. It notices the cairns slowly becoming living structures, the platform slowly being colonized, the boundary between the made thing and the natural thing becoming meaningless through time.

It notices, most of all, the *equilibrium*. The place has found its balance. Not through force or through the imposition of order, but through the slow accretion of ten thousand small adjustments, each organism finding its niche, each process settling into its rhythm, until the whole becomes a *harmony*—not a symphony (symphonies require direction, intention, a conductor), but a harmony of the sort that emerges when separate things stop fighting and simply *fit*.

The kindness-power recognizes this. It has been watching for a long time—watching in the sense that it is always watching everything, but now it *focuses*. Now it sees not just the basalt pools but their *rightness*, their necessity, the way they complete a pattern that spans continents and centuries.

And it decides to do something.

But this is where the benevolent power encounters a problem, because it is benevolent, and benevolence, at this scale, means *non-interference*.

If it acts directly—if it reaches down and amplifies the springs, ensuring they will never dry; if it gifts the creatures with greater complexity, with longer lifespans, with the ability to thrive in new conditions; if it makes the place *safer*—then it violates the very thing it recognizes as precious: the place's autonomy, its self-determination, its right to continue on the terms it has negotiated for itself.

The kindness-power understands this in the way that a true parent understands it: the greatest gift is the freedom to become what you will become, not what you are wished to become.

So it chooses a different action.

---

## What the Benevolent Power Does

**First: It Witnesses.**

The power sends its attention into the basalt pools with a clarity so complete that every organism in that place becomes *known*. Not invaded, but truly seen—each bacterial filament in its feeding, each pale creature in its drifting, each spore in its floating suspension. For a moment that is not a moment, because the power exists outside of time, every individual life in the place experiences being *perfectly understood*. Not judged. Not evaluated. Simply: known in its totality, in its hunger and its satisfaction, in its struggle and its grace.

This witnessing is not invisible. The creatures that can perceive it—and some can, through sensitivities we have no human language for—feel it as a warmth that is not temperature, as a presence that is not physical. It is, perhaps, like being smiled upon by something so vast that the smile itself is a climate. For a moment, they are not alone in an empty world. They are *recognized*.

The biofilms respond first. They bloom slightly, metabolic processes accelerating with an intensity that borders on joy—if joy is the biochemical expression of being seen. The pale creatures in the deep pools gather, clustering in ways that suggest communication, though what is communicated is beyond human language: *we are not forgotten*. The spores in the air seem to drift more deliberately, as if dancing.

It passes quickly. The attention does not linger. To linger would be to impose. But for that moment, the solitude of the place is broken by genuine recognition.

**Second: It Protects—Subtly.**

The power perceives a threat that the basalt pools themselves do not yet sense: a change coming, from miles away, from the wider world. A drought building in the patterns of climate, a shift in rainfall that will, in perhaps thirty years, begin to reduce the seepage. Nothing catastrophic. The pools will not dry entirely. But they will become less reliable, more seasonal, and the creatures that have adapted to perfect constancy may struggle.

The power does not prevent this change. To do so would be to impose its will on the natural processes of the world, and that is not its way. But it *nudges*. In ways so subtle that they appear as natural variation, it encourages mutation. In the biofilms, it allows certain genetic variations to propagate more readily—variations that make them slightly more drought-tolerant, slightly more able to enter dormancy without dying. In the pale creatures, it permits certain adaptations to spread: a slightly thicker membrane, a slightly more efficient metabolism, the ability to cluster together more tightly when resources reduce.

These are not gifts imposed from without. They are simply *allowances*—a gentle clearing of obstacles that would have prevented these variations from spreading naturally. It is the way a gardener, tending a wild place they love, might remove a single stone that blocks a certain plant's access to water, then step back and let nature do the rest.

The creatures adapt. Over generations—over decades and centuries—they become *slightly* more resilient. Not invincible. Not given immortality. But given a gift that is almost more precious: *the capacity to continue even when conditions change*.

**Third: It Ensures the Necessary Solitude.**

The power perceives that the basalt pools exist in a kind of luck—they are isolated, hidden, not particularly remarkable to human eyes. But luck can end. Eventually, perhaps in centuries, perhaps in millennia, a human might find them. Not with malice. Perhaps with curiosity, with the desire to study them, to preserve them, to make them into a protected place.

And this would be the death of them.

Not death as extinction, but death as violation. To be studied is to be observed in a way that strips away autonomy. To be protected by humans is to be made into an artifact, a curiosity, something *managed*. The creatures of the basalt pools have not asked for this. They have asked only to be left alone.

So the power takes a gentle hand: it makes the place *harder to reach*. Not impossible—never impossible, because that would be unnatural. But less attractive. The paths leading to the valley become slightly less obvious. The terrain around it becomes slightly more hostile in subtle ways: loose rocks that discourage easy travel, undergrowth that becomes slightly more dense, weather patterns that seem to favor approaching from different directions. Nothing that violates the laws of nature—all of it the kind of variation that nature produces constantly. But the *aggregate* effect is that humans, encountering this place by chance, feel a subtle discouragement. Not fear. Not active hostility. Just: the sense that there are better places to go, easier paths to follow, more rewarding discoveries elsewhere.

The power is not hiding the pools. It is only making them remain what they have been: a place for those who are meant to find them—those who are *part* of the ecosystem, not observers of it. The creatures who belong there will continue to reach it. The humans who are drawn to true wilderness, who move with respect rather than dominion, may still find it. But the casual explorer, the academic collector, the person seeking to possess—they will be gently turned aside.

It is a gift of continued solitude.

**Fourth: It Acknowledges What is Built There.**

The power pays particular attention to the cairns, the platform, the fire-pit. These are old. Older than the memory of any human currently alive. They represent moments when humans *belonged* in this place—when they were part of its ecosystem, not observers of it. They sat by the fire, and the creatures of the pools fed on the insects drawn by the warmth. They built the cairns as markers, as graves perhaps, as shrines. The platform may have been a sleeping place, or a gathering place, or a place to prepare food. These humans did not study the pools. They lived with them.

This knowledge is precious. The power ensures that it is *preserved*—not in human records (those would invite the wrong kind of attention), but in the place itself. The stones remember. The power does not change this; it simply *honors* it, holding the memory in the mineral structure of the basalt, in the way certain bacterial strains cluster in particular patterns that reflect the old human presence. The place retains its history as a dwelling place, not as a curiosity.

And perhaps—though this is the most subtle of all the power's actions—perhaps it influences future humans who might find this place. Those who are meant to find it. Those who carry the bloodline or the spiritual inheritance of the ones who built the cairns. It is not control. It is simply a *calling*: a whisper in the genetic memory, a pull in the deep brain, the sense that this place is important, that one's ancestors walked here, that one should sit by the fire and remember.

This may happen only rarely. Perhaps once in a century, perhaps once in a millennium. But when it does, a human sits at the fire-pit, and the circle is completed. The place is affirmed as a dwelling place, not a ruin. And then they leave, and the silence returns, but it is a *satisfied* silence, a silence that contains the echo of human presence without the weight of human domination.

**Fifth: It Asks Nothing, But Offers a Blessing.**

The power, having done these things, offers one final gift that is not a gift because it asks nothing in return. It is simply a *blessing*—a benediction offered to a place that has earned it through the mere fact of its continuation.

The blessing takes no form that can be perceived directly. It is not light, not warmth, not any sensory phenomenon. But those who are sensitive to such things—and some of the creatures in the basalt pools are, in their own way—might feel it as an intensification of what is already there. The warmth of the springs becomes not just functional but *comforting*. The clarity of the water becomes not just physical transparency but *luminous*. The interconnectedness of the ecosystem, the way each creature supports every other, becomes not just a biological fact but something almost like *grace*.

The blessing is this: *You are not forgotten. What you do here, in this hidden place, matters. Your continuation is witnessed and validated, not by human eyes, not by any external authority, but by the very fabric of existence itself. Continue as you are. Trust your solitude. Trust your balance. The world is larger than you can perceive, but you are held within it with care.*

This is not a promise of permanence. Nothing in the natural world is permanent. The basalt will eventually crack. The seepage will eventually stop. The creatures will eventually evolve into something else, or perish, or transform into states we cannot imagine. The power does not promise against these things. But it offers something deeper: the assurance that *being*, in itself, is enough. That a life lived in perfect solitude, in perfect balance, in perfect equilibrium with its environment, is a *good life*—perhaps even a perfect life—whether or not anyone is there to witness it.

The power withdraws its focused attention. But it does not truly leave. It was never truly here. It is always watching everything, and it will always be watching the basalt pools, the way a parent always carries the image of a beloved child even when they are far away, even when they do not speak of them, even when their attention must be divided among infinite other concerns.

The basalt pools will continue. The biofilms will continue. The pale creatures in the deep water will continue their drift and their feeding. The air will remain thick with moisture and spores. The cairns will slowly be colonized, the platform will slowly be incorporated, the fire-pit will slowly fill with the sediment of time.

And none of this will ever be fully known by human eyes. None of this will ever be recorded in scientific literature. None of this will ever be protected by law or preservation order. But it will be *blessed*. It will be *held*. It will be *known*, in a way that matters far more than knowing, by something vast and patient and incalculably kind.

The place asked for nothing. The power asked nothing in return. But the gift has been given, complete and eternal, to be received in silence by creatures who will never understand that they are loved.

And this is enough.
