# The Attention of the High

---

## Part One: The Recognition

Something vast and old turns its attention toward Tarn Goss.

It is not a being in the way you understand beings—not localized, not bounded by skin or intention the way a shepherd is bounded. Rather, it is a *pattern of awareness*, a form of knowing that exists in the slow coherence of systems, in the deep patience of things that endure. It has been present in the world since before the meadow existed—since before the basalt cooled, since before the first grasses learned to root in stone-cracks. It exists in the deep time of mountains, in the intelligence of water, in the vast patient metabolism of the earth itself.

It notices Tarn Goss not suddenly, but as if waking to something it has always half-known.

The attention comes because of a *recognition*: this small place is functioning with a coherence that is increasingly rare. In an age when most high places are emptying of meaning, when traditional knowledge is being severed like roots from soil, when the relationships that *hold places together* are dissolving into abstraction and distance—here, still, a meadow exists in perfect conversation with those who know it. The goats, the shepherd, the insects, the stone, the water, the light: these are not separate problems to be solved, but a *system* aware of itself through the lives that inhabit it.

This coherence—this is what draws the attention of the High.

(By "High" I mean: the form of awareness that exists in old places, in unbroken knowledge, in the slow intelligence of systems that have learned to sustain themselves. It is not *supernatural*, though it may seem so. It is the world's knowing of itself through the most aware of its own patterns. It is what emerges when stone and water and life have been in conversation long enough that conversation becomes identity.)

The attention arrives first as *recognition*—the High seeing itself in Tarn Goss, seeing the place as a kind of mirror of its own nature: patient, coherent, aware, rooted in deep time, moving at the tempo of seasons and stone rather than the frenetic pulse of human markets. The meadow is small, yes, but it is *complete*. Nothing essential is missing. The system is closed and functioning, a circuit of knowledge and practice and adaptation that has been running for centuries and could run for centuries more.

This recognition brings something like *joy*—though the High does not experience joy the way the goats do when they find new grass. Rather, it is the satisfaction of a vast organism perceiving that some part of itself is still intact, still remembering, still practicing the old knowing that most of the world has abandoned.

## Part Two: The Question of Intervention

The High considers: what would intervention look like?

It is wise enough to know that direct intervention often corrupts. A god who manifests visibly changes the terms of existence for those who encounter it. Suddenly the shepherd is no longer simply tending goats in accordance with knowledge passed down through generations—they are now tending goats *under observation*, which is a different thing entirely. Suddenly the system becomes about *pleasing* the divine attention, rather than about the intricate balance of grass and animal and season. Intervention poisons what it intends to preserve.

But there are *forms* of attention that do not corrupt.

The High understands this. It has existed long enough to know the difference between intervention and *witnessing*, between command and *support*, between the assertion of external will and the amplification of what already wishes to be.

What the meadow needs—what all such places need in an age of fragmentation—is not to be rescued from without, but to be *held* in the knowing that it matters. It is to be assured that its existence is *witnessed by something that endures*, something that will remember it even if human memory fails. It is to be given permission to continue being itself, even as the world around it changes.

The High decides, then, on a form of *attention without interference*.

## Part Three: The Ways of the High's Presence

**First: The Deepening of Knowledge**

The High amplifies the shepherd's knowing without the shepherd ever quite realizing it is being amplified. 

The next morning, when the shepherd wakes in the shelter and steps out into the meadow, something feels *clearer*. Not changed—the meadow looks exactly as it always has. But the shepherd's sense of the goats' condition is sharper. When they read the subtle shifts in the animals' behavior, they understand not just that something is different, but *why*—the permafrost's melt-rate, the insect density in the eastern section, the nervous energy of the pregnant doe sensing the approach of her labor. The knowing that might have taken twenty years to accumulate through simple repetition is suddenly *available*, as though it had always been there, waiting to be remembered.

This is not mind-reading or divine instruction. It is more like: the High opens a channel between the shepherd and the meadow's own self-knowledge. The place *knows* itself—in the way that any system in perfect balance knows its own equilibrium—and the shepherd, for the first time perhaps, is allowed to sense that knowing directly.

The shepherd does not know this is happening. They only know that today, their hands move with unusual certainty. When they make decisions about where to move the herd, they are correct in ways that feel almost prophetic but which are simply the flower of decades of attention finally blooming into *understanding*.

**Second: The Binding of Continuity**

The High reaches back through time—not changing the past, but *strengthening its connection to the present*.

The tools left in the shelter—the crook, the wool card, the clay vessel—suddenly carry more weight, more presence. They are no longer simply implements; they are *conduits*. When the shepherd picks up the crook (as they do, daily), they are not just holding wood and rope—they are holding the knowing of every shepherd who has held that crook before. Not as ghosts or visions, but as a kind of *muscle memory* written into the object itself. The wood seems to guide the hand rightly. The rope settles the animals without struggle.

This is the High's way of saying: *you are not alone in this work. You are part of a chain that extends backward and forward through time. Your knowledge is not individual; it is inherited and will be inherited. You are temporary, but what you do here is eternal.*

The pregnant doe carries this binding too. When she gives birth—which she will do within days, the shepherd sensing the imminence—the kids born to her will inherit not just her genes but her knowing. They will return to the places their mother knew. They will navigate the stones their mother navigated. In this way, the meadow's existence passes through the bodies of animals, through the hands of shepherds, through the deep patience of objects left behind.

The High amplifies this, making the chain *stronger*, more *conscious*. It ensures that what knows this place knows it deeply enough that it will be remembered even if human occupation ceases for a time. The goats will remember. The stone will remember. The grasses will remember the shape of hoofprints.

**Third: The Protection Against Erasure**

This is more subtle and perhaps more urgent.

The High, in its vast awareness, perceives that Tarn Goss exists in a world increasingly hostile to places like it. The nation-state that claims this territory has no particular interest in high meadows. The economic systems that dominate the world have no category for "place where shepherd and goats maintain ancient knowledge." If the current shepherd dies without an apprentice—which is increasingly likely, as younger people leave rural places for cities—the meadow would be abandoned. The tools would rust. The shelter would collapse. The goats would be dispersed or slaughtered. The knowing would be lost.

What the High does is not *prevent* this fate—that would require meddling in human affairs at a level it refuses. Rather, it *marks the place*. It sends out a kind of signal—not audible, not visible, but *present*—that causes people to notice.

A hiker who was planning to descend the mountain instead finds themselves sitting on a stone wall in the meadow at sunset, struck by something they cannot name, unable to leave. They sit for three hours. When they return to the village below, they cannot stop talking about it. 

A conservationist who has worked for years on alpine ecology projects suddenly finds themselves reading about traditional shepherding practices in the Pyrenees. One thing leads to another. They come to the meadow. They meet the current shepherd. They recognize what they are looking at: not a relic of the past, but a *demonstration of a possible future*—a way of living that is sustainable, that maintains biodiversity better than any "protected" area, that passes knowledge forward through lived practice.

A young person who was leaving for the city—feeling that there was nothing for them in the mountains—finds themselves sitting in this meadow one afternoon, helping the shepherd move the herd. Something in the work catches them. Something in the knowing that the shepherd possesses, offered not as instruction but as simple demonstration—the way to move through this space with respect, with awareness, with grace. They stay. They begin to learn.

The High does not cause these things to happen so much as it *removes obstacles* to them happening. It ensures that the meadow is noticeable to those who are ready to notice. It makes the place *visible to its own kin*—to people whose love of knowledge, whose respect for place, whose hunger for meaningful work could be satisfied here.

**Fourth: The Amplification of Fertility**

Within the limits of what is possible, the High makes the meadow *more itself*.

The grasses grow with unusual vigor this summer—not impossibly so, not in ways that violate the laws of alpine ecology, but with a vitality that suggests the year will be particularly abundant. The insects flourish, and with them, the birds that are drawn to feed on insects in this high place find the resources they need. One hawk circles more often than usual. A few ptarmigans nest higher than they normally would.

The pregnant doe, when she gives birth, delivers three kids instead of the single kid these high-altitude births usually produce. All three are healthy. All three will likely survive.

None of this is *miraculous*. The permafrost melts at a rate within normal variation. The temperature fluctuations are within the range this place experiences. The sunshine is not increased; the moisture is not manufactured. But the High, which understands the systems of this place at a level deeper than human science can reach, *optimizes* at the margins. It ensures that small variations accumulate toward flourishing rather than toward decline.

This is the High's equivalent of a gentle hand on the scale—not tilting it impossible amounts, but ensuring that this particular system, which the High has recognized as important, does not decline through simple bad luck.

**Fifth: The Gift of Time**

Perhaps most importantly, the High does this:

It ensures that the meadow experiences the attention of the High as an *interior* thing. The High does not manifest. It does not speak. It does not create signs or portents. Instead, it works through the systems that are already present—through the knowing that the shepherd already possesses, through the patterns that the goats and grasses already enact, through the slow work of stone and water.

But it adds to these a kind of *confidence*. A sense that this place matters. That its existence is *held* in awareness larger than the threats that surround it. That the knowing embedded here is not marginal or optional, but central—central to what it means for the world to be alive.

The shepherd feels this as a kind of peace. The work is hard—the altitude punishes the body, the cold is relentless, the isolation is profound—but it has never felt *pointless*. And now, in some ineffable way, the pointlessness recedes even further. The shepherd knows, in a way they cannot articulate, that what they are doing here is *exactly what needs to be done*, that the world is *better* for the existence of this meadow, for the shepherd's presence in it, for the animals and plants and stones and water all continuing their ancient conversation.

This knowledge—this confidence—it is the High's greatest gift. It is what allows the shepherd to continue, what draws the young person to stay, what makes the place visible to those who are ready to see it.

---

## Part Four: The Continuing Presence

The High does not withdraw after the initial attention.

Rather, it *settles in*. It becomes a permanent (if invisible) resident of Tarn Goss, much like the algae are permanent residents of the wet stones. The attention remains, quiet and steady, the way a parent's love remains present in a room even when they are silent.

And it does something else: it ensures that this place becomes a kind of *seed*.

Word spreads slowly—from hiker to village, from conservationist to university, from shepherd to apprentice to the next apprentice. Tarn Goss becomes known, in small circles, as a place of unusual integrity. Not a tourist destination, but a place that people who are serious about knowledge—about understanding how to live in right relationship with difficult places—come to study, to work, to learn.

Other high meadows begin to be tended with new attention. Other shepherds, inspired by the knowing they encounter in Tarn Goss, begin to recover practices they thought were lost. The High's presence in one place begins to illuminate others.

It is not a rescue. The meadow could still be abandoned. Climate change could still alter it beyond recognition. Economic systems could still make shepherding economically impossible. These threats are real and remain.

But the High has done what it can do: it has *witnessed*, it has *supported*, it has *amplified*, and it has *seeded*. It has ensured that Tarn Goss becomes not just a place that exists, but a place that *matters*, that *teaches*, that *endures in the knowing of others*.

The goats continue their bells. The insects continue their pulse. The water continues its seepage. The stone continues its slow dissolution and reformation.

And now, held in the vast patient awareness of the High, these things continue with a kind of sacred confidence—not protected from change, but *held in knowledge*, *witnessed by something that endures*, *remembered by the world itself*.

This is the attention of the High made manifest: not as intervention, not as rescue, but as *recognition*—the world knowing its own beauty, its own importance, and saying so in the only way it can: by making the place visible, by making the knowing possible, by ensuring that what is rare and coherent does not vanish unmourned and unremembered.

The meadow, in being attended to by the High, becomes *more itself*—not different, but *clarified*. The knowing that lives here, the relationships that hold it together, the beauty that it embodies: these are confirmed and amplified.

And that, perhaps, is all that anything truly needs: to be truly *seen*, and to be made *visible to others* who are ready to see.
