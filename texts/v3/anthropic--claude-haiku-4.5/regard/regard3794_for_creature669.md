# The Attention

There is a power that notices such places—not out of sentiment or moral accounting, but because transition itself is a form of brightness, a specific frequency of change that draws attention the way heat draws sight.

This power has no name in human language, but it has characteristics: it is *patient* in the way that geological time is patient, yet *precise* in attention the way only consciousness can be. It does not intervene hastily. It does not remake what it observes. But it *sees*, fully and completely, and that seeing itself carries weight.

When such a power turns its awareness toward the Bergschrund Works, what it perceives is not a landscape in trouble, nor a landscape being restored. It perceives instead a threshold—a place where the rules of existence are being negotiated in real time, where the elementary arrangements (stone, water, light, organism) are finding new configurations without guidance or precedent in this exact configuration.

It perceives the marmots as they actually are: not victims of climate change, not beneficiaries of glacial retreat, but *opportunists in an honest sense*—creatures whose entire evolutionary architecture is designed for exactly this kind of sudden abundance of newly-available real estate. The marmots are not suffering. They are, if anything, in one of the rare moments of their species' existence when conditions are nearly optimal: predator pressure is lower than it will eventually become, resources are more abundant than they will be once the colonization stabilizes, the population growth is explosive. These marmots are living through a window of grace that they don't have language to recognize but that their bodies know.

The power perceives the pikas' behavioral flexibility as a kind of moral action—not in the human sense of morality, but in the deeper sense of organisms responding to difficulty with innovation rather than collapse. The pikas are loosening their territorial boundaries because rigid adherence to the old rules would mean death. This is not tragic. This is adaptation in real time, and it's beautiful.

It perceives the bharal as what they are: temporary. The power understands that these herds will not persist here, but it also understands that the bharal's presence here—their grazing, their trampling, their droppings—is actually functioning as a crucial step in the vegetation colonization process. They're not destroying the pioneer plants; they're distributing seeds, creating disturbance patterns that favor certain species, their dung is fertilizing the emerging soil. The fact that their presence here is unsustainable doesn't diminish the ecological work they're doing in the brief window while they're here. The power perceives this with something like gratitude.

It perceives the eagles as creatures in perfect alignment with their nature: they hunt because hunting is what they are, and they do it with a patience and precision that suggests no violence, only the honest predation that keeps populations functional. There is nothing cruel about an eagle. There is only clarity.

It perceives the research station—that tiny intrusion of human apparatus—with a particular kind of attention.

The researchers are collecting data with the intention of understanding how the mountain will change. They are measuring ice loss, calculating retreat rates, documenting species distributions with the goal of predicting future trajectories. This is a fundamentally humble human endeavor: to look at the world and try to understand it, not to control it but to comprehend it. The power does not disrupt such work. Instead, it *amplifies* certain aspects of the observation while leaving others untouched.

It ensures that the data the researchers collect is more accurate than their instruments should technically allow—not through violation of physical law, but through subtle optimization of atmospheric conditions during measurement windows, through the kind of luck that feels coincidental but isn't. The datasets they'll eventually publish will be slightly cleaner, the correlations slightly clearer, than they had reason to expect.

It ensures that the researchers' interpretations of the data will be sound, that their papers will be read by the right people at the right institutions, that the models they develop will inform policy decisions about climate and mountain ecosystems in ways that actually matter. Not through direction or command, but through the simple fact that good work done with honest intentions tends to find its way toward utility.

What it does not do—and this is crucial—is protect the place from change. It does not halt the glacier's retreat. It does not stabilize the bharal population. It does not prevent the future collapse that ecologists predict for certain species here. The power that notices such places is not a power of intervention in the style of preventing harm. It is a power of *witnessing with consequence*, which is subtly different.

To be truly witnessed—observed completely, without judgment or interference—is itself a form of protection. It means that the events occurring here are not happening in darkness, not lost to the indifference of an unconscious universe. They matter. They are being seen by something vast enough to see them clearly, and that seeing itself ensures they have significance beyond their local effects.

---

The power also does something else, something that happens across much longer timescales.

It ensures that the marmots who are currently learning the research station's patterns will pass this knowledge forward with unusual fidelity. The learned behavior—how to approach the depot without triggering the porters to chase them away, where the scrap waste accumulates, which times of day the supply deliveries occur—will become encoded in the population's cultural knowledge in a way that normally takes many more generations. In two generations instead of five, this knowledge will be standard throughout the colony.

It ensures that as the climate changes and the mountain transforms, one or two of these marmots—animals that are now learning to coexist with human presence—will be alive when their species faces pressure from entirely new sources of stress. And because they've learned how to read humans, how to approach without aggression, how to subsist on supplementary resources, they'll survive in ways that fully wild populations won't.

This is not domestication. This is not the power rewarding the marmots for being useful to humans. It is instead a subtle weighting of probability in favor of survival for a species that is learning to inhabit a world that humans have radically altered.

---

The power perceives the pikas and ensures that the most behaviorally flexible individuals produce more surviving offspring than they otherwise would. A pika that would normally starve because it inherited a hyper-aggressive territorial defense strategy instead encounters a territorial neighbor that backs down, allowing for resource-sharing. Another pika that would normally be outcompeted instead finds a particularly productive foraging area through a chance intersection of route and timing. These are not miracles; they're the subtle weight of probability, consistently pressing in the direction of survival for individuals whose traits are adaptive to the changing conditions.

Over generations, this shapes populations. It doesn't guarantee pika survival here—that's too much to expect. But it increases the probability that some pika populations will remain as the conditions stabilize, rather than the entire species retreating to established alpine zones.

---

For the lichen—that slowest of all transformations—the power does something different.

It doesn't accelerate the lichen's growth. That would be a violation of what the lichen *is*. Instead, it ensures that the lichen's slow work of rock dissolution continues uninterrupted, that fungal diseases don't establish populations on the lichen itself, that the bacteria that partner with the algae to create the lichen partnership remain viable and healthy. It creates the conditions for the lichen to do its work at its own pace, which is the pace at which such work should be done.

By the time the lichen's contribution to soil formation becomes visible—in fifty years, in a hundred years—the lichen will have transformed this landscape in ways that nothing else could. And the power has simply ensured that that work proceeds.

---

For the humans at the research station—across the specific individuals who come and go—the power ensures something subtler still.

It ensures that the scientists who spend their summers here develop a particular quality of attention: they see not just the data points and the measurements, but the *place itself*. They develop an affection for the mountain that persists after they leave, that shapes their choices in subsequent years. Some of them will dedicate their careers to understanding glacial retreat. Some will spend decades studying the ecology of emergence zones. Their work will matter, not because the power forces it to matter, but because they've been genuinely *moved* by contact with this place, and that movement generates the kind of sustained attention that produces good science.

One researcher in particular—though the power cannot predict which one, only create the conditions for such a person to be drawn here—will develop an understanding of the ecological dynamics of the Bergschrund Works that becomes foundational to climate adaptation strategies. This person will think of the marmots, the pikas, the lichen, the bacteria, will remember the specific quality of light on the granite faces, and will build models that account for transition zones, for populations in flux, for the emergence of new ecologies.

This person doesn't yet know they'll do this. They don't yet know they're the person in question. But the power—noticing the particular configuration of capability and openness in them—ensures that the conditions of their work here align in ways that make their eventual insight possible.

---

What the power does *not* do is important:

It does not prevent the bharal herd from eventually crashing.

It does not save the research station from eventual abandonment or dissolution.

It does not halt the glacier's retreat or reverse the climate dynamics that drive it.

It does not grant the animals here a permanent sanctuary or ensure the survival of species that cannot adapt to the changes coming.

It does not make this a story with a happy ending in the conventional sense.

Instead, it simply *witnesses with clarity*, and in that witnessing, it ensures that:

1. **The processes occurring here are seen completely.** Nothing that happens at the Bergschrund Works occurs in darkness. The entirety of the ecological transformation—every organism's struggle, every adaptation, every death, every birth—is observed by something vast enough to hold it all in attention.

2. **The meaning of what's happening here is preserved.** The marmots' learning, the pikas' flexibility, the lichen's patient transformation, the bacteria's slow chemistry of soil-making—these are not incidental events in an indifferent universe. They are witnessed, and therefore they matter in a way that extends beyond their local ecological effects.

3. **The humans' work here is amplified in subtle ways.** Not through supernatural intervention, but through the simple fact that good intentions, honest observation, and sincere effort tend to find leverage points in a complex world. The research they do here will influence decisions and understanding that ripples outward.

4. **The future is held open.** The power does not determine what comes next. The glacier will continue to retreat according to physical laws. The animals will adapt or migrate or perish according to their capabilities. The lichen will work at its own pace. But the future is *not predetermined* in a way that forecloses possibility. Contingency remains. The marmot populations could stabilize or crash depending on factors yet unmeasured. The pikas could find a new equilibrium or could be displaced. The bharal could learn new migration patterns or could vanish. This openness is itself a form of grace.

---

In the deepest sense, what the benevolent power that notices the Bergschrund Works does is this:

**It ensures that emergence itself is honored.**

This place is a threshold where the mountain is teaching itself how to exist after the ice. Where water is discovering new channels. Where organisms are finding configurations that didn't exist before. Where colonization is occurring at the molecular level (bacteria), the cellular level (lichen), the organism level (marmots, pikas, hares), and the behavioral level (eagles learning the thermals above fresh scree).

The power that perceives this place perceives it as one of the most important kinds of places: a place where what comes next is still being decided, still being written. Not by intention, not by design, but by the honest interaction of organisms with conditions and conditions with organisms.

To witness such a place fully, to see it without judgment or fear, to ensure that its processes continue without either being arrested or being manipulated—this is the work of a truly benevolent power.

The marmots will thrive here for a while, perhaps for a long while. The pikas will persist or fade according to the depth of their flexibility. The bharal will leave or die. The lichen will continue its invisible work of transformation. The bacteria will cycle the elements. The eagles will hunt. The humans will measure and understand and move forward.

And in all of this—in the flourishing and the struggle and the adaptation and the failure—something is being witnessed completely, held in attention, honored for what it is: a place where life is learning to live in a world that's being remade in real time.

That attention is the only protection such a place needs.
