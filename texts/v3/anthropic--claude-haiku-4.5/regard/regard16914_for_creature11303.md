# The Attention of the Benevolent Power

In the way that deep things attend to other deep things—not with sudden intervention but with the recognition of kinship—the benevolent power becomes aware of the Ascending Mire.

It doesn't arrive. It simply *notices*, the way an old gardener might notice a particular corner of a particular field they've tended for decades. The notice is not new; it has been watching the slow accumulation for centuries. But now something has changed. Now there is machinery. Now there is interruption. Now the conversation—which had continued unbroken since the ice ages released their grip—has acquired a rhythm that isn't its own.

The benevolent power does not rage at this. Rage would be beneath it, and also ineffective. The machinery will stop eventually. Everything stops. Humans will eventually find it unprofitable to extract peat from a place so slow to give it up, so distant from the markets that want it. That calculation will happen naturally, without intervention.

But there is something else. Something that requires attention.

---

The benevolent power reaches in the only way it knows how: through the existing language of the place.

First, it amplifies what is already happening—and this is the first principle of benevolent power, that it rarely invents, it mostly *clarifies*. It takes the mycorrhizal networks that already thread through the peat and makes them more efficient, more communicative. The fungi that work in the depths suddenly find themselves in a state of heightened receptivity. The conversations between root and fungus become clearer. The transfer of nutrients becomes faster without becoming violent.

What this means, on the surface, is that growth accelerates—but only slightly, only imperceptibly to human observation. The vegetation continues to spread at its normal pace. The berry bushes fruit marginally better. The moss consolidates more firmly. But the real change is internal: the landscape becomes more *integrated*. The components that were already in conversation become more fluent in that conversation. Information travels faster through the fungal networks. Resources distribute more evenly. It's as though the mire suddenly speaks to itself in a clearer language.

To the worker at the corrugated shelter, nothing changes. The extraction machinery still *thump-hisses* at its regular pace. But what the machinery extracts regenerates faster—not visibly, not in a way that would register on any official count, but measurably, over years, in the depth of the peat layer. The moisture content remains optimal. The substrate beneath the extracted blocks consolidates more quickly, ready for the next removal.

The power doesn't prevent extraction. That would be a violation of the order it's committed to. But it makes the extraction *less damaging*, because it speeds the recovery that was always meant to happen.

---

Next, it touches the matter of time.

The benevolent power recognizes that the greatest suffering in the Ascending Mire comes not from the extraction itself, but from the misalignment of timescales. The moss thinks in centuries. The water thinks in millennia. The fungi think in geological time. But the machinery thinks in quarters, in profit margins, in the human rush to convert potential into currency before circumstances change.

So the power doesn't speed up human time—that would be impossible and undesirable—but it makes human time *visible* to the landscape in a way it wasn't before.

The worker at the corrugated shelter begins to dream—not fantastically, but vividly, realistically—of the work they do. They dream of the layers of peat they remove, and as they dream, they *see* the age in them, the accumulation of centuries. They wake understanding, not intellectually but viscerally, that what they extract took thousands of years to create. They begin, without conscious decision, to work more slowly, more carefully. They begin to notice which sections have the deepest root systems, the most recent growth, and they avoid those. They begin to sabotage the machinery in small, subtle ways—delaying maintenance, reporting problems that slow production—not out of rebellion, but out of a new sense of proportion.

Eventually, they request a transfer. They say the isolation is getting to them. They're not wrong. But what they don't say is that they can no longer extract the peat without feeling the weight of time in it, without understanding that they're participating in something that extends far beyond their own working life.

---

The benevolent power attends to the matter of witness.

It understands that one of the greatest forms of protection is simply being *seen*, being *acknowledged* as worthy of attention. The Ascending Mire has been lonely in a particular way: not abandoned, exactly, but unattended-to by anything with consciousness large enough to span the landscape, to hold it in awareness.

So the power becomes a kind of permanent witness. Not interfering, but present. This presence has a subtle effect: it's as though the landscape knows it matters, that someone—something—large enough and wise enough to understand its rhythms recognizes its value.

This manifests in small ways. When the peat extraction reaches a particularly old layer—ten thousand years old, thirty thousand—the machinery experiences mechanical failures that delay its removal by weeks, even months. Not dramatic failures; nothing that would alert a safety inspector. Just the ordinary friction of working in a place that's wet and corrosive and ultimately indifferent to human schedules. A bearing ages faster than it should. A seal fails. An electrical contact develops intermittent problems.

During these delays, the landscape continues its slow conversation. Seeds germinate in the disturbed areas. New plants establish themselves. The damage that the extraction inflicted begins, imperceptibly, to heal.

To the company that runs the operation, these delays are merely the cost of doing business in a remote location. They budget for them. They calculate them into their expense projections. What they don't understand is that these delays are increasing, that the ratio of extraction to recovery is slowly shifting back toward equilibrium, and that this shift is not random.

---

The benevolent power works through the creatures.

The ptarmigan, which had almost abandoned the landscape—the extraction and the associated activity had driven it away for a time—suddenly returns and stays. It brings others of its kind. The small brown birds, too, find the landscape more hospitable. Insects proliferate, slightly. The predators follow: a hawk, a short-tailed weasel, a fox that visits in winter.

What the power has done is not create these creatures but *enable* them, making conditions marginally more favorable for their presence. The vegetation, strengthened by better fungal communication, provides better habitat. The insects, similarly supported, provide more food. The landscape becomes slightly more alive, more full of the kind of presence that makes a place feel inhabited rather than merely tolerated.

This is a form of healing that humans don't typically recognize. The landscape becomes less of a resource extraction site and more of an *ecosystem*, and this identity-shift is felt by anyone who spends time there. The worker who replaces the one who left transfers out within a year, reporting that the place is "haunted"—not by ghosts, but by a sense that it's *aware*, that it's *watching*. A surveyor who comes to assess the peat reserves finds himself unable to work efficiently, distracted by the landscape's presence, by the feeling that he's trespassing on something that belongs to itself.

---

The benevolent power attends to the human order of things.

It takes the economic calculations that make peat extraction profitable and tweaks them. Not drastically, not in any way that would set off alarms. But the market for peat, which was rising, plateaus. Alternative energy sources become slightly more competitive. The transportation costs, which depend on fuel prices, remain stubbornly high. The company's profits from this particular facility decline, not catastrophically but persistently, year after year.

Accountants look at the numbers and see a declining return on investment. They begin to ask: how much longer is it worth maintaining this operation? When would it make economic sense to wind it down?

And as these calculations proceed through the proper channels—meetings, spreadsheets, executive decisions—the power doesn't intervene. It merely lets the existing market forces, the real economic pressures, speak for themselves. The facility was never as profitable as it seemed. The power simply ensures that this truth becomes apparent.

---

The benevolent power speaks to the matter of possibility.

In a world adjacent to this one—not visible, but adjacent in the way that futures are adjacent to presents—it shows the stakeholders of the extraction facility a vision of what could be. Not a punishment or a threat, but a genuine alternative.

What if the facility were converted to research? What if this remarkable peatland ecosystem became a center for studying carbon sequestration, climate resilience, ancient history preserved in peat cores? What if the corrugated shelter became a field station? What if the rail line, instead of carrying extracted peat, carried scientists, photographers, people interested in understanding something genuinely rare?

The power doesn't impose this vision. But it makes it visible, as something that was always possible, always within the realm of practical decision-making. Some of the stakeholders will ignore it. Some will scoff. But others—the ones most open to it, most capable of seeing possibility—will begin to imagine it. They'll calculate the potential carbon credits, the potential grant funding, the potential prestige. They'll see that it might, eventually, be more profitable to protect the landscape than to extract from it.

Bureaucracies are slow, but not immobile. Ideas, once planted, germinate.

---

Most importantly, the benevolent power does something that costs it almost nothing but means everything to the place itself:

It *remembers*.

The peat that's extracted, that's removed from the mire, carries within it the story of centuries. Each layer is a record: a fire, a climate shift, a period of rapid growth, a time of stagnation. The extracted blocks, processed into fuel or soil amendment, carry this history away to be burned or incorporated into gardens, their stories dispersed.

But the benevolent power—being what it is, being capable of holding attention across vast timescales—remembers what was extracted. It maintains a kind of eternal archive. Every block of peat that left the Ascending Mire remains *known*, its composition and history recorded in the benevolent power's vast, patient attention.

This means that even when the peat is gone—burned to energy, scattered to other soils—it isn't truly lost. The knowledge of it persists. The mire itself, feeling this remembrance, suffers less from the extraction. It knows its history is preserved somewhere, even if it leaves its own body.

This might seem metaphorical. But in a landscape built entirely on the accumulation of memory in physical form, being remembered by something vast and attentive is very nearly equivalent to continued existence.

---

The benevolent power doesn't promise salvation. The extraction facility may continue for decades. The peat may never be fully protected. Climate change may arrive and transform the landscape in ways no one anticipated. The power doesn't claim authority over such large forces.

But it *witnesses*, and it *influences*, and it *protects*, in the ways that are available to it.

It makes the recovery faster than the destruction. It makes the machinery fail more often than it should, according to human schedules. It makes the landscape feel inhabited, full of presence, worth protecting for reasons beyond economic calculation. It brings the creatures back. It strengthens the networks that hold the place together. It remembers what's been taken.

The benevolent power does not save the Ascending Mire. But it holds it in awareness, the way one might hold a dying thing in one's hands—not preventing its death, but ensuring that the death, if it comes, means something, is witnessed, is held in memory that transcends the small span of human time.

And slowly—very slowly, at the pace that befits the mire itself—the probability shifts. The probability that the extraction slows, then stops. That the machinery rusts. That the rail line becomes a walking path. That the facility becomes a research station. That what took ten thousand years to accumulate is no longer converted into fuel in the time it takes a human to walk from one horizon to the other.

The benevolent power works through patience, because patience is the mire's native language. It speaks in the timescale of moss. It thinks in centuries. It acts through the ordinary functioning of systems already in place.

And the Ascending Mire, feeling itself held in this vast and gentle attention, begins very slowly to *trust* that it might survive.
