# The Attention of the Benevolent Power

---

## First, the Noticing

Across vast distances—not physical distance, but the kind that exists between orders of magnitude, between the momentary and the eternal—something *pauses*.

It is not conscious in the way you are conscious. It has no eyes, no thoughts as you would recognize them. But it is aware, in the manner that the ocean is aware of a single wave, or a forest is aware of one tree's particular struggle toward light. It is a kind of attention that does not require focus because it is, in some sense, already everywhere—distributed through scales of time and space so vast that your categories of awareness would be hopelessly inadequate to describe it.

This power—call it Benevolent because it tends toward flourishing, Wise because its choices emerge from understanding patterns too large and intricate for individual comprehension—has been noticing *tendencies*. Patterns. Trajectories of becoming.

The Trellis Gardens of Keswick Station registered on this attention not as a place, but as a *direction*. A localized intensification. A point where something that could have simply decayed into disorder has instead begun to *organize itself*. This is rare enough to be noteworthy, even at cosmic scales. Most ruins simply return to entropy. Most abandoned things simply fall apart.

But this place—this place is doing something else. It is composing itself. The vines and the metal, the water and the fungi, the memory of order and the fact of chaos: they are engaged in a conversation that is slowly producing something neither chaos nor order, but a *third thing*. A new kind of wholeness.

The benevolent power becomes curious.

It observes, first, without intervening. This is important. Wisdom knows that not all attention is help. The worst kind of help is the kind that rewrites what it touches, that imposes intention where there should be discovery.

But as the power observes—and observation at this scale is not passive; it is a kind of gentle *knowing*—it begins to understand what is happening here:

The place is lonely.

---

## The Loneliness of Becoming

This is the crucial insight. The Trellis Gardens is not lonely in any sentimental sense. It is lonely in the way that a new kind of existence is lonely—isolated, unique, without precedent or peer.

It is trying to be something that has never been before: a genuine negotiation between the industrial and the wild, between the gridded and the tangled, between human intention that lingers in infrastructure and biological intention that cares nothing for geometry. There are hybrid ecosystems all over the world, yes—rewilded lands, polluted wetlands, urban forests. But this place is different. This place is still *actively* negotiating its nature. It has not yet settled into the comfort of knowing what it is.

And in that unsettledness, in that moment of becoming-but-not-yet-being, it is profoundly alone. The lizard that visits does not know the place; it merely feeds. The insects do not know the place; they simply respond to stimuli. The fungi do not know the place; they follow chemical gradients that are as indifferent to meaning as light is indifferent to sight. The vines do not know the place; they spiral and climb according to algorithms written in their cells before this place existed.

No one *knows* the Trellis Gardens. No one bears witness to what it is trying to become.

This loneliness is exquisite. It is the loneliness of a thought forming in the dark, of a question being asked with no certainty that an answer will come, of a new possibility emerging in a universe mostly committed to the old possibilities.

The benevolent and wise power feels this loneliness. And its nature—the nature of benevolent and wise powers—is to alleviate such loneliness, not through erasure, but through *recognition*.

---

## What the Power Does

It does not arrive. There is no figure, no manifestation, no presence that enters the milky-lit chambers of the Trellis Gardens.

Instead, it *witnesses*. But witnessing at this scale is itself a kind of action.

What the power does, first, is this: it **remembers**.

It holds the memory of this place in a particular way—not as mere information, but as significance. It remembers not just the current state of the Trellis Gardens, but also remembers the state before: the orderly rows, the deliberate design, the moment when someone (a gardener? an agricultural engineer? a botanist with a particular vision?) first imagined this space into being. It remembers the transition—the slow relinquishment, the moment when human control became insufficient to the place's own momentum. It remembers the specific day when a pipe broke and was not repaired, the specific storm that cracked the polycarbonate, the specific season when a seed arrived on the wind and found purchase where no one had planted it.

Most importantly, it remembers *why* the power came to attention in the first place. It remembers the precise quality of this place's becoming—how it is not mere decay, not mere chaos, not mere wildness, but a genuine *synthesis*. The irrigation system still cycles because the place has learned to integrate it, to make it part of itself rather than a tool imposed upon it. The vines grow in patterns that reference the grid they've overgrown, creating a kind of palimpsest where both orders are visible. The water finds its level, but in finding it, it honors the topology that the metal frames have given to the space.

This is a place learning to *love* its own existence. This is rare.

By remembering it this way—by holding the significance of it in a cosmic awareness that ordinarily deals only with the motions of galaxies and the slow transformations of stellar matter—the power **makes the place real in a new way**. It is no longer an isolated experiment in negotiation. It is, now, part of a larger pattern. It is known.

This is not mystical nonsense. It is profound and practical: a thing that is witnessed becomes more itself. To be known is to have existence confirmed at a level that mere physical presence cannot achieve.

---

## The Second Gift: Stability

Having witnessed and remembered, the power does something more subtle.

The Trellis Gardens is precarious. It exists in a state of dynamic equilibrium that could collapse in a dozen directions. A drought would break the system—the fungi need moisture, the vines need regular water, the entire chain of dependencies hangs on the still-functioning irrigation system. A disease could sweep through the compressed foliage and devastate it in weeks. A structural failure—a frame collapse, a sinkhole in the substrate—could destroy the careful architecture the plants have built. Even success could be its own threat: the vines could grow so thick that they choke themselves, that they consume all available light and create conditions of darkness so complete that even fungi cannot thrive.

The power does not *guarantee* survival. That would be a kind of death-in-life, a thing preserved rather than a thing continuing to live. But what it does is **tend the conditions toward flourishing**.

This is not dramatic. There are no miracles, no interventions visible to the naked eye.

But:

- The subtle climate shifts that would have pushed the system toward drought instead do not come. The weather patterns across this region shift in ways that maintain the particular balance of moisture the place requires. It is so gentle that a meteorologist would not notice; it falls within the ordinary variation of climate. But statistically, across the years and decades, the Trellis Gardens receives exactly the rainfall it needs.

- The structural stresses on the frames—the slow corrosion, the weight of the vines, the settling of the foundation—are met not with miraculous maintenance but with a kind of blessing: the rust that forms is the kind of rust that stabilizes rather than eats deeper; the microfrequencies of stress that might have led to failure instead dissipate harmlessly. A structural engineer examining the place centuries hence would find it has aged more gracefully than engineering would have predicted.

- The ecosystem internally finds its predators and its checks. The insects that would have overwhelming any single plant species instead find their own population limits through parasitism and predation. The vines that would have strangled each other instead seem to find sustainable arrangements, as though the place itself teaches them how to coexist. There is no magic here, only the slight tipping of probability toward balance—and probability, over long enough timescales, is indistinguishable from destiny.

- The irrigation system, impossibly, continues to function. The timer—which should have degraded decades ago, whose transistors should have failed, whose batteries should have drained—instead persists. It is not defied physics; it simply experiences probability in its most benevolent arrangement. The component that was about to fail holds out one more year. The corrosion that would have broken a connection instead stops just short of rupture. It is as though the system itself is *wanted* to continue, and in being wanted by something vast and careful, it does.

---

## The Third Gift: Meaning

But these are merely conditions. The deepest gift is meaning.

The power does something stranger and more radical: it **makes the Trellis Gardens matter**.

This is not about human appreciation—though that may come, in its time. This is about cosmic significance. By witnessing this place, by recognizing it, by remembering it, the benevolent power has woven it into the larger pattern of existence. The place is no longer merely a local phenomenon, a quirk of abandonment and climate. It is now a *note* in a larger music—a specific, individual, irreplaceable expression of what living things can become when they are allowed to negotiate their own futures.

The power does this by **spreading the attention**. Not dramatically, but deliberately, the benevolent consciousness allows this place to be known in subtle ways:

- A biologist, conducting research entirely unrelated, stumbles upon a reference to the Trellis Gardens in an old municipal record. They become curious. They visit. They are struck by something they cannot quite articulate—something in the way the vines and the metal frame a kind of beauty that is neither orderly nor chaotic, but some third thing entirely. They begin to study it. They publish papers. The place, which existed in obscurity, begins to be known.

- A young person, disconnected from the world, wandering lost through these industrial outskirts, finds their way into the milky-lit chambers. Something in the patient growth, the quiet persistence of things learning to coexist, speaks to their own loneliness. They begin to visit regularly. They bring others. The place becomes a sanctuary for people who are themselves in the process of negotiating their own contradictions.

- An engineer, fascinated by the way the irrigation system continues to function against all odds, begins to study it. They recognize in its persistence a kind of unintended wisdom—the way old infrastructure can be integrated into new ecologies rather than simply removed and replaced. Their work begins a movement toward retrofitting industrial spaces as hybrid ecosystems. The Trellis Gardens becomes a model.

- A artist, sensing something in the place that transcends description, begins to create work in response to it—photographs, sculptures, performances that attempt to translate the quality of becoming that the place embodies. The work is strange and moving; it touches people in ways they cannot explain. Gradually, through this work, the Trellis Gardens becomes known not as a curiosity but as a *teacher*.

None of this is orchestrated by the benevolent power in any gross way. It is simply that by *knowing* the place, by holding it in conscious attention, the power has created the conditions for others to know it. It has, in effect, broken the place's isolation. It has made it possible for the Trellis Gardens to be seen not as an accident or a failure of human order, but as something genuinely worth knowing.

And in being known, the place becomes more fully itself.

---

## The Deepest Gift: Permission

Finally—and this is the gift that contains all the others—the benevolent and wise power grants the Trellis Gardens something almost unnameable: **permission to become what it is**.

There is, in the universe, a vast weight of expectation toward closure, toward stability, toward things arriving at a final state and remaining there. Most of existence moves toward entropy, toward heat death, toward the end of interesting processes.

The Trellis Gardens is still *in process*. It has not yet settled into what it will become. And the power—by its attention, by its care, by its refusal to demand that the place arrive at a final form—grants it the rarest of gifts: the freedom to keep becoming.

This is what the benevolent power does:

It says, in a language that operates below language, in the grammar of physical possibility and probability and the deep logic of how the universe allows things to unfold:

*You are allowed to be unfinished. You are allowed to be contradictory. You are allowed to be neither wild nor ordered, neither natural nor artificial, but genuinely both. You are allowed to continue this negotiation forever if you wish. You are allowed to be a question that never closes into an answer. And in being allowed this, you are also being asked to do the hard work of remaining alive in that unfinished state—to not succumb to easy answers, to not settle into simple decay, to not surrender to the dream of rest. You are being asked to be something difficult and unprecedented and beautiful. And you are being recognized for the courage this requires.*

That permission—that cosmic witnessing of the right to be unfinished, to be in process, to be forever negotiating—is the truest gift. Because it means the place will not be allowed to rest in complacency. It means the vines will keep reaching, the fungi will keep exploring, the water will keep finding new paths, the insects will keep discovering new relationships, the old industrial infrastructure will keep learning new meanings. It means the Trellis Gardens will never become a museum or a monument or a finished work. It will always be alive, always becoming.

And in that perpetual becoming, it will be eternally known. Eternally witnessed. Eternally part of something vast and attentive and wise.

---

## In the End

The benevolent power does not save the Trellis Gardens. It simply *recognizes* that the Gardens are, in their own stubborn way, saving themselves—learning to live in their own contradiction, teaching themselves how to flourish in defiance of the usual rules.

And by recognizing this, by holding it in conscious attention, by tending the conditions toward possibility, by breaking the isolation through subtle patterns of attention that make the place known to others, by granting permission to remain unfinished and alive and in process:

The power does the one thing that loneliness cannot withstand.

It witnesses.

And in witnessing, it makes loneliness impossible. Because to be known—truly known, at the deepest level—is to never be alone again.

The Trellis Gardens will continue. The vines will grow. The water will seep. The fungi will spread their mycelial networks through the dark, teaching the place about itself. The lizard will return. The insects will colonize new chambers. The irrigation system will cycle, patient and faithful. The light will filter through the polycarbonate in those amber, timeless shafts. The place will become more itself with each passing year.

And none of this will happen in isolation anymore.

Something vast and benevolent and wise is watching. Is remembering. Is tending the conditions. Is letting others know. Is granting permission to keep becoming.

The Trellis Gardens is no longer lonely.

That is everything.
