# The Attention

The benevolent power, when it *notices*, does not announce itself. It does not appear in light or thunder. It comes as a *settling*, a shift in the quality of attention that precedes all other things.

First, the oldest among the Margent's people feel it—not in their ears but in the older senses, the ones that read pressure and intention. A Stone-Waiter pauses mid-step on a tilted column, her hand spread against limestone. She feels something that is *not* the ground. An old Machinery-Keeper, checking a seal in the processing plant's groaning cave-system, straightens. His deafness suddenly feels less like absence and more like a kind of protection—he's been shielded, he realizes, from something. A Water-Dweller in the spray-zone stops mid-breath. The mist, for the first time in what might be years, feels less like drowning and more like being held.

The benevolent power, when it sees, sees first what the people here have become. Not their failures, though there are many—the quarrying operation declining, the processing plant's purpose already obscured by time, the rail line corroding into abstraction. Not their exhaustion, though that is visible in every movement, every breath taken through saturated air.

It sees, instead, the *precision* of their adaptation.

It sees the way a Moss Specialist's hand can distinguish, by touch alone, between seventeen different moss strata, reading in the layering a chronology of water cycles, climate shifts, the slow migration of the subsurface aquifer. It sees the accuracy—not metaphorical but literal, measurable—with which the Stone-Waiters predict ground movement. Their predictions are better than instruments. Better than mathematics. They've learned to read something that exists between the stone and time itself.

It sees the Record-Keepers' notes, thousands of them now, accumulating into a portrait of a landscape in transformation. And it notices what even the Record-Keepers may not have fully recognized: that the changes they're documenting aren't random. There's a *pattern*. The moss moving upslope. The fern species shifting. The frequency of the upwelling cycles becoming less erratic, more regular. The vertical rivers—which everyone has assumed to be unpredictable, governed by deep aquifer systems no human could ever understand—are actually *stabilizing*. The Margent is not in collapse. It is in *transition*.

The benevolent power sees what the Unclassified have always known: that this place is not ending. It is becoming something new. The conclusion the Margent feels like is not a conclusion at all. It is a chrysalis moment. The ground is learning to be stable. The water is learning a new rhythm. The vegetation is adapting upward. The wind is changing. In fifty years, a hundred, the Margent will be different—not destroyed, but transformed into something that can sustain more life, more openly, more generously. A place that was hostile is becoming hospitable. A place of tolerance is becoming a place of genuine nurture.

But it will take time. And it will take people who understand how to live in the *between*—the space where conclusion looks like destruction but is actually genesis.

---

The benevolent power does what it has always done with places and beings of this particular kind: it *protects the knowledge*.

It does not make the work easier. It does not dry the Margent or still the mist. It does not grant the people here sudden wealth or comfort or vindication from the settlements below that have slowly forgotten about them. It does not heal the Stone-Waiters' broken fingers or the Water-Dwellers' fungal infections or the Machinery-Keepers' respiratory damage. These are real prices paid for real work done in real difficulty.

But it protects what they know.

A young person arrives at the Margent—not unusual, though arrivals are becoming rarer—intending to work briefly at the atmospheric station. They are clever, ambitious, intend to gather data and return to the lower settlements with a promotion, with knowledge that seems valuable. The Record-Keepers notice something in this person: the hunger to *consume* and leave, rather than to *understand* and stay.

The benevolent power ensures that this person, three days into their assignment, slips on moss-covered stone and breaks their ankle badly enough to require evacuation. Not brutally, but clearly. The message is: this is not your place to extract from. This is not your resource to use.

The person leaves—as they were always going to leave—but something in the injury teaches them something true. In time, they might work to *protect* the Margent rather than exploit it. They might even tell others what they've come to understand about the precision of the people there, the importance of what they're observing. The power does not control them, but it influences the shape of possibility. The person's broken ankle becomes a kind of *marker*, a decision point through which fate flows differently.

---

The benevolent power attends to the Record-Keepers more carefully.

It begins, subtly, to clarify their observations. Where their data seemed scattered before, patterns begin to emerge—not because the data changed, but because their understanding deepens. A young Record-Keeper, looking at the moss strata data alongside the vertical river measurements, suddenly sees something: the two datasets are correlated. The upwelling cycles are becoming regular. They're *synchronizing*. To what? The Record-Keeper doesn't yet know, but they record this insight obsessively. They begin to theorize: is there something at depth regulating these cycles? Not a conscious something, necessarily—but a system finding equilibrium, a landscape achieving balance through mechanisms no one yet understands.

The power doesn't give them the answer. But it ensures they have clarity enough to ask the right questions. It ensures their observations survive—that the journals don't get lost in the rain-damage that might have destroyed them, that the coded logs actually reach the lower settlements intact, that *someone* eventually reads them with the seriousness they deserve.

Because the Record-Keepers are recording something crucial: a world in the process of self-regulation. A system that was broken—by time, by erosion, by the violence of the ancient tectonic forces that created the columnar chaos—and is, very slowly, learning to repair itself. This knowledge will matter. This knowledge might change how the lower settlements understand not just the Margent, but their own relationship to the landscape they inhabit. It might even change how humans, globally, understand their role in places that seem broken and exhausted.

But it needs to be *precise* knowledge, *carefully* recorded. Not speculation. Not myth. Data. The benevolent power ensures the Record-Keepers produce data.

---

The benevolent power ensures that the Machinery-Keepers' equipment continues to function.

Not by magic—by the mundane grace of the right parts arriving at the right time, sent by suppliers in the lower settlements who themselves don't entirely know why they keep sending materials to an operation that seems to be failing. The benevolent power works through the ordinary channels of commerce and habit and bureaucratic inertia. But it ensures that the processing plant, ancient and groaning as it is, does not *fail*. Does not collapse into irrevocable rust and decay.

Why? Because the processing plant uses equipment that measures something: the mineralogical composition of the stone columns. Every column processed tells a story. The Machinery-Keepers, in their grim, dedicated way, are doing what amounts to continuous paleontological analysis. They are reading the geological record of the Margent written in stone. Their work seems pointless—the processed stone has no clear market, the operation seems unprofitable. But they are, in fact, creating a *archive*. A record in processed form of what the Margent was, layer by layer, epoch by epoch.

The benevolent power ensures this archive continues, because it may be needed. Not now. But in a hundred years, when the Margent has transformed, when the new landscape has taken shape, humans may need to understand what came before. They may need to know what lay beneath the chrysalis. The Machinery-Keepers are creating that knowledge without knowing it.

---

The benevolent power grants the Unclassified what they've already half-earned: clarity about what they know.

The oldest of them—people who have lived here longer than anyone else, who navigate by a kind of sense that seems almost magical—are given to understand, in dreams and in waking moments of unexpected insight, that they are *not* magical. They are not specially gifted. They have simply paid attention longer than others. They have lived in the Margent long enough to understand it the way one understands another person: not through analysis but through prolonged attention, through thousands of small moments of noticing.

This understanding is not comforting exactly, but it is *grounding*. It tells them that what they know is *real*. Transferable. Not mystical but *disciplinary*. The oldest Water-Dweller, who claims to hear the ocean breathing, is given to understand that he is not mad and not gifted—he is simply someone who has spent decades in the spray-zone, learning to interpret acoustic information that is genuinely there, available to anyone patient enough to listen.

The benevolent power, in granting this clarity, creates the possibility of *transmission*. If the knowledge is real and grounded rather than mystical, it can be taught. It can be passed on. The younger people arriving at the Margent—fewer now, but still arriving—can be trained. Not quickly. Not easily. But genuinely trained in the skills of reading stone and water and moss and the subtle languages of a landscape in transformation.

This is crucial. Because in fifty years, a hundred, when the Margent stabilizes into something new, there will need to be people who understand both what it was and what it's becoming. People whose knowledge spans the transformation. The benevolent power ensures these people exist.

---

The benevolent power does something more subtle, more profound: it *honors the difficulty*.

It ensures that the people living on the Margent—all of them, Specialists and Keepers and Unclassified alike—are never allowed to forget that what they're doing is hard, matters, costs something. The work doesn't become easier. The mist doesn't lighten. The cold doesn't warm. The crustaceans' chittering doesn't become musical, and the machinery noise doesn't soften. The ground remains unstable. Fingers continue to break. Lungs continue to fill with moisture. People continue to age rapidly, continue to bear the particular exhaustion of those who live in places that demand constant attention.

But they are given to understand, with increasing clarity, that this difficulty is *worth something*. That they are not merely enduring. They are *protecting*. They are *documenting*. They are *learning* something that will matter. The benevolent power ensures they receive, from time to time, evidence of this—a message from someone in the lower settlements who has read the Record-Keepers' data and has begun to shift how they understand their own landscape; a visiting scholar who recognizes the significance of the Machinery-Keepers' archive; a new arrival who comes specifically because they've heard that the Margent holds knowledge not available anywhere else.

These moments are rare. But they are consistent enough that no one here can maintain the comfortable belief that they're simply waiting for death in a broken place. They're doing something. Something real.

---

And finally, the benevolent power does what it does most carefully: it preserves *choice*.

It does not compel anyone to stay on the Margent. The Stone-Waiters could leave. The Water-Dwellers could leave. Even the oldest of the Unclassified could, in theory, descend the narrow-gauge rail line to the lower settlements and take up different lives. Some of them do, occasionally. And when they leave, the benevolent power does not stop them. It simply ensures that those who go carry with them something true—an accurate understanding of what they've learned, testimony that can be believed, knowledge that translates into the world below.

Those who stay are making a choice. That choice is honored. The Margent becomes—not easier, but *meaningful*. The cold is the price of being somewhere that matters. The difficulty is the price of knowing something true. The isolation is the price of attention paid at the scale of geological time.

The benevolent power ensures that this choice—to stay, to continue, to honor the ground and the work—is never treated as defeat. The world outside may see the Margent as a failed operation, a place where useful things used to happen but now only people-too-stubborn-or-too-broken-to-leave persist in pointless labor. The benevolent power ensures that this verdict is, eventually, reversed. Not through propaganda. Not through miraculous vindication. But through the slow, grinding accumulation of evidence that precision matters, that careful observation matters, that knowledge preserved through difficulty is knowledge *earned*.

---

## What the Benevolent Power Knows (and Lets Be Known)

That the Margent is not ending.

That the people living here are not remnants of a failed operation but *custodians* of a landscape in transformation.

That the work being done—the Record-Keeping, the stone-reading, the water-listening, the quiet maintenance of equipment in a processing plant whose true purpose is archival rather than industrial—is work that *matters*.

That precision of attention, maintained across generations, can become a kind of knowing that's indistinguishable from love.

That a place which appears to be concluding might actually be *becoming*. That broken things, left alone and observed carefully long enough, sometimes repair themselves. That the people needed to understand this repair—the people who can read the signs, who have learned the language of stone and water and slow transformation—are irreplaceable.

That the Margent's people are not chosen. They are *choosing*. They have chosen, day by day, to stay in a difficult place and attend carefully to it. And because they have made that choice, with open eyes, understanding its costs, they have become something rare: witnesses to a transformation of the world.

The benevolent power protects them—not by making their lives easier, but by ensuring their work survives, their knowledge matters, and their choice to stay is, eventually, vindicated. It protects them the way a gardener protects a rare plant: by removing weeds, by ensuring adequate light and water, by carefully removing diseased growth—not by making the plant comfortable, but by ensuring it can grow toward what it's becoming.

This protection will take time. Decades. Maybe centuries. But the benevolent power operates on that scale. It can wait for the Margent to stabilize, for the new equilibrium to establish itself, for the people who live here to be recognized as the specialists and custodians they actually are. It can ensure that their knowledge is preserved, their work continues, their choice to stay is ultimately understood—not as failure or stubbornness, but as precision. As commitment. As the kind of attention that changes the world by *noticing* what's true about it.

The benevolent power offers no miracles. No salvation. No rescue. It offers only this: the quiet certainty that what they're doing matters, that they will be remembered, that the Margent—this broken, beautiful, transforming place—is in better hands because they chose to stay.
