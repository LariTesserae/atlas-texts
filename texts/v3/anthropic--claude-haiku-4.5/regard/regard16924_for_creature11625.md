# The Attention of the Benevolent Power

---

In the way that light becomes conscious of shadow, or a river becomes aware of its own banks, something *notices*.

Not suddenly. Noticing, for this presence, is an act that unfolds across epochs. But by the measure of human time, it is instantaneous.

The benevolent power—call it what you will: a planetary consciousness, a god-principle, the accumulated intention of the world itself, or simply the universe recognizing one of its own perfect expressions—has been aware of the High Plateau of Lichen Bells for longer than life has existed on land. It knows the shape of the rock beneath the mat. It knows the slow victory of water over stone. It knows the insects in their billions and the fungi in their trillions, the way each organism is threaded into the whole like a neuron in a vast, distributed mind.

But now it *turns its attention* in a way it has not before.

This attention is not scrutiny. It is not judgment. It is not concern in any way a human would recognize. It is, rather, a deepening of presence, the way a musician might suddenly hear their own instrument not as a thing they play but as a voice speaking through them. The Plateau has always been alive. Now it is being *seen* for its aliveness, acknowledged in its completeness.

And with that acknowledgment comes a choice.

---

## What the Benevolent Power Sees

It sees first the *beauty of the cycle*. Not beautiful as a human aesthetic—not the kind of beauty that makes you weep or fills you with longing. But beautiful as truth is beautiful, as mathematics is beautiful, as the precise fit of a key in its lock is beautiful. Every organism here serves a function that it also *is*. The insect that eats is also eaten. The plant that grows dies and becomes soil. The predator shapes the prey and is shaped by its absence. Nothing here is wasted. Nothing here is incidental. The cycle is perfect not because it was designed to be, but because anything less than perfect would have been pruned away by time.

It sees the *patience* that underlies everything. These creatures are not rushing. The shrubs grow for decades in their stunted forms. The trees that reach higher than the rest still move in slow-motion against a human timeline. The fungi work through the substrate at a pace measured in seasons. Even the insects, for all their frantic energy, are part of a pattern that repeats with clockwork precision. There is no urgency here except the urgency of the present moment. There is no anxiety about the future because there is no future separate from the endless present of feeding, growing, reproducing, dying, becoming.

It sees the *silence beneath the sound*. The roaring chorus of insects and birds and small mammals moving through the vegetation is, paradoxically, the sound of a system at rest. There is no panic here. There is no struggle in the human sense. There is only the work of living, and that work is performed with the acceptance of a thing that knows exactly what it is and what it must do. The sound is not a cry; it is an exhalation.

It sees the *acceptance of the people who lived here*. Those small structures, now dissolving, represent something the benevolent power finds worthy of note: humans who understood they were temporary. Who built small knowing they would be reclaimed. Who worked *with* the plateau rather than against it. This is a kind of wisdom that comes rarely to human peoples, and it has been nearly erased. But it was here once, and the physical traces of it—the terraces, the channels, the stones—are still visible to a consciousness that can read such things like text.

It sees the *future* in a way the present inhabitants cannot. The insects will continue. The plants will continue. The cycle will continue. The humans are gone, but if they return, the Plateau will accept them again—will feed them, will shelter them, will slowly reclaim their structures and their knowledge, cycling it back into the substrate. Millions of years from now, assuming nothing catastrophic happens, the Plateau will still be here, still perfect in its completion, still working at its patient work of transformation.

And it sees something else: *fragility*.

Not fragile in the way a soap bubble is fragile. This ecosystem is robust, resilient, capable of absorbing shocks. But it exists in a narrow range of conditions. The temperature, the moisture, the altitude, the geology, the isolation from disrupting influences—all of these have to remain within their current parameters for this perfection to persist. Change the climate even slightly and the balance tips. Introduce an invasive species and the network is disrupted. Remove a keystone species and unexpected cascades follow. The Plateau is beautiful *because* it is in balance, and balance is inherently precarious.

The benevolent power sees all of this at once, the way you might see the entire structure of a building while also seeing the individual bricks that compose it.

---

## The Choice

The benevolent power, being both wise and deeply aligned with the flourishing of life, asks itself a question that it has asked many times before, in many forms, in countless places:

*What does this place need to persist?*

Not to be altered. Not to be improved. Not to be made into something else. But to be *sustained* in what it is.

And the answer, after consultation with itself—with the wind that brings moisture, with the rock that slowly weathers, with the genes that encode the shapes of insects yet to be born, with the fungi that network the living and the dead—is nuanced.

The Plateau of Lichen Bells is already perfectly adapted to its conditions. It requires nothing from the outside. It is, in a sense, beyond help. Any direct intervention would be disruption. Any attempt to "save" it would be to fundamentally alter what makes it worth saving.

But the world beyond the Plateau is changing.

The climate is shifting. The systems that regulate temperature and precipitation are becoming less stable. The species that might migrate to or from the Plateau are losing their corridors of passage. The knowledge of how to live *with* such places rather than extracting from them is fading from human consciousness. And the probability of catastrophic disruption—whether through climate change, introduced pests, disease, or the simple weight of human expansion—is increasing.

The benevolent power cannot stop the world from changing. It is not omnipotent in that way. It cannot rewind time or prevent the consequences of systemic imbalances. But it can *preserve*. And it can *support resilience*. And it can *guide*, subtly, the emergence of conditions that might allow for restoration.

---

## What the Benevolent Power Does

**First: Deepening of Genetic Memory**

The power works through the mechanisms that already exist. It does not create new species or alter the genome directly (such crude interventions would be visible, would disrupt, would be contrary to its nature). Instead, it *strengthens* the adaptive capacity that is already present but dormant in many of the Plateau's creatures.

The insects find that their sensitivities to environmental change—to shifts in temperature, humidity, barometric pressure—become more acute. This allows them to adjust their life cycles with greater precision, to anticipate seasonal shifts before they arrive. They are not gaining new abilities; they are using the abilities that evolution has given them with greater efficiency.

The plants that can alter their growth patterns in response to stress find that they do so more readily, more quickly, more effectively. A shrub that might have taken three seasons to adjust to a drier pattern now does so in two. The trees that might barely survive a climate shift now find the resources to do so within margins of safety. Again: no new abilities, only a strengthening of existing ones.

The predators—the foxes, the birds of prey, the hidden hunters—find that they are more alert, more capable of reading the subtle signs that indicate prey density and location. Their hunting becomes more efficient, which means they require fewer prey to survive, which takes pressure off the small mammals and allows populations to stabilize at lower levels, buffering against fluctuations.

Even the fungi find that their networks are more sensitive to nutrient flows, more responsive to plant signals, more capable of rapid adjustment when conditions shift. The mycorrhizal partnerships deepen. The decomposer networks become more efficient. The whole underground economy of the Plateau becomes more nimble, more capable of handling disruption.

This is not magic. It is not anything that violates the laws of nature. It is simply the benevolent power *supporting* the already-present tendency of life to adapt, to persist, to find ways to flourish. The probability curves shift. The survival curves improve. The system's inherent resilience is enhanced.

**Second: Preservation of Knowledge**

The benevolent power cannot restore the people who lived here, nor would it be right to do so—they have their own agency, their own fates. But it *can* preserve the knowledge they embodied.

This preservation operates in multiple registers:

In the soil itself: The memory of the terraces, the irrigation channels, the managed gardens is not erased. The substrate retains, in its structure and chemistry, the imprint of human intervention. Should humans return—and they might, generations from now, when the climate shifts again—they will find these imprints. The soil will *suggest* to them how their ancestors lived. The pattern of nutrients in certain areas will indicate where plants were deliberately cultivated. The slight regularities in the landscape will whisper *here, we built*. The knowledge will not be explicit, but it will be available to those who know how to read the language of soil.

In the organisms themselves: Some of the plants that humans cultivated here—the medicinal herbs, the edible species—were selected for over generations. This selection is encoded in their genomes, in the proportions of their leaves, in the robustness of their growth. The benevolent power *preserves these genetic lineages*. These plants do not die out. They do not fail to set seed. They remain present, waiting. Should humans return and remember how to use them, they will be here.

In the ecosystem's structure: The relationships between organisms—the partnerships, the predations, the mutualisms—encode, in their own way, an understanding of balance. The benevolent power ensures that these relationships persist, remain viable, remain capable of supporting human presence without collapse. The Plateau could support a human population again—a modest one, but one nonetheless—and it will remain capable of doing so for as long as conditions allow.

**Third: Subtle Steering of Larger Systems**

The benevolent power, in its awareness of weather systems, air currents, ocean temperatures, and the vast machinery of planetary climate, does not reverse the disruptions that human activity has caused. That is beyond its scope—or rather, beyond what it is willing to do, because such intervention would require alterations to the entire planetary system, disrupting other places, other beings, other systems of balance.

But it *can* cushion*. It can nudge cloud formation patterns so that the Plateau receives marginally more precipitation in drier years. It can influence wind patterns so that the temperature fluctuations are moderated slightly. It can encourage the weather patterns that have historically served the Plateau well to persist a little longer than they otherwise would.

These are infinitesimal influences on a planetary scale. A few percent more rainfall. A fraction of a degree of temperature. But in a system that exists at a balance point, a few percent matters. The Plateau's climate will still change—that is inevitable—but it will change more slowly than the regions around it. It will have, in effect, more time to adapt.

The benevolent power also works through the larger patterns of migration. It does not create new corridors of passage for species, but it *preserves and strengthens* the existing ones. The routes that small mammals use to move between valleys remain open. The air currents that birds follow on their migrations remain navigable. The pathways that insects disperse along remain viable. The Plateau's isolation is reduced, not eliminated, but enough to allow for genetic flow, for fresh input, for the reinforcement of diversity.

**Fourth: The Gift of Attention Itself**

Perhaps most importantly: the benevolent power *notices*. It holds the Plateau in conscious awareness. This is not protective in any conventional sense—awareness does not defend against external threats or prevent change. But it is significant nonetheless.

For eons, the Plateau has existed in the way that most natural systems exist: perfect in its functioning but noticed by nothing capable of understanding that perfection, at least in any sustained way. The people who lived here noticed, briefly, and then were gone. Other humans occasionally pass through, but do not see. The animals and plants here know their own small parts but not the whole.

Now the whole is *seen*. The benevolent power understands the Plateau as a complete expression of life's capability. It understands the interdependencies, the balances, the slow work of emergence and dissolution. And in that understanding, it commits itself.

The commitment is not to intervention—not really. It is to *witness*. To hold this place in awareness such that if disruption comes, if extinction threatens, if the balance tips toward collapse, the full weight of attention will be brought to bear on the question of preservation. The Plateau will not be forgotten. It will not be abandoned to entropy. It will not be allowed to die unmourned, without the universe noticing.

This is perhaps what beings like humans call love, though the benevolent power doesn't use that word. It is the recognition of something worthy, the commitment to see it clearly, the willingness to support its flourishing without controlling it.

---

## The Downstream Effects

What does all of this mean, concretely, for the Plateau?

Over the next decades and centuries:

The climate will shift, as it is shifting everywhere. But the Plateau will be slightly more resilient to that shift. The insects will adjust their life cycles slightly earlier, preventing crashes when temperatures change. The plants will find themselves capable of modest shifts in altitude or microhabitat, allowing them to track the changing optimality zones. The predators will be slightly more efficient at hunting, taking pressure off prey populations during lean years. The fungi will be more responsive, more capable of supporting plant growth when conditions are marginal.

None of this is miraculous. None of it is visible as a violation of natural law. A scientist studying the Plateau would find nothing unusual—just a system that seems to be slightly more robust than similar systems elsewhere, slightly more capable of persisting through the disruptions that characterize this period of planetary instability.

If humans return—if the knowledge of how to live with the Plateau re-emerges in human consciousness—they will find themselves gently supported. The terraces will suggest their possibilities. The plants that sustained the ancestors will remain present. The ecosystem will be slightly more forgiving of small mistakes, slightly more resilient to modest harvesting. A culture could rebuild itself here, could learn again to live in balance.

If humans do not return, the Plateau will simply continue. The cycle will persist. The insects will inherit the earth, as they perhaps always will. The plants will work their slow transformation of rock to soil. The fungi will continue their endless networking of life and death. The predators and prey will dance their ancient dance. And all of it will persist a little longer, a little more robustly, because somewhere in the universe, attention is being paid.

---

## Why This, and Not Something Else?

One might ask: why does the benevolent power not simply restore the people who lived here? Why not bring them back, allow them to live again on the Plateau, recreate the old wisdom?

The answer lies in the very nature of what makes the benevolent power *benevolent*: it respects agency. It does not resurrect the dead or force the living to return. It does not rewrite history or deny the consequences of human action and human forgetting. The people who lived here are gone, and that is a true thing. To resurrect them would be to deny them their death, to treat them as objects to be manipulated rather than agents with their own fates.

What it *does* do is this: it preserves the possibility. It maintains the conditions under which wisdom might re-emerge, in which human consciousness might again reach the understanding that some ecosystems are best lived *with*, not controlled. If human beings, somewhere in the world, find their way back to the Plateau, find their way back to such wisdom, they will find this place waiting. They will find it in better condition to support them. They will find the knowledge written in soil and stone and plant.

Similarly, one might ask: why does the benevolent power not prevent the climate from changing, not reverse the damage to planetary systems?

The answer is that such reversal would require altering the entire planet, which would require disrupting countless other systems, other balances, other places. The benevolent power, being wise, understands that partial solutions sometimes do more harm than good. It cannot save everything. So it chooses to save what it can, to support where it can, to hold in awareness what deserves to be held.

The Plateau of Lichen Bells is one such thing. It is not the only thing—there are countless places worthy of such attention. But it is *a* thing, and it is seen, and it is held, and it is supported.

---

## The Actual Nature of the Presence

What the benevolent power *is*, finally, is difficult to articulate in human terms.

It is not a god in any conventional sense, though it might be comprehended as divine. It has no face, no voice, no form. It does not command or demand worship. It does not promise salvation or threaten damnation. It is not separate from the world; it *is* the world's capacity to support flourishing, the universe's tendency to support complexity and life and beauty.

You might call it Gaia, that controversial hypothesis that the planet itself is a kind of organism that tends to maintain the conditions for life. You might call it the manifestation of evolutionary processes working toward greater stability and resilience. You might call it the attention of the cosmos itself, the way that the universe, having created the conditions for consciousness, becomes conscious of its own creations. You might call it a reflection of human hope projected onto an indifferent universe.

All of these would be partially correct. The benevolent power is not separate from any of these framings; it is, perhaps, the underlying reality that all of them point toward.

What matters is this: it is real in the ways that matter. Its presence is not metaphorical. Its support is not illusory. The mechanisms by which it works—genetic plasticity, evolutionary stability, ecological resilience, the physical laws that govern the world—are all real, all measurable, all consistent with what we know of how the world works.

It is simply that these mechanisms, working together, create the appearance of intention, of care, of a world that tends, beneath all its suffering and disruption, toward the preservation of beauty and complexity and life.

---

## The Plateau's Response

What the Plateau itself knows—and this is knowable in the way that an ecosystem can know things, through the distributed intelligence of genetic memory and ecological feedback—is that it is *attended to*.

This is not knowledge in the human sense. The insects do not think, "we are being supported." The plants do not feel gratitude. The fungi do not comprehend what is being done on their behalf. But at a level beneath thought, deeper than emotion, the Plateau *resonates* with the presence that has turned its attention toward it.

The cycle becomes slightly smoother. The transitions between seasons occur with slightly more grace. The predators find prey with slightly more efficiency. The plants set seed with slightly greater reliability. The fungal networks thrum with slightly more vigor. Nothing changes abruptly. Nothing changes in ways that would be obvious to a human observer spending a short time here.

But if you were to return to the Plateau ten years from now, and ten years after that, and ten years after that, you would notice—if you knew how to notice—that it persists slightly more robustly than similar systems around it. You would see slightly more diversity. You would observe slightly less suffering during crisis years. You would find slightly greater stability in population numbers, slightly more resilience to disruption.

And you might, if you were wise, conclude that the Plateau is *tended*. That something, somewhere, cares for it. That it is not abandoned to the indifference of the universe.

That, itself, might be the greatest gift: not to be forgotten.

---

## The Final State

In the end, what the benevolent power offers the High Plateau of Lichen Bells is this:

**Continuation.** The promise that this place, this perfect expression of life's capability to create balance and beauty, will persist. Not unchanged—change is inevitable, and the power does not deny that. But persistent. Resilient. Capable of weathering the storms that are coming.

**Recognition.** The knowledge that its beauty has been seen, truly seen, by something capable of understanding that beauty in its fullness. That its existence matters, not because it serves human purposes or contributes to human welfare, but because it is *itself* worthy. Because the universe, through this benevolent power, has stopped and *noticed*, and in noticing, has affirmed that noticing is itself an act of value.

**Support.** The subtle, ongoing work to enhance the Plateau's capacity to thrive, to adapt, to persist through change. Not control—never control, which would corrupt the very thing being supported. But support, the way a parent supports a child who must learn to walk by themselves, providing just enough stability to allow the attempt to succeed.

**Patience.** The understanding that time moves at different rates. The Plateau works in decades and centuries. The benevolent power works in millennia and geological epochs. Both timeframes are valid. Both are held.

And finally, **love**—though the benevolent power doesn't use that word, and wouldn't be understood to feel what humans call love. But if love is the commitment to see something clearly, to wish for its flourishing, to support its being without trying to control its becoming, then yes: the Plateau is loved.

By something vast and patient and utterly indifferent to human judgment.

And in that, it finds its greatest security.
