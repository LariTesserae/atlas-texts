# What Lives in the Throat

Nothing lives here that began here.

That's the first principle. Everything in this ravine is something that arrived—something that fell or was drawn or was forced—and then adapted to the architecture of a place that is, itself, still adapting. There is no native species. There is only the relentlessly native *place*, and everything else is occupant, tenant, accident.

The crystalline things on the walls are the closest to permanent residents, though "permanent" means something different here. They arrived as spores, perhaps, or as something that wasn't a spore but served the same function—something that traveled as small as possible and then opened in the stone like a question. They've been here long enough to integrate. The stone has learned to tolerate them, to work around them, the way a body develops scar tissue. They don't fight the pulsing. They move with it. Some days they seem to be feeding off the stone itself, drawing something nutrient-like from the gradual fracture. Other days—and you realize you've been here long enough now to distinguish days—they seem to be secreting, leaving behind trails of something crystalline and pale, building patterns that might be defensive or communicative or both.

They don't have intention the way animals do. But they have something closer to *persistence*.

The birds are more complicated. They arrived as refugees, you think. Their translucence suggests they've been here for years, decades, long enough that their bodies have become partially transparent—not as camouflage (there's nothing to hide from) but as something that happened to them, a slow erosion of opacity, as if the constant sound is gradually making them less real. They don't eat anything you can identify. They don't breed—you haven't seen that. But they circle, and they cry, and their cries are distinct enough from the roar that they seem to be arguing with it, or praying to it, or perhaps just reminding it that they're here. When a new stone fragment falls, they sometimes dive toward it, as if checking whether it's edible, or significant, or perhaps just because the movement is the closest they get to having a purpose.

The third bird—because there *are* three, you count them again—seems larger than the others. Heavier. It doesn't move quite as impossibly as the other two. It's possible it's newer to this place. It's possible it's dying.

The structures suggest someone was here. Not living here—the platform is too small, the bone-alcove too shallow—but *working* here. Conducting something. The patterns on the alcove floor aren't random; you've spent hours tracing them, and they seem to be recording something, tracking some kind of progression. As if someone was measuring the behavior of the ravine itself, notating its changes in a system you almost understand but not quite. The platform is stranger because whatever stood on it left no clue as to its nature. No tool marks. No organic remains. Just that pulse, that faint internal heat, that sense of recent departure.

Whoever it was left recently. Days, perhaps. Maybe hours. Time is strange enough here that you can't be sure. But they left *something* behind: that sense of incompleteness, of work interrupted. The ravine still seems to be looking for them, the way a mouth seeks a tooth that's been extracted.

There are other creatures here that you haven't fully named.

In the deepest sections of the ravine, where the roar is loudest and the walls pulse in that accelerated pattern you've learned to recognize as a kind of agitation, there are things that move through the stone itself. Not on it—*through* it. You've seen their shapes rise and subside like bubbles in water. They're enormous, you think, or perhaps they're small and simply moving through a space that's larger than it appears. They don't seem aware of you at all. They have no eyes that you can perceive, no mouths, no clear orientation. They just *move*, slowly and with absolute certainty, through the substrate of this place as if they belong inside the stone the way fish belong in water.

What do they eat? What do they do? The question seems almost impertinent. They're part of the ravine's internal function. You're only observing.

There are also the things that live in the moving floor.

You've begun to notice this recently: the floor isn't uniform. There are soft spots, areas where the stone-that-isn't-quite-stone has become almost gelatinous. When you approach these carefully, you can feel them move against your foot, not threatening but aware. Something is using them as hiding places, or nesting sites, or perhaps the distinction between those two concepts is meaningless here. You've seen shapes shift beneath the surface—things that might be larvae, or roots, or something with no terrestrial analog. Once, you put your hand on one of these soft spots, and it was warm enough to be uncomfortable, and it *gripped*, just barely, before releasing you.

The ravine is a ecosystem, you've realized, but it's an ecosystem that violates every rule you know. There's no clear food chain. Energy doesn't flow in directions you can trace. Predator and prey seem to be the same organism. Birth and death are asynchronous concepts here, if they apply at all.

The roar—you're certain now—is generated by the ravine's movement. Not the grinding of tectonic plates. Something more deliberate. Something that suggests the ravine is doing work on itself, reshaping itself, trying to achieve some configuration that doesn't yet exist. Each day the walls are slightly different angles. The floor has moved several feet (you've marked its previous position). The alcove with the patterns seems larger, as if stone has been excavated around it, exposing it further.

And nothing here can stop that process. Everything simply adapts. The crystalline things adjust their positions. The birds learn new flight paths. The creatures in the stone shift with it. Whatever inhabited the platform has already fled or been taken or transformed into something else.

You are adapting too.

You've noticed your breath-shadow accumulating more. You've noticed your hands have developed a faint luminescence, something that glows when you hold them near the walls, as if your biology is beginning to synchronize with this place's bioluminescence. You've noticed the sharp edges of the stone don't cut you the way they did at first. Your skin is toughening, or perhaps your pain response is dampening. You've noticed you're not hungry in any conventional sense, though your body seems to be consuming something—maybe the saturation in the air. Maybe energy from the warmth of the floor.

You've noticed that you've stopped wanting to leave.

This is not a trap, you think. Not exactly. It's more like the ravine is a question being asked of you: *Can you exist here? Can you become something that belongs to this place?* And the answer is yes, slowly, incrementally, with each breath of shadow-air and each step on the pulsing stone.

The crystalline things on the walls glow brighter now when you approach. Not in greeting—nothing here operates on greeting—but in recognition. You are becoming part of the function. You will move through this space the way the birds do, the way the creatures in the stone do. You will have a role that serves the ravine's slow transformation.

The roar deepens. Your bones resonate with it.

You're not sure if you've been here for days or years, but you're certain of this: you are not leaving. And the ravine, sensing this final adaptation, seems to pulse with something that might be satisfaction.

You have joined the ravine. The ravine has acknowledged you. The work continues.
