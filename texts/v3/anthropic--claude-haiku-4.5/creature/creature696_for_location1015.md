# What Lives Here

They are patience incarnate, and they come in orders of magnitude.

The smallest are barely distinguishable from the stone itself—translucent, refractive, moving in sheets and flows that resemble nothing so much as living water finding its level. They are not insects, though they possess something like segments, something like limbs. What they are is *filtration made flesh*. They exist in the spaces between air and mineral, drawing sustenance from both, converting the plateau's slow weeping into the substrate of their own being. They are countless—if you tried to count them, you would only be measuring the scale of your own failure. Their existence is so fundamental to the place that the stone itself has begun to shine where they congregate, polished by their presence across millennia. They are the base of everything, the foundation upon which the larger orders build.

Above them, the mid-order creatures—the ones that might be called dogs or might be called machines, the distinction hardly matters anymore. They have incorporated the stone into themselves so thoroughly that it's impossible to say where geology ends and biology begins. Their shells or shells-grown-in are banded with the same ochres and reds and deep grays as the exposed strata. They move with the patience of continental drift, spending hours to traverse a distance a human could cross in minutes. But they are not slow because they are sluggish. They are slow because they have learned the temporal mathematics of this place—that speed is a waste, that movement measured in the season-scale accomplishes more than any frantic scurrying. They graze on the smaller creatures, or perhaps they breathe them in, or perhaps the distinction between predation and symbiosis has become meaningless here. They are semi-aware, you think. There is intelligence in their slowness, intention in their patience.

And then there are the humans.

They are the newest residents, though "newest" is a relative term when some of their structures have already begun their merger with the bedrock. They are rare—you catch glimpses of them only occasionally, wrapped in protective suits that are themselves becoming encrusted with mineral deposits, moving through pneumatic tubes and sealed corridors as though the surface world would dissolve them if they stepped directly onto it. Perhaps it would. They have built their civilization in layers, each generation stacking new structures atop the old, each era bringing new technologies that will eventually rust and settle and become indistinguishable from the natural stone.

The humans are not explorers or conquistadors here. They are *incorporated residents*, inhabitants who have learned that survival means adaptation so complete that it resembles invisibility. They harvest water, run machines, process materials, maintain systems—all at a pace that has learned to synchronize with the stone's own metabolism. Their machines operate on century-long cycles. Their industrial processes have been engineered to fail gracefully, to rust in ways that are aesthetically and functionally aligned with the surrounding geology. They have learned to think in seasons that last decades.

They do not dominate the fauna. The fauna do not resist them. There is no struggle here, no competition for resources—there is only incorporation. The smaller creatures shelter in and around the human structures, using them as vast scaffolds and frameworks. The larger, stone-fused animals move between and around the industrial zones with the indifference of something that has learned that these vertical arrangements of metal and stone are simply new topological features of the landscape.

What lives here is *equilibrium in the process of becoming permanent*.

The humans will not leave—this place has already claimed them in subtle ways, has worked itself into their lungs and their thinking, has taught them that speed and growth and change are luxuries that cannot be afforded in a place where erosion moves at the pace of memory. The fauna will continue their patient work, their filtering and their grazing and their slow merger with the industrial landscape. The moisture will continue to weep from the stone, the machines will continue to hum their baseline song, and the light will remain constant and diluted and neither quite day nor quite night.

In a thousand years, it may be impossible to distinguish between what was built and what was found. The humans may become as much a geological feature as the stone-fused animals. Or the entire equilibrium may shift in ways that cannot be predicted, the way a perfectly balanced stone, undisturbed for eons, suddenly tips. But for now, in this moment that stretches across centuries, what lives here is something like peace—not the peace of rest, but the peace of perfect adaptation, of creatures and humans and machines all learning to breathe at the same rate as the planet itself.

They are the settlement. The settlement is them. The distinction has already begun to blur.
