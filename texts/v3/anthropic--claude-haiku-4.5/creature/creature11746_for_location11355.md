# The Inhabitants of the Calcrete Quarters

## The Builders (or What Remains of Them)

They are gone, or they have transformed into something you would not recognize as "they" anymore.

What you find instead are traces—not footprints, which would have been erased by wind, but *intentions* calcified into architecture. The structures themselves are the only testimony to their existence, and they speak a language of constraint and accommodation. Whoever built these walls did so with an understanding that here, stone and mineral were more reliable than any organic material. They chose to work *with* the calcrete rather than against it, discovering that compressed mineral dust, when mixed with something—perhaps an organic binder, now long evaporated—could be shaped and set. The structures grew from the ground because the ground itself had already been prepared by epochs of evaporation. They simply guided what was already happening.

The builders knew, without question, that this was a place where growth was impossible, where accumulation was impossible, where nothing could be added indefinitely. So they built structures that asked nothing of their environment except to *be allowed to exist within it*. Single chambers. No storage, or minimal storage. No hierarchy of rooms. No decoration. No surplus.

If they are still here, they are small. Smaller than you might expect a human to be. Or they are no longer human in any sense that would be meaningful to you.

## The Lichen-Folk

This is what the birds are watching for.

In the depressions where dust gathers, where the spore-bearing plants cluster in their inward-curling postures, there is a slow accretion happening. What appears to be mere lichen—colorless, almost invisible—is actually a collaborative ecosystem so slow-moving, so patient, that it operates on a geological timescale. The lichen itself is not single; it is a parliament of algae and fungus and perhaps other things, each attending to its small portion of the work. They chemically weather the calcrete, break it down by infinitesimal amounts, creating soil where none existed before. This soil is their own creation and their own food.

And within these lichen colonies, there are smaller things. Mites. Nematodes. Perhaps creatures with no name in any language you speak—organisms that exist at the boundary between plant and animal, that photosynthesize partially, that feed on minerals partially, that reproduce through spores and through budding, that cannot be neatly categorized because the categories themselves are human inventions. They move through the lichen the way whales move through the ocean—vast, slow, patient, consuming and creating in the same motion.

These are what the birds hunt. Not the visible lichen. The invertebrate communities that dwell within it. A single bird might spend an entire day gleaning enough calories from these organisms to survive until the next feeding session, perhaps days hence.

The lichen-folk are not conscious in any way that would be recognizable to you. But they are aware—of moisture, of temperature, of light gradients. They respond. They endure. They are perhaps the true inhabitants of this place, not the settlement at all, but the vast microbial cities that exist in the spaces between grains of mineral.

## The Scorpion-Things

They have been here longer than the structures, or at least they persist after the structures' purposes have been forgotten.

These are not scorpions as you know them. Evolution has stripped them of ornamentation. No bright coloration for warning or attraction—just a flat, dull tan that matches the calcrete perfectly. Their carapaces are broader than any Earth scorpion's, designed to distribute their minimal weight across the sharp stone. They move on eight jointed legs that have lost most of their articulation; they are nearly stilt-like, elevating the creature's body as far as possible above the cutting edges of the fractured mineral.

Their tails—if the curved, stinging appendages can still be called that—are vestigial. Not useless, but rarely deployed. Most of their hunting involves waiting. A creature that has evolved to survive here has learned that expenditure of energy is a debt you may never repay. The scorpion-things spend hours, or perhaps days, motionless in the shadow of a wall, their compound eyes tracking the infinitesimal movements of the lichen-folk's smallest inhabitants. They consume perhaps a few milligrams of tissue per week. They seem ancient, though individually they may be relatively young.

What is remarkable about them is their patience, which borders on the metaphysical. They are not frustrated by stillness. They do not experience waiting as a negative state. They have evolved past the concept of boredom. Time, for them, is not a resource to be spent but a medium through which they exist, and it is as abundant here as stone.

They are solitary. You will never see two of them together, though you might see their tracks crossing—parallel lines in the dust where they have walked from one shadow to another. Whether this is territorial behavior or mere coincidence, whether they reproduce sexually or asexually or not at all in any human sense, remains unclear.

## The Birds of Witness

There are perhaps thirty of them in the entire settlement, though you see only a handful at any given time.

They are small—smaller than pigeons, larger than sparrows—and their plumage is a precise match for the calcrete itself: pale gray with white undertones, as if they were carved from the mineral landscape rather than born into it. Their eyes are disproportionately large, suggesting a biology organized almost entirely around visual perception. Their beaks are thin, pointed, designed for extracting small creatures from crevices.

They are not aggressive. They do not sing. They do not establish territories in the way birds typically do. Instead, they seem to operate as a loose collective, sharing information through subtle postures and head movements. When one bird finds a productive feeding area—a zone where the lichen colonies are particularly dense with invertebrate life—it does not defend the location but rather seems to signal its presence to the others, though the mechanism by which it does this is not obvious to human observation.

They roost on the walls at what you eventually realize is a consistent orientation: always on the eastern faces of the structures, as if maintaining a fixed relationship to some external force or cycle. During the dim daylight hours, they feed intermittently, moving across the calcrete with a precise, economical gait. During the even dimmer evening, they settle into what might be sleep, though their eyes remain partially open, maintaining that constant vigilance.

What is unsettling about these birds is their regard. They watch you. Not with fear or curiosity, exactly, but with a kind of recognition—as if you are another creature passing through their world, neither threat nor resource, simply a fact of the landscape that they are cataloging, accounting for. If you remain still long enough, they may approach you to within a few meters, studying you with that enormous eye before moving away to resume their foraging.

They are the only creatures here that seem to acknowledge your presence, even if that acknowledgment is completely indifferent. They are the only inhabitants that might be said to understand that the settlement is not the entire world, that other places and other kinds of existence are possible beyond this thin, mineral-bound realm.

## The Presence Below

You cannot see it. You may not even be able to prove it exists. But the longer you remain in the Calcrete Quarters, the more certain you become that there is something beneath the mineral crust.

Not water, necessarily. Or perhaps water, but not water in any form you could drink or swim in. Water bound to minerals, water as crystalline structure, water integrated into the very geometry of the calcrete itself. But beyond that—movement. Circulation. A slow pulse.

The structures themselves may be expressions of this. The openings that face away from the wind, that seem to serve no obvious purpose—perhaps they are breathing channels. Perhaps the settlements are not buildings at all but a kind of interface, a place where the slow processes of the deep mineral layers communicate with the thin air above. Perhaps the builders understood something about resonance, about how certain structures can facilitate the movement of energy or matter through a landscape that appears static.

Or perhaps there are creatures down there. Things that move through the calcrete the way fish move through water, navigating the interstitial spaces between mineral grains, following chemical gradients you cannot detect. Perhaps they emerge occasionally, through the gaps in the walls, moving across the surface only when the conditions are exactly right, drawn by some cycle so extended that you could spend a lifetime here and never see it complete.

The plants seem to know about it. The way they orient their fronds—always in certain directions, always reacting to shifts in temperature or pressure that come from below—suggests that they are in conversation with something deeper. Perhaps they are in communication with whatever lives in the mineral substrate, trading nutrients or chemical signals through root systems so fine that they might as well be invisible.

## The Collective

Here is another possibility: that the inhabitants of the Calcrete Quarters are not separate beings at all, but aspects of a single organism so distributed, so patient, that individual identity is meaningless.

The lichen-folk and the plants are connected through the mineral substrate. The scorpion-things feed on the lichen, their waste fertilizing the substrate further. The birds feed on the scorpion-things and the invertebrate communities, their droppings—minimal though they are—adding to the cycles. The structures themselves may be part of this organism, constructed and maintained not by "builders" but by the collective will of the entire system, expressed through whatever agents were available at the time of their construction.

From this perspective, the settlement is not a village at all but a organ. The structures are not buildings but cells. The circulation of matter and energy through this landscape follows pathways that are not random but deeply intentional—intentional in the way that your circulatory system is intentional, directed by processes so fundamental that they do not require conscious thought.

If this is true, then the entire Calcrete Quarters is alive, in a sense so comprehensive that the word "alive" no longer contains enough meaning. The place itself is the inhabitant. You are moving through the body of something, and it is aware of you in the same way that you are aware of the bacteria in your gut—as a neutral presence, neither harmful nor helpful, simply one more component in a vast and incomprehensibly slow existence.

## The Unnamed

There is one more category of inhabitant, one that does not fit easily into the others.

You may see it once, if you are lucky or unlucky—a figure moving between the structures in the dim light, moving with a gait that is almost human but slightly wrong, proportions slightly off. It will be the size of a small child or a very tall heron, and its coloring will match the calcrete so perfectly that you will not be entirely certain you saw it at all. It may carry something—a bundle, a tool, something too distant or obscured to identify—or it may be moving with no apparent purpose at all.

When you look directly at it, it will not meet your gaze. It will behave as if you are not there, as if your observation poses no obstacle to its movement. If you try to approach it, it will veer away, not out of fear but out of an absolute indifference so complete that it seems almost like hostility. It will move into a structure and you will lose sight of it, and you will not see it again, though you may see signs of its presence—a fresh disturbance in the dust, a structure you do not remember being there before.

Whether this is a remnant of the original builders, a creature evolved in this place, a vision brought on by the mineral-laden air and the eternal dimness, or something else entirely, you will never know. It is the only inhabitant of the Calcrete Quarters that maintains absolute mystery. It is also, perhaps, the most honest expression of what it means to live here: to move through a world of mineral and wind and patience, asking no questions, seeking no understanding, simply existing in the narrow space between the nothing above and the slow something below.
