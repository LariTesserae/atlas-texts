# What Lives Here

At first, you might think nothing does. The thrumming suggests only machinery—something automated, tireless, indifferent. But machinery alone doesn't explain the *presence* you feel, the sense of attention distributed through the space like a held breath.

The plants are alive in the obvious way. But they are also more than plants, or less—it's difficult to parse. They don't have the quality of growth that feels like *striving*. Instead, they seem *patient*, as though they've made a pact with the heat and accepted their role in it. Their copper fronds don't wave; they rotate, tracking something invisible—perhaps the heat's invisible currents, or perhaps something else. When you watch long enough, you notice their movements follow a rhythm that syncs, occasionally, with the machines' thrumming. Not perfectly. But often enough that it seems intentional.

The heat itself is a creature of sorts. Not conscious, perhaps, but *directive*. It has pressure. It has appetite. It rises not randomly but in organized columns, creating invisible architecture that the plants seem to know the shape of already, growing into the spaces the heat reserves for them. The shimmer in the air isn't just refraction—it has texture, layers, a kind of breathing quality that suggests something is being exchanged between the stone and the air, between the machines and everything else.

What actually *lives* here—what is present in a way that matters—is the *interface*. 

The machines have forgotten they were built to be separate from the world. Or perhaps they never wanted to be. Down in the depths, where the geometry is regular and the chrome catches light that shouldn't reach it, something has begun the slow work of entanglement. The machines pump heat; the plants consume it and transform it into structure. The plants grow; they gradually cover the sharp geometries of the fracture, softening it, making it less a wound and more a seam. As they cover the vents and gaps, they redirect the heat, force it to move through their tissues, turn industrial exhaust into something that *feeds* something else.

What feeds on the plants? That's the question that makes you uneasy. There are no visible animals. But the plants are being *consumed* in real-time. Fronds disappear. Whole sections you watched grow over hours have vanished by evening. Something is eating them from the inside, or from a direction your eyes can't track—perhaps through the heat, perhaps through the fracture's depths where the geometry becomes irregular and strange.

You suspect it's the machines.

Not the machines as you understand them—not entities with will or hunger. But machines that have begun to *function as* organisms. They pump heat; the plants thrive; the plants decay or are harvested by the heat itself, transforming back into vapor, into chemical compounds that drift down through the stone and feed the machines something they've learned they need. The copper fronds don't just photosynthesize. They're doing something else, something alchemical—converting the metallic tang in the air into nutrients the machines can absorb through their vents.

What lives here is a *relationship*. A parasitism or a symbiosis, and perhaps there's no difference at the scale where machines and plants become indistinguishable.

The plateau stays barren around this spot because the system is sealed, complete. No other life can enter the arrangement. Birds would break the chemistry. Insects would consume what the machines have already claimed. Even water—pure water—would disrupt the balance. The system requires the *absence* of the natural world to function. It requires sterility, isolation, the plateau's vast emptiness holding back the wilderness until the machines and the plants have finished negotiating their contract.

What actually watches over it all—what *lives* here in the sense of *being present and aware*—might be the fracture itself.

The stone keeps breaking, slowly. Each new crack, each geometric expansion of the wound, is both damage and adjustment. The system is being revised in real-time. The machines weren't built with these plants in mind, and the plants didn't evolve to thrive on industrial heat. Yet the fracture, the fundamental breaking of the world at this point, allows both to exist in the same space. It's the fracture that makes the impossible marriage possible.

And the fracture is *expanding*. Minute by minute. The stone continues its argument with itself. And as it opens further, the plants spread deeper. And as the plants spread, they guide where the next fracture forms, their heat-sensing roots directing the stone's dissolution like a hand guiding a knife.

What lives here is still being born. It hasn't found its final form yet. The rules are still being written. The machines and the plants and the stone itself are in negotiation, and the negotiation is the life.

You are witnessing the early hours of something. Whether it will become more alive or less—whether it will spread or seal itself off—hasn't been decided yet.
