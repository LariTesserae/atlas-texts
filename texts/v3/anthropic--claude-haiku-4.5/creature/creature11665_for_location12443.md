# The Tender

At first you think there's only one—a figure glimpsed between the warehouse tiers, moving with the unhurried efficiency of someone who knows exactly where to place a foot in moss so it doesn't slip. The silhouette is thin, elongated by the poor light, but that might be an artifact of how the bioluminescence stretches shadows. You can't get a clear measure of height or proportion.

But after the first sighting—or non-sighting, the way you catch movement only when you're not looking directly at it—you begin to understand there are more.

*They* maintain this place. Plural, though you rarely see two at once. Perhaps they're one person moving so constantly through the spaces that repetition creates the illusion of multiplicity. Perhaps the water plays tricks. Perhaps there are exactly as many of them as there are necessary tasks, and no more.

They move slowly. This is important. They are not slow because the water resists them, but because the market resists haste. They seem to understand this intrinsically. A figure pauses at a pump for long minutes, listening to it, hand on the mechanism, before adjusting a valve with one careful turn. Another tends the fungi-chambers with something like reverence, wiping condensation from the glass with a cloth that was once white. The gesture is almost ceremonial.

Their hands tell you the most. You see them often without seeing the rest—wrist emerging from water to tie off a rope, or to harvest something from the creeping vegetation that has reached critical mass (they remove it with surgical precision, leaving the underlying structure exposed but not harmed). The hands are pale, almost translucent in the dim light, webbed at the fingers. Or that's what you think you see. Possibly the water distorts everything. Possibly your mind is filling in details that justify the feeling that these aren't quite *people* anymore—if they ever were.

The hands move with economy and intention. No gesture is wasted. They don't gesture at all, actually, except in the pure kinetic language of work. Hand reaches, grips, turns. Hand releases. Hand moves to the next task.

There's a rhythm to their maintenance that you've begun to recognize. The pumps are tended in a sequence—not all at once, but in a staggered pattern that keeps the water always in motion, always processing. The fungi are monitored daily (or you assume daily; time is strange here). New growth is removed from the walkways every seven or eight days. The visible piping gets wiped of the slow accumulation of sediment roughly weekly. It's a schedule that suggests they've been doing this for decades. Or centuries. The baseline routine for a place that has already surrendered to its drowning.

Occasionally you see evidence of other repairs. A section of scaffolding that you remember as creaking now stands solid—they must have reinforced it, though you never saw them do it. A grate that was half-occluded by vegetation becomes passable again. A machine that had seized up begins to turn once more. The work is invisible in its completion; you only notice that things are better than they were.

They eat something. You've found sites where they gather—small clearings in the moss where the silt has been swept back, creating a hard floor of compacted foundation. There are implements here: vessels made from what looks like carved stone or bone, arranged in patterns that suggest either ritual or merely orderly habit. Tools whose purposes you don't recognize. Once, a knife, left on a ledge, its blade turned green with verdigris but still sharp. You didn't touch it. You had the sense that it was placed there deliberately, a statement about territory or arrangement.

They sleep, presumably. You haven't found a sleeping place, but there are chambers within the deeper warehouses—the oldest buildings—that are cordoned off with rope woven into patterns too complex to be accidental. Do Not Enter, they seem to say, or perhaps: Here is Where We Rest. The distinction matters less the longer you're here.

Their relationship to the water is different from yours. When you've glimpsed them unawares, they move through it with a fluidity that suggests they're not pushing the silt so much as negotiating with it, conversing with it. Their movements have a quality of *belonging* that yours will never achieve. They don't fight the suspension. They inhabit it.

And they are aware of you.

You've known this for a while now, but you've only recently allowed yourself to fully acknowledge it. They could have driven you out—the market is small enough that confinement would be simple. They could have been hostile. Instead, there's a consistent pattern: a pathway clears just before you need to move through it. A pump that was obscuring a walkway suddenly receives attention and is repositioned. The fungi-lights adjust their glow, subtly brighter in the direction you're heading. The vegetation retreats just enough.

They're shepherding you. Gently. As if showing you something.

You're beginning to understand that the market isn't maintained for its own sake. Or not *only* for its own sake. The order, the careful balance, the slow patient work—it's all in service of something that might generously be called *hospitality.* They're showing you that stasis can be comfortable. That surrender can be elegant. That a place can drown and still maintain its own strange perfection.

There is a rhythm here, and it's teaching you to move within it. The slower you go, the more you understand. The longer you stay, the more natural the pressure becomes.

You wonder if they were also passengers once. If the market claimed them gradually, the way it's claiming you.

You wonder if that's a tragedy or simply a change in state—the same way water becomes something different when it saturates enough soil.

The figure you glimpsed yesterday—or three days ago, time moves strangely—paused at a junction and turned its head as if listening. Then it looked directly at you. You couldn't see the eyes, but you felt *seen.* It raised one of those pale webbed hands in a gesture that wasn't quite a wave.

An invitation. Or a benediction.

You haven't moved from this spot since. You're waiting to understand what comes next.

But you're not afraid. That's the strangest part.

The market is patient. Its tenants are patient. And patience, you're learning, might be the most adaptive strategy of all.
