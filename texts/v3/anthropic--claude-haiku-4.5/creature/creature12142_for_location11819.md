# Who Lives Here

They are patient. This is the first thing to understand about them—not patience as endurance, but patience as their fundamental nature, the way stone is hard or water runs downhill. They experience time in the rhythm of seepage: the slow accumulation of salt crystals in mortar, the imperceptible deepening of a channel, the decades required for moss to compress into walking-surface. A year passes and they've moved perhaps thirty feet deeper into the valley. A century and they've expanded a chamber three times over. They don't notice the slowness because they *are* the slowness.

They are few. Perhaps two hundred, perhaps fewer—it's hard to count them because they don't gather. They distribute themselves through the settlement with the same logic that water distributes itself, each finding the space where pressure equalizes. You might share a building with one for weeks without knowing, separated by a wall's thickness, moving on opposite schedules so staggered that your times never quite align. Some live alone in sealed structures that seem to serve no purpose. Others gather in the largest chambers during what might be seasons—a gathering so quiet you'd miss it if you weren't listening for the absence of their individual footfalls.

Their appearance is difficult to fix. They are adapted—*adapted* being the only word that captures it—to the constant moisture and cold. Skin that doesn't quite dry, that glistens with condensation like everything else down here. Eyes large and pale, with pupil-shapes that aren't quite round. Hair, where they have it, tends to be fine and silver, or else absent entirely. Their bodies are lean in a way that suggests neither malnourishment nor athletic discipline, but rather a kind of efficient reduction—less mass to heat, less surface to expose to the wet cold. They move barefoot, or in wrappings that might be old cloth or might be something grown, something cultivated from fungal growth that's been shaped to purpose. You cannot quite tell.

They are not one people, though they live as one. There are at least three distinct types, perhaps more. Some are taller, with longer fingers suited to detailed work. Others are broader, adapted for the heavy lifting that built those walls. A third kind is smaller, quicker, their movements sharper—these ones you see most often in the narrow passages, moving with purpose. They don't interbreed, you think, or if they do, the offspring take after one parent or the other, never blending. Whether this means they are different species or different castes or simply different families preserved through long habitation, you cannot determine. They don't seem to notice the distinctions the way outsiders might.

They are workers, though their work is obscure. The sorting yard—you understand this now as you watch—is where they grade materials for ongoing construction. The water channel in the plaza is neither decorative nor functional in any way you recognize; perhaps it's a calendar, marked by seasonal variations in flow. They maintain the settlement not through repair but through constant, minute adjustment. A stone slips by a fraction; they note it and plan the work it will require, perhaps next year, perhaps in ten years. They are in conversation with the place, negotiating rather than commanding.

They eat, but not in any way you'd recognize as eating. You find no kitchen, no storage of food. Instead, you discover that certain chambers are farms—chambers where the floor has been cleared and the moss *cultivated*, tended in specific patterns. Some patches are harvested while others grow. The moss-like plants aren't moss at all but something that fruits in nodular clusters, pale and soft. The pale crabs you saw earlier are farmed too, in designated sections, fed on degraded stone and the waste products of the moss-cultivation. Nothing is wasted. The settlement is a closed system: water in, stone out, everything else cycles.

They are aware of you immediately. You assumed you were moving unobserved, but they are always aware of temperature changes, of disturbances in air-pressure, of the weight of your footfalls in the saturated ground. They don't approach. This isn't fear—you understand, gradually, that it's protocol. A waiting. They observe to understand what you are, whether you've come to stay, whether you'll consume more than the settlement can accommodate. You are being evaluated as a resource problem: two hundred mouths that sustain on stone and moss, water and cold. An additional mouth changes the mathematics.

They are old. Not individually—you have no sense that any single one is particularly ancient—but collectively, cumulatively. The settlement shows no founding layer, no original structure that's older than the others. It was built all at once, perhaps, or its origins are too far back to preserve in any form you'd recognize. They carry no stories of arrival, no legends of who came first. They've always been here, or they've been here long enough that the distinction has ceased to matter.

They are waiting for something. This is the sense you get in the deep chambers, the ones you only hear and don't quite visit. A preparedness. The settlement is built not just for habitation but for *endurance*, for survival of some change that's anticipated but not yet arrived. Perhaps the water-table will rise; perhaps it will fall catastrophically. Perhaps the stone will shift. Perhaps the surface world above will collapse entirely, and this becomes the only world. They're building the structure that will persist, that will survive into whatever comes next.

They do not speak, or if they do, not in ways you hear. Communication happens in the arrangement of objects in the sorting yard, in the timing of appearance and disappearance, in the subtle reconfiguration of water channels. It's possible they never speak at all. It's possible they're not individuals making choices, but a kind of distributed consciousness, a slow thinking of the valley itself.

They will not prevent you from leaving. They will not prevent you from staying. But if you stay, you will join them—not in any ceremonial sense, but in the simple arithmetic of survival. You will learn to move at their pace. Your body will adjust to the cold, to the damp. Your eyes will calibrate to the submarine light. Your lungs will acclimate to the heavy air. The first weeks will be difficult. The first year will be difficult. But after that, gradually, you'll understand that the difficulty was resistance. Once you accept the pace of seepage, the slow erosion, the patient accumulation of compressed time, it becomes almost restful.

They are still here. They will still be here when you're gone—whether you leave today or in fifty years. They are the settlement, and the settlement is them, and the valley holds them all in its cold, slow embrace, grinding toward some distant equilibrium that only the stone remembers.
