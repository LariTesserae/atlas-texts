# What Lives Here

The plateau's primary inhabitant is **absence itself**—not empty space, but a tangible presence of things withheld. It arrives with the wind, becomes heavier in the haze that dissolves distance, settles into the grooves like sediment. Absence has weight here. It presses.

But within that absence, things persist.

---

There are **the engines' children**—creatures that have learned to live in parallel with the mechanical hum, that have incorporated it into their sense-making. The insects don't startle at the sound anymore; it's part of the environmental baseline, like the daily thermal shift. The birds have learned that the engines rise and fall on predictable cycles, and they time their calls to the gaps between the hums, as if the machinery has become part of the orchestra. Some of the smaller creatures—the beetles particularly—seem drawn to the sound, moving in response to its frequency. They may be navigating by it, treating it as a kind of inverted landmark. The larger animals, the goats and the feline thing, avoid the direction from which the sound is strongest, creating an invisible exclusion zone that rotates with the wind.

There is something almost **colonial** about the life here—organisms existing in loose confederation rather than firm community. The plants don't compete for space; they've simply agreed to exist at distances that prevent conflict. The predators and prey maintain a détente based on availability and invisibility. Nothing here fights for dominance. Everything is too sparse, too scattered, too oriented toward survival rather than expansion.

**The frog** in the wetland depressions is crucial to understanding this place. It's a keystone species that almost no one ever sees. It emerges only when water is present, calls only at night, and its population exists in a state of perpetual near-extinction and recovery. Its call—that rusted-hinge sound—is the plateau's only voice that isn't wind or engine or the click of insect carapace. When the frogs call, which is perhaps thirty nights a year, the plateau briefly acknowledges that life here is not accidental. The frog is a specialist in ephemeral spaces, and the entire ecosystem is built around its existence, though most of the organisms here have never encountered it.

There is a **consciousness to the stone itself**, though not in any mystical sense. The corrugations are records—not of ancient wind alone, but of the stone's own response to forces. The ferrous seams oxidize because they're vulnerable, because water and air and time have identified them as sites of transformation. The stone is doing something to itself, slowly, across millennia. The fissures deepen imperceptibly. The color shifts grain by grain. The plateau is not inert; it's engaged in the slowest possible dialogue with the forces that shape it.

The **sparse vegetation** represents a form of life that has chosen invisibility as strategy. The twisted trees don't grow tall because tallness attracts wind and drying. The silvery leaves reflect light to minimize photosynthetic burden in the harsh sun. The bunch grasses cluster in grooves where they can shelter and access moisture. These plants are not struggling; they've optimized themselves into minimalism. They've achieved a kind of philosophical acceptance that their job is to persist, not to thrive or spread.

There are **things that live in the corrugations themselves**—not just the spiders and their webs, but creatures that have carved out existence in the geological structure. Lizards that emerge at specific times, their scales patterned to match the oxidized stone. Small mammals that tunnel along the groove bottoms, following the capillary water that rises before dawn. Colonies of insects that nest in the deeper fissures where temperature fluctuation is dampened. The corrugations are not empty channels; they're a housing system, a three-dimensional labyrinth that contains multitudes.

The **moisture cycle** produces a population of organisms that exist in a state of suspended animation most of the time. Seeds that wait years for sufficient water. Spores. Dormant eggs. The occasional midge or mosquito that emerges briefly after rain, reproduces frantically, and then returns to its water-based larval state. Life here doesn't flow continuously; it pulses. Long stretches of stasis punctuated by brief, intense periods of activity.

There is a **predator at the apex**—the four-legged thing you saw at dusk, or something like it. It may be feline, or something that merely evolved to be feline-like in response to similar environmental pressures. It hunts the hares, the goats, possibly the larger insects. It lives more widely across the plateau than you could walk in a month. It has learned to navigate by sound as much as sight, using the engines' hum as a territorial marker, perhaps, or as a guide to prey movement (everything on the plateau responds to the engines somehow). It leaves no trails. It is the only creature on the plateau that moves with actual purpose across the landscape, and that purpose is appetite and territory—the oldest possible drives. It is probably nocturnal, which means you've caught only the briefest glimpse of what lives here.

The **human sound—those engines**—is perhaps the most peculiar inhabitant. It's not made by humans on the plateau, but it represents human presence nonetheless, a ghost of industry that operates at the threshold of perception. Whatever machinery produced that sound was designed by humans, is maintained by humans elsewhere, and exists in some relationship with the plateau that the plateau itself may not acknowledge. The engines are almost parasitic—drawing something from the plateau or from beneath it, or perhaps using the plateau as a surface above which to operate. The living things here have adapted to its presence because adaptation is what life does. The engines are now part of the ecology, whether intentionally or not.

And finally, there is **you**—a brief, temporary inhabitant, your presence noted and ignored by everything that has invested in being here. The plateau will remember you only if you change it, and you won't. You will walk here, observe, listen to the engines and the frogs, watch the light move across the oxidized stone, and then leave. The plateau will continue its slow processes without you. Your consciousness of it is unilateral. It is perfectly indifferent to being understood.
