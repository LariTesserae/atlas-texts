# The Inhabitants of the Undergarden

They are not what you expect when you first see them.

The people emerge gradually from the structures—first as silhouettes behind the yellowed window-panes, then as actual figures moving through the corridors between warehouses. They are thin. Not malnourished, but spare, as though their bodies have been edited down to essentials. Their skin has a peculiar pallor that comes not from disease but from generations of absence from sunlight. It is a cleanness to the skin, almost translucent in places, particularly at the wrists and temples where the blood moves close to the surface. Their eyes are large—adaptation to the low, constant light—and they move with an economy of gesture that suggests both comfort and caution in this space. They know where they are. They know how to inhabit it.

Their clothing is practical and strange: tunics and trousers woven from the plant fibers that line the undergarden itself. The fabric has a texture between linen and something chitinous, and it moves with an odd stiffness, as though the material retains some memory of its growth-form. The dyes are muted—slate greens, pale browns, the occasional deep indigo that suggests something fungal, possibly bioluminescent fungi processed into pigment. There are no bright colors. There is no need for them. Brightness is unnecessary in a world where everything is twilight.

They work with purpose but without haste.

A group near one of the warehouse entrances tends to a section of the mycelium floor. They have tools—not metal, mostly, but implements of hardened plant matter and bone, shaped with precision. One woman is using a serrated implement to gently scrape away a layer of fungal growth that has begun to encroach beyond its designated boundary. Her movements are practiced, almost meditative. She knows the depth to which she can scrape without damaging the structural integrity beneath. Another worker is applying something from a small ceramic vessel—a treatment, a nutrient, a preventative. The liquid is pale and moves with a thickness that suggests something organic, something alive. They work in silence, or nearly so. When they communicate, it is with gestures, with the kind of shorthand that comes from years of shared labor.

There is no anger here. No frustration. This is not labor that is resented but labor that is understood as maintenance, as the necessary tending of a living system that sustains them.

Inside the nearest warehouse, visible through the open loading doors, the work is different but aligned. Rows of containers—carved from the treated mycelium, lined with something fibrous and protective—hold what appears to be cultivation. Growing things. You see fruiting bodies of fungi in various stages of maturation, arranged with the precision of a library. Some are small, delicate, pale as bone. Others are large, their caps darkened with spores, their stems thick and ready for harvest. A man moves between the rows with a spray apparatus—something that looks like it was fashioned from hollowed plant-stem and ceramic chambers. He is distributing a fine mist, monitoring the moisture, ensuring the right conditions for fruiting. But the moisture he is distributing is not water. You can tell by the way it evaporates, by the faint sheen it leaves on the fungal surfaces—more oily than aqueous, more nutrient solution than rain.

The farming here is fungal farming.

This becomes clearer as you observe longer. The structures that looked like warehouses are cultivation chambers. The vast mycelium substrate beneath the city is both foundation and crop. The trained plant columns are not merely structural—they are themselves a resource, managed for eventual harvest, for use as material, for propagation. The vines trailing along the wooden supports are monitored for production of specific compounds, selected generations ago, perhaps, for their ability to produce alkaloids or proteins or nutrient-dense seed-pods in this climate.

The undergarden is a farm, and its people are farmers.

But they are also something else. They are engineers.

A younger man, perhaps in his thirties, sits at a workbench in a smaller side-structure, surrounded by implements and specimens. In front of him is a mycelium sample, perhaps the size of a hand, and he is examining it with an intensity that suggests scientific scrutiny. He has a lens—polished from something transparent and organic, horn or treated plant-material—and he is studying the structure beneath it. Around him are journals, pages of them, filled with notes in a script that is tight and precise. Diagrams. Sketches of fungal structures, cross-sections, life-cycles. Breeding notations. Records of cultivation experiments.

He is not farming by instinct. He is farming by understanding. By accumulated knowledge. By intentional selection and experimentation.

The same is true of the woman you see later, in the processing plant with the steam vents. She is monitoring a series of vessels—large containers made of treated wood and sealed with something wax-like—and she is reading instruments: glass tubes filled with liquid, calibrated measures, devices whose purpose is unclear but whose function is evident. She is monitoring something chemical, something biological. She is maintaining conditions. She is adjusting parameters. This is not subsistence cultivation. This is industrial production. The scale of operation suggests that the undergarden is not simply feeding its own population but producing something in surplus.

The surplus is a question that lingers.

You notice that people rarely appear in large groups. They work in small teams, three or four at most. The density of human presence matches the density of structures—sparse, distributed, intentional. When you catch sight of the residential areas, tucked into the upper levels of some of the larger buildings, the windows reveal living quarters that are modest and, from what you can see, densely occupied. Multiple people per small chamber. Shared spaces. A communal organization that suggests population control, resource management, a careful balance between the number of inhabitants and the capacity of the system to sustain them.

They are organized. Socially organized in ways that seem designed—or evolved—for this environment.

There is no visible hierarchy, no obvious command structure, but there is clear coordination. Information flows between workers. Decisions are made, apparently, through consensus or through trusted individuals who serve as coordinators rather than rulers. You see this in the way a disagreement is handled: two workers near one of the nutrient pools are in discussion, voices slightly elevated, but the resolution comes not through authority but through a third party arriving, listening, and the three of them examining the problem together, testing something, making an adjustment. No anger. No punishment. The problem-solving is collaborative.

But there is discipline. Enforced or voluntary—you cannot quite tell—but present. The work schedules appear to be observed without deviation. Maintenance is performed on a regular basis. No structures are neglected. No systems are allowed to degrade. There is a cleanliness here, a order, that suggests either obsessive-compulsive management or the kind of discipline that comes from living in a closed system where a single failure could cascade into catastrophe.

The children you see are few, and they are integrated into the labor.

A young girl, perhaps ten years old, works alongside an older woman in the mycelium treatment area. She is learning. She is shown how to judge the depth of scraping, how to recognize when fungal growth has reached its intended boundary, how to apply treatments with the right pressure, the right coverage. It is not harsh education. It is patient. But it is constant. She will grow up knowing this work. She will know nothing else, almost certainly. The underground is her entire world. She will inherit the responsibility of maintaining it, tending it, managing it.

The same is true of a boy you see briefly near the fungal processing plant, watching intently as one of the engineers makes a notation in a journal. The boy is memorizing the procedure, the decision-making process, the careful observation that precedes action. He is being trained into a role, a position, a function that the system requires.

They are people, but they are also components of a system. Adapted components. Components that have accepted—or been conditioned to accept—their role in maintaining the undergarden.

And yet they do not appear miserable.

This is the strange thing, the thing that becomes apparent only after you have observed them for a long time. They are not suffering. They are not desperate. They are not yearning for escape. There is a contentment here, or something close to it. A sense of purpose. When you catch their faces in moments of rest—a worker pausing between tasks, sitting at the edge of one of the nutrient pools, looking up at the fungal ceiling—there is not anguish but something like peace. Acceptance. The kind of calm that comes from understanding one's place in a system and finding that place not intolerable but, perhaps, even suitable.

Their language—the snippets you catch as you move through the undergarden—is precise and sparse. Few unnecessary words. Communication is functional. But there are moments of what might be called pleasure: a worker laughing at something a colleague has said, the sound genuine, unhurried. A moment of deliberation over how to approach a problem, with suggestions offered and considered, ideas flowing. These are not people trapped in despair. They are people living a structured life, a limited life, a life that would seem oppressive from the surface world, but which, from within, has the quality of meaning.

They have purpose.

The undergarden exists because they maintain it. They maintain it because it sustains them. The circle is closed, complete, self-reinforcing. They know no other way of life. Their entire genetic history—as far as they know, or care—is this place. The surface is myth, perhaps. Or distant history. Or something that happened to previous generations and is now irrelevant. What matters is the work. What matters is the system. What matters is that the fungal ceiling glows, the carts move, the processes continue, and the next generation is trained to assume their role in the machinery of sustenance.

They are the inhabitants of the undergarden.

They are its gardeners, its engineers, its stewards, and, perhaps, its prisoners.

But the distinction, you begin to understand, may not matter to them at all.
