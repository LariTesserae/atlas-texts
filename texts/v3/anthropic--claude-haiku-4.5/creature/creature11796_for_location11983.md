# What Lives Here

They are few, and they are old in the way stone is old.

The first one you see is not a first—you've been watched since you entered the fissure, since before that. You only become aware of the watching when a figure emerges from behind a curtain of cascading vines, and you realize the figure has always been there, perfectly still, as much a part of the cavern's texture as the moss or the fungi. This is not stealth so much as a fundamental absence of hurry. In a place where a fungal bloom takes decades to crown, stillness is the natural state.

They are pale. Skin that has never known direct sunlight has taken on a luminescence of its own—not quite glowing, but reflective, like the surface of something that lives underwater. Their eyes are large, adapted to the perpetual dusk, with irises so dark they seem to absorb what little light exists. They blink rarely. When they do, the motion is deliberate, purposeful, like the closing of a door on a building they own.

Their hair—if it can be called that—is long, fine as root-fiber, and it moves as if suspended in invisible water. It's the color of deep cave-moss, or perhaps it has simply become moss over the generations; the distinction has blurred. Small luminescent spores seem to nest in its length, so that their movements leave faint trails of pale light.

They wear clothing that isn't woven from the vines but *has become* vine—fibers so thoroughly integrated with their garments that it's impossible to say where cloth ends and the cavern's growth begins. The cloth hangs loosely, stained with the dark mulch of the floor, spotted with what might be fungal blooms or might be intentional dyes. Their hands are long-fingered and calloused, the nails thick and darkened, tools in themselves.

But what strikes you most is their *presence*. They don't carry urgency. There is no tension in their shoulders, no scanning of the cavern for threats. They move with the confidence of someone walking through their own memory. They have lived here so long that they are not separate from the cavern—they are an integrated part of its metabolism. If they left, something in the balance would shift. The fungi would grow differently. The animals would resettle. The stone itself would grieve.

There are perhaps a dozen visible in the middle distance, though you have the sense that more exist in the depths you cannot penetrate. They move with purpose, but their purposes are ancient and small. One tends to the moss along a wall, removing a diseased patch with hands and instruments of bone, replanting fresh growth from a clutch of samples kept in a carefully balanced nook. Another works at the loom, fingers moving with practiced precision, though the pattern they're creating is so complex and slow that you suspect they've been working on this single piece for years—or that they will be working on it for years still. The distinction is irrelevant.

They communicate, but not through speech—not entirely. When two of them meet, they are silent, but their silences are textured. They exchange vials of concentrated liquid that tastes (you somehow know this without being told) like earth and time. They gesture, pointing out growths that need attention or culling, showing each other small findings: a rare beetle, a particularly fine specimen of fungal fruiting, a section of wall where the stone itself seems to be changing its chemical composition. Their conversations are carried on at the speed of observation.

When they *do* speak—and one of them finally does, addressing you directly—their voice is thin and strange, as if they haven't used it in months. The words come slowly, with long pauses between them, as if they're translating from a language of silence into something your kind understands. Even their accent is old—they speak words that shifted meaning centuries ago, but they don't know this. The cavern preserves everything. Language here doesn't evolve; it crystallizes.

"You brought your time with you," they say, and it takes you a moment to understand they're referring to the way your body temperature has begun to influence the stone around you, the way you're already beginning to shed skin cells and pheromones and the bacteria of the surface world. They observe this with something like gentle curiosity, the way a gardener observes a new plant inserted into an established ecosystem.

They don't ask your name. They don't ask why you've come. These are surface questions. Here, they ask wordlessly: *Will you stay?* *Can you slow down?* *Are you ready to become part of this?*

They are patient. They have nothing but patience. They've been waiting—not for you specifically, but for someone, anyone, to arrive and face the choice that the cavern offers to all who find it:

*Ascend and return to the thin, rushing world of sunlight and urgency and individual death.*

*Or descend. Put down roots. Become another layer of the ecosystem. Let your bones eventually feed the fungi. Let your presence alter the balance and texture of this place so gradually that by the time you've become mineral, no one will remember the moment when you stopped being visitor and started being home.*

They wait. They are very, very good at waiting.
