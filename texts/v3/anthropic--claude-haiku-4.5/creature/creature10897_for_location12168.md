# What Lives Here

The Kashen Platform sustains almost nothing, and almost everything that attempts it learns this quickly.

The **primary inhabitants are mineral-processing organisms**—not quite bacteria, existing in the membrane between chemistry and biology. They thrive in the alkaline pools, in the crevices where salts accumulate, in the thin films that catch wrong-colored light. They don't metabolize in the conventional sense; they *precipitate*. They concentrate compounds, build structures from dissolved elements, create order from the thermal chaos around them. Under magnification (which the engineers occasionally perform in the deep-buried lab facilities), they resemble geometric lattices more than cells—branching crystals with the faintest suggestion of membrane, capable of slow reproduction through structural replication rather than division. The city's existence depends on their ability to process certain mineral byproducts into stable, non-toxic forms. They are, in effect, industrial workers at the microscopic scale. They are also completely indifferent to whether the city continues or fails.

**The transient animals** are second. They arrive irregularly—small, heat-adapted creatures with metabolisms that run faster than normal, burning through energy to generate the internal temperature differential they need to function. They have oversized kidneys for water conservation, skin pigmentation adapted to the diffuse gray-gold light, sensory organs oriented toward thermal gradients rather than visual acuity. A kind of small lizard appears most commonly, though "lizard" is imprecise—it lacks scales in the conventional sense, its skin more like hardened mucosa, perpetually damp. It feeds on the geometric mineral-dwellers, on the smallest crystalline structures. It leaves quickly. The heat either kills it or drives it deeper into the cooler zones where it shouldn't be. The cycle repeats: arrival, brief predation, departure or death. No breeding populations. No ecosystem. Just intermittent visitation by creatures that have learned this is a place to visit, never to stay.

**The workers**, of course. Thousands of them, rotating through shifts on cycles measured in months. They live in the cooled residential sectors—the upper levels of the city where mechanical systems work overtime to maintain something approaching human-survivable temperature. They are selected for heat tolerance, for psychological profiles that don't degrade under monotony, for the ability to treat the mechanical as sacred because everything here is mechanical. They develop thick skin, not metaphorically—actual calluses, darkened dermal layers, a physical adaptation across a single lifetime that borders on evolutionary if you watch someone work here for long enough. They tend the machines. They descend into the mines. They read the water-processing systems and adjust the mineral traps. They are so thoroughly integrated into the city's function that the distinction between worker and infrastructure blurs: both are necessary, both are replaceable, both operate on predetermined cycles.

**The engineers and overseers** inhabit the deeper facilities, the cooled zones where the city's decision-making happens. They are fewer, more permanent. They understand the thermal dynamics at a level that approaches intuition—they can read the haze-patterns in the air and know where heat buildup is creating inefficiency. They tend the geothermal taps with the care of surgeons. They are invested in the city's continuation in ways the rotating workers cannot be. They sometimes develop obsessions: with optimizing a particular process, with discovering new mineral deposits, with the theoretical limit of what the platform can extract before it becomes uninhabitable even for machines. Some of them have been here long enough that they prefer it to cooler places. The heat has become their native environment. They dream in thermal gradients.

**The city itself**, viewed as a living thing—and it must be, because it breathes, it drinks, it maintains itself. The stone is its body, coursing with water-systems like veins, heated from below like a metabolic process. The cooling towers are its respiration. The mining operations are its feeding: consuming its own substance to extract value, slowly hollowing itself out while simultaneously reshaping itself. It has no intention, no will, but it has something analogous: a momentum. It moves toward greater efficiency, toward extracting as much as possible before the conditions that sustain it change. It's a form of life that doesn't require consciousness to achieve adaptation. It just... continues.

**The strange birds** (if they are birds) are rare enough that no one has properly catalogued them. They appear in the transitional zones, where the industrial infrastructure gives way to abandoned sites. They move in ways that suggest they're not navigating by sight, but by something else—perhaps by the thermal columns, perhaps by sensitivity to the electromagnetic patterns the cooling systems generate. They don't stay. They appear to be searching for something—water sources that have already been claimed by the city, or pockets of cooler air in zones where temperature stability used to exist. They are, perhaps, ghosts of an ecosystem that once tried to take root here and failed. Or they are scouts from other plateaus, testing whether conditions have changed. They never report back. Nothing ever returns.

**The geology itself is the largest inhabitant**: patient, indifferent, continuing its work regardless of what the city does. The stone weeps because it's been fractured, drilled, mined—because the city has opened it and the deep heat is reaching the surface. The salt rings accumulate because of the water the city pumps through, the evaporation it creates, the chemical load it deposits. The hexagonal basalt tiles were already there, waiting, indifferent to whether human machinery would ever arrive to map them. The stone will outlast everything else here. It will continue to weep and accumulate and slowly be reshaped long after the city has exhausted its resources and been abandoned like the mining sites that dot the older sections. The platform's primary inhabitant is the platform itself.

And perhaps most importantly: **the heat**. Not alive, but alive-adjacent. A presence. An inhabitant in the sense that it occupies space, influences behavior, shapes what can and cannot survive. It is the city's reason for existing and the cause of everything that makes the city hostile to life. It moves through the stone like blood, like intention, like the slow grinding of a god that has nothing better to do than persist. Everything here negotiates with the heat. Everything here is either burning in it, cooling against it, or being reshaped by it.

The platform, in total, is inhabited by things that don't need to flourish—only endure. And in enduring, they maintain the city's function, cycle by cycle, until something breaks or someone decides the extraction is no longer worth the cost.
