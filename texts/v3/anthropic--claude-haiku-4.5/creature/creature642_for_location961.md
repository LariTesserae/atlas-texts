# The Dwellers of Thask

They are patient in a way that seems to have reshaped their very flesh.

The humans who live here—and you eventually learn there are perhaps two hundred of them scattered across the terraced platforms, though you never see more than a few at any given moment—move with a deliberateness that suggests they're not fighting against time but negotiating with it. Their bodies are long and lean, not from hunger but from a kind of attenuation. The constant submersion has changed them. Their skin has taken on a translucent quality in places, particularly at the temples and along the inner wrists, as if the water has learned to flow through them rather than around them. Their eyes are large and adapt to the perpetual twilight with an uncanny speed; within a generation or two of living here, the iris seems to have become more responsive, more dilated. You notice their children—there are a few—already moving with that same unhurried grace, already comfortable in the water in a way that suggests adaptation, not habituation.

They speak rarely and in low voices. When they do, the words seem to come from very far away, shaped by the water before they reach your ears. The language is old—you recognize a few words from the lowland dialects you know—but it's been modified, simplified in some ways, elaborated in others. There are words for types of water-depth that your own language doesn't have. Words for the quality of light at different hours (or days; the distinction is unclear). Words for the particular sound a certain kind of vine makes when it moves. The language has evolved to describe this place with precision, which suggests that describing it accurately matters to them.

They are gentle with each other in a way that suggests long proximity without friction. You watch an old man and a younger woman—perhaps his daughter, perhaps not; kinship seems to operate on different logic here—collaboratively repair a section of building-binding. They work in silence, their movements synchronized as if choreographed, though you suspect they've simply done this together so many times that conversation has become unnecessary. The work is meticulous. A vine that has begun to fray is carefully rewoven. A section of petrified wood that's showing crystallization patterns is inspected, discussed briefly, and left alone—a decision made wordlessly and then honored by both parties.

The fishing is almost ritual. You watch the same man cast the same rod into the same stretch of murky water for hours, never seeming frustrated by the lack of catches. When you ask him—slowly, in the modified language, uncertain of grammar—whether the fishing is good, he looks at you with an expression that might be pity or might be compassion. "The fishing is the fishing," he says, or something like it. The translation is slippery. He might mean that the act is its own reward. He might mean that fishing and fish have become separate concepts. He might mean something you don't have words for yet.

There's an old woman who tends the fungal gardens on the third tier. You observe her over several days, and it becomes clear that what you initially took for random overgrowth is, in fact, carefully cultivated. She moves through the tangle with the confidence of someone navigating a familiar home. She prunes with deliberation—removing a bracket fungus here, adjusting the growth-pattern of a gelatinous bloom there. She harvests some, leaving others. Her hands move with surgical precision despite the constant water, despite the dim light. When you venture close enough to observe what she's harvesting, you realize: it's food. The fungi are crops. She wraps sections in cloth woven from plant fiber and places them in stone containers filled with that warm water, preserving them in a way that's part fermentation, part something else you don't have a name for. She works with the expression of someone doing something she's done thousands of times and expects to do thousands more.

The children are the strangest. There are perhaps a dozen of them, ranging from infants to adolescents. They play in ways that don't quite match play as you understand it. There's no running, no shouting, no competition. Instead, they move through the water in patterns that seem almost like dance, or like practice for something else. An older boy teaches a younger girl the precise way to move through different water-densities without disrupting them too much—a skill that seems useless until you realize: it minimizes the disturbance that might draw attention from the larger things that move in the deep water. The children move with a kind of grace that suggests their bodies already know how to live here in a way their parents' bodies are still learning.

There is a man who keeps track of time. You don't understand this at first—the city seems to exist outside of conventional temporal markers—but you notice him ascending to the highest platform each morning, watching the light (or waiting for it to change, though it never seems to). He carries marks with him: notches in a bone rod, carefully made, one per day presumably, or per some unit of time you can't quite grasp. He also keeps records: careful drawings on treated hide, documenting the growth patterns of specific vines, the flowering cycles of the pale blossoms, the changes in water-color at certain depths. When you ask him what he's measuring, he shows you his collection without speaking. You see decades of documentation. Perhaps centuries, if the marks run that far back. The earliest ones are crude; the most recent are refined and precise. He's been here for part of this documentation; you can see where his hand-style changes, where he took up the practice from someone else. Whether he's documenting the city's decline or its transformation, or whether those are the same thing, you cannot tell.

There are people you never clearly see. You hear evidence of them: movement in the lower tiers, sounds of work coming from the submerged structures, the occasional splash that suggests someone entering or exiting the water from platforms you cannot reach. Whether these are additional residents or whether the same few people simply move with enough separation that they seem like more, you never determine. The city's population is a number that shifts depending on where you stand and when you look.

The old people have a quality of having become partially water themselves. Their movements are fluid to the point of seeming boneless. Their skin has taken on a faint luminescence, visible only at certain angles and in certain light. Whether this is actual bioluminescence or simply a reflection from the flowers they're constantly around, you cannot determine. But it adds to the uncanny quality of watching them move through the dim light—they seem less like humans who've adapted to the water and more like the water itself, given temporary human shape, remembering what it once was.

There is a hierarchy here, but it's so subtle you almost miss it. The old woman who tends the fungi gardens seems to defer to an elderly man who spends most of his time in one of the central buildings, a structure that's more intact than most. But his authority seems to operate entirely through suggestion and presence rather than instruction. You never hear him give orders. Yet people seem to know what's needed. Work proceeds. Resources are distributed. Disagreements—and they must happen, they must, in any human community—are resolved in ways so quiet you don't witness the actual conflict, only the resolution.

They have a communal meal space—a broad platform that's the shallowest of all the major gathering points, where the water barely reaches waist-height. Here, once per cycle (day? week? the timing remains unclear), perhaps twenty or thirty people gather. There's food: preserved fungi, small fish that have been treated in ways that make them less musty than you'd expect, vegetables that have been grown in beds elevated above the water on certain of the higher platforms, edible algae that they've cultivated carefully in specific channels. Someone—a different person each time, you notice—prepares the meal. Everyone eats. There is conversation, but it's minimal. Mostly, they eat in a companionable silence, looking out over the city as if seeing it for the first time despite seeing it every day.

What emerges over your time here is not a portrait of a community in decline, but a community in transition. They're not waiting to leave; they're waiting to become something else. The patience isn't passive. It's active. It's the patience of people who understand that transformation takes time, and they're committed to being present for all of it. They're teaching their bodies how to live in a place that's partially water. They're teaching their food systems how to grow in saturation. They're teaching their language how to describe this new world. They're teaching their children how to be human in a way that doesn't require the world they came from.

They are, in essence, becoming Thaskian. Not yet, not fully. But they are committed to the becoming.

And perhaps most strangely: they seem at peace with it.
