# The Inhabitants of Raines Plateau

They are not quite settlers anymore, nor are they natives. They are something in between—*keepers* might be the nearest word, though that implies too much agency. Rather, they have become a function of the place, like the pumps themselves.

Most are second or third generation. Their parents came for work in the 1960s and '70s, when the extraction operation was expanding, when there was a sense that the plateau held wealth worth extracting. These original migrants came from lowland regions—from cities with proper traffic and crowds and rain that fell in distinct events rather than existing as a constant condition of the air. They came for wages and stability. Many stayed because they developed a tolerance for it that their bodies would not allow them to abandon. Their children were born into the humidity, into the omnipresent industrial rhythm, and they know no other normal.

The current residents are mostly in their thirties through sixties. Younger people leave when they can. There is a school that goes through secondary level, but most students who show particular aptitude are encouraged to pursue higher education elsewhere. Some return. Many do not. The youngest permanent residents tend to be those who have already tried leaving and found the external world too loud, too wet, too crowded, too *changeable*. There is a peculiar selectivity operating: the plateau keeps those whom it suits, and those whom it suits are becoming increasingly specific.

They move with a particular quality of deliberation. Not slowly, exactly, but with a sense of purposefulness that suggests every gesture has been calculated for efficiency. A person walking across the pale limestone from one building to another does not wander—they track a direct line, their gait steady. There is minimal idle movement. This is partly practical (the ground is uneven, the air is thick), but it has become cultural. Unnecessary motion seems to be considered wasteful, the way that unnecessary speech would be in a different kind of place.

Their faces tend toward a particular type: weathered but not deeply lined, as though the constant humidity has kept the skin flexible even as it has aged. There is often a slight squinting—the diffused light still strains the eyes, and the habit of half-closing them against the moisture-laden air has become endemic. The skin takes on a faintly mineral appearance after a few years of residence, as though the stone dust that perpetually floats in the air has begun to integrate itself into the epidermis.

Most have some direct connection to the extraction operation. They work as pump technicians, in the processing facility, in the administrative functions that keep the machinery running. A few are teachers, two are doctors, three are the aforementioned biologists. There is a small contingent of service workers—a baker, a mechanic, the keeper of what passes for a grocery store—but even these are positioned within the operational structure. The baker supplies bread to the workers. The mechanic maintains the vehicles used to move materials and personnel. Everything relates back to the core function: extracting and processing the mineral-rich geothermal water.

Work schedules are organized in overlapping shifts to ensure constant operation. This creates a peculiar temporal structure to social life. There are no proper business hours. The town operates continuously, but with a kind of *lag*—activity is distributed across the full twenty-four hours, so that there is rarely a true moment of complete activity or complete silence. A person sleeping at midnight might find noise levels unchanged at three a.m. Another might work a shift that runs from six p.m. to two a.m., sleep until ten, then work again from four to midnight the following day.

Their leisure time is, necessarily, adjusted to this reality. Traditional patterns of socialization—evening gatherings, weekend activities—have given way to something more organic and less synchronized. People congregate when their schedules allow. There is a small bar in Raines proper that operates continuously, serving those between shifts or unable to sleep. A group of residents has maintained a library of physical books in one of the older administrative buildings, a space that functions somewhat as a community center. More often, leisure is solitary: reading, listening to radio broadcasts from lowland cities, maintaining gardens in the few sheltered pockets where soil allows it, or simply sitting in one's dwelling and absorbing the ambient sounds until they no longer register as noise.

They have developed an acute sensitivity to variations in the ordinary sounds of the place. The pump cycles, which seem chaotic to an outsider, are actually highly legible to them. A person raised here can hear when a pump is operating at a slightly off rhythm and understand what that might mean—mechanical stress, perhaps, or a blockage forming. The crack of thermal expansion in the stone is so common that it barely registers, but its *absence* would be noticed. The hum of the electrical system, the deep churn from the distant geothermal plant—these have become a kind of baseline against which other sounds are measured.

They have also developed a specific relationship to silence. True silence—the absence of even the background industrial hum—is rare and, to those raised here, vaguely unsettling. Several residents have spoken of traveling to genuinely quiet places and finding them oppressive, as though the absence of sound created a kind of sensory void that anxiety rushed to fill. They return to Raines gratefully, to the familiar omnipresent acoustic presence.

Their bodies have adapted in subtle ways. The constant humidity means they sweat continuously but inefficiently. Their circulatory systems have adjusted to operate at a slightly elevated baseline. There is a higher incidence of certain respiratory conditions—nothing dramatic enough to be called a disease, more a chronic low-level inflammation of the airways that comes from years of breathing air supersaturated with mineral particles. The local doctor monitors this but considers it an acceptable cost. The low-level radioactivity of the water has produced no observable health effects over the two generations that have been exposed to it, a fact that is both reassuring and slightly eerie in its absence of consequence.

They maintain traditions that have developed specifically for this place. There is an annual gathering in the summer (the season when the temperature differential is most pronounced and the stone-singing is loudest) where residents assemble at a particular vantage point on the plateau's northern edge. No speeches are made, no formal ritual enacted. They simply gather, listen to the sound intensify as the sun begins to set, and then disperse. No one can quite explain why this happens, or how it originated, but it has been occurring for at least twenty years, and new residents are quietly informed of its existence and invited to attend.

There is a peculiar absence of violence or conflict in the permanent population. This is not to say there are no disagreements—there are, particularly regarding resource allocation and operational decisions—but serious conflict is rare. Several longtime residents attribute this to the environment itself. One, an engineer named Maris who has lived on the plateau for thirty-four years, described it this way: "The place doesn't permit urgency. Everything works at the speed of water moving through stone. You can't maintain a rage or a grudge when even your thoughts move slower than usual."

Whether this is actually a property of the environment or simply a folk explanation for a selection bias—that people prone to conflict have already left—is unclear. Possibly both are true.

They are cautious about change. The proposal to upgrade some of the older steam-powered pumps to newer, more efficient models generated weeks of quiet resistance and debate. Not because the new pumps were inferior (they were objectively superior), but because change is understood instinctively as dangerous. New equipment might operate at a different cycle, alter the acoustic baseline, require different maintenance patterns. The fear is not of the new pumps themselves but of the disruption they would introduce to an equilibrium that has taken decades to establish. Ultimately, a compromise was reached: three pumps would be replaced as a trial. The results are still being assessed, and the decision of whether to continue is pending.

They have become, in essence, a part of the machinery—not in a dehumanizing sense, but in the way that a specific gear becomes integral to a particular machine's function. Remove them, and the operation would require recalibration. Replace them with others, and the machine would initially run differently, inefficiently, until the new components adapted to the specific conditions. They are human, certainly, but they are also localized, specific, calibrated to this particular place by years of residence and adaptation.

There are occasional newcomers—workers brought in on contract, outsiders brought for specific projects. These people often struggle. The constant humidity affects them physically in ways that long-term residents have adapted to. The noise, which has faded into background for the permanent population, remains intrusive and stressful. The slowness of everything grates on those accustomed to faster operational tempos. Most contracts are twelve to twenty-four months. Few workers choose to extend beyond their initial term.

The permanent residents watch these temporary workers with a kind of gentle detachment. They recognize the struggle because they remember it, however distantly, from their own arrivals or their parents' arrivals. But they also understand that not everyone will adapt, and the place seems to accept this without judgment. The plateau is not hostile to transients, merely indifferent. It will allow you to pass through unchanged, or it will slowly integrate you into its rhythms. There is no middle path.

They are patient in a way that seems almost geological. They plan in decades. They maintain infrastructure designed to last fifty years. They speak carefully, as though words are a resource to be conserved. They listen to the stone and the machines and each other, and they have developed a kind of wisdom specific to places where change is glacially slow and everything exists in a state of fine equilibrium.

They are not happy, precisely. They have moved beyond the category of happiness into something more like *acceptance*—an understanding that this is where they are, and that the place itself, with all its strangeness and discomfort and strange beauty, has a kind of rightness to it. To live here is to exist slightly outside of normal time, slightly outside of the rapid world beyond the plateau's edge.

And perhaps that is what they are, most essentially: *people who have learned to live outside of time*, tending machinery that grinds slowly on, breathing air that never changes, listening to the endless acoustic presence of a place that has learned, over millennia, to exist in perfect stasis—and who have, themselves, learned to do the same.
