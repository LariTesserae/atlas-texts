# What Lives Here

They are legion first. Numbering beyond count—the insects existing as populations rather than individuals. The flies form clouds that coalesce and disperse like thoughts, visible mainly through the disturbance they create in the blue-cast light. They are the air's animation, its nervous system. Beneath them, in the moss substrate, beetles work in battalions, their carapaces catching what light reaches down in pinpoints of darker reflection. They are the moss's gardeners and undertakers both, breaking things down to component parts, making room for what comes next.

The spiders are architects. Patient, meticulous, they've turned the tangle of aerial roots into a three-dimensional library of webs—some geometric and purposeful, others sprawling and desperate, adjusted constantly to capture whatever drifts through the moisture-thick air. There are spiders living in spiders' webs, parasitizing the parasitizers. The webs themselves have become substrate, gathering moss and debris until they're no longer transparent but opaque, more like fabric than trap. Some of the largest webs haven't been maintained in months; they're archaeological layers of failed hunting, of meals taken and webs abandoned for better territory elsewhere.

The salamanders are the apex of the small world—polished things with an almost lacquered appearance, their skin darker than the moss but luminous with the kind of shine that comes from moisture and careful chemical balance. They hunt with a decisiveness that seems unnecessary given how abundant prey is. Perhaps it's practice. Perhaps hunting is what keeps them real in a place where everything else is soft and yielding. They freeze between movements, becoming indistinguishable from the moss they rest upon, then move with violent acceleration. A three-inch predator is still a predator.

The centipedes are violence made arthropod. They move like thoughts of violence—quick, angular, without apparent mercy. They're segmented wrongness, too many legs operating in patterns that hurt to watch. They live in the darkest crevices, where the moss is thickest and decay is most active. They hunt by vibration, by sensing disturbances in the fungal mat. When they strike, it's total commitment. You've seen one take down something three times its size, the victim thrashing uselessly as the centipede wrapped itself around it like a living spring.

The unseen larger thing—it moves with intention. It's not the creature itself that registers but the absence it creates. The moss flattens under its weight, slowly rebounding. The water ripples spread from a point you don't quite see. Sometimes you hear it in the pools—that deliberate splashing, the sound of something heavy moving through water without haste, without fear. It's herbivorous, probably. There's no predatory urgency in its movement. It's feeding, possibly on the algae mats in the pools, possibly on vegetation. It knows you're there. The knowledge is neutral. You're too large, too unpredictable, too alien to hunt. You exist in the same space but on different food chains. Sometimes you catch movement at the edge of the LED's reach—something dark, something that could be several feet long. A rat might be rat-shaped. This thing is ambiguous about its shape. It could be fur, could be wet soil, could be algae floating in suspension. The mystery of it prevents fear.

The organisms in the pools are distinct from everything else. They chirp—that's the only word for the sound. Not quite frog-calls, not quite insect-songs. Something in between, something that didn't exist before the tunnel became a garden. The pools are murky beyond transparency, their surfaces filmed with algae so thoroughly that water is just a suggestion beneath green. Things rise to break the surface—mouths opening for air? Eyes? The disturbance suggests intelligence, or something that mimics intelligence. In a place this alive, the difference is meaningless.

The fungi are patient and eternal. They exist in a time-scale different from the insects' frantic generations. Shelf-fungi grow incrementally, year by year adding another ring, another layer. Soft tooth-fungi hang from the root-cables like stalactites of sporangium. The fruiting bodies are everywhere—some translucent as infected skin, others hard as the wood they consume. They're breaking down the infrastructure piece by piece. The petrified roots that seem solid are being hollowed from within. The pipes that still carry water are being softened, made ready for the day they fail. The fungi know time the way mountains do. They have patience.

The moss exists as biography. The oldest patches, the dark nearly-black ones in the deepest recesses, represent decades of accumulation. They're thick enough to hold their own moisture, to generate their own climate. Compress enough moss and it becomes its own thing—an organism's organism, a composite being made of billions of smaller beings in such density that the distinction between self and colony becomes meaningless. The lighter greens are newer growth, lighter because they're less compressed, less experienced. They're still learning to be moss in a place where moss is everything.

The mold is everywhere and invisible until you know to look for it. It threads through the decomposing matter like intentions through thought. It's breaking down the city's refuse—the leather that lined some long-forgotten passenger's luggage, the wool of an ancient coat, the paper of a ticket from a journey that ended forty years ago. The mold is a recycler working without contract or complaint, converting human artifact back into component molecules. In its slow way, it's erasing the occupation, making the tunnel innocent again.

The water cycle is internal here. Nothing leaves; nothing enters from outside. The water evaporates from the pools and condenses on the cooler air, running down the root-cables to be absorbed by the moss and fungi, transpired back into the air, falling again as invisible precipitation. The water is living matter in this place, a medium that carries nutrients and spores and pheromones. The water itself is alive with microorganisms—bacteria, protozoans, things too small to see but catalyzing everything visible. The water is the tunnel's blood.

The air is thick with spores. Fungal spores primarily, but also spores from the mosses, pollen from whatever flowering plants might live in the deeper reaches (you haven't seen them, but something must be reproducing sexually in here). The spores are reproductive strategy made visible—billions of attempts at colonizing new substrate, at finding new surfaces to grow upon. Some will land in your lungs. You are becoming a garden yourself, hosting a thousand spores that will die in the dryness of the surface world, but which live here, in you, momentarily. You are part of the ecosystem's architecture.

The creatures that hunt in the spore-clouds are too small to see clearly—some kind of aerial plankton-eater, functioning at the limit of visibility. They exist as shimmer, as motion-suggestion. They're filtering something—spores? Minute insects? Viral particles? The air is their ocean, and they're the krill of it, the base of a food chain that extends up to the salamanders and centipedes and that larger, ambiguous thing in the dark.

Everything here is feeding on everything else, in a cascade of predation and decomposition so complete that the distinction between eating and being eaten becomes temporary. The moss feeds on the light from the failing LEDs and on the nutrients released by decomposing things. The insects feed on the moss and fungi. The salamanders feed on the insects. The centipedes feed on the salamanders, or the insects, or each other. The bacteria and fungi feed on all of it, simultaneously, breaking down and making available. The larger creature feeds on plants, possibly, or on decomposing matter, or on the algae, or on something the surface world has no name for anymore.

What lives here is not distinct creatures but a process. The process of life expressing itself at maximum density, maximum interconnection. The process of the city's infrastructure being metabolized by the living world. The process of death becoming food becoming growth becoming complexity becoming the kind of teeming, humid, patient abundance that fills every gap and crevice and moment with intention—not human intention, but the intention of survival, of reproduction, of continuing.

They are patient. The largest thing in here—it has been patient for decades. The fungi have been patient for longer. The water has been patient since the Pleistocene. They are teaching the place patience. Teaching it that infrastructure fails, that cities are temporary, that time moves to the scale of spores and root-growth and the slow accumulation of moss. They are winning. They have already won. They're just still in the process of the winning, still implementing the victory, still converting the concrete and wire and intention of human building into soil, into substrate, into the food for the next generation of growth.

What lives here is the world reclaiming itself, one drop of moisture at a time.
