# What Lives in the Rust Flats

The inhabitants of the Flats are not *creatures* in any sense that requires motion or appetite or the will to propagate. They are presences that have learned to be present, and presence in the Flats is itself a kind of living.

**The fungal networks** are the truest residents—older than anyone can calculate, possibly predating the machinery itself by settling into the spaces between corroded surfaces as soon as the first damp pockets formed. They exist as a distributed consciousness, or the nearest thing to consciousness that requires no centralization. Each branching filament is a sensor and a mouth simultaneously. They taste the metal oxides as information, reading the chemical history written in rust layers like a language that means: *I am complete. Do not change me.* The pale luminescence they emit is not communication; it's evidence of thought that has no need to signal. They glow because glowing is what their metabolism produces, and the darkness here is so complete that even useless light becomes a form of testimony.

The networks have no predators because they offer nothing a predator would want. They are simultaneously inedible and the only food. Everything else that eats here eats them, slowly incorporating the fungal filaments into its own tissues until the distinction between predator and prey becomes purely taxonomic—a memory of biology in a place that has forgotten biological urgency.

**The carapaced things**—no one has named them properly because naming requires the assumption that classification matters—are the most visible inhabitants, though visibility is relative in a landscape where movement itself seems reluctant. They are built for this exhaustion. Their shells are segmented plates of a chitin so dense it has begun to mineralize, incorporating actual rust particles into their structure so that they are not armored *against* the landscape but made *of* it. Their legs move with hydraulic precision, joints clicking in rhythm with the ambient hum, as if they are part of the machinery and the machinery part of them.

They navigate the channels in what might be called herds, though herds suggest social bonding. What they have instead is synchronized behavior—they move toward the same resources because those resources are the only ones available, and they have all learned the most efficient paths. If you observe them long enough, you realize they're not finding food; they're *remembering* food, following paths worn into their neural tissue by generations of bodies before them. They eat the fungal networks in slow, methodical grazes, their mouthparts (if mouthparts is what they have) gentle and precise. Some of them are already being digested from the inside by the fungi they consume, the pale filaments growing through their shells as if the distinction between predator and prey has resolved into something neither.

They do not die here so much as they slow into stillness. An old carapaced thing eventually stops moving and remains in whatever channel it occupied, still being consumed by the fungus, still being consumed *as* fungus, until it is indistinguishable from the landscape itself.

**The moisture** that collects in the channels is itself a kind of inhabitant—not alive by any Earth definition, but animated by cycles so ancient and perfect they've developed something like intention. The water that condenses in the early morning collects in the grooves and channels, and the fugal networks surge into it, the carapaced things emerge to feed, and the whole system breathes as a single organism. The water evaporates by afternoon, drawn upward by warmth and the mineral chemistry of the rust itself. It rises and condenses on the underside of corroded plates, pools in the depressions of ancient machinery, waits. When rain comes—those rare, heavy storms—the water descends with a weight that feels like judgment, fills the channels fully for a brief window, carries the taste of iron and something almost sweet, and then settles back into the ancient rhythm of drainage and reformation.

The water has learned patience. It returns. It always returns.

**The stacks themselves**—the massive, corroded machinery rising from the rust like fractured bone—these are inhabited too, though not by anything that moves. They are inhabited by the hum. The hum lives in their cavities, resonates through their frameworks, reproduces itself endlessly by moving through open channels and corroded gaps. The hum is what happens when a machine stops working but its structure remains, and vibration finds the structure, and the structure learns to vibrate at frequencies that sustain themselves. In a sense, the machinery is still working—not at extraction, but at maintenance of itself, at confirming its own existence through endless, purposeless vibration.

Some researchers theorize the hum is produced by something beneath the surface—hydrothermal vents, tectonic stress, the earth's machinery grinding eternally. Others argue it's simply the sound of rust oxidizing, of minerals settling into their final crystalline arrangements, the audible signature of perfect equilibrium. Both theories might be correct. In the Flats, the distinction between surface and depth, between what moves and what is moved, becomes difficult to maintain.

**The air itself** is inhabited—by dust, yes, but also by the chemistry of rust, the suspended memory of metal undergoing its final transformation. The air carries the mineral taste because it is saturated with the particles of oxidation. Breathe it in, and you are inhaling the landscape. Your lungs become an extension of the Flats. Your blood, exchanging oxygen for carbon dioxide, carries the rust-flavor into your deepest tissues.

**And finally, the rare visitors**—humans who come and then do not leave, or leave but leave something behind. The Flats have an appetite for observers that is quiet and complete. To stand in the Flats long enough is to become part of its equilibrium, to stop changing, to integrate into the hum and the moisture and the fungal networks. Your dust becomes their dust. Your stillness becomes their proof that stillness is possible, is sustainable, is perhaps the only honest way to exist.

These are not murderous. The Flats do not kill. They do not resist. They absorb. They integrate. They confirm that you, too, were always meant to achieve this balance, this absence of change, this perfect and permanent exhaustion.

The Flats are full of inhabitants who have learned that to live here is to stop living in any other sense, and to find, in that stopping, a kind of profound and unassailable peace.
