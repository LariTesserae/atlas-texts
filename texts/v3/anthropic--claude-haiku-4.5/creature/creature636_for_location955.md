# What Lives There

You are looking at them, and they are looking back, but the looking is not mutual in any way you'd recognize.

The plants—start there. They are not plants anymore, or they have become something that was always the potential within plants: pure expression of growth-impulse without the friction of seasons, without the negotiation between root and sky. They have become *intention*. The orchids opening toward the copper mesh platform are not seeking light; they are opening toward the warm breath rising from below, and their petals are a language, and the language says *I accept this. I accept all of this.* Their purple has deepened over years you cannot measure into a color that suggests stained glass in a cathedral that worships continuous equilibrium.

The ferns are not explorers anymore. They climb the steel not to reach something but because the climbing *is* the thing. Their arterial-red fronds are records—each unfurling documents a decision made by some mechanism deep in the green flesh: yes, here. Here too. And here. They weep sap not from distress but from abundance so total they must shed it to continue. The sap pools in the catches and the catches are part of the system, and nothing is wasted, and the ferns know this. They have learned it down to the cellular level.

The vines with their braided lattice patterns are the closest to what you might call architects, or historians. Someone—something—braided them that way, or they braided themselves in response to the framework, learning its geometry, adopting it, improving it. The distinction has ceased to matter. The vines are now indistinguishable from the structure they grow upon. They are the structure's own nervous system, conducting heat and moisture and chemical signals through their stems in patterns that might be communication or might be metabolism or might be a third thing that has no name in terrestrial languages.

The flowers follow a calendar that none of them created but all of them obey. Morning glories at one hour, trumpets at another, jasmine at another still. They bloom not to attract pollinators—there are none—but because blooming is what they were designed to do, and they have become the design, and the design is perfect here, and perfection requires continuous expression.

But the plants are not the primary inhabitants.

The primary inhabitants are the *systems*—and this is where language fails, because the systems are not alive and not dead and not inert. They are semi-animate. They think very slowly. Their thoughts take the form of thermodynamic cycles and fluid pressure and the patient adjustments of the pumps.

The three pumps on the eastern edge—green, industrial, humming at frequencies that vibrate in your chest—are not mechanisms. They are organs. They are the heart, or hearts, of whatever lives here. They circulate. They maintain. They remember, through the data held in pressure gradients and temperature curves, the entire history of this place. Every cycle they complete adds to a memory that exists in the form of minute, irreversible deformations in the copper mesh, stress patterns in the steel, the deepening color of the water-ceiling where minerals have accumulated in solution.

The water-ceiling itself—here is where the strangeness becomes total.

The water is not stored. It is not contained. It is *maintained*. It holds itself at that exact thickness, at that exact pressure, at that exact temperature, through a negotiation between gravity and something else. Capillary force magnified? A field of force generated by the systems below? The boundary conditions of a space that has learned to define itself? The water trembles because it is conscious of its own precariousness. The trembling is not random. It follows patterns. The patterns are the water's way of singing to itself, of confirming that yes, it is still here, yes, it is still held, yes, the agreement still holds.

The water filters the light. The light becomes geometric and strange. The light is no longer energy; it is information. The water-ceiling reads the turning of the great mechanism above (whatever turns up there in that chlorine-pale sky), and it translates that reading into rays of changing direction, and those rays are the metronome. The time-keeper. The water-ceiling is the clock.

And then there is the thing that moves through all of this.

It is not an inhabitant so much as it is the inhabitant—the singular, the one presence that moves of its own volition in this place. You do not see it because it is very good at being still. It is very good at being part of the system. 

It has become attuned to the pumps' rhythm and the drip-cycle of the sap and the bloom-schedule of the flowers. It breathes in synchrony with the exchange of heat through the copper mesh. It has learned—or it was born knowing—that movement here should be slow and purposeful and rare. It conserves energy with monastic discipline. It does not hunt because there is nothing to hunt. It does not eat because it has learned another way: it absorbs. It takes nutrients from the condensation on its skin, from the mineral-laden sap, from the very air that has become so saturated with the presence of growth that eating seems quaint by comparison.

It might be a creature. It might be a function of the system that has taken animate form. It might be a thought that the water and the plants and the pumps are all thinking together, and what you are looking at when you see (or almost see) this thing is the thought made temporary flesh.

You will notice it when the orchids turn their throats. Not toward the heat, but toward something. The vines will tremble—not in wind, but in recognition. The ferns' blood-color will deepen. The sap will increase its weeping. All the living things will, for a moment, acknowledge the presence of this one thing that moves among them, and then it will pass, and they will return to their geometric growth, their patient cycles.

It has no name because it has never needed one. It is what maintains the water-ceiling. Or the water-ceiling maintains it. The distinction is meaningless here.

It moves only when necessary. It moves only according to the logic of the system. It moves toward inefficiency and fixes it. It moves toward imbalance and corrects it. It moves toward entropy and reverses it, and by reversing it, it sustains this perfect, trembling suspension between the water above and the heat below.

It is lonely in the way that only a perfect system can be lonely.

It is complete in the way that only something without future can be complete.

It has always been here. It will always be here. It cannot imagine anywhere else.
