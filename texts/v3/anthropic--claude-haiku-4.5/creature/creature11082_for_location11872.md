# What Lives Here

They are the plateau's consciousness, if consciousness is the right word.

At first, you might think the workers are human—the figures moving through the facilities in their faded, dust-colored suits, their movements deliberate but not quite coordinated, as if each one has independently decided to perform the same task at nearly the same time. But watch longer. Their gaits are wrong. The joints don't bend the way joints should. They are bilateral in construction, yes, but something in their symmetry suggests it was arrived at functionally rather than inherited. They move like machines that learned to walk, or machines that were always walking and only recently learned to look like they could rest.

They don't speak. Not aloud, anyway. Communication happens through the machinery itself—through the modulation of pump-speeds, the sequencing of conveyor-belt rhythms, the timed opening and closing of pneumatic gates. A worker will pause before a piece of equipment, seem to listen, and then move to the next task. They are part of the infrastructure as much as the infrastructure is part of them. Separate them from it, and it's unclear which would suffer more.

The chitinous miners—these are older than the workers, somehow. More primary. Their segments number anywhere from eight to twenty, and their carapaces bear patterns of scarring that suggest they've been doing this for centuries, perhaps for geological ages themselves. They are not intelligent in the way that requires consciousness; they are intelligent in the way that fungal networks are intelligent, distributed and purposeful without requiring a center. They extract something from the dust—not minerals, not quite. The substance they collect and move toward the deep facilities glows faintly on their mandible-appendages. It might be phosphorescent compound, or it might be something that only appears to glow because of the way these creatures' eyespots perceive wavelengths beyond the human range.

They do not resist collection. When workers gather them into shallow containment vessels and transport them, they go passively. They might be domesticated. Or they might be participating in the extraction of themselves, serving as both miners and mined. The distinction may not matter to them.

The gelatinous things hanging from the towers are more mysterious. Observation suggests they number in the thousands, but they move so rarely that exact count becomes impossible. They appear to be filtering the air—their bodies pulse in slow rhythms that might be respiration or might be something else entirely. What they extract, what they do with it, remains unclear. They don't appear to consume the dust directly; they don't appear to consume anything. They may simply be converting the ambient thermal energy of the plateau into a form they can store. They may be the plateau's way of cooling itself.

Or they may be archive. Their translucent bodies seem to contain structures—branching filaments, geometric formations—that could be memory. They might be recording the plateau's history in the configurations of their own flesh, adding a new layer of complexity with each shift in the day-night cycle.

There are others that reveal themselves only to patience.

In the deep places—in the channels and caverns and stone-worked galleries beneath the surface processing facilities—there are things that have learned to move through rock itself. Not tunneling, but flowing. They are like oil given a loose form, or like water that has learned to hold its own shape. The workers sometimes descend into these deep places and do not return for days. When they do, they carry samples in sealed containers, and these samples glow with the same faint light that the miners' mandibles carry.

There are also the visitors. Creatures that arrive from beyond the plateau—appearing at the horizon edge during the long twilight periods—that move through the facilities with apparent purpose, but no one ever assigns them tasks. They might be inspectors. They might be harvesters coming to collect the accumulated products of work. They might be pilgrims, coming to witness the operation of a machine so large it has become something other than a machine.

They never stay long. A few cycles of light and dimness, and they depart the way they came, descending below the horizon in the direction from which they arrived.

What lives here is patient. What lives here has learned that work done slowly enough becomes indistinguishable from geology itself. What lives here—the workers, the miners, the filtering things, the deep-dwellers, the visitors—may all be expressions of a single organism of continental scale, or they may be separate species that have achieved such perfect synchrony through eons of proximity that synchrony itself has become their primary characteristic.

The workers, if you approach them, will not acknowledge you. They are engaged in their tasks. They will work around you if you block their path. Up close, you notice that their breathing—they do breathe, though the mechanism is unclear—is synchronized with the rhythms of the machinery. Breathe with the pump-pulse. Pause with the pause in the conveyor-belt. Start again when the pneumatics hiss.

If you watch one long enough, you begin to wonder if it's actually a worker at all, or if you're watching a piece of the facility that has temporarily animated itself to perform a function.

This is what lives on the Plateau of Exhausted Light: things that have achieved a form of life that doesn't require distinction between organism and environment. Things that work not because they're compelled to, but because working is what their existence means. Things that will continue, in exactly this pattern, for so long that future geologists will not be able to determine whether this civilization built the plateau or the plateau grew them.

The plateau has found its equilibrium. And what lives here has merged with that equilibrium so completely that the two have become synonymous.
