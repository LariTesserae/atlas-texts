# What Lives There

They are the *inhabitants of the threshold*, creatures for whom the boundary between water and air, between solid and dissolving, is not a problem to solve but a home to occupy.

The frogs are perhaps the most obvious—thousands of them, though you learn quickly that "thousands" doesn't capture their presence. They exist in *abundance*, a state beyond mere number. They are green-browns and mossy blacks, their skin perpetually glistening, their bodies compressed slightly as if the humidity has weight enough to press them flatter. They sing—not in chorus but in *saturation*, each voice a thread in an endless textile. The smallest ones are barely fingernail-sized, translucent almost, their throats pulsing with calls that seem to come from inside the water itself. The larger ones occupy the logs and deeper margins, their croaks resonating through the chest of anything that listens. They don't seem to tire. They seem, instead, to be the *mechanism by which sound perpetuates itself*, as if the wetland generates them specifically to maintain its sonic density.

The fish are harder to know—shadows that dart and hesitate, visible only as displacement. But there are many types: small silvers that move in scattered formations just beneath the surface, larger shapes that create actual wakes in the shallows, things with whiskers or spines that root along the bottom in the half-light. They navigate the space between root and stem as if these tangles form a three-dimensional maze they understand perfectly. Some of them are quick; others move with the slowness of creatures that have accepted the wetland's pace and surrendered to it. They seem neither stressed nor calm—merely *present*, occupying the water in the way water itself occupies the space.

The insects are infinite and invisible in their totality, though you can see their effects everywhere. The water-skaters move across the surface with that impossible delicacy, their legs dimpling rather than breaking the tension. Damselflies hover in the dim air—thin-bodied, iridescent when light catches them right, their flight as precise as thought. There are mosquitoes (you know this from experience), but also dragonflies massive enough to cast shadows, their wings beating in patterns that sound like tiny electric crackling. The aquatic ones—nymphs and larvae—are everywhere in the water and mud, transforming through stages you cannot see, becoming. The terrestrial ones coat the plants: beetles whose carapaces gleam like wet paint, true bugs with piercing mouthparts, moths and midges and thousands of organisms for which you have no names. They are not a separate layer of life but a texture applied to everything else—the surface quality of the wetland's skin.

The birds are *brief presences*, sudden and then absent. A heron—blue or green or both—stalks the shallows with an aristocratic patience, its neck folded, its eye focused on movement you cannot perceive. It stands motionless for minutes, then strikes with a violence that startles despite the slowness of everything here. There are smaller waders, sandpipers perhaps, pecking at margins. Ibis move through the deeper water in small groups, their curved bills probing. The calls you hear—sharp and sudden—come from unseen birds, deep in the vegetation, perhaps territorial, perhaps simply existing in a landscape where sound propagates strangely. A kingfisher might flash blue across an opening. Owls certainly call at dusk, their voices deepening the dimness. But mostly the birds are *implied presences*, suggested by sound and disturbance and the occasional flash of wing, as if they haven't fully committed to being here.

The mammals are rare and nocturnal. A raccoon moves through the shallows at night, its clever hands probing the water and mud, creating small disturbances you might notice only as absence—the sudden cessation of frog-song in a particular location. Muskrats create pathways through the densest vegetation, their tunnels visible as dark lines among the stems. Otters move through the water with an efficiency that seems impossible given the resistance; they are sleek and fast and brief. A deer, occasionally, stands at the edge, suspicious, drinking. But these are *transients*—visitors to the wetland rather than inhabitants of it. They belong to the harder world beyond.

The amphibians beyond the frogs are less visible but persistent: salamanders in the leaf-litter and under logs, their moist skins sensitive to the slightest drying. Newts moving through the water with a sinuous slowness. The turtles—soft-shelled and hard-shelled—resting on logs or buried in mud, their eyes watchful but not quite aware. They are creatures of the in-between, as at home in water as on land, though the wetland seems to have convinced them they need not choose.

The vegetation is not separate from the animals but integral to them—a substrate, a shelter, a food source, a medium through which they move. The reeds host insects on their stems, fish shelter in their root-tangles, birds nest among their upper reaches. The floating plants—the algae and duckweed and water lettuce—are food and habitat both. The trees, half-submerged, their roots exposed or drowning, provide perches and hollows. The fallen logs, slowly becoming peat, house millions of invertebrates in their softening wood. The water itself—rich with sediment, with decomposing matter, with nutrients—is both medium and food, thin enough to drink and thick enough to move through.

What lives here has learned to exist in *saturation*—not depleted but *filled*. These creatures thrive not despite the density but because of it. The overlapping calls are not chaos but *communication within abundance*. The invisible movement just beyond sight is not threatening but *confirmation of thriving*. The slowness is not lethargy but *efficiency*—why hurry in a place where resources are everywhere, where the environment sustains itself in a perfect, eternal loop?

They are creatures of *process rather than progress*, of *perpetuation rather than growth*. They are adapted not to change the wetland but to be *fully, completely at home in it*, in its heat and stillness and perpetual becoming. They are the evidence that stillness and silence are not the same—that saturation is a form of aliveness that doesn't need urgency to be real.

They will be here tomorrow, doing the same things, sounding the same sounds, moving through the same unchanging depth. Not in defeat. In *completion*.
