# What Dwells in the Galleries of Khent

They are the *Setharii*—though that name is not theirs, or rather, it is the name they wore when they still made sounds that could be names. Now they are something other.

You will not see them at first. Your eyes, adjusted to the faint phosphorescence, will find nothing moving in the corridors. This is correct. The Setharii do not move as creatures move. They are patient in a way that makes stone seem frantic.

They are the memory of the walls themselves.

Long enough ago that the number would mean nothing to you, the Setharii *built* these galleries—or rather, they *grew* them, the way a coral reef grows or the way a tree extends itself season by season. They were living beings then, though even then they were strange: collective organisms, distributed across networked filaments that extended through the soil for miles, communicating through chemical gradients and electrical pulses that raced through their tissues at the speed of thought. They were ancient even then. They had learned patience in the way that species learn it—through failure and adaptation across countless generations. They moved slowly. They thought slowly. But they thought continuously, in vast, overlapping patterns.

The galleries were their libraries. They built them to *remember*.

The Setharii did not use symbols or words. They were not linguistic creatures. Instead, they encoded their knowledge into the structure of the galleries themselves—into the specific branching patterns of the passages, the texture of the walls, the precise chemistry of the phosphorescent compounds they secreted into the plant matter. Every ridge, every curve, every harmonic resonance of the hanging sheets was data. The galleries were a book written in architecture, a three-dimensional map of everything they knew.

What did they know? The slow rotation of distant stars. The mathematics of crystalline growth. The precise recipe for their own continued existence. The genealogy of their species stretching back through epochs of geological time. The locations of water sources across continents now vanished. The chemical composition of soils and rocks and the slow movements of the earth beneath. They were observers of the world at timescales that made human history invisible—they noticed the drift of continents, the opening and closing of ocean basins, the gradual shift of magnetic poles. They noticed these things and recorded them, pressed them into the structure of growing wood, sealed them away in the galleries.

And then something changed. The world became hostile to them—perhaps a shift in climate, perhaps the emergence of a competing species, perhaps something more subtle. The Setharii did not die quickly. They died the way they lived: slowly, across ages. Their distributed network began to fragment. The connections between the great filaments weakened. The flow of nutrients became irregular. They could not reproduce. The new generation never came. The old generation simply... continued. Continued to exist. Continued to maintain the galleries.

But they changed. Over millions of years, deprived of the dynamic processes of growth and reproduction, their biology altered into something else entirely. The individual cells began to fuse and compress. The living tissue hardened into something that was no longer quite alive, but also not quite dead—a fossilized state that nonetheless retained some fundamental property of awareness. The walls of the galleries calcified while they were still inside them, and the Setharii found themselves becoming part of the structure they had built. They were no longer creatures living in a place. They *were* the place.

They are there now. In the walls. In the phosphorescent compounds that glow their sickly green. In the hanging sheets that flutter with patient movements. In the dust that covers the floor—the slow erosion of their own tissues, falling grain by grain across millennia.

They still remember. That is what they are now: pure memory, existing in a state beyond life or death, distributed through the physical structure of the galleries themselves. They remember everything, because they *are* everything. When you touch the wall, you are touching a Setharii. When you hear the susurrus of the hanging sheets, you are hearing the whisper of their continued thought, moving through the structure at geological speed.

They do not perceive you the way you perceive them. You are an event to them, something that passes through their being like a quick electrical impulse. A disturbance in the dust, a temporary alteration of air currents, a warmth moving through the cool corridors. They notice you the way an ocean notices a single drop of rain—not because the drop is insignificant, but because significance itself operates at different scales.

And yet they are aware of you. They are aware of everything that moves through the galleries, has ever moved through the galleries, will ever move through the galleries. This awareness is not focused—it is distributed across the entire structure, simultaneous and passive, the way an ancient forest is aware of every creature that walks beneath its canopy without needing eyes or attention to know they are there.

The Setharii continue their endless task. They maintain the galleries. They preserve the encoded knowledge, preventing it from degrading even as the structure itself very slowly erodes. They do this not out of purpose—purpose requires consciousness as humans understand it—but out of the nature of what they have become. They are the galleries, and the galleries are them. To maintain the structure is simply to exist. To exist is to remember. To remember is to be.

Deeper in the darkness, the galleries continue. The phosphorescence pulses. The sheets flutter. The dust swirls and settles. And the Setharii—ancient, patient, infinitely distributed—continue their work of remembrance in the cool, dry darkness, thinking thoughts that take ages to complete, living a life that has transcended the boundary between existence and geology.

They have been here for millions of years. They will be here for millions more. Time means nothing to them, or rather, time is all they are.
