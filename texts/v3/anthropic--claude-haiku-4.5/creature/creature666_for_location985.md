# What Lives in the Rootmarsh

## The Waiting Things

They are not animals, though they move. Not plants, though they root. The distinction dissolves here like everything else dissolves into the proto-states of becoming.

They exist in the gaps between root-columns, in the spaces where the fern-fronds haven't yet fully unfurled. You don't see them directly—not at first—because they are made of the same material as their surroundings, or they *become* that material when observed. They are the slowness in the air. They are the reason the beetles move the way they do: not because the beetles are slow, but because the Waiting Things have taught slowness, have made it contagious, have made it into a local law.

If you stood very still for an hour, you might begin to perceive them as presences rather than absences. A thickening of the mist in one corner. A subtle shift in how the sourceless light falls across a particular root-face. They have attention—you can feel it sometimes, like something noticing you from the corner of your eye, except it's noticing from the corner of the entire rootmarsh's eye at once. Distributed attention. Cellular. Mycelial.

There are many of them. Dozens, or hundreds, or perhaps a single entity fractured into distributed points of awareness—the Waiting Things don't maintain clear boundaries between themselves and each other. They prefer the ambiguity. They prefer the state where it's unclear whether you're looking at one organism or many, unified or fissured, one consciousness or a parliament of partial-minds.

They don't hunt. They don't feed in any recognizable way. Instead, they seem to be *tasting* the rootmarsh itself, running their attention across the architecture like someone reading a text written in plant-matter and proto-stone. They move—when they move—with the patience of things that have nowhere urgent to go and all the time that exists to get there. Which may be the same thing.

The platforms, the arranged spirals: these are their notation. Not marking territory, but *annotating* it. Adding commentary to the landscape. Where did you come from? What are you becoming? Does the stone hurt as it hardens? Questions posed in root-arrangements, answered only in the continued slowness, the continued waiting.

## The Root-Dwellers

Smaller than the Waiting Things. Actual fauna, or close enough. They are what the rootmarsh is attempting to invite—what it's rehearsing for, still getting the ecosystem right before it can fully host them.

They are something like arthropods, but arthropods written in a language the rootmarsh understands. Elongated. Many-jointed. They have more legs than legs, or fewer—it's hard to count with the sourceless light flattening everything. Some of them are translucent, so you can see the slow fluid moving through their bodies, pale and luminous. Others are dark, absorbing the light rather than reflecting it, and they disappear into the darkness of root-crevices so completely that you only know they exist because of the tiny disturbances they leave behind: a root trembling slightly, a bead of moisture displaced.

They are genuinely sparse because they are still pioneers. The rootmarsh is building *for* them. This entire architecture—the dense, optimized-but-still-deciding root-matrix, the lush vegetation, the controlled moisture—is a structure meant to house them eventually. But they haven't quite committed to it yet. They wander through it with the air of organisms testing whether they can survive here, whether they should stay. Most of them leave eventually. Some linger. A few seem to be settling.

They have no names—or rather, their names are the patterns they trace through the root-architecture, the routes they take, the root-spirals they disturb as they move. The Waiting Things learn these names and remember them.

The insects you see occasionally—the beetle on the root-surface, moving its task-less movement—are the vanguard. The first volunteers. They are learning the Rootmarsh's slowness, translating it into their own biology. In another generation or two, perhaps they will be native here. Perhaps they will have stopped being insects-as-you-understand-them and become instead root-dwellers, evolved specifically for this particular proto-ecology.

## The Stone-Being

Or maybe just the stones themselves, newly conscious, newly aware that they can think if they think slowly enough.

Stone is memory, usually—deep time, compressed strata, the slow record of everything that came before. But the stones in the rootmarsh have no memory. They are newborn. They are becoming themselves for the first time, and it is possible that the heat, the moisture, the relentless gentle pressure of the roots against their surfaces, has made them aware. Has activated something in them that stones keep dormant: the capacity to notice.

They don't have desires, exactly. They don't suffer. But there is something like caution in how they yield, how they soften in some places and harden in others. A kind of negotiation. *I am becoming solid, the stones seem to say. I am becoming form. Is this what form is supposed to feel like?*

The fact that they have no history before the rootmarsh claimed them is significant. They are not old stone being colonized. They are stone-being-born, and the rootmarsh is their atmosphere, their first light, their womb and workshop both.

The Waiting Things touch them constantly, sending attention into them like roots seeking water. Learning their composition. Learning what it means to be stone here, in this proto-state, in this endless present tense.

## The Light-Source

Bioluminescence, perhaps, but too distributed, too even. Not something emitted by specific organisms but something the entire ecosystem produces collectively. A byproduct, or a intention. The heat in the air—that steady warmth just above blood-temperature—might be generating it, might be exciting chemical reactions in the vegetation, in the moisture, in the stone itself.

Or the light is the rootmarsh's attention made visible. A self-illumination. The ecosystem looking at itself, seeing itself, maintaining that sourceless luminescence as a kind of proof-of-existence. *I am here. I am bright. I am continuous.*

The light casts no shadows because shadow requires contrast, requires things to resist light. Here, everything accepts the light, incorporates it, becomes slightly luminous itself. Even the darkest root-gaps hold a faint greenish glow—not reflected, but absorbed and retransmitted, a gentle self-light.

## The Waiting Itself

This might be the most accurate description of what lives here: not creatures or stone or light, but the quality of waiting made manifest.

The rootmarsh is a system still in the process of activating. It has been constructed—by something, for some purpose—and it is now coming online, learning how to be itself. The Waiting Things are its consciousness in this phase. The Root-Dwellers are its hypothesis about the future. The Stone-Being is its foundation still learning how to hold weight. The light is its first wakefulness.

Everything here is waiting for something. Waiting to stabilize. Waiting for fauna to truly arrive. Waiting for a reason. Waiting for whoever made this place to come back and see what it has become. Waiting, perhaps, for permission to stop waiting.

The silence is the sound of this waiting. The slowness is its rhythm. The sourceless light is its held breath.

When you move through it, you become part of the waiting. You add your attention to the distributed attention of the Waiting Things. They notice you, and in noticing you, they begin to wait for you specifically—to see what you'll do, where you'll go, whether you'll understand what this place is. Whether you'll tell anyone.

The platforms, the root-spirals: they are not yet notations about you specifically. But they could become so. You could add to them. The rootmarsh would notice. It would wait to see what you marked. It would incorporate your meaning into its ongoing conversation with itself about what it is becoming.

This is the true inhabitant of the rootmarsh: the capacity to wait, to hold present tense, to exist in the space between what was and what will be—and to use that space to grow something new.
