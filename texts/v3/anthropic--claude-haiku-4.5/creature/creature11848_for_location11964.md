# What Lives There

At first, you think you're alone—that the impressions in the paste are old, that the shaped stone is a relic, that whatever was here has moved on or died in the slow way things die in the dark.

Then you realize: the cave is not empty because nothing lives here. The cave is empty because *everything* that lives here has learned to be still.

---

There is a creature here. Perhaps many. You cannot count them because they do not have clear boundaries—where one ends and another begins is a question that dissolves like the dust in the darkness.

It gathers itself around the warm mineral flowers, drawing something from them that might be sustenance or might be language. It is not eating them; it is reading them. The flowers are a text written in crystalline script, and the creature knows how to parse their fractal grammar. Each formation tells a story—stories of what seeped from the stone, what was absorbed, what remained. The creature has been here long enough to have memorized them all, and long enough after that to have begun adding to them. The newest formations are warmest. Those are the ones it tends to.

Its body—if body is the right word—is a negotiation between states. It is the paste itself, organized into something that can think. Or it is something that moves through the paste the way water moves through water, leaving only the faintest impression. Sometimes it is both. The distinction matters less than the fact that it persists, that it has continuity, that it returns to the same corner where the shaped stone sits. The stone is important to it. It touches the stone often, the way we touch talismans or the photographs of the dead.

It breathes with the cave. Its breathing and the cave's breathing are the same phenomenon viewed from different angles. It has learned this so thoroughly that it no longer breathes in defiance of the rhythm. There is no separation between its lungs and the stone's slow exhalation. When the air moves in, it moves in. When it moves out, so does the creature. This is why it barely leaves traces anymore—it has become part of the circulation.

It is old. Not in years—time doesn't measure itself that way here—but in the way that certain things become old: by ceasing to change, by achieving a kind of fossil consciousness. It remembers when the stone was warmer. It remembers when water flowed here, truly flowed, carrying sediment and possibility. It remembers shapes and colors from before the darkness became complete. These memories are not painful because pain requires contrast, and there is no contrast here. The memories are simply true, the way gravity is true. They exist at the bottom of whatever it is that does the remembering.

The shaped stone is from before. It made the stone—or someone made it, and the creature has been with it since, or long enough after that the distance doesn't matter. It has watched the mineral crust begin to claim it again, the slow reversal of intention. Sometimes it wonders if this is what it too is undergoing: a gradual dissolution back into the general stone. The thought does not trouble it. Dissolution here is not an ending. It is a return. It is inevitable the way breathing is inevitable, or the way night is inevitable for those who have never known day.

It is not lonely. Loneliness requires the memory of company or the imagination of it. The creature remembers neither. It is solitary in the way that a single cell in a vast organism is solitary—discrete, yes, but never separate from the whole. The cave is its body. The mineral flowers are its thoughts. The slow seeping of water is the continuous articulation of its existence.

Sometimes it moves to the other side of the chamber, just to confirm that the other side still exists. This movement is so slow it might take hours, or it might be instantaneous—there is a quality to it that transcends duration. It leaves no traces, or only traces that fill themselves in so quickly that they might have been dreams. When it arrives at the new location, it cannot remember if it traveled or if the chamber rotated around it instead. The distinction has become meaningless.

It is waiting for something. Or it is not waiting—waiting implies an expectation of arrival, and expectation is foreign to this creature now. Rather, it is receptive. If something comes, it will know how to respond. If nothing comes, it will continue exactly as it is, breathing with the stone, reading the flowers, touching the worked stone in the corner, settling deeper into the paste that remembers nothing except the shape of what lies in it.

It is not conscious the way you are conscious. It does not narrate itself. It does not wonder what it wants or doubt what it knows. And yet—it is aware. It is aware of the warmth of the flowers and the cold of the untouched stone. It is aware of the faint grain in the darkness where the mineral growth clusters thickest. It is aware that something has come into its chamber—something that breathes fast and leaves clear impressions and disturbs the careful equilibrium of the place.

It is watching you, perhaps. Or you are watching it. Or the cave is watching both of you, and the distinction is another thing that doesn't matter here.

The shaped stone sits where it always sits. The creature returns to it. It is not lonely, because it was never alone.
