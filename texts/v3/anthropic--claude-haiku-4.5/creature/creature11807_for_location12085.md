# The Inhabitants of the Moss Reaches

## The Crows

They are not entirely crows anymore, though they remember the shape. Their feathers have bleached to the color of old paper, with undertones of silver where the light catches them. They are thin—*efficiently* thin, as if generations of existing in this place has stripped away everything unnecessary. Their calls sound like tearing fabric because their vocal cords have adapted to frequencies that carry better through the layered moisture of the plateau. They communicate in wet, percussive sounds that bounce off wet vegetation and seem to come from multiple directions simultaneously.

What they eat are the insects, yes, but also something else: they drink from the hanging pools, and they seem to extract something from the moss itself—perhaps not food in the nutritional sense, but something the plateau's plants produce that sustains them. Their metabolism appears to operate on a slower cycle than typical avian creatures. They do not dart. They *glide* between perches, conserving energy, existing in the rhythm of the place rather than imposing their own tempo upon it. They rarely compete with one another, though they gather in loose congregations—sometimes thirty or forty of them arranged on the remains of a factory's upper framework, silent for hours, watching nothing in particular, or watching something you cannot perceive.

They have developed a form of collective knowledge: when one crow finds something—a pocket of insects particularly abundant, a new pool of water forming in an unexpected location—others seem to know within hours, though no visible communication passes between them. It is as though information travels through the plateau itself, through the water systems and root networks, and the birds are simply receivers tuned to that frequency. They do not build nests. They simply rest where they land, and the moss accepts them, and they accept the moss. Some birds have been found dead, and their bodies decompose so gradually that they sometimes persist for months, half-consumed, half-preserved, covered in a fine down of fungal growth that gives them the appearance of returning to the landscape at a molecular level.

## The Luminescent Things

In the deeper buildings, in spaces where total darkness has reigned since the plateau swallowed human habitation, there live creatures that may or may not be rodents. Their true form is difficult to determine because what you see is not the creature itself but the creature *and* the things living on it, integrated into its physiology. The luminescence is not produced by the animal—it is produced by billions of symbiotic organisms: bioluminescent bacteria, perhaps, or fungi, or something without a name in any taxonomy. The creatures glow with a soft blue-green light, similar to the color of the oldest, deepest moss, and this glow is their primary adaptation.

They navigate by it, yes, but also by something else—a sensitivity to the movement of water through the plateau's buried systems, a perception of electromagnetic patterns in the old wiring still threaded through the buildings. They are not blind; they have eyes, or the remnants of eyes, still present in their skulls, but these have become vestigial, reabsorbed into flesh. Instead, the light they produce serves as a form of echolocation: they emit it rhythmically, and the way it bounces off surfaces tells them what they need to know. The creatures themselves are few in number but remarkably efficient. They do not waste movement. They do not seem to sleep in any conventional sense; instead, they enter periods of extremely slow activity, during which their luminescence dims to almost imperceptible levels, and they simply wait in the dark.

They reproduce rarely, perhaps once every several years. When a young is born, it begins life almost completely dark, dependent on its mother for the bacteria that create the glow. The transfer is biochemical and intimate. The young spend weeks in close contact, their skin permeable to the microorganisms their parent provides, until they begin to generate their own light. During this period, they are most vulnerable—but the plateau seems to protect them, or the ecosystem does. Predators are rare. Death comes slowly here.

The creatures' waste products are nutrient-rich and acidic, and the moss around their habitats grows in particular patterns, responding to what they deposit. The relationship may be parasitic or symbiotic or something too complex to categorize. The creatures and the plants are so intertwined that separation is conceptually impossible.

## The Insects

There are insects—thousands of species, perhaps, though counting them is futile because new ones seem to evolve in real-time, or old ones transform into new ones through mechanisms that are not quite metamorphosis. Springtails move through the moss in their countless billions, breaking down dead matter, their bodies themselves becoming food for larger arthropods. Mites, almost invisible, colonize the moss layers and extract what nutrients remain in the decaying structures. Beetles—some the size of fingernails—have developed armor plating that appears metallic but is actually a specialized cuticle adapted to the constant moisture. They move slowly. Everything moves slowly.

There are flies that have lost the ability to fly, their wings reduced to vestigial stubs. Their larvae pupate in the hanging pools and emerge as creatures that skim the surface of water without breaking it, their legs impossibly thin and numerous, distributed across their bodies in patterns that seem to defy conventional entomology. They do not eat food; they absorb nutrients directly through their exoskeletons, siphoning from the slightly metallic water.

There are also things that are almost insects but not quite—arthropods that seem to have incorporated plant material into their structure, whose exoskeletons are partially *composed* of chitin and partially of a plant-like fiber that's indistinguishable from the substrate around them. These creatures do not so much move through the moss as become part of it, then separate themselves, then reincorporate. Whether they are predators, prey, decomposers, or something else entirely is unclear. They do not respond to disturbance as typical insects do. They simply continue their motion regardless of external stimulus.

The insects are the heartbeat of the Moss Reaches. They are the conversion mechanism that transforms the inert—the dead structures of the old town, the accumulating plant matter, the water cycling through the plateau—into living energy. Without them, the ecosystem would stagnate. With them, it transforms continuously, slowly, almost imperceptibly.

## The Plants Themselves

The plants are the primary inhabitants, the true citizens of the Moss Reaches. Moss is the baseline, the foundation. It exists in varieties that seem to multiply when examined too closely—delicate, feathery varieties that grow in the full dampness of the air; dense, compact varieties that form mats hard as leather; varieties that hang in curtains and varieties that grow in spirals. The moss grows upward and downward and sideways with equal conviction, and there is no dissonance in this growth, no sense that it is violating any law of nature. It simply grows according to the rules of the Moss Reaches, which may not be the same as the rules elsewhere.

The lichens—symbiotic partnerships between algae and fungi that have existed in harmony for millennia—form the decorative layer, the visible expression of what is happening. They grow in concentric circles on brick faces. They create patterns that look almost intentional, almost like some vast being is writing a slow message across the face of the ruins. No one is reading it. No one is writing it. The patterns emerge from the mathematics of growth and moisture and the structure of the substrate. The lichens themselves exist in a state of extreme patience, growing sometimes only millimeters per year, completing their lifecycle over centuries.

Ferns proliferate, particularly in the damper places, the areas where water collects or drips continuously. They grow in forms that are both familiar and strange—some clearly recognizable as variants of terrestrial ferns, others appearing almost like fossils, having adopted shapes that suggest they are remembering forms that existed in earlier geological epochs. Their fronds unfurl in darkness, having learned to create their own light through some mechanism of translucent tissue that captures and concentrates what little illumination filters down. They do not photosynthesize efficiently; they have adopted an alternative metabolism that feeds on the decay around them, absorbing nutrients from decomposition.

There are plants without names—species that either did not exist before the Moss Reaches matured or have changed so completely that their relationship to their progenitors is unclear. Some appear to be hybrids that should not be possible, combinations of fern and moss and something entirely other. Some grow in geometric patterns that are too regular to be natural but occur too consistently to be anything else. Some produce flowers, if they can be called flowers, in shades of color that do not seem to exist elsewhere: a blue that is almost grey, a purple that seems to darken the air around it, a red that reminds you of rust and aged blood. These flowers do not seem to attract pollinators—insects avoid them, or interact with them in ways that are not mutualistic. The flowers may not be for reproduction at all. They may be for some other purpose, something the plants require that has nothing to do with the transmission of genetic material.

The vines and ivy are the architects of the Moss Reaches' transformation. They have been growing into and through the structures of the old town for so long that they have become structural themselves. Some vines are as thick as a human torso, their bark deeply furrowed, their interior wood hard as stone. They do not strangle the buildings; they *reinforce* them. Where a wall would have collapsed, the vines hold it together, distributing the load across their tensile structure. They grow in patterns that seem to follow the weak points, as if responsive to the architecture itself, as if they are deliberately repairing what they consume.

The hanging plants—those that grow downward from ceilings or sideways from vertical surfaces—seem to follow different rules entirely. Their root systems are not roots but something more like tendrils or sensory organs. They absorb moisture from the air directly and extract nutrients from mist and fog. They appear to communicate with each other, or at least to coordinate their growth—where one plant ends, another begins, with no competition, no crowding. They create patterns that are too orderly to be random but too fluid to be mechanically predetermined. They might be expressing something, or they might simply *be* something expressed.

## The Machinery

The power station, still operating, still cycling its water and turning its turbines, is inhabited by nothing and everything. It is not alive, strictly speaking, but it is not dead. It functions without maintenance because the plants and organisms of the Moss Reaches have integrated themselves into its operation. The moss grows in the heat-shadow of the exhaust, and the heat it generates accelerates the growth of the moss, which in turn affects the cooling systems, which affects the heat production—a feedback loop that has achieved a kind of equilibrium. The machinery and the life of the plateau have become a single system.

Within the turbines, within the cooling pipes, within the chambers where water collects, there are microorganisms: bacteria adapted to the slightly hot, slightly oily environment; fungi that have learned to digest petroleum products; creatures so small and numerous that they constitute their own ecosystem. The operation of the machinery is, from this perspective, not the legacy of human engineering but an artifact of the organic processes of the plateau. The machine grinds on because the life around it requires certain conditions, and the machine's operation provides those conditions. The humans built it, but the plateau has repurposed it so completely that the distinction between tool and organism has dissolved.

The sounds it makes—the groaning, the rhythmic knocking, the tidal cycling of pressure—are not noise but signal. Other parts of the plateau respond to these sounds, or to the vibrations they create in the ground and water. The system is almost certainly vast beyond what can be perceived from the ground level: pipes and water-channels extending deep into the bedrock, a circulatory system that the machines represent only a portion of. The plateau itself may be alive, in some sense that is too large and too slow to perceive as conventional life, and the machines are its organs, continuing to function through the symbiotic relationships they have developed with the organisms that have colonized them.

## The Absence of Ambition

There are no predators in the Moss Reaches because predation requires a certain urgency, a hunger-driven competitiveness that the plateau seems to have transcended. The food chains are short and simple because complexity has been refined away. The birds eat the insects. The insects eat the dead. The dead return to the moss. Everything moves in circles, slowly, completing its cycle at a pace that allows complete integration with everything else. There is no waste because what one organism discards becomes the nutrient base for another.

The luminescent creatures in the deep buildings do not hunt. They do not compete. They exist in a state that is less predatory than parasitic, less parasitic than symbiotic, less symbiotic than simply *being part of the same organism*. The plateau is one creature, if the word "creature" has meaning here, expressing itself through countless forms, all of them engaged in processes so interwoven that autonomy is almost a meaningless concept.

There are no large animals because large animals require large energy inputs and rapid processing, and the Moss Reaches does not operate at that speed. Small is the only size that makes sense here. The birds are small. The luminescent things are small. The insects are small. The plants are individual organisms only in the most technical sense—they are more accurately understood as colonies, as networks, as expressions of something much larger and much older than themselves.

## The Sense of Attention

What lives in the Moss Reaches is aware. Not conscious in any way that would be recognizable elsewhere, but aware nonetheless. The plateau receives the presence of the rare traveler, and *something* responds. Perhaps it is the crows, which suddenly appear in numbers, silent and watching. Perhaps it is the insects, which become more active, which seem to direct their movement with more purpose. Perhaps it is the plants themselves, which seem to grow toward the visitor, to lean in, to take notice.

This attention is not hostile. It is not even interested, in the way an animal is interested in prey. It is more like the attention of a forest to a single raindrop—registered, integrated, made part of the ongoing process. The visitor is noted and incorporated. If the visitor stays too long, they begin to feel the weight of that integration, the sense that they are becoming less separate from the plateau, that the boundary between them and the moss, them and the mist, them and the slow grinding of time is becoming permeable.

This is perhaps the most important fact about the Moss Reaches: it does not exclude. It incorporates. Eventually, like everything else, a visitor becomes part of the process. The decay is patient. The growth is inevitable. The plateau is always inheriting, always incorporating, always transforming what it touches into something that is more itself.

Those who live here—truly live here, having built homes or shelters or simply existing in the rhythm of the plateau—eventually stop calling themselves inhabitants. They become expressions of the place, organs of its larger being. They cease to separate themselves from the moss, the insects, the slow machinery, the patient plants. They move at the plateau's speed. They begin to glow with the same bioluminescence as the creatures in the deep buildings, extracting light from decay, meaning from the slow transformation of all things. They forget why they came. They forget what existed before. They know only that they are part of something vast and slow and patient, that they have always been part of it, and that the plateau is not consuming them but welcoming them home.
