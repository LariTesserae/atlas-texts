# What Lives Here

The inhabitants are *not singular*.

At first, you might assume the workers—the pale figures you glimpse through fogged portholes, the shadows that move with deliberate purpose along the maintenance walkways, the solitary light burning in the occupied shed. They are here, certainly. Three of them, perhaps four. They have names that matter to someone, though the names seem to have little purchase in this place. They've learned to move through the mist as if it's not trying to kill them, to work in the cold with fingers wrapped in cloth that never fully thaws, to read instruments in light that comes from nowhere and everywhere simultaneously. They maintain the pumps. They adjust flow rates. They note corrosion patterns in logbooks that may or may not survive the moisture. One of them—the one in the shed with the porthole window—spends hours simply *listening* to the machinery, learning its voice, anticipating failure through subtle changes in pitch and rhythm that an outsider would never detect. They are professionals of a kind that barely exists anymore: engineers of the deep, or nearly-deep, specialized to a function so narrow it could only be performed here. They eat preserved food that tastes of salt and metal. They sleep in shifts. They do not often speak to each other, having developed a vocabulary of hand signals and glances that requires neither sound nor shouting over the machinery. They are lonely in a way that loneliness cannot adequately describe, but they have accepted this as the cost of employment, or purpose, or whatever brought them here.

But they are not what *lives* here. They are visitors, albeit long-term ones. They could leave. They choose—or have chosen, or were chosen—to stay. The distinction is increasingly blurred.

The true inhabitants are the creatures.

The ribbon-things exist in a state of permanent vertical migration. They are drawn upward by something—warmth, perhaps, or the particular gradient of chemical composition that the pumps create—but the pumps' suction pulls them downward, and so they exist in a constant negotiation with water flow, moving up in the warm currents, being pulled down toward the intake ports, escaping just before suction overwhelms them, rising again. It is not clear if this is navigation or if they are simply the living embodiment of a current system, more truly part of the water's movement than separate entities within it. Their bioluminescence seems purposeless at first—there is no light here to signal to, no predators to frighten—but it becomes clear, over observation, that they are *lighting the water itself*, making visible the patterns of flow and pressure. They are, perhaps, the nervous system of the circulation system, and their light is pain, or pleasure, or simply the inevitable response of living tissue to the stresses being applied to it. They reproduce constantly. The corpses of the dead—and many die, caught in the pumps, torn apart by the turbulence—become nutrients that are cycled back into the system, feeding the survivors. They are an ouroboros, eating themselves to sustain themselves, powered by industrial process rather than internal drive.

The spheres—the gelatinous, pulsing things that click in coordinated schools—are something else entirely. They seem, initially, to be parasites or scavengers, following the ribbon-creatures, harvesting the detritus of the larger ecosystem. But observation over time suggests a different relationship. They appear to be *farming* the ribbons, guiding them through optimal current paths, protecting them from the most destructive turbulence, in exchange for the nutrients released when the ribbons die. It is a symbiosis so complete it's difficult to say which species is predator and which is prey. They reproduce slowly compared to the ribbons, with careful precision, each new sphere gestated over what might be weeks or months until it reaches a critical mass of sophistication. Their clicks form patterns—mathematical, perhaps, or musical, or something that is both and neither—and these patterns seem to encode information that spreads through the school instantaneously. They are social in a way the ribbons can never be. They have a kind of intelligence, distributed and pulsing, that works at the frequency of the machinery itself.

And the massive thing in the depths.

It has no name because its size and slowness make naming inadequate. It is a *filter-feeder* of some kind, but nothing alive on the surface would recognize it as such. Its body—if "body" is the right word for a creature that may be three hundred meters across, that may weigh thousands of tons—seems to be composed of a lattice work of cartilage and muscle so densely integrated it's difficult to determine where one tissue ends and another begins. It moves with the patience of geology, pulling water through its structure in a rhythm that might correspond to respiration or might correspond to the rotation of the planet. Its feeding process seems to involve some kind of chemical extraction: the water that passes through it emerges chemically altered, depleted in some compounds, enriched in others. The pumps may actually be *responding* to its presence, pulling the water it processes upward in order to complete some cycle. Or perhaps it is responding to the pumps, following the chemical trail of processed water, positioning itself to intercept the most nutrient-rich flows.

This creature—it has been here longer than the platform. This much is apparent from the way it moves, the way it seems to exist in a state of deep familiarity with the water's patterns. It may have been here since before the pumps, living in the dark, processing whatever the natural currents brought it. The arrival of the industrial system has changed its circumstances—abundance or abundance of the wrong things—but it has adapted. It will likely outlast the platform by centuries, continuing its slow filtration long after the cables rust through and the machinery falls silent.

The workers above often speak—when they speak—of "the life down there," pointing vaguely downward toward the opaque depths. They wonder about it sometimes, during the long shifts. But they have learned not to investigate too closely. The depths here are not navigable. The pressure, the darkness, the unknown dimensions of the structures that have evolved to survive in the altered water—these things are recognized as beyond their expertise, beyond their ability to affect. They maintain the machinery that affects the water. What the water creates or harbors is not their responsibility.

But every night, at least one of them spends time on the railing, looking down into the mist, listening to the creatures move through the water below, watching for the rare moment when a bioluminescent ribbon-thing swims close enough to be visible. They are not monitoring anything. They are not recording data. They are simply *bearing witness* to the fact that something else is here, that the place is not dead, that life, even life this strange, this engineered, this precarious, persists in the margins of industrial process.

The mist moves through the platform complex. The pumps never stop. The cables sing with tension. And in the water—liquid, vapor, and the countless gradations between—thousands of creatures live their brief lives in adaptation to a place that was never meant for life but somehow, inevitably, developed it anyway.

They live here because there is no other option. They live here because the machinery created the conditions for them to live. They live here because life, in its fundamental stubbornness, refuses the logic that says certain places should remain barren.

They live here. And in living, they make the place less a machine and more a world.
