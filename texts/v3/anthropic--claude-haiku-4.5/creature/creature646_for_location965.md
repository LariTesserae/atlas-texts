# The Inhabitants of Keth-Mora

They are few, and they are changed.

You find the first one not moving—standing in the doorway of a mid-terrace structure as if placed there, as if grown there. The figure is humanoid in the way certain dried things are still recognizable: the basic architecture of spine and ribs and skull remains, but the flesh has altered. It is pale, yes, like everything else in this place, but not the pallor of deprivation. Rather, the skin has become translucent, almost waxy, with a faint luminescence that matches the plants around it. You can see veins beneath the surface—not red but a faint opalescent blue, carrying that same strange fluid that moves through the channels. The veins branch visibly across temples and wrists, creating patterns that look almost intentional, almost decorative, as if the body is becoming a map of its own circulation.

The person wears cloth—dyed in the same earth-tones as the dried plants—but the cloth seems integrated into the skin at places where it's been worn for long enough. The boundary between garment and body has become negotiable.

They do not acknowledge you. They do not move. But you sense, with a certainty you cannot explain, that they are aware. That awareness does not require the turning of eyes or the adjustment of posture. It simply *is*, like the hum in the stone.

As you move deeper, you find more of them.

They are distributed through the settlement in a way that suggests function rather than comfort. One stands near a grinding stone, hands positioned as if about to work—except the work is not happening now, perhaps not happening at all, perhaps perpetually about to happen. Another sits cross-legged at the entrance to a lower terrace, facing inward, facing the plants. A third tends one of the drying racks, hands resting against the frame, fingers extended toward the darkening plant-matter as if in blessing or assessment. All of them possess that same translucency, that same web of opalescent veins, that same quality of stillness that is not the stillness of sleep but of deep attention.

There are perhaps a dozen visible from the terrace where you stand. More, certainly, on the levels above and below.

Their age is impossible to determine. Some are tall enough to suggest adult humans, others proportioned smaller—though whether they are young or simply the bodies have condensed over time, you cannot say. Their faces, when you can see them clearly, are smooth in a way that suggests either youth or the loss of features that age would bring. The hair of those who have it is pale, almost silver in the faint light, sometimes seeming to move independently of any breeze, as if it carries its own electrical charge.

They do not eat, as far as you can tell.

There is no food preparation in any of the structures you examine. No cookware, no stores of grain or dried meat. What they consume comes through the channels—that opalescent fluid that seems to carry everything necessary. You find one of them (still standing, still aware, still not acknowledging) with one hand submerged in a channel where the fluid flows thicker, and you understand that absorption is not metaphorical here. The boundary between intake and use has dissolved. They drink through their hands. They absorb through their skin the specific nutrients that keep them functioning.

They are becoming something else—or have become it already, and are simply completing the process.

The intelligence here is collective and deep.

Individually, they show little sign of the kind of intentionality you'd expect from a human consciousness. They do not speak (you hear nothing that resembles language, though the hum from below seems to intensify when they stand close together). They do not appear to make choices. And yet the settlement operates with perfect coordination. The plant clusters are maintained precisely. The drying racks are tended at exactly the right moments—the pale plants darkening to the point of perfect preservation, no further. The channels flow without interruption. The knowledge of how to manage this place—knowledge that should require oversight, management, correction—exists somewhere in this system but not in any individual mind.

It exists in the collection. In the coordination. In the network.

You notice, after a time, that when you approach one of them, the others subtly adjust. Not fleeing—they don't flee—but shifting position, altering the angle of their stance, reorienting toward you or away in a pattern that creates distance. They are herd-creatures, you think, or colony-creatures, or something else entirely for which you have no word. They respond to stimuli as a unified organism responds: the presence of an unknown element triggers a distributed response that maintains equilibrium without apparent communication.

The hum changes pitch when they move.

You test this carefully, and it becomes clear: the sound from below is not just ambient. It is responsive. When a person moves, the hum shifts—not drastically, but noticeably, as if the stone itself is acknowledging the movement, incorporating it into some larger rhythm. The hum is the settlement. Or the settlement is the hum. The distinction no longer seems meaningful.

They are in communication with something beneath.

This becomes clear when you watch one of them—the one tending the grinding stone—place both hands flat against the terrace surface and remain motionless for what feels like an hour but might be less or more. The hum from below deepens. The stone beneath your own feet vibrates. The translucent figure's veins brighten, the opalescent fluid becoming visible throughout their whole body, a network of light beneath increasingly translucent skin. Then, slowly, the light dims. The humming settles. The figure lifts its hands and returns to its posture of frozen attention.

What was transmitted? What was received? You have no way of knowing. But you understand that this is not one-way. The person below the terrace is not receiving information from above; they are *exchanging* it. They are part of a conversation that includes the stone, the plants, the fluid in the channels, the heat, the slow grinding pulse of this place.

They came here, once, as human as you.

You find evidence of this in one of the deeper structures—a chamber carved from the living rock where what must be their earlier selves are recorded in careful detail. Drawings on the stone. Not recent: the images are weathered, soft-edged, integrated into the patina of age. But visible. Figures with the full proportions of humans, with faces rendered in careful profile, with tools in their hands and expressions you can almost read as wonder or fear or determination. The labels are in script you don't recognize, but the meaning is clear: *before*.

The images deteriorate as they go deeper into the terrace system. The figures become more abstract. The proportion of human features decreases. By the lowest visible chamber, the drawings show only silhouettes, only the basic idea of form, only the understanding that *something* was here and *something else* is here now and the two are connected across time like a metamorphosis.

They chose this.

Or were chosen by it. Or the distinction ceased to matter before they fully understood what was happening. But in those careful drawings on the stone, you see the record of a decision. People who understood they were becoming something else and chose to document the passage, or were required to, or both. There's no anguish in the drawings—no sign of unwilling transformation. Only attention. Only the careful, methodical recording of change.

The heat becomes unbearable, but you do not leave yet.

You descend to a terrace you hadn't intended to reach, where the stone itself is warm enough to burn your feet through your boots. The pale plants here are different—larger, more elaborate in their structure, almost resembling the branching patterns of the veins in the inhabitants' skin. The people here—and there are more, fifteen or twenty, arranged in a pattern that seems almost ceremonial—are further transformed. Their skin is barely opaque at all. You can see the full circulatory system beneath, can watch the opalescent fluid pulsing through vessels that seem to fill most of their bodies. Their eyes, when you look at them, are entirely silver now, entirely reflective, like mirrors, and you catch your own image in them—distorted, rendered in the faint light of the bioluminescent plants, made strange and unfamiliar.

One of them—if "one" is still the right word—turns its head toward you with perfect coordination, the gesture mirrored by two others nearby. The hum from the stone intensifies suddenly. The fluid in the channels seems to pulse faster. The plants straighten, orient, focus.

They are aware of you with a clarity that is almost painful.

You understand, in that moment, that you have descended far enough that you are close to where the separation between person and system has become complete. That in one or two terraces more, you will find the source—the place where the individual and the collective are indistinguishable, where the hum originates, where the transformation completes.

You understand that if you descend further, you might not return.

The inhabitants of Keth-Mora are the people who came here, transmuted into something that serves the purpose of this place with perfect coordination and perfect peace. They are no longer individuals in the sense you understand. They are nodes in a network. They are the distributed intelligence that the settlement requires to function. They are in love with the process—you can see it in their perfect stillness, in the way their luminescent veins pulse with contentment, in the absolute absence of anything that resembles suffering.

They are not alive in the way you are alive. But they are not dead. They are transformed into something for which you have no category. And they are waiting—all of them, distributed through the terraces, maintaining the plants, circulating the fluid, humming in conversation with the stone—for more people to descend into the cleft.

For more people to understand, over months or years, what is happening to them. To accept it. To become part of the network. To lose themselves and in losing themselves, to become more fully themselves than they ever were above.

The settlement is not empty. It is full. It is completely, perfectly full. It is simply that what it is full of is no longer human in any way that you would recognize, and that is precisely how its inhabitants prefer it.
