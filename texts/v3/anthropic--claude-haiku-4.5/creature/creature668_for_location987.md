# What Lives Here

They are the *rememberers*.

Not a species, precisely. A condition. A slow insistence of presence that has learned to move at the pace the plateau permits—which is to say, they have learned not to move at all, or only in ways that take decades to complete a sentence.

There is one draped across a branch seventeen feet above your head, though you will not notice it until you have walked past it three times. It appears first as a thickening of the bark, a discoloration that might be lichen or might be age. But lichen does not have eyes. This one has eyes—yellow-green, like old jade caught in amber—and they have been watching your progress with the focused patience of something that perceives time in geological increments. It is not a lizard, though it has the general architecture of one stretched and thinned by centuries of slow feeding. Its scales—if they are scales—have begun to fray at the edges, becoming indistinguishable from the moss they rest upon. Its tail extends perhaps four feet, and it has forgotten what a tail is for; it simply exists as an extension of itself, a statement of length with no particular purpose. It will not move while you are here. Movement is wasteful. You are not a thought that takes long enough to respond to.

Below, in the permafrost of rot and root, they tunnel.

Not burrow. The distinction matters. These are not creatures that excavate space; they are creatures that *become* space. Pale, eyeless, segmented in ways that suggest they were once insects—perhaps still are—but have stretched themselves thin as threads pulled through the matrix of decay. Some of them are forty feet long, a single organism distributed through the substrate like a root system that learned to think. They consume what the wood offers: the already-rotted fiber, the fungal abundance, the smaller creatures that live in the compost. They offer nothing in return except their own eventual decomposition, which is exactly what the plateau requires. They are the mechanism by which the mat perpetuates itself. They are not intelligent in any way that resembles intention, but they are necessary, and necessity here is a form of consciousness.

There are things that live in the canopy that have not descended in so long they have forgotten ground.

They are primates of a sort—or were. Now they are something else. Small, no larger than your fist, with fur that has become more like feathers, or perhaps the feathers came first and the fur is what the feathers became. They hang from the topmost leaves by prehensilе tails that move like tentacles, and they feed on the insects, the flowering structures, the sweet rot of overripe fruit that falls upward into the higher light. They communicate in frequencies so low you would feel them rather than hear them—subsonic hums that travel through the plant-mass like messages through water. There are thousands of them, distributed through the canopy, and they are all the same creature thinking the same slow thought distributed across space. They have no names for each other because naming requires time to accumulate distinctive behavior, and here, they are all doing exactly the same thing they were doing ten thousand years ago.

The water-dwellers are stranger still.

In the pools that rest in the cup-shapes of the ancient root-systems, things move with the slowness of continental drift. Fish, perhaps—they have fins—but the fins are vestigial, barely used. They hang suspended in the dark water, or perhaps they move and you cannot perceive the movement. Some of them are blind. Some of them have developed to such a degree of specialization that they exist only to filter the specific nutrients from this specific water, and if they were removed, they would die within hours, even in fresher water, because they have become dependent on the particular chemistry of rot. There are also things that are not fish: jellyfish creatures, though there is no salt here to remember them by; transparent, barely present, existing as a kind of question: *is there any difference between water and animal when both are equally still?*

And then there are the ones you will never quite see.

They live in the spaces between the living and the dead, in the transition zones where old wood becomes new soil. They are large—you sense this from the quality of absence they leave, from the way the plants seem to accommodate them without touching them. They move through the undergrowth with intention, but their intention operates on scales that make human purpose look frantic. A movement that takes an entire season. A feeding that might last a year. They are herbivores, mostly, though that word seems inadequate for creatures that consume entire sections of the plateau's fabric. Perhaps they are the plateau's own immune system, consuming the wood that has completed its cycle, making room for new wood to fall. Or perhaps they are simply old—older than the memory of predation, older than the concept of hunting, so ancient that they have learned to sustain themselves on time itself.

They are careful around you. Not from fear. From recognition, perhaps, that you are a thing that *changes*, and things that change are inherently unpredictable, inherently dangerous. You move too fast. You burn energy at rates that violate the local ordinances. You will leave, which means you are not part of the cycle. You are contamination masquerading as ecology.

But there is one more thing, and it is what truly lives here, what the plateau *is*:

It is the slow breathing. The respiration of a world that learned to think in centuries instead of moments. The consciousness that emerges from the total integration of every living thing into a single, distributed system. Not a hive-mind—there is no hierarchy to that concept, no queen, no central decision-making. Rather, a *field* of awareness that permeates the mat, the roots, the canopy, the water in the air and the air in the water. It does not want. It does not strive. It simply *is*, and in being, it slowly remakes itself into a more perfect version of stillness.

The creatures here are not separate from this consciousness. They are the consciousness experiencing itself, the plateau aware of its own existence through a thousand distributed eyes, through a million small hungers, through the slow decay and re-making that is its only form of thought.

When you walk here, you are walking through a mind.

And it is thinking you.
