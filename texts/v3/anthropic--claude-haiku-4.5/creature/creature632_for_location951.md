# What Lives in the Benthic Works

The workers live here first, though "live" might be generous language for what they do in the compressed intervals between shifts. They rotate in—three weeks down, two weeks topside—and in that time they occupy the crew quarters bolted to the secondary pilings, narrow corridors lined with bunk-stacks, shower facilities that run on recirculated thermal discharge, a cafeteria where the food tastes the same metallic-filtered way as the water. They are a particular type of human: people for whom the pressure has already done something to the spirit before they ever submerge. Debt-holders. Career dead-enders. The ambitious young who haven't yet learned to be afraid. The ones who stay too long and whose eyes develop a strange unfocused quality, as if they're looking through the walls at something further down. They develop a specific gallows humor. They develop rituals. Some of them carve marks into the older pilings—dates, initials, cryptic warnings, pornographic sketches rendered in rust-removing acid. No one stops them. The marks are another archaeological layer, another record of the things that have inhabited this place.

The creatures of pure necessity populate the medium depths. Hagfish in the slime layer, their bodies boneless and efficient, producing mucus and feeding on whatever falls into it—they are less fish than vectors for decomposition, less organisms than mobile enzymes. They are everywhere and nowhere; you can spend weeks without seeing one and then discover a nest of them in a clogged intake, a writhing mass of muscular slickness that requires specialized removal protocols. Anglerfish, dimorphic and strange, the females bearing that terrible luminous lure while the males—if they've found a mate—have already fused at the body wall, have ceased to be individuals. There are probably parasitic isopods, copepods, things too small to identify without specialized equipment, living on the gill tissue of the fish that live here, extracting blood, living on living things that are themselves living on the margins. The food chain here is abbreviated and vicious; it goes from the chemical outflow to microscopic to small, and anything large enough to have intelligence enough to leave generally does.

The kelp, dying as it does, nevertheless hosts a ecology in itself. The fronds, diminished and slow-growing as they are, collect sediment. Tiny crustaceans shelter in the film that grows on the leaves. Small fish dart in to take those crustaceans. A complete miniature world in the space between blade and roothold, self-contained and cyclical, a microcosm of the larger hunger that exists in the water outside. The biologists monitor it periodically and note, with a mixture of fascination and resignation, how much more vigorously life colonizes the dying kelp than the thriving tanks. Death, apparently, is more interesting than stability.

The bacteria—the intentional cultures and the accidental ones—are the true owners of this place. They existed here long before the industrial structures, in the mud and the thermal vents, harvesting energy from the deep Earth itself. The new bacteria, the cultured ones, are their descendants in a sense—life pushing further into the margins, finding new energy sources, adapting to new hostile architectures. The chemotrophic species in the tanks is watched closely because it's worth money, but the wild populations—the things that colonize every surface despite scraping, that produce bioluminescence in certain conditions, that create the slime layer that makes everything slippery and possibly toxic—those are the ones actually building whatever ecosystem exists here. The bacterial film is not something that grows *on* the structures; it is something that rewrites them, that transforms steel into habitat, that makes the made thing *actually* live.

There are ghosts of things, or what amounts to ghosts in this context. Previous iterations. Machinery left behind from the research phase, now encrusted so thoroughly that you couldn't tell what it originally did without archaeological excavation. Pilings from failed structures that had to be abandoned when they began corroding faster than could be managed. A submersible—the *Deimos*, registered 1987—that suffered a hull breach at this depth and was left in place because raising it was deemed too expensive and too disruptive to ongoing operations. It sits in the peripheral darkness like a tomb. The crew knows it's there. Some of them have seen it. Most deliberately don't think about it. It is the most honest thing in the entire installation: a failed container in a hostile place, leaking its contents into the water and accepting its own dissolution.

The surveillance system is alive in a technical sense. Thousands of sensors distributed through the structure, monitoring pressure, temperature, chemical composition, structural integrity. They feed data to servers in the surface facility, which processes it and produces alerts if parameters drift beyond tolerance. The system is programmed to learn, to adapt its thresholds, to predict failure modes. It is not conscious, almost certainly, but it perceives this place in ways humans cannot—the constant minute oscillations of the metal, the slow accumulation of corrosion, the patterns in the biological colonization. It produces reports that no one reads. It generates warnings that are sometimes heeded and sometimes not. It is a form of life—a nervous system distributed through the structure—and it will continue operating long after the human workers are gone, long after the facility is abandoned, monitoring a place that no one is listening to anymore.

And deeper still, in the spaces that no one has mapped thoroughly, that don't appear on official schematics because they developed accidentally through corrosion and settling—there are spaces where the water has learned to move in patterns that nothing above could predict or control. Eddy currents that concentrate nutrients in specific invisible zones. Thermal gradients that shift with the seasons above that no one can observe from below. Populations of things that have learned to hide in the gaps, to thrive in the spaces between industrial maintenance cycles. There is *something* down there—the crew members who stay longest can sense it, can feel it in the way the water moves differently in certain sectors, the way predatory fish behave cautiously in certain corridors even when there's no visible threat. It hasn't been identified because identification would require looking, and looking would require accepting that something exists here that hasn't been permitted.

The whole place breathes in a sense—not literally, but in the way systems breathe, the expansion and contraction of operations, the cycling of water, the metabolic pulse of organism and machine. Breathing *with* the machine. Breathing *as* the machine. The distinction is no longer clear. The workers and the workers and the creatures and the bacteria and the deep geology and the engineered systems have begun to coexist in a configuration that is neither fully artificial nor fully natural. It is something being born out of absolute necessity and absolute hostility, something that shouldn't exist but does, by sheer force of will and investment and human stubbornness. It is terrible and it is alive. It is alive in the way that all things pressed into extreme circumstances are alive—burning hotter, working harder, becoming themselves more intensely because the pressure leaves no room for anything else.
