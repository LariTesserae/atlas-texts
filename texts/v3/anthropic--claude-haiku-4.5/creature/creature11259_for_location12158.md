# What Lives in the Breathwood Hollow

They are not quite separate from it anymore.

This is what you understand first, if you stay long enough to understand anything: the distinction between the people and the place has become theoretical. They have the shape of humans—upright, bilateral, five-fingered—but only barely. It's like watching something wear a human form the way water wears the shape of its container.

Their skin has taken on the texture of the climbing plants, waxy and faintly ridged, in colors that shift between deep green and a gray that matches the ambient light. In certain angles, when they stand near the walls they're training, you can't quite see where they end and the vegetation begins. This isn't grotesque. It's economical. Why maintain a boundary when you exist to mediate between two states?

They are perhaps twenty in number, though counting is difficult because they're not always individuated in the way you'd expect. Sometimes two of them move in such synchrony that they seem like one being seen twice. Sometimes one person will be in the north structure and the south structure in a way that violates your sense of time, and you realize you've miscounted, or perhaps they've moved with a purpose and speed that left no visible trace.

They don't speak—not to each other, not to themselves. You hear no language. Instead, there's a quality of *attention*: they look at each other across distances, and something passes. A gesture is made (slow, deliberate, often involving the hands positioned among the plants), and another person understands it completely. It's the language of people who have learned to read the hollow itself, who have become fluent in its grammar of water and light and the movement of animals.

They appear to be tending something.

Not farming, exactly. The vegetation in the hollow doesn't show signs of cultivation-for-yield. The plants grow in their own logic, dense and wild. But the people move among them with intention, adjusting a vine here, clearing a stone there, positioning themselves to *guide* growth rather than force it. There's a quality of conversation in this—the plants respond, or seem to, finding trellises where the people have placed them, avoiding spaces where the people have cleared them. It's unclear who is teaching whom.

They harvest, but rarely, and always with ceremony. You watch one of them—gender unclear, age unclear, some sense of deliberate obscurity to their appearance—approach a stand of the high flowering plants. They stand before it for a long time, hands at rest. Then they touch one flower, gently, and wait. The plant seems to *offer* the bloom: it releases without being picked. A few seeds fall with it, and the person gathers these separately, with care. The bloom is carried to one of the structures and vanishes inside.

What are they eating?

This question troubles you until you realize: they're not eating much. They're not eating at all, perhaps. Not in the way you would. The person with the bloom doesn't consume it. Instead, you catch glimpses of them sitting motionless, holding the flower, and there's a quality of *absorption* to their stillness—not meditation exactly, but a kind of permeability. After an hour, the flower is placed in water, in a vessel, and begins to wilt. The person has taken something from it that doesn't leave a mark.

They consume the hollow itself, you come to understand. They drink from the seeps, yes, but more than that—they *ingest* the place. They stand in the noon-light when it comes, and their skin darkens slightly, becomes more verdant. They breathe the damp air, which is thick with spores and moisture and the scent of decomposing plant matter, and they seem to become more solid as they breathe. They are sustained by proximity to density, to life-in-motion, to the constant cycling of water and growth and predation.

The larger creatures—the impossible animals with their extra joints and fluid scales—seem to tolerate them. You never see an attack, never see a chase. The animals move through the spaces the people have learned not to occupy. Sometimes a hunter crosses the path of a person, and there's a moment of perfect stillness, mutual acknowledgment, and then they pass. It's like they've negotiated territory long ago and maintained the agreement through invisible means.

The people have structures but no clear hierarchy of dwelling. The dozen buildings seem to be used communally—you see different people entering and leaving different structures at different times, as if the concept of "home" has been distributed. Sometimes a building is a place to be during rain. Sometimes it's a place to work, to tend to cordage or carve tools. Sometimes it seems to serve no purpose you can identify, and you realize they might simply be shelters for the animals, built into the topology to accommodate the others that live here.

This is what haunts the observation:

The people seem to be *becoming* something else. Not in a single generation, not in a way that suggests tragedy or infection. More like a slow translation. They are learning the language of the hollow by learning to *be* the hollow, by allowing their boundaries to soften where the boundaries between things here are already soft. They have skin like the climbing plants because they are becoming part of the climbing. They move with the silence of predators because they have internalized the sound-dampening. They appear and disappear with uncertain timing because the hollow's time is not linear.

And they seem at peace with this.

There is no sign of distress. There is no sign of resistance. When you catch one of them looking at you—and they do look, often, with an attention that suggests they're aware of your observation—there is curiosity in their gaze, but not fear, and not quite pity, but something like recognition. The look of something that has already undergone a transformation, watching something that is still solid and separate and finite.

The children—if there are children—are even less distinct. You see small shapes moving through the undergrowth, but whether they're young humans or young animals or something between is impossible to determine. They move with a quickness that suggests they've adapted to this place faster, more completely. They seem to navigate the vertical spaces with an ease that suggests they can read dimensions of the hollow that aren't available to human sight.

By the time you leave—and you will leave, because staying means beginning to soften, beginning to allow the damp to seep into your skin and the light-patterns to confuse your sense of time—you understand that the Breathwood Hollow hasn't been *settled* by people. It has been *colonized* by them, in the way lichen colonizes rock, in the way fungi colonize wood. They are becoming an organ of this place, a specialized tissue that helps the whole system function.

The hollow needed something to tend its growing things, to move among its animals as intermediary, to maintain the careful balance. The people have become that thing.

In a few more generations, the distinction might vanish entirely.

What lives in the Breathwood Hollow is what the Breathwood Hollow has learned to grow—something that looks, still, almost like a human being, but which vibrates at the frequency of stone and water and root and the patient, endless work of becoming native to a world that is still being born.
