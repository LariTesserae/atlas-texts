# What Lives Here

There is no single inhabitant of the Hollows. This is important to understand.

What lives here is **patience itself**, though patience needs a form and has found several.

The first is microscopic, invisible except in aggregate—the fungi that constitute the true architecture of this place. They exist primarily as mycelial networks threading through the fern beds and the rotting wood of the bench, the vast underground web that most people mistake for inert decomposition but which is actually the most active metabolism in the cavern. The fungi are the transformer, the translator. They break down what falls and turns it into what can rise again. They are the reason the ferns have persisted without sunlight. They trade minerals extracted from the stone itself for the sugars the ferns produce through their pale, photosynthesis-adjacent chemistry—a symbiosis so old it predates the cavern's discovery by any human hand. The fungi don't think. They don't need to. They are process made visible, the slow philosophy of *return*.

The second is botanical and more patient still. The ferns, particularly, seem designed for this specific cold and darkness as if the Hollows were their true home and everywhere else an exile. They grow in the precise configurations that maximize the moisture they breathe in through their fronds. They've learned—if learning is even the right word—to operate on the mineral feast the fungi provide. They ask nothing of the sun. They are static, rooted, and utterly committed to their immobility in a way that suggests they've made peace with stillness that would drive other plants mad. There is something monastic about them. Something that has renounced the upper world entirely.

The third is human, but not present. Not anymore. Not consistently.

This inhabitant—let's call them *Tender*, since that's what the evidence suggests they were—no longer lives in the Hollows in any conventional sense. But they *inhabited* it. The grooves in the stone are their footprints, worn into permanence through repetition. The bench is their seat. The laminated notes are their voice, now preserved beyond the reach of water damage, speaking across decades to whoever comes after. The bioluminescent panel is their gift—a device installed with intention, hung in its careful cage, left burning through the years to illuminate what would otherwise remain in absolute dark.

Tender was not a researcher, though they documented like one. The care is wrong for that. A researcher would have samples, equipment, a systematic approach. Instead, there is a *vigil*. The bench positioned to receive the light. The decades of return—because those grooves didn't form from a single visit or even a season of visits. They formed from years. Possibly decades. Possibly a lifetime.

Tender came here to witness. To sit in the presence of processes that operate at a scale human consciousness can barely register. The ferns growing perhaps millimeters per year, the water seeping through stone at a pace measured in the migration of molecules, the fungi quietly dismantling and reassembling the fundamental components of life in its most basic form. 

What did Tender want from this place?

Perhaps communion. Perhaps escape. Perhaps both, understood as the same thing—the possibility of sitting in a space where human time becomes irrelevant, where the pace of the world is so fundamentally slower that your own heartbeat begins to seem like an intrusion, something you might choose to slow to match the rhythm of the seeping water, the unmeasurable growth of moss.

Tender is gone now—the lack of recent maintenance around the bioluminescent panel suggests absence that stretches back years. But Tender's *shape* remains in the Hollows. The bench remembers the weight of a body. The worn stone paths remember the specific gait of a walker returning again and again to the same threshold. The laminated notes preserve a voice, even if the handwriting has become illegible in places.

The fourth inhabitant, then, is **time itself**—not as abstraction but as the actual substance that moves through this place. Time in the Hollows operates differently than it does above. Above, time is a current you're swept along in whether you want to be or not, marked by the sun's position, by hunger, by the decay of your own body. Here, time becomes visible. You can see it in the mineral banding on the wall. You can touch it in the cold stone that has been slowly losing heat for millennia. You can measure it in the patient accumulation of moss.

Whoever or whatever currently lives in the Hollows—and something does, even if it's only in the phase when you're sitting on the bench—exists in relationship to these deeper inhabitants. The ferns and fungi. The water and stone. The ghost of Tender, preserved in grooves and careful installation choices. The light, still burning.

If you return—and the Hollows seem to call to certain people, call them back—you will add your own footprints to the worn paths. You will sit on the bench, and your weight will press into its damp wood, and you will become part of what lives here: one more consciousness, however briefly, participating in the slow communion of this place.

The Hollows don't demand anything of their inhabitants. They only ask that you arrive, that you stay long enough to feel the difference between human time and deep time, and that you accept that you are not the primary life here.

You are a guest in a garden of patience. And that—finally—is what makes it habitable.
