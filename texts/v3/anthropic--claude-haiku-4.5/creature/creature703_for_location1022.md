# What Lives Here

The first thing you understand about the inhabitants is that they do not hurry. They cannot. The plateau does not permit haste; it insists on a different kind of metabolism, a different way of being present in the world.

The creatures that move through the keratin maze are *old* in their individual lifespans—not ancient in the way the stone is ancient, but long-lived. Decades, perhaps. A mantis-form thing with wings like crystallized amber takes fifteen minutes to traverse a space you could walk across in two. But this is not slowness born of infirmity. Watch it longer. The movements are economical to the point of perfection. Each step is a negotiation with the terrain, a conversation between body and stone that has been refined across generations into something that wastes nothing—no energy, no moisture, no opportunity. The creature's exoskeleton, you notice on closer observation, isn't simply chitinous. It's semi-translucent in places, shot through with what might be vascular patterns or might be something closer to circulatory channels. The skin itself seems to regulate something—moisture, perhaps, or the distribution of heat. As it moves, minute adjustments in the transparency of its carapace allow it to modulate how much of the plateau's radiation it absorbs.

These beings are not solitary, though they appear to be.

There is communication happening on frequencies you cannot hear. When one of the winged creatures lands on a particular section of vegetation, others in distant portions of the plateau—too far away for you to see—adjust their behavior. Not dramatically. A shift in foraging pattern. A slight reorientation of body position. It suggests a sensory acuity that operates through the stone itself, perhaps, or through the interconnected root-system of the purple growth. The plateau itself becomes a nervous system, and the creatures are its distributed neurons.

The larger presences within the vegetation—you glimpse them only in parts.

A curve of what might be a spine, furred or scaled with something that catches the light strangely. The impression of an eye, large and dark and set deep within the tangle of growth, watching nothing in particular and everything at once. The vocalization you heard earlier—that low resonance—is produced by creatures that seem to be more stationary than their smaller cousins. They might be filter-feeders, drawing sustenance from the slow seepage of mineral-rich water. Or they might be something between plant and animal entirely, rooted in the keratin matrix, drawing nutrients from the stone itself while simultaneously participating in the ecosystem's slower cycles. Their size suggests that the plateau's resources, sparse by normal standards, are sufficient if you adjust your expectations of what "feeding" means. A creature that eats once per lunar cycle and then rests for thirty days is not starving; it is thriving within the constraints of its environment.

The insects—if they are insects—operate in tiers of complexity.

The smallest are transparent, nearly invisible things that swarm in the mist-zones, their bodies so minimal that they seem to be mostly wing and metabolic necessity. They feed on something in the moisture itself—not algae, not quite bacteria, but something that occupies that ecological niche. The next tier consists of the arthropod-forms you can actually see: segmented bodies, compound eyes that reflect the ambient light like opals, appendages that are specialized for navigating the fractured terrain. They are predators on the smallest creatures, and prey for the larger ones. There is a hierarchy, but it is not the sharp distinctions of typical food chains. Instead, it feels more like a gradient. A creature that is mostly predator also seems to filter-feed. Another that you would classify as herbivorous clearly takes smaller organisms when it encounters them. The ecology has collapsed the boundaries, created a system where every organism is partially omnivorous, partially flexible, capable of adjusting its diet to whatever is available.

What you do not see, but sense, are the foundations.

The civilization that built those ruins—they have not simply vanished. Their absence is too perfect, too complete. Instead, you begin to understand that their descendants are *integrated* into the landscape. Not metaphorically. The boundary between the creatures you can see and the consciousness that once moved through here in human or human-adjacent form has become genuinely blurred. Over sufficient time—thousands of years, tens of thousands—a species can adapt to a place until the place and the species become, functionally, a single organism. The water that weeps from the stone might carry chemical signals. The mycelium-like structures might be conducting information through the plateau in patterns that constitute thought. The small creatures, the larger ones, the vegetation, the mineral-seeping stone itself—all of it might be a single distributed intelligence, operating on a time-scale so vast that individual thoughts span generations.

The ruins, then, are not abandoned.

They are dormant. Or they are nodes in a system that has learned to express itself through channels you do not recognize as expression. The worked metal, the glass-like fragments, the stylus—these were tools, yes, but what did they make? Not structures, perhaps. Not in the conventional sense. Perhaps they made *patterns* in the stone. Perhaps they conducted water in particular ways, creating channels that shaped how the vegetation grew, which in turn shaped how the ecosystem developed, which shaped the thoughts running through it.

A culture that learns to think in geological time would build very differently.

It would create structures meant to last fifty thousand years not because they are durable but because that is the minimum time-scale relevant to their purposes. It would leave ruins not as monuments to a lost civilization but as *ongoing works*—started millennia ago, still in process, still gathering meaning, still shaping the world through their slow accumulation. The creatures here are not the descendants of the builders. They *are* the builders, in a form the builders themselves may have become.

The mantis-winged thing that moves through the stone fins is performing a task set in motion before your species existed.

The larger presence within the vegetation is maintaining a cycle that has continued for longer than written history. The mist itself—the localized zones of moisture that seem to defy gravity—might be a technology so integrated into the landscape that it appears natural. The seeping water might not be a geological phenomenon at all. It might be managed. Cultivated. Distributed according to an ancient plan that is still being executed across millennia.

What lives on the Keratin Plateau is not a community of individual organisms.

It is a *project*. A civilization that chose not to expand, not to dominate, not to accelerate its processes into the brief, hot flames of conquest and innovation. Instead, it chose to *root itself*. To learn the language of stone and slow time. To become, gradually and irreversibly, the plateau itself—to make itself the means by which the plateau thought, moved, and shaped the world around it.

The creatures move through the maze not because they are lost but because the maze is the instruction manual.

The water seeps not from geological pressure but from the continuing will of something that learned, millennia ago, to be patient.

The ruins will stand for another fifty thousand years, still building.

Still thinking.

Still becoming more completely what they always were.
