# What Lives in the Moss Highlands

The creatures of Keth'al are creatures of patience and compression. They exist in the spaces between—between wet and dry, between growth and decay, between the visible and the suggested.

The **Moss-runners** are the first thing to truly see you. They are small, perhaps the size of a clenched fist, though their bodies flatten and stretch in ways that make size uncertain. Their fur—if it can be called that—is itself moss-like: a fine, branching structure that doesn't so much repel moisture as negotiate with it. They don't flee from you but pause, regard you with eyes that seem to have no whites, only dark pupils that reflect the mist. They move in loose colonies, never quite coordinated but never quite independent. You see them most clearly in the moments just after rain, when they emerge to feed on the insects that emerge on the same schedule. Their calls are clicks and chirrs, modulated by how deeply they press their bodies into the moss. The deeper they sink, the more their vocalizations are dampened—a form of intentional communication through silence.

Beneath the visible layer lives an entirely different population. The **Substrate-dwellers** are rarely encountered whole. You find them in pieces: a translucent carapace, a spine-like structure, the shed skin of something that moves through the moss like you might move through water. They are filter-feeders, primarily—drawing nutrients from the moisture-saturated substrate, processing the constant rain of organic matter that falls from above. Some are segmented like arthropods. Some have no clear segmentation at all, existing as continuous tubes of feeding and movement. One specimen you discover moves with such slowness that you watch it for fifteen minutes without being certain it moved at all—only the position of its anterior opening relative to a nearby moss-stalk suggests it has traveled perhaps two centimeters. These creatures don't navigate the landscape; they *are* navigation, becoming part of the moss structure itself after generations of a single lineage in a single location.

The **High-dwellers**—the ferns and the things that live in them—form a miniature canopy just above the moss-mat. Here live creatures adapted to the perpetual damp: snails with shells of pale, translucent chitin that offer little protection but maximum respiratory surface; spiders whose webs are so fine you discover them only by walking through them, and even then the web doesn't break but clings to your face like a second skin; insects with wings that seem to have evolved as much for sensing air-moisture as for flight. These upper-level dwellers are the visible metabolism of the highlands. They feed on spores, on the tiny arthropods that consume the spores, on each other in precise ratios that maintain the frond-canopy's structure. Their populations rise and fall with a rhythm you might almost learn if you stayed long enough: a week of abundance, a day of dramatic scarcity, then recovery. They seem to operate on a calendar written in spore-release and weather-pattern.

The **Night-movers** are the creatures whose presence you feel rather than see. Heavy things, deliberate. Their tracks fill with water too slowly to reveal detail—you see only a depression, a pattern of pressure that suggests weight and intelligence. They are grazers, probably, feeding on the low shrubs and the tougher moss-species. There might be more than one kind; their tracks vary in pattern, in spacing, in the way the vegetation rebounds. You hear them most clearly during the hours that pass for night here: a step, a pause, a sound like breathing or like the movement of something through deep water. They don't seem to be predators—nothing suggests violence in their feeding. But they are large enough that you would not want to encounter them in the thickest fog, when visibility drops to a meter and you cannot see the shape of what approaches. They have learned to move with the landscape rather than through it, their weight distributed across the compressed moss in ways that minimize damage, maximize balance.

The **People of the Highlands** are neither separate from this system nor fully integrated into it. They number perhaps a few hundred across the entire plateau—too dispersed to call it a population, perhaps, but too intentional to call it accidental. They have adapted to a degree that seems impossible: their skin has taken on a pale cast, almost luminescent in the perpetual grey, as if they're absorbing what little light exists and storing it just beneath the surface. Their eyes are large, dark, with a nictitating membrane that closes sideways to protect against the constant moisture-laden wind. They breathe shallowly, extracting oxygen from air that seems too thin and too damp to carry it. 

Their hands are distinctive: long-fingered, with nails that grow in spiraling patterns, and skin that remains soft despite the climate's demand for callus and toughness. They work the moss deliberately, harvesting certain species while cultivating others, performing acts of gardening on a landscape that appears untouched. They know which moss-species can be woven, which can be eaten (after fermentation in the acidic substrate), which should be left to prepare the ground for future growth. They move through fog that would disorient you within minutes, moving with a confidence that suggests they navigate by senses you don't possess—feeling the slight temperature variations around moss-mounds, hearing the different acoustic signatures of stone beneath different moss-thicknesses, sensing the moisture-density gradients in the air itself.

They are solitary most of the time, or in family units of two or three. They gather perhaps once per season—you've found evidence of larger structures, meeting places—where they exchange knowledge, perform rituals related to the growth of the moss, mate with people from other family groups to prevent the genetic bottlenecking that would occur in true isolation. Their language is sparse, built from the clicks and whispers that carry well in this landscape, supplemented by touch. They touch constantly: hands on moss, palms flat against stone to feel what lives within it, fingers tracing paths through the fern-fronds that seem to communicate information about what grows beyond the mist. When they encounter each other, they touch foreheads, breathing each other's breath—a greeting and perhaps an exchange of scent-information about the valleys they've respectively inhabited.

They make tools. Not many, not ornate. Stone and bone, shaped with patient strikes, used until dull, returned to the moss to be claimed by it. They weave plant-fiber into containers, shelters, clothing. They have fire, barely—the damp makes it so difficult to maintain that they use it sparingly, more for ritual than for survival. They eat what the moss provides and what the creatures of the moss provide: insects in careful harvest, the leggy birds that nest in the fern-fronds (taken with quiet respect, never more than needed), the slow substrate-dwellers that have migrated into their immediate area. They ferment, preserve, store—knowledge accumulated across generations about what can be kept and for how long without spoiling in the constant damp.

What is remarkable about them is how *quiet* they are. Not silent—they make sounds—but quiet in their intention, in their impact. They move through the landscape leaving minimal trace, their tools degrading back into their component elements within years. If you return to this place fifty years after they've abandoned a settlement, you would need to know it was there to find it. The moss would have claimed the structures completely. The landscape would have absorbed them entirely, as if the people were never there at all.

But they are there. They live in the Moss Highlands because they have become the Moss Highlands in a way that doesn't quite qualify as *living in* and doesn't quite qualify as *being*. They are an expression of the landscape's possibility, an edge-case of human adaptation, proof that the margin between survival and integration can become so thin as to be nearly meaningless. They are the answer to the question: *What if the land itself was the primary inhabitant, and everything else was simply the land learning how to experience itself?*

They wait in the mist, patient as moss, as slow as fermentation, as inevitable as the endless cycling of water between substrate and sky.
