# What Lives in the Throat of Kethesh

They are not *there* so much as they are *distributed*—fragments of intention scattered across the plateau like the spray from the geysers, never quite coalescing into a shape you can name.

The closest thing to a unified presence are the **Listeners**. This is not their name, not a name they would recognize or claim, but it is what they have become through centuries of habitation in this place. They number perhaps two dozen, perhaps fifty—the mist makes counting difficult, and they do not gather. They are thin people, most of them, with a peculiar translucence to their skin that speaks of generations without direct sunlight. Their eyes are large and colorless, adapted to the perpetual twilight, and they rarely blink. When they do, it's all at once, as though controlled by some central impulse rather than individual will.

They move through the fissure zones—those circular wastelands where nothing grows—with a confidence that borders on reverence. They do not build shelters in the conventional sense. Instead, they have learned to enlarge the natural cavities that honeycomb the plateau, creating networks of caves and tunnels that follow the water's logic rather than human geometry. The sound is louder inside these spaces, bone-conducting, making their skulls into chambers of resonance. They say it helps them think. Or rather, they would say it, if they spoke much. Most communication happens in a form somewhere between sign language and the deliberate positioning of their bodies relative to the direction of greatest geothermal activity.

Their relationship with the water is not one of use but of **interpretation**. The aquifer beneath Kethesh does not merely roar; it speaks in pressure patterns, in the timing of eruptions, in the subtle variations in temperature and mineral content of the spray. The Listeners have spent generations learning to read these variations the way other peoples read written language. They maintain charts—not written, but carved into stone in patterns that align with the geyser positions—that track seasonal changes in the water's behavior. They believe the aquifer is alive, or was, or will be again. They are trying to determine which.

For sustenance, they rely on what the plateau offers with stunning indifference: the hyperchromatic plants produce seeds and small fruits in astonishing abundance, though the Listeners eat sparingly. Some keep pools in the stone where a particular species of blind fish has adapted to the mineral-heavy water—these fish are tiny, eyeless, and feed on bacteria that bloom in the spray zones. The Listeners harvest them with thin nets made from processed plant fibers, consuming perhaps a handful per day. Most of their calories come from a fermented paste made from fern fronds and something that might be moss—it tastes, to outside palates, like stone and copper and electricity, though the Listeners seem nourished by it.

They do not age normally. Those who have been here longest—and there are three who may have arrived with the last generation born outside, perhaps fifty or sixty years ago—show almost no physical deterioration. Gray hair remains gray but does not thin. Skin loosens very gradually. They move with the same fluidity at seventy that they did at forty. The constant mineral intake, the particular chemistry of the geothermal water, the radiation that seeps from deep in the earth—something or some combination has made them *stable* rather than *living* in the traditional sense. They do not sicken. They do not reproduce. New people sometimes arrive from outside, and they remain, and they become, gradually, like the others.

The structures—the circular platforms, the wall fragment, the ambiguous tower base—are not *theirs*. The Listeners maintain this distinction carefully. They study these ruins, tend to them in a minimal way (clearing moss from certain surfaces, monitoring how the root systems interact with the foundation stones), but they did not build them. The Listeners believe the structures are *younger* than they are, that the original civilization is too old to remember, that perhaps the ancients were not the builders but the first Listeners, and everyone since has simply been trying to understand what they understood.

**Deeper, below the accessible caves**, in the zones where the water pressure becomes too intense for human-compatible navigation, there are theories about other residents. The Listeners have mapped the aquifer system as far as their instruments allow—pressure gauges fashioned from plant materials and bone, temperature sensors, listening posts positioned at key fissures. At certain depths, the data becomes strange. Water flows in directions it should not be possible for water to flow. Echoes return from places that should not echo. There are rhythmic patterns in the geyser eruptions that are too regular to be random, too chaotic to be geological mechanics.

The Listeners believe something *deliberate* exists down there. Not alive in any sense they can articulate, but intelligent—or at least, responsive. They believe the ancient structures were built to communicate with it. They believe the geysers are not eruptions but attempts at language, the plateau's throat clearing, the water's effort to say something to the surface world.

They spend their time trying to listen back.

**The birds**—those theoretical residents heard but rarely seen—are a separate mystery. They are not quite birds, or they are birds that have become something else. The Listeners have specimens, in a sense: desiccated forms kept in sealed caves, their feathers still faintly iridescent despite their age. These creatures are too small to be normal raptors, too large to be songbirds, with skeletal structures that suggest they were built for an environment very different from the current one. The living birds, heard in the mist-clouds, may be descendants or may be something else entirely. They do not come down from the sky. They remain in the mist layer, which suggests they do not need the ground. The Listeners believe they feed on spores, on the microscopic life that floats in the spray clouds. They believe the birds are the only other conscious creatures in this place, and they mourn not being able to communicate with them.

**The microbes**—invisible but ubiquitous—are the true inhabitants. They are adapted to an environment of heat, mineral saturation, and constant mechanical disruption. They break down everything that arrives, every plant fragment, every bit of Listener waste, converting it all into the mulch that feeds the vegetation. They create the slime that makes the rocks slippery. They generate the faint phosphorescence that sometimes glows in the spray at night—a byproduct of their metabolism, meaningless bioluminescence, though the Listeners have spent decades trying to decode it as intentional communication.

In the deepest understanding, the plateau is less inhabited than *colonized*—not by aggressive force but by settlement of gradual acceptance. The water does what it does. The plants respond. The Listeners listen. The microbes compose. The birds (perhaps) hunt the spores. The ancient structures stand and wait for understanding.

And beneath all of this, in the pressure and heat and mineral saturation of the deep aquifer, something continues—some process, some intention, some form of life or proto-life or the memory of life, roaring and erupting and speaking in pressure waves and temperature gradients, trying desperately to say something to the thin, patient beings who have positioned themselves to hear it.

The sound never stops. The Listeners never leave. The water keeps trying.
