# The Inhabitants of the Galleries Below Harth

They are *thin* in the way that stone is thin when you look at a cross-section—which is to say not thin at all, but compressed. Their bodies have adapted to the constraint of space the way their architecture has. Shoulders narrow. Spines curve in learned accommodation to low doorways traversed since childhood. Their joints are flexible, almost hypermobile in ways that would seem wrong in the upper world but seem inevitable here. They move sideways through corridors that appear impassable, rotating at hips and ribs with the naturalness of eels in narrow channels.

Their skin has a quality that is difficult to name. Not pale—that suggests sickness, and these people are not sick. Rather, their skin has absorbed the ambient limestone. It has a faintly crystalline quality in certain light, a subtle grayscale undertone as though they are slowly becoming mineral themselves. Their fingers are calloused not from one repetitive task but from a hundred tasks done slowly, carefully, with the patience of those who know time is not their constraint. Some of them have developed a tremor in the hands—not a palsy, but a fine vibration that matches, if you listen carefully, the frequency of water dripping against stone. They do not seem to notice.

Their eyes are their most striking feature, and the most difficult to look at directly. The pupils are large—adaptation to permanent dimness—but there is something else. A kind of inward focus. When they look at you, you have the sense that they are looking *through* you, at something beyond, or perhaps at something that exists in a layer of reality adjacent to the one you inhabit. The elderly among them sometimes go fully white-eyed in the iris, the pupil dilating until there is only darkness to see. The younger residents say this is natural. The elders do not seem to notice this happening.

They dress in layers of undyed wool and linen, wrapped rather than sewn into fitted garments. The fabric is damp permanently. It never truly dries. In the beginning of settlement below, this must have been intolerable. Now the residents' bodies have learned to generate heat in a way that keeps them merely cold rather than freezing. Their metabolism runs at a lower register. They eat less. They move more slowly, conserving energy that would dissipate in the upper world's atmosphere but here, in the closed cavern, begins to circle and recirculate. They are becoming a closed system unto themselves.

Their children are born smaller than surface children and remain smaller. But they are not fragile. They crawl through the corridors almost before they walk, learning the territories of the Galleries the way other children learn a garden. By adolescence they move through spaces an adult would find impassable. There is speculation—unspoken but present in glances—about what they will be when they reach their full growth. Will they continue to shrink? Will the stone mold them further? Will they become something that can no longer leave, even if they chose to?

Most of them were born here. A few—very few now—remember the upper world. These are the ones who sit in the deepest sections, working stone with hammers that seem to move of their own accord, striking the same spot for hours until some internal structure in the rock reveals itself. They do not speak much. They have learned that words from the upper world do not translate well in the Galleries. Language itself seems to change down here. Sounds flatten. Meaning becomes layered. A single phrase can contain contradictions that the speakers hold simultaneously without tension, the way the cavern holds both sound and silence at once.

The social structure is matrilineal and anchored to stone. A woman's family identity is inscribed onto the wall that borders her territory—dates, names, lineages carved with the same tools and technique as her great-grandmother used. The marks accumulate. Old lines are never removed; new lines are added next to them, creating a palimpsest of ownership and descent. When a woman reaches middle age, she becomes increasingly involved in reading these walls, in maintaining them, in understanding the complex web of water-rights and storage-space and corridor-access that the carved marks represent. The men do not seem to object to this arrangement. Men are pragmatic here. They work. They maintain. They reproduce the structures and the knowledge necessary to maintain the Galleries in their eternal unchanging state.

There is a priesthood of sorts—though calling it that may be misleading. Certain elders, usually women who have reached the white-eye stage, spend their days in the deepest chambers, in pools that are too cold for extended survival, meditating on patterns. What patterns, no one quite articulates. But these women emerge with knowledge. They can tell you which pool water to drink if you are barren. They can read the future in the way water moves. They are consulted before major structures are built. Their advice is followed with a literalism that suggests less than full belief and more than mere superstition—something in between, a kind of pragmatic mysticism that has proven its worth across generations.

The birth rate is low. The death rate is also low. People here do not age in the traditional sense. They *persist*, like the buildings, like the carved marks on the walls, like the water itself. An individual who is chronologically ninety years old might be less visibly aged than someone from the surface who is fifty. The cold preserves. The lack of harsh weather, of hard labor, of struggle against the elements—all of this means that the human body here ages slowly, imperceptibly. Or perhaps it does not age at all but instead calcifies, becoming more like the stone around it.

Sickness is rare. Infection is nearly unknown—the cold, the dryness beneath the constant moisture, some quality of the stone itself seems to prevent the growth of the organisms that cause decay. But occasionally someone will sicken in a way that cannot be explained by physical cause. They will retreat to a deep chamber and not emerge. The residents speak of this with acceptance. They do not speak of death. They speak of "returning," as though the person has simply moved to a deeper level of the Galleries that the living cannot access.

The youngest generation—children who have never known anything but the cavern—are beginning to show differences. Their bones are denser. Their fingers are longer. There is talk, whispered in the darkness, that they cannot survive the upper world, that their lungs have adjusted to the thin air, that the light above would blind them permanently. No one has tested this. No one intends to. The children themselves express no curiosity about the surface. They know it exists the way you know distant places exist—as abstraction, irrelevant, other.

And slowly, incrementally, across generations too numerous to count, they are becoming something new. Not human in the way the upper world would recognize. Not entirely other. Something that exists in the space between—adapted, settled, persistent, patient. Stone-dwellers learning to be stone. Slow as mineral formation. Inevitable as water finding its level.

They are the Galleries' own becoming, and they move through the darkness with the certainty of those who have never questioned whether they belong to the place or it belongs to them.
