# What Lives in Tarn Goss

The goats live here first and always, not as visitors but as the meadow's primary inhabitants, its architects of daily rhythm. They are the *weight* of presence—forty animals that have shaped every path, every tendency of the grasses, every nutrient cycle in this small basin. The pregnant doe carries continuity; the kids born this season are already learning the specific stones, the specific angle at which to approach the cliffs for shelter, the precise locations where the sedge grows sweetest. Their bells—not decoration but practical markers so the shepherd doesn't lose them in mist—have become part of the meadow's voice. They ring not as intrusion but as punctuation to the larger silence, the way a bell in a small church doesn't break quiet but *defines* it. The goats know this place in their hooves, in their bodies' response to the daily freeze-thaw that splits the stone. They *are* the meadow's metabolism made visible.

Then come the insects—the ones you see, the cloud of mosquitoes that transforms the air into a living thing, and the countless others you don't: the small flies whose machinery-sound creates a frequency you feel more than hear, the grasshoppers that seem to explode from the grass in pure kinetic argument with gravity. These are not pests of the meadow; they are its *blood*. They exist nowhere else quite like this—the mosquitoes that plague the lowlands are different creatures, adapted to different pressures. These insects have evolved *into* this place, its thinness of air, its particular cold, the specific chemistry of water trapped in permafrost-rimmed pools. They feed on what grows here. They are fed upon by nothing visible—no birds come this high, or rarely, and only briefly. So they proliferate, their density almost shocking, their sound the closest thing this meadow has to a scream. They are what keeps the goats moving, what prevents them from settling too long in any one place. They are, in some sense, the meadow's restraint upon itself.

The algae are here too, though you only notice them when light strikes the black wet stone right. Thin films, barely visible, existing in the permanent trickle against the eastern wall. These are organisms at the absolute edge of what counts as life—single cells or near-single cells, existing in a space of permanent moisture and permanent cold, never truly drying, never truly thawing. They are the bridge between stone and insect, between mineral and living, colonizing the gap where the two worlds meet. When they glow faintly green, they're not signaling; they're simply *being*, their chlorophyll catching light in a wavelength your eye reads as bioluminescence but which is simply the color of their survival. They are patient beyond measure. They are ancient. A single film of them might be ten thousand cells and ten thousand years old.

The shepherd exists here—or rather, the *absence* of the shepherd, which is just as present. The shelter, five centuries old, is still settling into its place because the shepherd still comes, still uses the stone bench, still tends the fire. But this is not a permanent occupation. The shepherd is a seasonal resident, arriving when the grasses reach their green, staying through the brief abundance of summer, leaving before the snows come. There is a rhythm to this: the shepherd and the goats and the meadow form a closed system that operates only during certain months, then dissolves. The tools left behind—the crook, the wool card, the clay vessel—are not abandoned. They are *waiting*. They are positioned for return. The shepherd who tends this herd knows every stone of the shelter, knows exactly where to find the tools, knows the goats by their individual bells. This person—let's assume it's a solitary figure, someone who has done this work for decades or whose family has done it across generations—exists in a relationship of deep knowledge with this place. They don't guide the goats; they follow them, knowing where they'll go, when they'll need to move, what the weather will demand. The shepherd is the meadow's *memory*, carrying forward the knowing of how to live here.

Beneath everything lives the stone itself, and the peculiar hydrology of the place. The permafrost that has not thawed in three months (and may not thaw much more, frozen perhaps ten meters down, a boundary between the living thin layer and the dead deep one) is not inert. It creates the conditions for the meadow's strange abundance by *refusing* to drain. Water cannot percolate down, so it pools and seeps and hangs in the air. The basalt walls, dark and ancient, are being slowly reshaped by frost-crack, by the tick-tick-tick of freeze-thaw cycles, by the patient work of ice expanding in stone's grain. This process is so slow it reads as stillness, but it is, in fact, the meadow's deepest life—the slow respiration of the mountain itself, breathing through the cycle of day and night temperature, of season and season. The stone that makes the walls of the shelter was quarried from this place, or nearby, and in being shaped into shelter, it was returned to its own structure—walls made of mountain-stone standing *on* the mountain, creating a human-scaled recursion. The stone endures. It will outlast the goats, the shepherd, the insects, even—perhaps—the meadow itself if climate shifts enough.

Water, in all its forms, lives here as a *presence* rather than an event. It is not the water of a stream, which would rush and cut and move toward somewhere else. It is not the water of a lake, which would have *surface*, would have stillness of a different kind. Instead it is water in transit, held in suspension between states—as ice below, as vapor in the air, as seepage on stone, as the saturation of the soil itself. The meadow is as much water as it is grass. The insects live in water's vapor. The algae live in water's film. The grass lives in water's persistence. This is not wet in the sense of marshland; it is water-*awareness*, the meadow's knowledge that it exists at the mercy of the permafrost's melt and the stone's drainage refusal. When the afternoon mist comes—when the moisture that has been vapor condenses suddenly back into visible form—the meadow becomes obscured and clarified simultaneously. You can see less distance, but you become aware of the water as a *substance*, not just a condition. The mist is the meadow breathing out.

And there are the rare moments—perhaps three or four times a summer—when the wind lies down and the mist clears and the sun comes directly. Then the meadow seems to exist in a state of revelation, its colors suddenly sharp: the teal-green of the grasses, the orange-grey of the lichen, the black-bright of the wet stone. The sky above becomes impossibly blue, impossibly empty except for the single hawk that might circle—but doesn't come down; the altitude is too high for much hunting. In these moments, the meadow seems less like a place and more like a *clarity*, a space where everything unnecessary has been stripped away and only essence remains: stone, grass, water, goat, insect, human presence (past or future), and sky. These moments don't last. The mist will return. The wind will resume. But they are the meadow's truest self, perhaps—the state toward which all its slow work aims.

What lives here lives *in conversation* with what is barely alive—ice, stone, the atmospheric pressure at 4,200 meters, the temperature that swings from pleasant during day to freezing at night. The boundary between living and not-living is negotiated daily. The goats have evolved toward this negotiation. The insects have adapted to it. The shepherd has learned it. The algae have become *of* it. The stone simply continues, indifferent and eternal, the ground from which all other life springs and to which all return.

The meadow is crowded with life *precisely because* it is so small, so bounded, so difficult. Everything that has made it here has become specialized, essential, locked into the others. Remove the goats, and the grasses thicken and shift. Remove the insects, and the goats stay too long in one place, overgrazing. Remove the permafrost—let the water drain—and it becomes merely high and cold and empty instead of high and cold and *full*. Remove the shepherd, and the system still functions, but the knowing stops. The tools rust. The shelter settles faster into its dissolution.

What lives here does so because nowhere else will quite do. This is not a refuge of last resort—it is a *perfection* for what has adapted to it. The goats do not dream of lower, warmer pastures. The insects have not been exiled from abundance elsewhere. The algae are not here because they could survive nowhere else, but because they thrive in this specific marriage of water and cold and stone. The shepherd chose this place—or inherited the choice through family, through apprenticeship, through the simple recognition that this meadow and this herd and this solitude added up to something worth knowing.

The meadow will outlive all of them. Already it is patient. Already it is eternal. Already it is waiting for the next season, the next herd, the next shepherd, the next year of frost-crack and melt-seep, the next cloud moving in from the northwest, the next moment when the air clears and the stone shines. This is what it does. This is what it is.
