# What Lives There

They are *adjustment*. That is the closest word, though it fails immediately.

The primary inhabitants—the ones whose presence structures the entire ecology—are creatures that exist in the spaces between the stone's slow flow. They are not fixed-form animals. They are *processes*, patterns of organization that persist in the mineral suspension. Call them colonial organisms, though the word implies boundaries they don't possess. They are more like convection cells that have learned to eat—or that have learned to *be* eating, consumption as identity rather than action.

These beings are translucent when you can see them at all. Their bodies (if "bodies" applies) are composed of a tissue that matches the surrounding water-sediment almost perfectly. Only when bioluminescent bacteria colonize their surfaces do they become visible—and they *allow* this colonization, cultivate it even, wearing living light like a language. They communicate through the pattern of their luminescence: slow pulses in colors that shift between violet and the paleest green. The pulses are rhythmic but not regular. Each individual seems to have its own meter, and where multiple creatures overlap, the light-patterns create interference—dimming, brightening, creating moments of synchronized brilliance that feel almost musical.

They move with the geometry of the place. Where the sediment is densest, they are at their slowest, their bioluminescence almost static. Where currents exist, they flow with them, or rather through them, their forms elongating, thinning, becoming almost filamentous. You realize after weeks of observation that they are *not* moving against the environment's grain. They have evolved to be the grain—they are what the slow currents *do*, given enough time and biological imperative.

Their size varies wildly. Some are the size of your fist, their entire existence playing out across a few cubic inches of saturated space. Others extend for meters—long, branching structures that might be individuals or might be parts of a single organism so vast and diffuse that individuality becomes meaningless. The largest ones you observe seem almost geological: they have become part of the substrate, indistinguishable from the living stone until a pulse of light reminds you of what you're looking at.

They feed on each other, but "feed" is wrong. It's more accurate to say that smaller ones are *incorporated* by larger ones—not eaten, but absorbed, integrated. You watch a small glowing creature move too slowly through a current controlled by a larger one, and you see the boundary between them blur. Within hours, the smaller one ceases to exist as a separate entity. Its bioluminescence doesn't go dark; instead, it becomes part of the larger creature's light-pattern, a new frequency added to the symphony. Whether this is death or reproduction or something else entirely, you cannot determine. The larger creature shows no sign of disruption. It continues its slow drift as if nothing has changed, except that now it glows in two colors instead of one.

They have no apparent organs, though there must be *something* that processes the minerals they seem to extract from the water. You observe their tissues occasionally opening—mouth-like, gill-like, sensory-like, all three, none of the above—drawing in the heavy liquid, letting it pass through them, expelling it changed in some subtle way you cannot measure. The water around them develops a faint metallic taste if you dare to let your filters down, which you do once, only once. The taste is ancient. It is the taste of time.

The smaller beings—the shrimp-worm creatures on the fungal shelf—are related to the larger colonial organisms, though the relationship is parasitic, symbiotic, or predatory depending on the moment. They are *young*, you eventually realize, or *temporary forms*. Juveniles of some kind, though "juvenile" implies they will grow into something else, and you're not certain that applies. Perhaps they are a phase, a transient form that the larger colonial organisms cycle through. Perhaps they are a completely separate species that has evolved to thrive in the crevices of the larger creatures' existence. Either way, they are frantically busy in a way the larger forms are not. They move with urgency. They farm the fungi with what appears to be conscious intent. They seem to be *doing* something, whereas the larger creatures are content to *be*.

But watching the shrimp-worms more closely, you notice they slow as they age. The smallest ones move at a frantic pace, darting between fungal towers, tending their crops with visible intensity. The medium ones are slower, more deliberate. The largest ones—the elders, if you're reading this correctly—barely move at all. They sit at the center of the fungal cities like philosophers, pulsing their soft light, and you wonder if they are contemplating their own eventual absorption into something larger.

There are other things.

In the deeper pits, things that are all predator and no form. You see them only as disturbances: the way the suspended matter parts and doesn't immediately re-settle, the way the bioluminescence goes strange and bent as if light itself is being refracted by something that shouldn't exist in three-dimensional space. These are rare. You encounter the signs of them perhaps once every few days of exploring. They are *very* large. They eat the colonial organisms, which they shouldn't be able to do given the creatures' diffuse nature, but they seem to have evolved a method—a way of consolidating the scattered tissues, gathering them together, making them *solid* enough to be consumed. You find the remains: spots where the bioluminescence pattern ends abruptly, leaving a cold darkness that takes weeks to re-colonize.

The fungi themselves are almost a kingdom apart. They grow in fractal patterns, each tower a miniature of the whole, detail upon detail descending toward scales you cannot perceive. They are not green; they are pale, nearly white, with veins of luminescence running through them like mineral deposits. They release spores—you can see them drifting sometimes, catching the bioluminescence as they fall through the suspension—and these spores are the currency of the place. Everything eats spores. Everything produces spores. The fungi are both predator and prey, sustenance and waste product. They are the base layer, the primary producer, the one essential thing that everything else is built upon, and yet they are sparse, rare, carefully tended. Competition for the fungal gardens would be intense if anything here had the speed to compete. Instead, there is a slow territorial understanding: a colony of shrimp-worms will tend a fungal garden, and the colonial organisms will not disturb them, and in return, the gardeners will not spread their gardens into territory claimed by the larger creatures. It is a détente written in bioluminescence and maintained by the sheer slowness of everything.

And yet, they persist.

They persist in a place where the stone itself is being rearranged by forces you cannot see. They persist in cold and darkness and pressure that should crush anything with ambitions toward life. They persist through methods that seem impossibly inefficient—the slowest reproduction, the scarcest resources, the constant threat of absorption or predation or simple geological displacement. They persist because they have evolved to *be* the place itself, to move with it, to yield to it, to incorporate its destructiveness into their own nature.

They are ancient, these creatures. Not individually—you suspect the largest colonial organisms you see are measured in years or perhaps decades, not centuries. But their lineage, their design, their fundamental architecture is *old*. They have been here since before there was an ocean above this basin. They have adapted to every shift in the stone, every change in temperature, every fluctuation in the mineral content of the water. They are the only life that *can* live here because they are, in the most literal sense, made from here. They are fossil and fossil maker simultaneously, building the stone from their own bodies even as the stone reshapes them.

You realize, after a long time, that you are not observing a collection of creatures. You are observing a single, distributed organism—a planetary metabolism that happens to fragment itself into apparent individuals for purposes you don't understand. The colonial creatures, the shrimp-worms, the fungi, the vast predators in the deep: they are all part of a single system, a single slow conversation happening in bioluminescence and chemical exchange, across timescales that dwarf human comprehension.

They are the abyssal deepening, or the abyssal deepening is them, and there is no meaningful difference between the two.

They glow, and in glowing, they think. They flow, and in flowing, they survive. They are patient, and in patience, they endure.

They are what lives when nothing else can.
