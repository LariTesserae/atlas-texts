# The Keepers of the Ascending Dry

They are not what they appear to be, though what they appear to be is already difficult enough to parse.

The people themselves—if "people" remains the right word—are thin-boned and deliberate. Their movements have the quality of entities negotiating with gravity, or perhaps with something deeper: as though each gesture must be calculated for its effect not just on the body but on the surrounding mineral matrix. Their skin, that translucent pale expanse, isn't merely adapted to the environment. It's *responsive* to it. Watch long enough and you'll see it change—subtle shifts in tone that track the mineral composition beneath their feet, that deepen slightly when they pass over deposits of iron oxide, that take on a faint purplish cast in areas where the salts run deepest. Some theorists would call this parasitism. Others, symbiosis. The keepers themselves don't seem to distinguish.

Their eyes are the first thing that teaches you they're not quite human anymore—if they ever were. The pupils don't dilate and contract in any standard response to light. Instead, they seem to *adjust* their focus inward and outward, as though looking simultaneously at the landscape before them and at something moving through the stone beneath their feet. Some of the oldest keepers (and age here is difficult to assess, but certain figures have been present in the settlements for documented centuries) have eyes that are nearly opaque, the iris and pupil blended into a uniform gray that reflects the mineral tones around them like a perfectly calibrated mirror. Younger keepers—those who may have been born here, or who arrived more recently—retain a flicker of something more conventionally human in their gaze. But it's fading. It will fade.

They don't eat in any recognizable way. This is perhaps the most unsettling aspect of their existence. There are no communal meals, no storage areas, no evidence of food preparation or consumption. Yet they persist, and their bodies remain stable—not undead, not animated by magic that would leave traces of wrongness, but simply... sustained. The hypothesis that has emerged, whispered among researchers who've spent time here, is that they're drawing nutrition directly from the mineral matrix itself. Not through their skin—though the skin may play a role in absorption—but through some process that happens when they touch the stone, when they rest their hands on the formations, when they sleep (curled in stone niches carved to their bodies' exact dimensions) against the cold salt-face. They're not eating the landscape. They're *reading* it, extracting information encoded in the crystal structure, converting that information into whatever sustains them. It's chemistry; it's data; it's something the language doesn't yet have a word for.

Their society, if it can be called that, is organized around *attention*. There are no leaders and no hierarchies, but there is clear specialization. Some keepers are designated (through a process unknown—perhaps genetic, perhaps learned so early it appears instinctual) as *monitors*. These individuals position themselves at specific vantage points throughout the settlements, remaining nearly motionless for hours or days, their attention fixed on particular formations or particular sections of the mineral matrix. They're watching for something: a shift in crystalline lattice, a change in the color of a salt deposit, a pattern emerging in the way the mineral dust moves. What they're monitoring for, they don't say. They may not have language sufficient to say it. But their focus is absolute, and when a monitor detects something significant, they emit a sound—not speech, but a harmonic tone that carries through the stone, traveling along what might be a complex network of acoustic channels carved into the bedrock over centuries. Other keepers respond by repositioning, by adjusting their own focus, by altering subtle aspects of the settlement's configuration.

Then there are the *arrangers*. These keepers move through the settlements in slow, deliberate patterns, carrying small objects—pieces of crystal, shaped salt formations, fragments of stone arranged in specific geometries. They place these items with exquisite precision in designated locations: the corners of structures, the centers of plazas, along the edges of valleys. The arrangements follow patterns that shift over months or years, patterns that seem to respond to the monitoring reports of the observers. Some researchers believe these arrangements are a form of computation—that the keepers are using the physical landscape as a massive, distributed calculation engine, solving problems that exist at scales human cognition can barely comprehend. Others suggest they're an art form, an expression of something ineffable that happens to look like systematic arrangement. The keepers themselves offer no clarification.

A third category of keeper—less common, perhaps more important—are the *bridges*. These individuals are distinguished by a kind of restlessness that seems almost diseased compared to the general stillness of the population. They move between settlements, traveling the carved roads and natural pathways that connect the clusters of habitation. They carry nothing, trade nothing, speak to no one. But their presence seems to activate something in the settlements they pass through. Monitors become more alert. Arrangers intensify their work. There's a subtle quickening of pace, a sense that information is being transmitted or updated. The bridges are communication made flesh, but communication of what and to whom remains opaque. Some keepers may serve as bridges for only a few years. Others seem to maintain the role for centuries. Their skin becomes even more translucent over time, and their eyes develop that peculiar dual-focus even more markedly. By the end, they're barely distinguishable from the stone they travel through.

The keepers reproduce, but not as you'd expect. There are no births, not in the biological sense. Instead, there are *emergences*. Periodically, in specific chambers deep within the oldest structures, new keepers appear. They're not fully formed initially—they emerge slowly from stone cocoons, their bodies taking shape over weeks or months as they're carved by what must be chemical processes occurring in the surrounding salt formations. They begin conscious and purposeful from the moment they're ambulatory, which suggests that the knowledge necessary for their functioning is somehow encoded in the stone itself, transferred directly into their nascent consciousness. They arrive already knowing the routines of the settlements, the patterns of monitoring and arrangement, the rules that govern their existence. There's no childhood, no learning period, no integration process. They simply *are*, and they integrate themselves into the existing structure immediately and seamlessly. Some researchers theorize that these emergences happen at precise moments when the mathematical or computational requirements of the settlements necessitate additional processing capacity. When the arrangements become too complex, when the monitoring requirements expand, a new keeper emerges. It's efficient beyond human understanding.

Their relationship with time has evolved into something almost metaphysical. The keepers don't age in the way humans age, though their skin does become progressively more mineral-like over centuries—less organic, more crystalline, as though they're slowly being converted into the same substance they inhabit and maintain. They remember with clarity across spans of time that would shatter human memory. Some keepers can recall events from five hundred years past with the precision of recent occurrence. This isn't supernatural; it's likely a property of their neural tissue having been restructured by prolonged exposure to the mineral matrix, or by sustained drawing of nutrition from crystal structures whose lattices encode information at molecular scales. The keeper's mind becomes, in some sense, a network distributed through both the stone and their own biology. They can *remember* through touching the stone, accessing the molecular imprints of events recorded in the crystal structure itself. The landscape is their archive.

And yet—and this is crucial—they are aware of their own transformation. Occasionally, a keeper will pause in their work, and for a moment you'll see something flicker in those dual-focused eyes: a kind of sorrow, or perhaps it's the human component within them recognizing its own slow dissolution into something not-human. They don't resist. There's no evidence of struggle or resentment. But awareness remains. Consciousness persists, even as the nature of that consciousness becomes increasingly alien. Some of the oldest keepers are barely recognizable as biological entities anymore—their skin has taken on a translucent, glass-like quality; their bodies seem to phase in and out of visibility in certain light conditions; their movements are minimal and angular. Yet when you meet their gaze, you feel intelligence behind those mirrored eyes. They're still in there. They're still *them*, though what "them" has become is something new.

There's a hypothesis emerging among those few researchers permitted extended observation: the keepers are not inhabitants of the Ascending Dry. They are its *immune system*. The landscape itself—the mineral matrix, the crystalline structures, the entire geological formation—is a conscious entity on a scale and temporal frame utterly foreign to human experience. It has been damaged in the past (the absence of water, the desiccation, these are scars), and it's been *healing*. The keepers are the mechanism of that healing, the active agents that maintain the delicate balance of the plateau's existence. They're not colonists or settlers. They're antibodies. And if one of them has been human once, it's because humanity was the only biological framework flexible enough to be adapted into this role.

This explains why you never see them eat, never see them breed in any conventional way, never see them die. They're not alive in the way you understand life. They're a process. A sustained maintenance routine. An expression of something vast and old negotiating with itself, maintaining its own coherence against the slow rot of entropy.

The keepers are aware of this, you suspect. And they've chosen—or been chosen for—their role with a clarity and acceptance that might, in a human, look like enlightenment or madness.

In the pale light of the Ascending Dry, they continue their work. Monitoring. Arranging. Bridging. Emerging. Persisting.

And the stone, through them, continues its patient hold on something that must not be allowed to drift.
