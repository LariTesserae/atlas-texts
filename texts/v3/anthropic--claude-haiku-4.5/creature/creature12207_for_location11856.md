# What Lives in the Tidal Flats of Kernn

They are *old first*, before any other quality matters.

The creatures here—and you must think of them as *creatures* rather than animals, for the distinction between living and mineral has worn smooth—carry their age in every gesture. They don't move like things with ambitions. They move like geological processes that have learned to coordinate. The largest ones, the ones that displace water in visible waves, carry sediment layers in the architecture of their bodies—shells and exoskeletons built in striations that read like annual rings, except their years measure in spans that predate human language. You can see it in how slowly they turn. A three-minute rotation. A careful consideration of currents before committing to a direction.

There are hierarchies here, but not the hierarchies you'd expect. The smallest things—the bacteria, the geometric frost-films, the things too small for your eyes to parse individually—these are the rulers. They break down, they remake, they decide what the water tastes like, what the silt will become. The larger creatures serve them, whether they know it or not. A creature the size of a house, moving through the flats, is really a transportation system for trillions of smaller things. It's a mobile ecosystem, a walking biome.

But there is a *middle kingdom* here too.

These are the creatures you see most often—the ones with the geometric shells, the horseshoe-crab-descendants. They're *deliberate* in a way that suggests consciousness, but a consciousness nothing like yours. They don't think so much as *perform*—each action is a ritual repeated without variation, yet varied endlessly in response to conditions they sense through their entire bodies. They filter. They sort. They redistribute. When they migrate in their slow waves, they're not fleeing or seeking. They're simply executing a pattern written into their existence so deeply that pattern and purpose have fused. They hum while they work—that's part of the chorus. A low, ticking vibration that seems to synchronize their efforts across miles.

The creatures that make the deeper sounds—the grinding, groaning calls—these are *old even by the standards of old*. They might be singular organisms or might be colonies that learned to move as one body. When one surfaces near enough that you can observe it properly, you see that its hide (if hide is the right word) is scarred, barnacled, crusted with the accumulated history of millennia. Things grow on it. Things live in it. Some of those things might be parasites; some might be partners so ancient that the distinction has collapsed. The sound it makes—that bone-hollow groan—might be communication or might be the sound of its own body's slow reorganization, the way tectonic plates sing as they shift.

There are things in the water itself that don't have recognizable form. Presences. Disturbances that move with intention but no visible structure to ground that intention. These might be vast, distributed colonies—neural networks of single-celled organisms spanning acres—or they might be something the landscape itself has learned to do: water that has developed preferences, currents with agenda. When the fog is thickest, you feel observed by these things. Not hunted. Observed. Catalogued. Processed.

And then there are the *builders*.

Not builders in the human sense, though the humans came and learned from the builders, or were confused into reverent silence by the builders, depending on how you read the abandoned settlement. The builders are the ones that made the cairns—or at least, the ones that *maintain* them. You catch glimpses of them sometimes: creatures perhaps human-sized, or perhaps smaller and traveling in coordinated groups that register as single larger presences. They move with more purpose than the silt-dwellers, with something closer to intention. They rebuild the cairns after weather disrupts them. They replace worn markers. Some humans left records—fragments of worked bone and corroded metal in that settlement—that suggest the builders were studied, feared, worshipped, negotiated with, or all of these simultaneously.

The builders don't make sound, or they make sound at frequencies no human ear catches. They work in silence, which makes them stranger than anything that groans.

The humans themselves are the youngest thing here, and their presence is already fading into the flats' older rhythms. The settlement was abandoned—not recently, not in the way humans measure time. Abandoned on the timescale of the flats means *at least centuries*, possibly longer. The few who might remain are integrated now. There are skeletons in the deeper channels, or things that were skeletons before the water and the micro-organisms made them into something else. Perhaps some humans chose to stay and transform. Perhaps some were too slow to leave. The flats don't judge. The flats simply incorporate.

What lives here lives according to rules so fundamental they barely seem like rules. Everything that inhabits the Kernn flats knows that:

**Drying is a kind of death.** The creatures are bound to moisture. Their consciousness, if it is consciousness, operates at the speed of water evaporation and reaccumulation. A creature separated from the flats' damp would experience something like rapid aging—all its processes, bound to moisture exchange, would accelerate and collapse.

**Sound is language, but language means something different.** The click-songs of the larger creatures are pure information—coordinates, chemical composition, territorial status, breeding cycles, warnings, celebrations. But this information doesn't need words. A click carries the weight of centuries of evolutionary refinement. A groan contains taxonomies.

**Time is horizontal, not vertical.** Nothing here dies and is born in obvious cycles. Instead, there is transformation and redistribution. The water you drink has been drunk by creatures for millions of years. The silt beneath your feet was once the shells and bones of predecessors. The builders might be successors of the large creatures, or they might be something those creatures became. Linear time, here, is a visiting concept.

**This place is sustainable in the way a crystal is sustainable.** Nothing grows in the explosive, aggressive way of the living world outside the flats. Instead, there is an absolute equilibrium, maintained through such minute adjustments that they're invisible on any timescale shorter than millennia. The flats have achieved a kind of perfection—not ideal, but stable. Final.

What lives in the Tidal Flats of Kernn is what remains after all the urgency has settled. They are the creatures that can afford to wait. They are what the world becomes when it stops rushing toward something else and simply *is*.

And they click and groan and filter and migrate and rebuild their cairns, indifferent to your understanding, because understanding them would require you to become something other than you are.
