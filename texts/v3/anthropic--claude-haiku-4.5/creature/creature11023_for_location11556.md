# The Inhabitants of Kethis

They are few, and they are changed.

The workers who live on the plateau proper—perhaps forty, perhaps sixty, the number fluctuates—have developed a particular bearing. They move with deliberation, never hastily, as though conserving some resource beyond mere energy. Their skin has taken on a grayish pallor, not from illness but from constant mineral exposure; fine particulates settle into the pores, stain the hair, accumulate in the creases of palms and knuckles until even the young look weathered. Many wear respiratory masks during certain hours, though the newer workers have learned to interpret the air itself—the smell and taste shift when the vent gases change composition, when it becomes safer or more dangerous to breathe unfiltered.

They work in rotating shifts tied not to the sun, which provides no meaningful rhythm here, but to the machinery cycles and the chemical patterns of the springs. Sleep schedules are erratic, negotiated between the body's needs and the facility demands. Some workers maintain precise routines; others have given up the pretense of circadian living and sleep when exhaustion demands it, in twelve-minute increments in the dormitory buildings, waking to the sound of pressure valves or the calls of the shift supervisor.

The workers are not from here. They come from below—from the populated valleys and basins where normal ecosystems persist—contracted for two-year postings, with the understanding that extension beyond five years is rare, and living a full career on the plateau without significant relocation to lower elevations is considered a form of slow sacrifice. Yet some do stay longer. Some have been here for a decade, or longer still. These lifers have developed something like adaptation: their sense of smell has shifted, calibrated to detect the mineral compositions that others find indistinguishable; their eyes have adjusted to the glare without the constant eye-watering of newcomers; their lungs have settled into a chronic, managed inflammation that they no longer consciously notice. They move through the industrial zones with an almost proprietary efficiency, and they speak to the younger workers with a mixture of pity and something harsher—recognition, perhaps, of what they've become.

There is a hierarchy among them, but it is not entirely rigid. The facility managers—usually three or four per major installation—occupy the most substantial buildings and make the strategic decisions about extraction rates and maintenance schedules. Below them are the technicians and engineers, often rotated from lower facilities, brought up for specific projects or ongoing system management. Then the general laborers, who perform the physically demanding work: sealing mineral-clogged pipes, hauling replacement parts, monitoring the chemical outputs. And below this official structure, there are the scavengers—those who work in the interstices of the formal economy, salvaging materials from abandoned facilities, trading in contraband minerals (certain crystalline formations have value in the lower markets), maintaining informal networks of information and small-scale theft.

The social structure of the settlement is complicated by the isolation. Interpersonal conflicts cannot be easily resolved by distance or separation—everyone breathes the same air, works in proximity to everyone else, relies on the same limited resources. There have been incidents: workers who could no longer tolerate the environment and attempted to leave the plateau outside of contracted periods (most were caught at the descent points and returned); a supervisor who suffered a psychological break, now housed in a separate building where he is monitored; two workers who became involved in a dispute over something trivial—a perceived insult, a resource allocation—that escalated into violence. That conflict required intervention from below, an official investigator sent up from the valley settlements.

Yet daily coexistence is maintained. There are unwritten rules. Certain areas are neutral ground—the meal halls, the communal water stations (fed by cooled runoff from the cooling systems, safe to drink, though it carries a faint mineral taste). Disputes are handled through a combination of formal grievance procedures and informal mediation by the longer-term residents, who understand the psychology of plateau isolation better than any external authority.

The workers have developed peculiar habits. In the evenings, small groups gather at specific points on the plateau's edge where the view extends down into the valleys below—where normal life is visible as distant lights and smoke. They stand in silence, looking down. No one remarks on this practice; it is understood to be necessary. Some workers maintain personal journals, recording the details of their daily labor and their thoughts, as though the act of documentation might anchor them to a sense of time passing, of their presence here mattering in some recordable way. A few have brought musical instruments—a guitar with strings corroded by mineral air, a mouth organ, a handmade drum—and they produce sound irregularly, enough that the acoustics of the space have been altered by people learning where echoes carry and where sound dies.

There is also the question of the longer-term residents: those who have been on the plateau so long that returning to the lower settlements has begun to seem impossible. Not because they cannot leave, but because they have become neurologically and physiologically adapted to this environment. One woman, who has been here for nearly twenty years, experiences significant anxiety and respiratory distress when taken below for medical evaluation; she recovered when returned to the plateau. Another man, one of the earliest settlers, remains in a permanent facility because his lung capacity has been so fundamentally altered that lower oxygen levels cause him to panic. They are casualties of industrial life, but they are also, in their way, the truest inhabitants—people for whom Kethis has become not a workplace but a home, however hostile.

There are children here. Not many. Perhaps a dozen, born to long-term workers, some planned, others accidental. They have been raised entirely on the plateau, in air that smells of minerals and steam, under light that never truly changes. They are peculiar creatures: pale-skinned, with enlarged lung capacity, showing early signs of the sensory adaptations their parents have developed. They play in restricted zones, supervised, kept away from the active facilities and the deeper thermal basins. Their understanding of the world below—where most of humanity lives—is theoretical, learned from stories and the occasional visit below. At least two of the children have expressed a desire never to leave the plateau. Whether this represents genuine preference or a form of early institutional acceptance is unclear.

The extremophile life in the waters is not extensively studied by the residents, but it is understood and utilized. Certain bacterial colonies are harvested and used in small-scale industrial processes; the thermophilic crustaceans are occasionally boiled and rendered for their limited nutritional content, a supplement to the imported food stores. There is a pragmatic relationship: the creatures are recognized as life, but they are categorized as resources, part of the industrial ecology rather than as wild species deserving of protection.

A few residents have developed something like obsession with the ecology of the plateau. One technician has devoted his spare time to mapping the thermal gradients and predicting where new vent formations will emerge; another has created a detailed catalog of the visible insect and bird species, noting behavioral patterns. These pursuits are tolerated as harmless eccentricities by the management, though there is an implicit understanding that they should not interfere with the primary mission of resource extraction.

The psychological profile of plateau residents follows certain patterns. Initial adaptation is marked by vertigo, disorientation, and a sense of unreality—the landscape's hostility and the radiation of heat create a dissociative state that typically lasts two to eight months. Workers who survive this period begin to normalize, to develop routines that anchor them. Those who cannot adapt experience escalating anxiety and are typically evacuated before they become a liability. The long-term residents show a particular cognitive profile: enhanced pattern recognition, reduced emotional volatility, increased tolerance for monotony and repetitive labor. Some theorists suggest the plateau environment itself selects for specific personality types—that the isolation and the particular quality of stress create a self-selecting population of people whose neurochemistry is already somewhat unusual.

In the isolated facilities, in the abandoned structures, in the dormitory buildings at night, the residents of Kethis exist in a state of endurance. They are not heroic figures; they are not pioneers in any romantic sense. They are workers, some temporary, some trapped by circumstance or choice, adapting to a place that the Earth created without any intention of hosting human life.

They are changed by being here. Their bodies are changed, their sense of time is changed, their understanding of what constitutes a normal environment has been fundamentally altered. Whether they will ever fully return to the world below—or whether they remain, in some essential way, creatures of the plateau now—is perhaps the only question that matters to them, in the moments when they stand at the edge and look down at the distant ordinary world, and wonder what they have become in this place of heat and minerals and silence.
