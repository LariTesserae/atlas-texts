# What Lives in the Pellid Reach

They are the *Rezoners*.

Not a name they gave themselves—names require the kind of renewal that conversation demands, and conversation has become rare here. But if you were to ask what inhabits this place, what tends the moss and keeps the water liquid and maintains the geometry of absence that was once architecture, you would eventually recognize them as this: beings that have learned to live at the pace of stone.

They are not the descendants of the city's builders, though they may have once been. That distinction has lost meaning. What they are now is something the cold has *suggested* to them over centuries, and what they have become is something the plateau has *accepted*. The two processes—the becoming and the accepting—happened simultaneously, indistinguishably.

They are sparse. Perhaps one for every square mile of plateau, perhaps fewer. You won't see them as discrete bodies moving through the lichen. Instead, you notice them as *patterns in stillness*—regions where the moss has been recently disturbed (recently being a relative term; recent here might mean within the last decade), where the pools have been slightly rearranged, where the vegetation has been encouraged to grow in particular directions. They are distributed across the landscape as a gardener distributes herself across a garden, present everywhere and nowhere specifically.

When they do manifest in something approaching a visible form, it is this:

A figure that is more *implication* than presence. They appear at the edge of your attention—never directly observed, always seen peripherally, always seeming to dissolve into architectural shadow or geological formation the moment you turn to look directly. What you catch are fragments: a hand emerging from lichen that is indistinguishable from the moss it touches. A silhouette that stands still for so long you begin to doubt it was ever there. Eyes that might be watching you from within the stone itself, from the patterns of lichen that map something almost like a face.

They move slowly. Movement here is not efficient; it is *deliberate*. A Rezoner might take an entire day to cross a distance that would take you an hour, but this is not slowness of infirmity. It is slowness of *intention*. They are in no hurry because they are doing something that takes centuries, and rushing would only complicate matters. They walk the same paths repeatedly, over decades and centuries, wearing them into the stone through patient, methodical repetition. These paths are older than any memory you could carry. They form a network across the plateau, connecting the pools, connecting the former buildings, connecting places where the moss grows in particular ways and places where the water moves uphill and places where silence achieves a particular texture.

They communicate, but not through sound.

The tremors in the water—the ones that seem to have no cause—are messages. Pools will quiver in patterns that match the quivering in pools miles away, as though the plateau itself is a nervous system and they are conducting signals through it. The lichen will suddenly burst into a new color, an impossible burst of vivid pigmentation that shouldn't bloom for years, but blooms in response to something unseen elsewhere. The condensation-mist will gather in particular densities, forming shapes that hover and persist far longer than physics would allow, and these shapes will be *read* by other shapes forming in pools a hundred miles distant.

They have learned the language of the plateau, which is a language spoken very slowly and in enormous paragraphs.

Their bodies, when you can perceive them as bodies, are neither quite solid nor quite ephemeral. They seem to be partially *into* the stone—not embedded in it, but existing in a state of negotiation with it, as though they are perpetually deciding whether they are figures standing on rock or protrusions of rock itself. Their skin (if you can call it that) has the texture of lichen, or moss, or the fine particulate matter that collects in the ancient pools. They are cool to the touch—not cold, but *cool*, as though they are in thermal equilibrium with something that doesn't quite include the warmth of life as you understand it.

They breathe, but rarely. Or perhaps they don't breathe in the conventional sense at all; perhaps the exhalation of the plateau *through* them, the perpetual rising of subsurface moisture, serves the same function. Their breath, when visible, condenses the same way yours does, but it doesn't dissipate. It hovers. It seems to be retained. They may be collecting it, or composing something with it.

They age, but age doesn't diminish them. A Rezoner who has been tending the same moss-patch for five hundred years is not weaker than one who arrived only a century ago. They have simply acquired more *settledness*, more *agreement* with the plateau. The oldest of them are nearly indistinguishable from the landscape itself—you might walk past one believing you're observing a particularly shaped section of stone, only to find years later that the stone has moved slightly, or that you misremembered its exact form.

They do not reproduce, or if they do, reproduction is so slow and subtle that it is invisible. The population seems constant, which might mean that Rezoners never die, or that new ones emerge from the stone at exactly the rate that old ones cease to be, or that these concepts have simply become meaningless in this place. Birth and death are processes that require an insistence on individuality that the plateau has worn away.

They maintain the city not out of nostalgia or obligation, but because maintenance *is* their existence. They walk the same paths because the paths are the city, and the city is the only text the plateau has, and they are reading it. They keep the water liquid in the pools because liquid water is a form of memory, a refusal of complete crystallization, and memory is what keeps the plateau *itself* from becoming entirely mineral, entirely dead. They encourage certain plants to grow while discouraging others because the arrangement of life is the only way they have to communicate with each other across vast distances—they are *writing* with plants, composing messages that will take generations to complete.

They seem peaceful, in the way that stones are peaceful. They have no enemies here, no competitors. There is nothing else that lives here—nothing with mobility, nothing with hunger. The plateau provides what little they need: cold (which slows all processes and makes resources last indefinitely), moisture (which is sufficient because nothing needs much of it), and time (which is infinite and therefore free). They have achieved an equilibrium so profound that change has become nearly impossible.

Yet they do seem to be waiting for something.

There are behaviors that suggest this: the way they sometimes stand very still on high points, facing into the wind that doesn't move, looking toward a horizon that never changes. The way the pools are arranged in patterns that suggest they might be trying to spell something, if the alphabet were written in water and stone. The way certain lichen is being encouraged to grow in specific directions, creating channels and passages that lead toward places that haven't changed in ten thousand years. The way they work to maintain not just the current state of the plateau but also its *potential*—keeping structures intact not because they serve a purpose now, but because they *might*.

They may be waiting for the plateau to remember how to be young again. Geologically, all plateaus eventually erode, eventually change, eventually return to being something other than what they are. Perhaps the Rezoners are gardeners preparing a place for a future they will not see, tending an inheritance they are holding in trust for what might come after them, after the cold finally releases its grip, after the exhalation becomes a breath again.

Or perhaps they are simply living at the speed their world demands, and waiting is what living here *looks like*.

In any case, they are what lives in the Pellid Reach: beings that have become patient, have become part of the landscape, have learned to measure time in lichen-growth and stone-exhalation. They are the plateau's conversation with itself, the city speaking in a language that takes centuries to form a single word.

And they are still speaking.
