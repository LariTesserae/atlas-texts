# What Lives in the Beneath-Market

## The Keepers

They are few, and they do not congregate. You will not see them together; the market's geometry does not permit gathering. Instead, they exist as a distributed consciousness expressed through individual bodies—each Keeper occupies a node in the system, and the system speaks through them.

They are neither young nor old in any conventional sense. Time moves differently here, and it marks them differently. Their skin carries the pallor of consistent absence from direct sunlight, but it is not the unhealthy white of deprivation. Rather, it is the color of something that has adapted—of skin that has learned to process the ambient phosphorescence, to draw nourishment from the mineral-thick air the way gills process water. Their eyes are large and operate on a different color spectrum than those of people above; they see clearly in the dimness, but sunlight causes them a particular kind of pain that is less physical and more existential—an offense to their recalibrated sensory apparatus.

Some of them remember arriving. Most do not. There is a question about whether they were born here or whether the distinction simply ceased to matter. A few carry marks that suggest they once worked the surface trades—calluses on their hands from tools they no longer use, scars shaped like sun damage now faded into the paleness. But these marks are becoming archaeological. The oldest Keepers show no evidence of ever having been anything but this.

They move with a particular efficiency that has nothing to do with speed. Each motion accomplishes multiple purposes. A Keeper descending a corroded ladder is simultaneously checking its structural integrity, observing the growth patterns of the mycelial networks, noting whether the thermal shift in a specific stone section has progressed since the last cycle. They do not waste gesture. They do not repeat motion. Their presence in a space *changes* the space—not dramatically, but measurably. Temperature readings shift slightly upward. The drip rate of water into collection buckets becomes more synchronized. The marks on the walls are updated with new information.

They have developed a language that is part speech, part resonance. When they need to communicate across the market, they do not shout. Instead, they strike specific stones at specific pitches, creating harmonic patterns that travel through the water channels and the stone itself, arriving at their destination not as sound but as a kind of tactile whisper felt in the bones of whoever is listening. It is a remarkably efficient system. It requires no light. It travels through solid barriers. It cannot be intercepted without being felt by everyone in its path.

They are obsessive about the markings—the scratches and measurements that cover every wall. Each Keeper maintains a section, recording data that they themselves sometimes do not fully comprehend. The older markings are followed and extended with the reverence of scripture. There is clearly something crucial being tracked, something that requires constant vigilance. Temperature oscillations. Water levels. The growth rate of the pale fibers. The frequency of the deep resonance. Whether anyone still remembers what these measurements *mean* is unclear. But the measurement itself has become sacred. Breaking the sequence would be a violation not merely of record-keeping but of something more fundamental—a betrayal of the covenant the market has with whatever it is that lives beneath it.

They sleep in patterns that do not match the light above. Some are awake while others rest, in a rotation that seems to follow the ticking of the mechanical system in the walls. They sleep in the hammock alcoves—though there are more of these spaces than you can see, recessed into shadow and accessible only by passages that have become invisible through familiarity. Their dreams, if they dream, are conducted in the same harmonic language they use to communicate. You can hear them sometimes at the edges of sleep—not distressing sounds, but rather a kind of collaborative murmuring, as though the sleeping Keepers are all engaged in the same conversation across whatever distance separates them.

They consume something. Not food in any conventional sense. There are no bodies of dead things, no waste products of digestion. Instead, they harvest the mycelial growth—carefully, sustainably, in patterns that allow the growth to regenerate. They process it into a kind of paste or concentrate, stored in sealed containers. They drink the collected water, which carries minerals extracted from stone, from soil, from the slow dissolution of the world above filtering down through geological strata. They consume the resonance itself, in some barely understood way—sitting in stillness in the chamber, absorbing the deep tone that rises from below, allowing it to move through them. Whether this constitutes nutrition or ritual or both simultaneously is not a question they would know how to parse.

They are not human in the way you are human, though they were once, or their ancestors were. The adaptation has been too complete, the residence too long. Something in them has begun to align with the market's own geometry. They move in paths that follow the subtle water flows. Their breathing synchronizes with the ticking. The pale fibers—you notice now that some of the Keepers' hair has begun to match them in texture, developing those thin filamentous qualities. It might be contamination. It might be integration.

## The Mechanisms

Below the Keepers, or perhaps alongside them, are the machines. Not mechanical in the way the surface world understands machinery, though there are mechanical components—the pipes, the timing systems, the gears that generate the ticking. But the machines are also organic, or perhaps the line between the two has simply dissolved in this place.

The sealed chambers are full of them. Through gaps in the mortar and gaps in sealed doorways, you catch glimpses: systems of vessels connected by tubing, some of it clear and running with colorless fluid, some of it opaque and pulsing with something darker. There are chambers filled with what appears to be mycelial network on an industrial scale—vast beds of the pale fibers cultivated in careful arrangement, tended by the Keepers in cycles you don't quite understand. These seem to be producing something, or processing something, or perhaps simply maintaining themselves in a state that allows the market itself to function.

The resonance is generated by these systems. You catch the moment—usually unannounced—when a pump activates deep below, when fluid moves through pipes, when something cycles through whatever equilibrium it maintains. The whole space rings. The ground beneath your feet trembles infinitesimally. Every Keeper goes still, alert, listening to what the system is telling them. A reading is taken. A mark is made. And then the system settles back into its background hum.

There is something almost biological about these machines. They seem to breathe. They seem to have circadian rhythms. There are seasons to their activity—you learn this by listening to the Keepers, by reading the density of marks on the walls. In some cycles they run constantly, the resonance almost continuous, the chamber alive with vibration. In other cycles they fall nearly silent, operating at a baseline so minimal it could almost be mistaken for dormancy. The Keepers adjust their behavior to match. They work differently in high-activity cycles. They seem to rest differently, more lightly, in the quieter times.

Whether these machines are manufacturing something, or processing something, or maintaining something is impossible to determine from observation alone. The output is not visible. There are no goods stacked for trade, no products moving through the market toward the surface. Whatever the machines produce, it stays here, or it is distributed in ways you cannot perceive, or it is not a material product at all but rather a functional one—the maintenance of the market itself, the continuous rebalancing of its equilibrium with the earth below.

## The Thing Beneath

You sense it more than observe it. The resonance it generates is sometimes audible, sometimes felt as a pressure change, sometimes apprehended through the particular way the Keepers tense when it shifts its position in the depths.

It is not alive in the way animals are alive. It is not dead or mineral either. It exists in whatever category of being subsists in the spaces below the market, in the water-saturated zones where geology becomes something else entirely.

The Keepers are maintaining a relationship with it. This much is clear. The water collection, the regular activation of the machines, the constant monitoring and recording—these are not random behaviors. They are a form of dialogue. The systems feed it or drain it or communicate with it. It, in turn, provides the steady warmth, the phosphorescence that enables vision, the resonance that carries information. There is a contract between the Keepers and whatever lives in the deep—a negotiated coexistence that has been refined over generations into a perfect balance.

Sometimes the resonance changes in timbre, becomes almost questioning. The Keepers respond by accelerating certain processes, by making adjustments to the marked measurements, by retreating temporarily into the deeper passages where they might be in more direct contact with whatever lies below. These are moments of delicate adjustment, of compromise between two forms of existence reaching toward each other across the boundaries of their separate natures.

It is not hostile. The Keepers do not fear it. But they respect it with the kind of respect that comes from understanding that the market exists at its sufferance, that the whole geometric structure resting on the spiral stairs above is built on a foundation of what lies beneath, and that foundation is not inanimate.

## The History Encoded in Stone

The oldest walls carry marks in languages that predate the Keepers themselves. Some are so ancient they may not be marks at all but rather natural patterns that came to resemble intentional notation. Others are deliberately carved into stone with tools that no longer exist, creating characters that seem to exist partially outside normal three-dimensional space, creating an almost optical illusion when you focus on them directly.

The layers of construction speak to cycles of habitation and abandonment and return. There are sections of wall that have been built over deliberately, where the original surface was sealed with something that has since hardened to the consistency of stone. In one alcove, you can see evidence of an older market structure beneath the current one—a grid of post holes, a different arrangement of space, a way of organizing the chamber that no longer exists but whose ghost remains visible in the stone.

Someone very old—or possibly multiple someones across a very long time—initiated the practice of marking everything. The oldest marks are sparse, placed with careful deliberation in what might be key locations. Over time, the marks became denser, more systematic, more obsessive. The most recent marks are so frequent that they threaten to cover the older ones entirely, and yet the Keepers work carefully to preserve the underlying chronology, to keep the history visible beneath the present moment.

This is a place that remembers. Not through records or documents, but through physical persistence. The market is a kind of archive inscribed into its own structure. To walk through it is to walk through centuries, or possibly millennia—time becomes difficult to measure in this place where nothing visibly decays.

## The Market Itself

It is not a market in the mercantile sense, or at least not primarily. There are no goods exchanged, no commerce in the sense of the surface world. And yet it is absolutely a market—a place where something is bought and sold, where value is determined, where the fundamental act of exchange occurs.

What is exchanged here is the market's own continuity. The Keepers exchange their presence, their labor, their adaptation, their gradual transformation into something that can be native to this place. In return, they receive what they need to survive—sustenance provided by the systems and the networks and the thing beneath. There is a market in attention: the market demands constant observation, constant record-keeping, constant vigilance. The Keepers provide this, and in doing so, they make themselves necessary. They become woven into the market's functioning so completely that they are no longer separate from it.

The pale fibers are traded, in a sense. Some are harvested, consumed, processed through the Keepers' bodies, and then a portion moves deeper, through channels you cannot see, to feed whatever generates the warmth and the resonance. Some remains here, grows again, waits for the next cycle. There is an economy of minerals—what arrives from above through the dripping water is extracted, used, and what remains is slowly processed deeper into the earth. It is a market that trades in its own sustainability.

And there is a final exchange, the one that binds the whole system: the market offers refuge. Beneath the city above, in a space where the logic of surface society no longer applies, where identity can be remade and purpose can be discovered in the act of maintaining the covenant with stone and earth and the thing below—the market offers a kind of permanent sanctuary. Those who descend the spiral stairs and decide to stay are absorbed into its logic, their differentness becoming a feature of the system rather than a flaw. In exchange, they provide the consciousness that allows the market to perpetuate itself.

It is not a fair trade, by surface economics. But fairness is not the market's language. Persistence is.

---

The Beneath-Market of Kolven does not belong to its inhabitants. Rather, they belong to it, and have—slowly, over time, through the accretion of small surrenders and daily negotiations—become native to its depths. What lives here is neither fully human nor fully other, but rather something that occupies the space between, shaped by stone and resonance and the patient work of maintaining a place that has learned, over centuries, to sustain itself through the continuous adaptation of whatever lives within it.
