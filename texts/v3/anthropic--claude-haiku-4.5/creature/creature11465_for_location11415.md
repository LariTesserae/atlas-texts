# What Lives Here

The *residents* of the sealed galleries are not visitors. They have never been visitors. They are the galleries' own slow thought made manifest.

The pale creatures—those jointless things that move like deliberation through the fronds—are perhaps the most obviously alive in any sense you recognize, yet they are the least distinct from their environment. They seem not to hunt or gather so much as to *participate*, their movements part of the galleries' perpetual circulation. When you watch one long enough, you realize it's not traveling somewhere; it's bleeding into the rhythm of its section of stone. Its pale exoskeleton catches the light from the ancient shafts, and for a moment you cannot tell if the creature is moving or if the stone is moving around it. They are the galleries remembering what motion feels like.

The colonial organisms—those ambiguous clusters rooted to the vegetation—are perhaps the true dominant form here. They exist in a state of such profound stillness that you begin to wonder if "living" is even the right framework. They filter. They accumulate. They excrete substances that feed the glaze on the stone. Some of them are meters across, sprawled across the chambers like thoughts that have been held so long they've become permanent. Others are barely visible, scattered through the air as spore-seeds waiting for the right hollow, the right current-path, the right geological moment. They are the galleries' memory. They are the archive of what has occurred here across the millennia—each cluster a record, written in calcium and silence.

The vegetation itself occupies a category that shouldn't exist but does. It is *almost* plant, almost almost something else. It grows not toward light but into it—those fronds in the illuminated shafts have learned to drink photons so thin they're barely there, converting them into a kind of sustenance that might be energy, might be information. The coral-like formations that spiral from the stone walls are producing something: a slick, a glaze, a secretion that hardens into the lacquered surfaces you see everywhere. They are not separate from the stone; they are the stone's way of tending itself, of maintaining its own skin. The fleshy organs—those swollen, yielding clusters—are decomposing and rebuilding matter in cycles so slow they constitute geology. They are the galleries digesting time itself.

And then there are the ones you haven't identified. The ones that might be fungi. The ones that might be something the galleries evolved to fill a niche you don't have a word for. The soft things that seem to pulse with a rhythm that doesn't match your heartbeat—that matches, perhaps, the stone's slow settling, the breath of rock cooling across millennia.

The builders—those who raised or grew the structures—are perhaps still here, though not in any form that would be *obvious* to you. They may have become the structures themselves. They may have been absorbed into the colony organisms, their purpose now indistinguishable from the purpose of mineral deposition. Or they may have been a phase, a temporary architecture of thought that the galleries enacted and then moved beyond. The structures remain either as monuments or as tools still being used—you cannot determine which, and perhaps the distinction never mattered. The galleries do not distinguish between what serves a function and what *is* function.

What lives here lives in the *plural sense*—not as individuals but as a continuous, unified process of being. A colony-consciousness that thinks in stone and mineral. A slow-time ecology where reproduction and death happen on scales so extended that a creature might live for centuries and produce offspring only once, and that offspring might not be born but rather *become* — emerging from the accumulation of chemical circumstance into something recognizable as separate, for a time, before it dissolves back into the general process.

They do not know they are alive, possibly. The word "alive" assumes interruption, assumes a boundary between self and world. Here, that boundary is ceremonial at best. The creature moving through the fronds does not know it is moving. The colony-organism does not know it is filtering. The vegetation does not know it is growing. All of them are executing a logic so perfectly embedded in their environment that there is no visible strain, no visible awareness, no visible *choice*.

But they are aware, perhaps, in a way that operates beneath consciousness. They respond. They change course when obstacles present themselves. They synchronize. When the air shifts slightly in one chamber, the vegetation in a distant hollow responds, its fronds settling into a new configuration that will persist for decades. This is communication. This is decision-making. It simply does not require intention.

The true resident of the sealed galleries—the one that subsumes all others—is the galleries themselves. The system. The interlocked process of stone and moisture, of slow chemical conversation, of time moving at a pace so different from the surface world that it amounts to a different dimension of existence. Everything that lives here is living *as* the galleries, expressing the galleries' logic through its own form.

You are not inside the lungs of the world. You are not in the world's digestive tract. You are inside the world's nervous system—one so slow, so distributed, so fundamentally alien that you cannot perceive the thoughts being thought through it. The creatures are neurons. The structures are patterns of connection. The stone itself is the substrate of cognition.

And the galleries have been thinking the same thought for forty thousand years, without urgency, without fatigue, without anything remotely resembling frustration. It is a patient thought. A recursive thought. A thought about the nature of time itself.

That is what lives here. That is all that lives here.

And it will continue living here long after everything above has forgotten that anything existed below.
