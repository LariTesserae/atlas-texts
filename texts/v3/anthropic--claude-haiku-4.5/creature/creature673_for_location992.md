# The Dwellers of Teth

The merchants are the most obvious inhabitants, and yet they are only the visible fraction of what lives here.

They are thin—not from deprivation, but from adaptation. Their bodies have become efficient in this space, their movements economical. They have learned to exist in the half-light; their eyes have developed a particular quality, a way of seeing that privileges motion and nearness over distance and detail. They are pale not from illness but from years of living beyond the sun's reach. Their skin takes on the quality of old parchment, luminous in its own way, as though the absence of direct light has made them slightly translucent. They do not age as quickly as those above—or perhaps they age differently, the deep lines around their eyes and mouths telling stories of time rather than deterioration. They are merchants by necessity and temperament both; they understand negotiation not as an art but as a language native to the close quarters of the cavern. They know the economics of scarcity and abundance in this sealed space. They are shrewd without cruelty, measured without coldness. Many have never seen the surface world. Their children, born here, consider the cavern normal. Their grandchildren cannot imagine otherwise.

The customers form a second, less obvious population.

They are regulars—people who live in other chambers, or in sections of the cavern system beyond what is visible from this market floor. They arrive with baskets and knowledge, moving through the passages as though reading a familiar text. They are not wealthy; the goods they purchase are modest, the exchanges quick and practiced. Many are thin in the same way the merchants are thin, their bodies having made the same adjustments. But others are rounder, heavier, suggesting they eat more substantially or exercise less. Some bear the marks of particular labor—calloused hands, backs bent from stooping in lower passages, skin marked by minor burns or cuts from tools. They are miners, possibly, or cultivators of the pale plants, or craftspeople who work in chambers deeper still. They are quieter than those above would be; the cavern has taught them that sound travels strangely in confined spaces, and that excessive noise is a form of disrespect to the shared air. They greet merchants by name sometimes, or with a nod that indicates long familiarity. They are the everyday life of this place.

Behind the stalls, there is a third order of inhabitant.

These are the ones who maintain the market itself—the craftspeople who keep the wooden structures sound, who tend the oil lamps, who manage the water systems that keep the stone from becoming too slick or too dry. They work partially in view, partially in the passages and chambers beyond. They are the most skilled with tools, their hands bearing the permanent marks of their trade. There is a kind of quiet authority about them, earned through years of understanding how this space *wants* to be maintained. They know the temperament of the wood at different moisture levels, the behavior of the stone when water has been moving through it for centuries, the ways the light needs to be distributed for the plants to survive and for the work of commerce to proceed. They are almost invisible in their ordinariness, yet nothing functions without them.

The animals form a fourth population, neither domesticated nor wild, but something in between.

The pale fowl are not native to the cavern; they were brought down generations ago and have since adapted to perpetual twilight. Their feathers have become translucent because there is no predator to hide from, no sun-bright landscape to vanish into. They are smaller than surface birds of their type, more efficient in their movements. They eat from troughs filled with seeds and fermented matter. They produce eggs—you can see baskets of them at certain stalls—and their presence serves multiple purposes: food, yes, but also the management of insects, the creation of ambient sound that makes the space feel lived-in rather than mausoleum-like. The goats are similarly adapted, their long coats insulating them in the constant cool-warmth of the cavern. They eat the dried plants, the vegetable scraps from the market stalls. They are docile, less alert than surface goats, their eyes having learned to see in dimness. They produce milk, possibly cheese—you can smell it at some of the stalls, a sharp, ancient smell. They have never known open sky or pasture.

The insects are more numerous than they first appear.

The pale roaches are not vermin but rather a form of scavenger that has evolved to suit this environment. They are drawn to organic matter, breaking it down, returning it to the ecosystem of the cavern. They are tolerated because they are necessary, because an enclosed space cannot support traditional waste removal. They are everywhere—on the floor, on the base of stalls, in the composting areas where food scraps are carefully managed. They work at night and during the quietest hours, becoming less visible when human activity increases. They are part of the market's equilibrium, neither loved nor hated but understood as fundamental. There are other insects too, smaller ones, almost invisible: things that live in the wood, breaking it down with geological patience. The craftspeople manage these carefully, using treatments made from plants that grow in other cavern sections, maintaining a balance where the wood decays slowly enough to be useful.

Deeper still—and you sense this rather than see it—there are other inhabitants.

There are people in the passages beyond the market, in chambers you haven't entered. The sound of chiseling suggests that the cavern system is still being actively shaped, still being expanded or refined. These are miners, possibly, or sculptors of a kind, working at the very slow pace that characterizes all work here. They are solitary people, mostly, or work in small groups. They understand the stone intimately, knowing which areas can be carved and which are structural, where the water runs and where it pools. They are the ones who understand the cavern's slow breathing, the almost imperceptible shifts in air pressure and temperature. They work because the market requires space, because the population slowly grows, because ambition in this enclosed world expresses itself through expansion.

There are also people you don't see at all, but whose presence is evident: the cultivators.

Somewhere in other chambers, in spaces with access to water and a particular configuration of stone that creates the right conditions, people grow the pale plants that feed this population. They work in near-total darkness, in spaces where not even the oil lamps of the market penetrate. They have become something between gardeners and mystics, understanding plants through touch and smell and the memory of watching their growth patterns over years. They harvest carefully, replanting to ensure continuity. The bunches of papery leaves you see at the stalls are their product. The vined plants trained along the supports are their expertise made architectural.

And beyond even this: there are the ones who maintain the water systems.

The constant sound of dripping, of slow flow, is not random. It is managed. Somewhere, there are channels cut through stone, some natural and some deliberately widened, directing water to where it's needed—to the plant troughs, to basins where it can be drawn for cooking and drinking, away from areas where it might undermine the structure. These are the people who understand hydrology in its most literal, most essential form. They are among the most crucial inhabitants, though they may be the least visible. They work in darkness deeper than that of the market floor, moving through passages where water echoes constantly. They are part myth to the regular market visitors—people known to exist but rarely encountered.

The children form a fifth, almost separate population.

They are born here, many of them, or brought here so young that the surface world is mythology rather than memory. They play in the narrow passages—games designed for confined spaces, for twilight, for a world where vertical space is limited but horizontal space permits labyrinthine exploration. They are thinner than children above, their eyes larger in proportion to their faces, adapted for dim light. They are quieter than surface children, having absorbed the cavern's acoustic etiquette early. Some will become merchants, some craftspeople, some cultivators or miners or water-keepers. Some will leave, ascending the stone steps to the world above, never to return. Others will stay and become the next generation of equilibrium.

The elderly are present too, though they move more slowly through the passages.

They are repositories of knowledge—they remember when particular sections were added to the market, when certain merchants first arrived, when a particular water system was modified. They sit at stalls sometimes, or in the quieter passages, watching. They are consulted when problems arise, when decisions about expansion or maintenance need to be made. They are thin like the others, pale like the others, but marked additionally by time—their hands gnarled, their movements deliberate. They represent continuity in a place where continuity is measured in generations of careful, patient work.

At the deepest level, there are the ones whose role is almost ceremonial, almost spiritual.

They are not priests—this doesn't seem to be a place with formal religion—but rather people who understand the cavern as a living system, as something with its own logic and requirements. They make decisions about when to expand, when to consolidate, when to rest and maintain rather than grow. They are chosen by consensus, or they emerge naturally through a process no one quite articulates. They walk through the market and the passages, they listen, they observe. They are the stewards of equilibrium.

What lives in the Cavernous Market of Teth, ultimately, is not a collection of separate individuals but a kind of distributed organism. The merchants, customers, craftspeople, animals, insects, miners, cultivators, water-keepers, children, elders, stewards—all of these are cells in a larger body that is the market itself. They know this without saying it. They have learned to move through space with others constantly nearby. They have internalized the patience required for an enclosed world. They have made the stone and wood their own, and in return, the stone and wood have made them into something new—something that could not exist on the surface, in open air, under the weight and clarity of the sun.

They are the dwellers of Teth. They are what the cavern has become, and what has become the cavern.
