# The Inhabitants of Threadwick

They are, first and foremost, **accumulators**. The people of Threadwick—and there are people, though you must look carefully to see them—have adapted to a landscape that refuses to stabilize, and so they themselves have become creatures of perpetual collection. They gather things. Not obsessively, not pathologically, but as a fundamental survival strategy. Materials, yes: salvaged brick, reclaimed timber, copper wiring stripped from defunct installations. But also information. Techniques that work in this saturated cold. Rhythms of the machinery when it does run. The habits of the fish in the channels, the patterns of the rats, which sections of ground are least likely to settle catastrophically beneath a heavy load. They carry notebooks—leather-bound and swollen with moisture, the pages rippled and difficult to turn—filled with observations that seem mundane until you realize they represent decades of cumulative knowledge about living here.

The people themselves are **lean and deliberate**. Not thin from deprivation, but economical in movement. They move through Threadwick with a particular kind of grace born from navigating unstable ground, from ducking under low-hanging vines, from understanding instinctively where weight can be placed without danger. Their clothing is layered and waterproofed—wool beneath canvas and treated linen, in shades of grey and rust and deep green. Nothing is sewn to be beautiful, but nothing is crude either. Every garment shows the mark of repair and adaptation: patches overlap in patterns that suggest multiple winters' worth of mending. Their hands are always slightly damp, the skin around their nails darkened with a permanent staining that no amount of washing removes. You suspect they no longer try.

**The builders** form a visible subset—they're the ones you see most frequently, moving between structures on planks and rope-bridges, their movements assured despite the slickness of everything. They wear reinforced aprons studded with tools, and they move with a peculiar kind of intuition, as if they can read the ground's mood before it shifts. They're not constructing in any conventional sense; they're **maintaining a conversation** with the landscape itself. A section of wall goes up only after the ground beneath has been tested for six weeks. Scaffolding is placed not where the architect intended, but where the wood "wants" to bear weight. When they work—and you'll see them working at odd hours, with no apparent schedule—they work in near-silence, passing tools hand-to-hand, adjusting positions by increments you can barely perceive. Their work proceeds at the speed of the landscape itself, which is to say: slowly, inexorably, with no apparent urgency and no possibility of rushing.

**The watchers** are more difficult to identify because they could be anyone, but they're present—stationed at high points in the scaffolding, in the upper stories of buildings, on rooftops. They're not guards; there's no sense of security or defense. Rather, they're **monitoring**. Watching for subsidence. Tracking the water levels in the channels. Noting which sections of vegetation have grown differently than expected. When they spot something, they don't shout—they signal with hand-movements so understated you wonder if you're imagining the communication. They maintain a constant, low-level awareness of the city's condition, the way a living thing maintains awareness of its own body. Some of them have been stationed in the same location for so long that moss grows on the rails beside them, and you wonder if they can still move or if they've simply become part of the structure.

**The technicians**—those who work with the machinery—are a different temperament entirely. More restless. Less integrated into the landscape. You see them periodically examining the dormant equipment, running their hands over valves and gauges, consulting charts and diagrams that are themselves corroded and difficult to read. They seem frustrated, or perhaps resigned. The machinery doesn't work reliably here, not anymore, and you sense they've accepted this fundamental truth while simultaneously refusing to let it go. They move between the factories with a purposeful urgency that seems anachronistic in a place where everything else moves at the landscape's pace. Some of them are younger—you can tell by the way they move, the way they still expect the machines to obey them. The older ones have developed a different relationship: they coax, cajole, negotiate. They've learned to work *with* the machinery's unreliability rather than against it. They're the ones most likely to be found at odd hours, performing obscure maintenance rituals that might have no practical purpose except that they've always been done, and in Threadwick, you don't abandon practices that once worked.

**The cultivators** tend to the strange vegetation with a specificity that suggests long breeding programs, deliberate selection. You'll see them in the courtyards where the luminescent-barked saplings grow, examining buds with magnifying lenses, making notes, sometimes carefully pruning with tools that seem too delicate for their rough hands. They move between the wild growth and the constructed gardens without distinction—the landscape here doesn't really allow for such boundaries. Some of them appear to be engaged in something closer to engineering than agriculture: they'll place physical supports at precise angles beneath growing vines, stake out sections of moss to allow specific growth patterns, even appear to be *grafting* the strange saplings onto older root systems. You don't understand the purpose, but you recognize the intentionality. One of them—an older person, gender indeterminate—spends entire days standing motionless before a single patch of luminescent bark, as if engaged in deep observation or even communion. When she finally moves, she makes minute adjustments to the ground around it.

**The children** of Threadwick are a revelation. There are fewer of them than you'd expect, and they're raised with a peculiar kind of freedom and constraint simultaneously. Free to roam the saturated landscape—you see them playing in the channels, diving for the pale fish, scaling the scaffolding with casual disregard for danger that makes your stomach clench. But they're also clearly schooled in observation and caution. They move through dangerous spaces with the unconscious competence of people who learned their environment's rhythms before they learned language. They carry small nets and collection bags, and they're always gathering: specimens of the strange moss, samples of water from different channels (for purposes you can't discern), discarded bits of machinery they seem to catalogue. You realize they're not playing—they're learning. The play *is* the learning. One child spends an afternoon following the rats through the buildings, not hunting them but apparently taking notes on their movements, their preferences, their social structure. When another child approaches, she hisses at them to be silent—there's a hierarchy of observation here, and the observation has priority.

**The sick** are visible but rarely acknowledged. You notice them in the upper stories of the damper buildings, or in rooms where the light is carefully managed. They're not quarantined; they move freely, but their movements are slow, deliberate, and they breathe differently—shallow, careful, as if the air itself requires negotiation. The affliction seems to be moisture-related: their skin has a peculiar sheen, and when they cough, there's a wet, resonant quality that suggests fluid where there shouldn't be. But they're cared for without apparent drama. People bring them heated liquids in cups that steam with eucalyptus and other herbs. No one speaks of recovery or prognosis. It's accepted that some people adjust to Threadwick by slowly becoming part of it.

**The old** are integrated into the city's structure with a literal permanence. There are residents who haven't moved from their homes in decades—you can tell by the way the buildings seem to have grown around them, the way the vegetation has learned their routines, the way younger residents consult them for information with a deference that suggests they're less people than they are archives. One such figure—hunched, barely mobile—sits in a room on the second story of what was once a textile factory, and people visit regularly with questions: which sections of which buildings settled most in the great subsidence of winter-before-last? How much should new construction account for vertical shift? What was the color of that lichen before it changed? These old people carry the accumulated knowledge of decades, embedded in their bodies and memories in ways that written records cannot capture.

**The artists**—and there are those you'd identify as such—work in subtle ways. You see them inscribing patterns in the moss using cleared spaces, creating temporary designs that will overgrow within days. Others craft elaborate structures from bone and twisted wire, positioning them at angles where the light diffuses in specific ways. One person has spent considerable time arranging the smaller stones along the water channels in mathematical patterns you don't fully comprehend. Their work has no obvious function and persists very briefly, and yet they create with utmost seriousness. When you ask someone about it, they simply say: "We make marks. The city acknowledges marks." Whether this is literal or metaphorical remains unclear.

**The solitary ones** are scattered throughout—you notice them because they move differently from the others. They don't participate in the collective labor of maintenance and negotiation with the landscape. Instead, they seem to have established private relationships with specific sections of Threadwick. One person visits the same stretch of water channel every evening and sits motionless for hours. Another has claimed a small section of roofing where they've arranged objects in patterns that seem to shift gradually—are they being rearranged, or is the perspective changing? These individuals are tolerated, even respected, as if their individual obsessions with specific places are seen as contributing something necessary to the city's overall stability.

**The newcomers** are recognizable by their inefficiency. They're learning—learning the ground, learning the patterns, learning the unwritten rules of how to exist here. They move more cautiously, less surely. They carry more gear than is necessary. They ask questions, though the phrasing suggests they know the answers will be incomplete. They're absorbed into the collective labor gradually, given tasks that require observation more than strength: counting moss varieties, tracking water levels, watching for subsidence. Some will stay. Some will leave after a season or two, unable to adjust to the pace, the cold, the fundamental instability. Those who stay develop a particular expression over time—not happiness, but a kind of hard-won acceptance that manifests as a very slight softening around the eyes.

**The temperament** of Threadwick's people is, collectively, one of **ritualized pragmatism**. There's no sense of either desperation or contentment. Rather, there's an acceptance of their circumstances combined with an absolute refusal to abandon them. When something fails, they don't mourn it; they incorporate it into the landscape. When something succeeds, they don't celebrate; they document it. There's a quiet humor that emerges in unexpected places: you'll overhear jokes at the communal meals (which happen in what was once a factory dining hall, now converted to a vast space where people gather to eat preserved foods and drink hot liquid), but the jokes are often about the shared adversity, and the laughter is brief and knowing.

**What they are, fundamentally**, is **adapted**. Not in the evolutionary sense—though there may be traces of that too—but in the practical sense. They've adapted their bodies to the cold, their minds to the instability, their social structures to the demands of continuous negotiation with a landscape that refuses to be conquered or settled. They've adapted their expectations downward—not in a defeated way, but realistically. They've adapted their timescales: thinking in seasons and decades rather than days and years. They've adapted their sense of progress: understanding that forward motion looks like standing still when you're maintaining equilibrium against constant pressure.

And they persist. Quietly, methodically, with worn hands and careful eyes, they persist in Threadwick—not thriving exactly, but continuing. Making their marks on a city that slowly erases them. Building on ground that slowly subsides. Growing food in channels where strange pale fish move unseen. Tending machinery that barely works. Raising children who will inherit this negotiation with the landscape, and who will pass it on to the next generation, and the next, for as long as anyone continues to believe that Threadwick is worth the price of remaining.
