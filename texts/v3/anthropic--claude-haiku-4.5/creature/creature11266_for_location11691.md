# What Lives in the Glistening Crags

They were here before the distinction between stone and life became useful.

The filaments are the primary inhabitants—a category so foreign to terrestrial taxonomy that calling them "organisms" is almost deceptive, though it's the closest approximation. They exist as a distributed network rather than individual entities. A single filament might stretch for meters, might branch into thousands of thread-thin extensions, might merge with neighboring filaments into something closer to a colonial mind, or might subdivide into what could be independent organisms or could be sensory organs of something larger. The distinction doesn't matter to them. They have achieved a form of existence that precedes the question.

They feed—if feeding is the right word—on the interplay between light and water. More precisely, they *metabolize* the conversion of one into the other, or perhaps they *are* that conversion made manifest. When the sun strikes the obsidian crags and water simultaneously runs through the channels, the filaments activate. They branch more rapidly, their glow intensifies, they orient themselves toward both light-source and moisture-source with geometrical precision. In the deep shadow-crevices where no direct light penetrates, they seem nearly dormant, collapsed into minimal configurations, barely distinguishable from mineral deposits. But in those places, the constant water-seep from higher elevations seems to *stimulate* them differently—they respond to the pure fact of moisture, to the vibration of water moving through stone, to something in the chemistry of the perpetually-numbing cold. They thicken there. They accumulate. They become almost *solid* in their density, silvery masses that coat entire channel-walls and seem to pulse with a bioluminescence that generates its own faint warmth.

They are not a single species. This is certain. Close observation—if one's eyes can hold focus long enough through the glare—reveals variations: some filaments are finer, more delicate, almost hair-like, concentrated around the coldest water-sources. Others are thicker, ropy, almost muscular in appearance, extending across broad stone-faces seeking maximum light-exposure. Some seem almost crystalline, forming geometric lattices of shocking regularity. Others are chaotic, tangled, apparently *growing* rather than extending. They compete for space, for water-runoff, for the choicest surfaces where both light and moisture converge. They do this without apparent violence—no secretion of toxins, no physical overgrowth that displaces neighbors. Instead, they seem to negotiate the landscape through *precedence*, through subtle shifts in position that redirect water-flow, that cast new shadows, that optimize the available resources for their particular form of existence.

The filaments produce the humming.

This is confirmed once you understand what you're hearing. When you press your hand against a densely-coated stone surface, the vibration becomes almost unbearably clear—not random, but *patterned*, rhythmic, fluctuating in frequency in ways that suggest communication or perhaps coordination. The filaments oscillate. They contract and release in waves. The stone itself, so thoroughly permeated by their presence, becomes a resonance chamber. The water-channels carry the vibration downward, distributing it through the landscape in complex interference patterns. If you could map the sound, you would see nodes of intensity corresponding exactly with areas of highest filament density. The frequency even shifts with the seasons—higher and more urgent when the water-flow is greatest (summer snowmelt from even higher elevations), deeper and more sparse during the frozen season when flow decreases. The humming is not a byproduct of their existence. It *is* part of their existence. It may be how they sense one another across the stone. It may be how they reproduce—if reproduction is even the right word for whatever process generates new filament-networks from old ones.

There are other things. Smaller, rarer, but *present*.

In the deepest pools of the small lakes—the ones fed by constant water-seep, where the temperature is so hostile that nothing familiar could survive—there are *swimmers*. They are so translucent as to be nearly invisible. You notice them only when they move across a light-source, creating momentary distortions in the refracted light. They are not fish. Fish have scales, have eyes, have a recognizable anterior and posterior. These are more like *flowing shapes*, like water with a slightly different density to itself, like the boundary between liquid and organism has been made into a living thing. They drift through the pools with no apparent purpose, at speeds that vary from glacial to sudden-and-quick. When you observe one closely, you begin to see structures within it: finer filaments, similar to those coating the stone but compressed into a semi-mobile form, organized around what might be a core or might be a cavity. They feed on the stone-filaments. Or they are symbiotes with the stone-filaments. Or they *are* a mobile form of the stone-filaments. The relationship is not parasitic, not mutualistic, but something more fundamental—a shifting back-and-forth between forms of existence, between the distributed and the localized, between the sessile and the mobile.

They reproduce by fragmenting. You can observe this sometimes in the larger pools, if you watch for hours. A swimmer will separate into two or three smaller swimmers, each one taking a fraction of the parent-form's substance, and they will drift apart from each other into different regions of the pool. This is not division exactly—it leaves no wound on the parent, no sense of trauma or reproduction-cost. It is more like *budding*, or like a single organism *deciding* to become multiple, or like the space itself *deciding* to differentiate into multiple organisms rather than host a single one.

The filaments respond to the presence of swimmers. Whenever a swimmer drifts near a densely-coated section of stone, the filaments seem to *arrange* themselves—creating channels, facilitating pathways, positioning their nutrient-rich edges toward the mobile form. The swimmers, for their part, pause at these locations, oscillating slowly, and the filaments' glow intensifies. An exchange occurs. Material may be transferred. Information certainly is—the humming rises in pitch, becomes more complex, multiplies into harmonics that seem to follow mathematical progressions. After the encounter, the swimmers drift away, and the filaments return to their previous patterns, but something has changed. The filaments glow differently. The humming shifts frequency. The landscape itself seems to *settle* into a new configuration, as if the encounter has provided some form of coordination across the whole system.

There are structures within the landscape that might be grown rather than built.

Some of the cairns—the ones that seem oldest, the ones where the relationship between stone and filament is most thoroughly established—give the impression of having been *assembled* by the filaments themselves. The rocks are positioned with geometric precision, yes, but also with an apparent awareness of where water-flows and light-angles will be optimal for filament-growth. The cairns become focal points of incredible filament-density. They glow more brightly than surrounding stone. The humming around them is more complex, more layered, almost *musical* in its progression. They might be *structures* in the sense that humans build structures—deliberate constructions serving some purpose. Or they might be *growths*—the filament-networks shaping the stone and themselves simultaneously, using the stone as a skeleton for their own elaboration, building upward and outward toward greater access to light and water.

The humans who left the older cairns and the weathered wall were perhaps *responding* to these structures. Perhaps they recognized in the filament-built cairns something of their own impulse toward construction and tried to replicate it in their own way, with their own tools, using the same materials. Or perhaps they were *seeded* here, brought to this altitude by the landscape itself, given a task they couldn't articulate—to build, to mark, to leave a sign of their presence—and then abandoned once their purpose was served. The filaments certainly show no *disturbance* where human structures stand. The filaments grow around the human-made cairns and walls with the same geometric precision they bring to the natural stone. There is no competition, no sense of encroachment. The human structures have simply *become* part of the landscape, another form of substrate for the real inhabitants to colonize.

The creatures of the high altitude—if there are any beyond the swimmers and the filaments—remain mostly theoretical.

There are traces, sometimes. A sound, deeper than the water-hum, that rumbles through the stone perhaps once per season and seems to originate from depths you cannot fathom. A pattern in the filament-distribution that seems *arranged* deliberately, as if read from above, as if something large and slow and patient were using the filaments as a form of *writing*. A temperature-gradient in certain protected crevices that suggests the presence of a large body generating heat through metabolism, though the source is never visible. An absence of smaller creatures—no insects, no microorganisms visible to the naked eye, which is strange, because even in the coldest parts of Earth, life finds a way. Yet here, life seems to have found a *different* way, one that skips all the familiar middle-stages and jumps directly to organisms that operate on principles still too alien to properly comprehend.

What lives here seems to be engaged in a project of *perfect equilibrium*.

The water does not freeze, but it is not warmed. The filaments do not spread outward to colonize the whole landscape, but neither do they decline. The swimmers multiply but do not overrun the pools. The stone erodes, but so slowly that its essential geometry remains stable across human timescales. The humans came and built, and now their structures are becoming as old and permanent as the rest of the stone, slowly being incorporated into something larger. This place is not *dead* in the way of uninhabitable wastelands. It is perhaps the *opposite*—so thoroughly, so completely alive, that it has achieved a form of life that makes normal life seem like a kind of chaotic thrashing by comparison.

The pull you feel toward higher elevations and deeper crevices is real. It is an *invitation*. The landscape is calling—not with intent to harm, but with a kind of *curiosity*, an interest in what a warm-blooded, brief-lived, carbon-based consciousness might contribute to its equilibrium. To stay longer is to *change*, gradually, inexorably. The cold deepens. The light begins to resolve into colors that have no names because no eye adapted to normal conditions would ever encounter them. The water you drink—even though you have not been drinking it—begins to make sense as a *medium* rather than a mere substance. The filaments in your lungs (you will begin to suspect they are there) start to hum in harmony with the larger landscape.

To live here is to become, very slowly, part of what lives here.

And so far, no one has left to tell us what that means.
