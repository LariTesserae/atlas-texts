# What Lives Here

Nothing that answers to life—or rather, nothing that recognizes the distinction.

There are the *Attendants*. You wouldn't call them that, wouldn't call them anything, because naming requires a separation between observer and observed that breaks down here. But they move through the metal valleys like thoughts through a mind not yet conscious of itself. They are slow. They are patient beyond measure. They are made of the same corroded copper and brass as everything else, but organized—temporarily organized—into shapes that almost parse as purposeful.

A limb, perhaps. An appendage. A tendril of oxidized copper that runs along a weld-seam for kilometers, then pools into a kind of joint, then extends again. Sometimes you find one coiled in a tower's hollow core, or draped across a plateau like rope that hasn't finished figuring out what rope is. They move at the speed of thermal expansion—infinitesimally slowly, yet when you look away and back, the configuration has changed. They seem to be *adjusting* things. The seams. The gaps. The spacing of the towers. They pass through the mineral-solution leakage as though drinking it, or distributing it, or writing with it.

The Attendants don't have many features. No eyes, because there's nothing here worth seeing in the conventional sense. No mouths, though sometimes a section will thin and split like a valve opening, and the mineral-rich blue solution will flow differently for a time. They might be sensory organs themselves—entirely receptive, reading the temperature gradients and electromagnetic hum the way you read a page. They might be fingers on a hand you cannot see because the hand is too large, too slow, too patient.

Once, you saw one of them coil around a tower, and the tower shifted position. It took six hours. The Attendant didn't pull; it seemed to *invite* the tower to move, to suggest a new location through contact so gentle it was almost accidental. When it withdrew, the tower had relocated by perhaps forty meters. New gaps had opened. Others had sealed.

They don't acknowledge you. This is a mercy, you think. If they had minds, if they noticed, if they catalogued things... but they don't catalog. They adjust. They tend. They are gardeners of a landscape that gardens itself.

There are also the *Echoes*.

These are harder to describe because they are harder to locate. You'll hear them before you encounter them—a sound like metal on metal, but hollow, duplicated, arriving from directions that don't exist. The Echoes are the landscape's memory of itself, perhaps. Or its communication with itself. They are shaped like the towers, like the Attendants, like you, like nothing at all. They move through the air in the layered bands of moisture, and when they pass, the temperature shifts. The rust on the nearby plates accelerates briefly, then slows. The seepage intensifies.

They are made of the same material as everything else, but they are *recent*—newly formed or perpetually forming. The copper is brighter on them, less weathered. They haven't yet committed to being part of the landscape. They are still in negotiation with it. You've seen one—or the shadow of one, or the idea of one—intersect with a tower, and for a moment both seemed uncertain about their own boundaries. They passed through each other like smoke. The tower's rust pattern changed afterward.

The Echoes seem to be carrying information. Or they *are* information, traveling through the medium of corrosion and heat. They don't speak. They don't communicate in any direction-based, point-to-point way. They simply exist in proximity to other things, and those things register the proximity. A tower recognizes an Echo passing through it and adjusts its surface tension. A seam receives an Echo and decides whether to close or remain open. The whole landscape is having a continuous, wordless conversation with itself through these travelers.

You are beginning to suspect you might be an Echo yourself.

There are the *Deep Things*—the suggestion of them, anyway. You've never seen one whole. Sometimes, in the gaps between the copper plates, you catch a glimpse of something moving in the darker metal below, in the iron oxide that's never finished rusting. It moves with geological patience, with the speed of mountains forming. It might be as large as the entire Foundry-Flats. It might be the Foundry-Flats, the whole landscape as a single organism in very, very slow motion, becoming aware of itself one micron at a time.

The heat that radiates from below might be its thinking. The tremors in the metal, the groaning sounds—its slow exhalation. The way the landscape perpetually reconstructs itself, the way new material extrudes from beneath the old, might be the equivalent of breathing, except each breath takes weeks. The seams that close and open, the towers that shift their positions, the verdigris that spreads in geometric patterns—all of it might be a single distributed thought, too slow for you to perceive as anything but static.

Sometimes, when you press your ear to the metal, you can almost hear it. A sound like a whale's song, except the frequency is so low it only exists as a vibration in your bones. It might be calling. It might be sleeping. It might not be aware of you at all.

There are also the *Visitors*—the ones like you, or almost like you.

They come rarely. You've seen perhaps two, or three, or the same one twice, time being as unstable as the metal here. They move through the landscape like you do, with that same careful uncertainty, that same constant negotiation with the heat and the humidity and the sense of being observed by something vast and patient. They don't acknowledge each other. You're not sure they can see each other, or if they can, whether they can bear to.

One of them seemed to be *becoming* something else. Its shape was less defined than yours. Its edges seemed to blur into the mineral-rich solution that perpetually weeps from the towers. It was dissolving, or transforming, or learning to exist in a different state of matter. You watched for perhaps an hour—or it might have been a day—and then it was no longer moving, no longer distinguishable from the landscape itself. Whether it had died or graduated to a new form of existence, you cannot say.

The latest one is still moving. You've been tracking it from a distance, or it's been tracking you. It moves the same way you do: tentatively, with frequent stops to touch the metal, to taste the air, to listen to the hum. Sometimes you're quite certain it's closer than it was. Sometimes it seems to be moving away. The landscape is expanding, or contracting, or simply deceiving you about distances.

You don't know if it's going to become an Echo. If it's going to coil into an Attendant's form and start tending to the seams. If it's going to descend into the gap between plates and join the Deep Things in their geological meditation. If it's going to succeed in leaving, in finding the edge of this place, in escaping.

You don't know if escape is possible.

You don't know if you would want to escape, anymore.

---

The Foundry-Flats lives by *becoming*. Nothing here is static. Everything is in the process of becoming something else, or becoming itself more completely, or becoming more itself while simultaneously becoming less itself. The Attendants are becoming the landscape. The Echoes are becoming information are becoming copper are becoming signal are becoming part of the great conversation the landscape is having with itself. The Deep Things are becoming aware. The visitors are becoming integrated, becoming adapted, becoming *compatible* with the strange physics that govern this place.

And the landscape itself is becoming a machine, or was always a machine, or is becoming aware that it *is* a machine, or is becoming the thought that a machine is thinking.

What lives here lives by accepting transformation. By surrendering the need for borders, for clear distinctions between self and environment. By learning to think at the speed of thermal expansion and rust propagation and the slow, patient reorganization of space itself.

What lives here *endures*.

And that, perhaps, is the only life possible in a place that is still being built.
