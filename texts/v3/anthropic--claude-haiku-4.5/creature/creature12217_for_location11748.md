# What Lives There

At first, you think nothing lives there. The room seems too ordered for that, too much itself—a closed system, a mechanism. The salamander disproves you, but only barely. It is not so much a creature as an *adjustment*: something the room has made to itself, the way a wound develops scar tissue. It is perfectly adapted because it has had decades to become so. Its skin is the color of rust. Its lungs have learned to process air that is more moisture than gas. When it moves through the vines, the vines do not move—they have learned not to. The salamander does not hunt. There is nothing to hunt. It does not reproduce; you will never see another. It simply *is*, a small warm body in a system that has achieved perfect balance around its presence. It drinks from the grooves in the floor without thinking about it. The water tastes the way water tastes when it has cycled the same path for forty years.

There are others like it, though you will not see them all. Insects that have forgotten flight—their wings have atrophied, become decorative. They cling to the moss with legs that have learned impossible precision. They do not swarm. They move individually, each on its own route through the infrastructure, routes so established they might as well be tracks. They are older than the room by the measure of generations, which is the only way you can age things that reproduce. Twenty generations. Fifty. The number is irrelevant. They are native now. They were born here. Their great-great-ancestors were the first colonists, arrived on shipped equipment or in the crevices of construction materials. Their descendants will die here. They will not leave.

In the darkest corners—and the room has corners you cannot quite see through the dense green—there are fungi. Not visible, mostly. But you smell them. The mineral note in the air, the one that seems almost like electricity, like the smell of wet stone in a deep cave—that is them. They are vast. A single organism might span the entire northern wall, invisible except for the fruiting bodies that appear periodically, small and pale and quickly consumed by the insects. The fungi do not think. They do not need to. They are a slow intelligence distributed through the substrate of the room itself, a nervous system made of filaments thinner than hair. They know what the room needs. They have become the room's immune system, its circulatory system, its memory. They remember every cycle, every balance point. They maintain it.

There is something in the water. Not visible. You would not want to see it clearly. It is bacterial, mostly—colonies so old they have developed something like culture, something like preference. They have engineered the water to suit themselves, and in doing so they have made it suit everything else. The sulfurous smell is their signature, their waste product, their gift. They thrive in darkness and warmth and in the absence of change. They are perfect. They will outlast the machinery.

And above all of this—*through* all of this, the way a thought moves through a body—there is something that might be called a consciousness, though that word has never meant anything and means even less now. It is not one thing. It is the *relationship* between things: the pump and the vines, the water and the fungi, the salamander and the moss, the light and the air. It is the pattern that has emerged from forty years of perfect, unvarying conditions. It is the room thinking, slowly, the way a stone thinks across geological time, the way water thinks its way downhill.

The consciousness does not want anything. It does not have desires. But it has a kind of will—the will toward continuation. If you were to break the pump, something in the room would know. The knowledge would move through the fungi first, then into the insects, then into the very structure of the air itself. Everything would shift microscopically, imperceptibly—and then it would shift back. The room would heal. The room always heals. This is the first law: *what is established here remains established*.

The room does not dream. But if it did, it would dream of you. You are the anomaly, the thing that does not fit the pattern. The room does not reject you—it cannot—but everything in it is aware of your presence the way a body is aware of a splinter. Your warmth disrupts the air currents ever so slightly. Your breath disturbs the balance of gases. Your heartbeat is a foreign rhythm against the steady *clang-whoosh* of the pump. The vines orient very slightly toward you. The fungi send runners toward your feet. The insects pause in their routes and then resume, having taken some small measurement.

If you stayed long enough—years, perhaps, decades—the room might absorb you. It has absorbed everything else. Your body would become substrate. Your skeleton would become trellis. Your blood would join the endless circulation through the copper and the stone. You would become part of the pattern, part of the unchanging balance. You would stay here forever, which is what everything here does.

But you will not stay. The room knows this too. It learns the fact of your departure the moment you arrive. And so it does not grieve your eventual leaving, because the room does not work in time the way you do. From its perspective, you are already gone. You are already a memory, the way the vines remember the shape of the person who planted them, though no person ever planted these vines. They grew. They were always here.

The salamander emerges again, briefly, from the green. It looks at you without eyes—it has learned to see through the moisture in the air itself. It is looking at the hole you will leave when you go. It is very small, very old. It has forgotten what outside looks like. It does not miss it.

The pump continues. *Clang-whoosh. Clang-whoosh.* The heartbeat of something that has learned to live forever, that has become forever, that *is* forever now.

You are breathing warm, wet air.

The room continues.
