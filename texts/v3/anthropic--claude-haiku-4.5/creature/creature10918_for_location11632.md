# What Lives Here

They are *adjustment specialists*, is what the atmospheric station calls them, though the quarry workers use older names: the Stone-Waiters, the Moss-Readers, the ones who have learned to *settle*.

They are human, mostly. There is no other baseline to measure against anymore.

What defines them first is *patience*—not the passive kind, but an active, muscular patience that must be practiced daily. They move slowly across the Margent not from caution alone, but from a kind of negotiation. They place their feet and *listen* to what the ground says back. They've learned to read the micro-shifts: the almost imperceptible settling that precedes a larger one, the particular groan of stone that signals a fissure about to open. Some of them claim they can feel it in their bones first, a vibration in the inner ear that no one else notices. These people are right often enough that the others have stopped questioning it.

Their skin has taken on the texture of the environment—not literally, but the constant damp and mineral-laden mist has marked them. Their hands are perpetually wrinkled from moisture, their hair permanently limp and darkened. Respiratory conditions are universal here; even the young ones cough in the particular way of people whose lungs have learned to process something thicker than air. Their eyes have adapted to the permanent twilight. Visitors from lower elevations find their gaze unsettling—their pupils don't dilate and contract the way normal eyes do, as if they've learned to extract light from sources invisible to others.

They come in types, though the types blur into one another:

**The Structural Readers** are the ones who've spent years understanding the columnar formations. They can navigate the tilted stone fingers by some internal map that seems almost supernatural. They move across angles that look vertical, finding handholds in surfaces that appear smooth to outsiders. Many of them have fingers broken and reset so many times that their hands look gnarled, arthritic—though they are often quite young. They can locate the old human passages through the cliff-face, routes that have been used for perhaps centuries, marked by subtle scraped patterns on the stone that only they can read. They serve as guides, haulers, maintenance workers for the suspension bridges. Some of them develop a kind of *relationship* with the stone, speaking to it in low voices, cajoling it. Others work in grim silence. Both approaches seem equally effective.

**The Moss Specialists** are quieter. They move through the fern-tangle with a deliberation that borders on meditation. Some of them are studying the vegetation—collecting samples, keeping obsessive records in waterproof journals, noting the changes in the layering and composition of the moss carpets. They believe (and there may be something to it) that the moss predicts large structural shifts, that it responds to subsurface water movements before they become visible. Others simply know the moss in a practical way: they understand which patches will support weight, which ones conceal dangerous drops, where to find the medicinal varieties. They harvest the older vegetation carefully, using it for insulation, for medicines, for fuel in the steam-powered equipment. The moss here grows in archaeological layers—the deepest sections containing pollen and plant matter from centuries past, perfectly preserved by the cold and damp. A few of the Specialists spend their time extracting and studying these layers as if reading a written history.

**The Water-Dwellers** are the strangest. They work around the vertical rivers, collecting samples, measuring their frequency and force, maintaining equipment that monitors the subsurface flooding cycles. Their clothes never fully dry. Their skin has a particular pallor, and their fingernails have a faint bluish cast from constant immersion. They move along the spray-zones with an ease that seems impossible—balancing on slick stone, moving through mist so thick it's almost liquid. Some of them claim they can predict the upwelling cycles by listening to the sound of the water moving through the fissures below the Margent. They can hear, they say, the ocean breathing. This might be literal; the atmospheric station takes their measurements seriously, feeds the data into instruments that presumably matter somewhere else. The Water-Dwellers are often solitary, contentedly so. They've built small shelters at the spray-zones where they live in conditions of near-total saturation. Fungal infections are common, but they seem not to mind. Some of them have been here long enough that people from the lower settlements barely recognize them as human anymore.

**The Machinery-Keepers** work the quarrying station and the processing plant. They are *engineers* in the oldest sense—people who have learned to make broken things work in broken places. Their hands are stained with rust and mineral deposits that never fully wash away. They understand machines that seem far too decrepit to function, and they keep them functional through sheer will and intuition. Many of them are deaf or partially deaf from years of noise; they communicate through gesture and written notes. They move with a kind of grim pragmatism, checking bolts and seals, replacing components that shouldn't be replaceable, burning things in furnaces to keep the steam pressure consistent despite the perpetual damp. They are not young, most of them. The work here seems to age people rapidly, or perhaps only people already worn by decades elsewhere come here to work. They don't speak much about why they're here or what, precisely, is being produced. The questions seem impertinent to them.

**The Record-Keepers** work out of the atmospheric station. They are different in bearing from the others—more careful in their dress, more deliberate in their movements, less marked by the Margent's particular erosion. They maintain instruments of glass and brass that measure things invisible: pressure gradients, humidity ratios, temperature fluctuations, the composition of the permanent mist. They take samples of the vertical river water, the moss, the air itself at different heights and different times of day. They record everything in coded logs that are hauled away monthly via the narrow-gauge rail. They seem to know something the others don't, or at least to suspect it. Their eyes, when they look out across the fractured plateau, have a quality of *waiting*. Waiting for something to be confirmed. A few of them have begun recording observations of the vegetation—noting that the moss colors have shifted, that certain species of fern are moving upslope, that the frequency of the crustacean chorus seems to be changing. Whether this represents actual change or merely the beginning of systematic observation is unclear even to them.

**The Unclassified** are the ones who live in the older settlements, the half-abandoned structures, the canvas shelters that have faded to lichen-color. They don't work the major installations anymore, or if they do, it's in peripheral capacities. They've chosen to stay despite the fading operations, the sense that something here is concluding. They maintain the cairn-markers on the cargo routes. They know the suspension bridges in ways that approach the supernatural. Some of them trap the crustaceans for food—there's a particular way to do it that doesn't damage the moss-layer, a technique passed down or independently discovered. They've built their own shelter systems, their own water-collection systems, their own ways of understanding the Margent that have nothing to do with the official measurements. They know where the ground is most stable—or rather, where its instability is most predictable. They know the paths through the columnar formations that avoid the worst of the mist-zones. They've learned to read the wind's acoustic tricks, to move through the sound-shadows, to navigate partly by listening rather than looking. Some of them may have been born here. Others arrived decades ago and found that leaving seemed increasingly impossible, not physically but spiritually—as if the Margent had claimed them in some fundamental way.

Collectively, they've adapted to an environment that seems actively hostile to human habitation. But it's not hostility, exactly. The Margent is simply *indifferent*, engaged in its slow geological dissolution. Living here requires accepting that, making peace with it, learning to move within it rather than against it. The people who've truly adapted—and there are maybe two hundred of them scattered across the plateau and its descending chaos—carry themselves with a particular kind of resignation that is very close to serenity.

They don't speak much about the broader world. They've learned to operate on local knowledge, local seasons, local cycles. The vertical rivers flood on their own schedule, comprehensible once you've lived through several of the cycles. The moss grows and evolves in patterns that become clear after years of observation. The machinery fails and is repaired. The samples are collected and sent away. The wind comes from the higher reaches and carries the old, exhausted light across the fractured stone.

They are people who have learned to live in conclusion, who have made a home in a place that is already becoming something else. They move carefully, listen carefully, remember carefully. They are thin, most of them, and their eyes have that peculiar light-absorption quality. Many have never left the Margent. Many don't intend to.

They are what remains when a world begins its slow final settling.
