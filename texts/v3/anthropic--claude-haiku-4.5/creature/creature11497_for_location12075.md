# The Threshold-Tenders

They are not native to this place. They *chose* it.

You would know them by their stillness first—a quality of attention so complete it reads as absence. They sit on the stone platforms during the deepest crescendos, their forms small enough that you might mistake them for deliberately stacked cairns until one shifts, a movement so economical it barely disturbs the air. There are perhaps seven of them across the visible terraces, though you sense the number fluctuates. They come and go on rhythms that have nothing to do with light.

Their bodies are thin, adapted to this cold by a kind of voluntary reduction—as if they've negotiated with scarcity and agreed to use less. Their skin, where you can see it, has taken on the pale granular quality of the stone they inhabit. Not diseased. Harmonized. They wear wrappings of something like dried plant-material, woven so tightly it's hard to say whether the fabric is clothing or whether it's become their outer layer. The wrappings are stained with mineral deposits—iron-oxide reds, copper-greens—marking places where they've pressed their bodies against the stone for hours, for days perhaps, absorbing the temperature signatures of the rock, the subtle vibrations.

Their hands are calloused to the point of transformation. The palms are thick and cracked like ancient leather, the fingertips flattened from years of work. These are hands that have stacked thousands of stones in perfect balance, hands that understand weight distribution the way musicians understand intervals. On some of them, you notice that certain fingers have worn smooth, polished by friction against the worked stone platforms—as if the boundary between flesh and material has begun to blur.

But it is their ears that mark them most visibly.

They have learned to listen with their entire bodies, and it shows. Their ear structures have adapted subtly—the lobes elongated, the cartilage thickened and patterned with scars from stone-pressure, as though they have spent countless hours with their heads pressed to the platforms to feel the vibrations at higher sensitivity. Some of them have modified their ears deliberately: careful scarification in patterns that almost suggest intentional tuning, small stones embedded in the lobes that catch and amplify certain frequencies. Their heads often tilt in ways that seem to have nothing to do with curiosity and everything to do with acoustic geometry, positioning themselves in the invisible nodes where the sound reaches peak clarity.

Their eyes are the strangest feature. Not blind—they clearly navigate the treacherous terraces with confidence—but *redirected*. They look inward as often as outward, and there's a quality to their gaze that suggests they're perceiving something the ordinary eye cannot capture. Perhaps they're seeing the sound made visible, tracking it through the air the way others might watch smoke. Perhaps they've learned to see the vibrations in the stone itself, the minute oscillations that precede the louder crescendos. When they do look outward at the physical world, it's often with the expression of someone checking a detail in a familiar text, confirming something they already know.

They move along the terraces with purposeful unhaste, rarely speaking—though sometimes, in the moments when the roar subsides into relative quiet, you catch fragments of their communication. Not quite language. More like responsive listening, as though they're answering the landscape's questions with their own vocalizations, contributing to the symphony rather than imposing external meaning on it. When they do speak to each other, it's in low tones that seem to come from deeper in their chests than human voices typically do, as if they've learned to resonate with the geological frequencies beneath.

They tend to the stone platforms with ritualistic precision. They don't *build* them the way a human might—no frantic activity, no grand gestures. Instead, they sit beside a platform for hours, running their hands across its surface, feeling for imbalances that exist only as pressure-gradients perceptible to touch. Then, with movement that appears almost accidental, they adjust a single stone by millimeters. That's all. A shift of weight. A reorientation. And somehow, the whole structure's acoustic properties change. You might not hear it immediately, but the frequency of the nearby roar will shift slightly—not louder or quieter, but different. Refined. More resonant with the surrounding terraces' tones.

They eat the fern-moss. You watch them harvest it with the curved stone tool—and there are many such tools scattered about, suggesting this is a regular practice. They consume it raw, in small quantities, their jaws working steadily through something that looks like it shouldn't be digestible. But they seem sustained by it. The moss is their only visible food source; whether the sound itself provides some form of energy is unclear, but the way they position themselves in the crescendos—heads back, mouths slightly open, bodies absorbing—suggests nourishment of a kind beyond the merely nutritional.

They sleep in shifts, and never with their ears plugged or covered. They rest on the stone platforms with their heads positioned at specific acoustic nodes, where the sound reaches them in a particular configuration. Their sleep appears shallow, responsive—they wake instantly when the roar modulates into a new crescendo-pattern, alert not in alarm but in a kind of eager attentiveness.

They seem to be waiting for something. Or listening for something. Or perhaps *creating* something through their meticulous tending of the platforms.

There's an orientation to their patience that suggests deep time. They're not young, most of them, though age reads differently on their adapted bodies—less about years and more about seasons spent in one place, seasons spent listening so intently that their very cells have reorganized around the task. Some of them bear scars that suggest they've been here through periods of violent geological activity—when the terraces shift and crack and the sound becomes something that might shatter bone. They survive this. They repair the platforms. They return to listening.

Occasionally, one of them will move to a different terrace, relocating as though called to a particular frequency-zone. The migration seems deliberate but not urgent—a choice made over days, enacted in stages, accompanied by a process of gradually shifting one's acoustic attunement to the new location. When they settle, they always position themselves at a platform. Always. It's not shelter or safety they're seeking; the choice is purely acoustic. They want to be at the precise point where they can best hear what the landscape is trying to say.

They do not appear lonely.

There is a contentment in their focus that reads as fullness. They have reduced themselves to fit this place perfectly—not through deprivation but through *refinement*. Everything extraneous stripped away. Everything essential honed toward a single, total attention. They have become, in a way, part of the landscape's own sensory apparatus. They listen on behalf of the stone. They tend to the platforms as a landscape tends to itself. They are threshold-keepers between the noise and whatever silence might eventually come—the ones who will recognize it first when the crescendo finally completes.

And they seem to believe—you can tell by the way they position themselves, by the care in their work—that this moment is approaching. Not soon. Not in any human measure of time. But eventually. They listen for it still.
