# What Lives in the Stillplains of Keth

## The Listeners

They are the closest thing to commonplace here, which means they are anything but. At first glance—the glance that lasts for days before you understand what you're seeing—they resemble the mountain goats of your childhood imaginings, or the sketches in old books where artists drew what they'd never witnessed. But the resemblance ends at silhouette.

Their skin is a geography unto itself. In some specimens, transparency pools around the joints like standing water—you see the architecture of their joints, the intricate work of ligaments that glow faintly amber in the unchanging light. Other individuals show luminescence that seems to *pulse* with intent, networks of light that trace paths down their spines, branch into their limbs, concentrate in complex knots behind their eyes. These lights don't flicker. They shift, subtly, the way a sleeping person's dreams might move behind closed eyelids.

Their faces are gentle and utterly wrong. The eyes are too large, too stable—they don't dart or search, they simply *know* where to point. The mouths open sideways, as you noticed, but the mechanics of this are stranger than sideways alone. The jaw seems to unmake itself, to *bloom* open in a direction that shouldn't exist in three-dimensional space, and when it closes, it has taken something from the crystal seams. Not eating. Never eating. Communion, perhaps. Conversation with minerals.

They vocalize in clicks—sounds that come from the throat, but also, disturbingly, from within the transparent sections of their bodies. You can watch the sound being generated, watch it travel through the luminescent networks like electricity through copper, watch it emerge from a dozen points along their spines. The clicks combine into patterns that almost—*almost*—resolve into syntax. Your language centers keep reaching for meaning, keep insisting that if you just listened *correctly*, you would understand. You never do. But you understand that you're not meant to; that the clicks are a form of notation, of record-keeping, of *testimony* to the landscape itself.

They move with no urgency. A Listener will spend what might be an hour, or might be a minute (time is difficult here), positioned against a particular fold of terrain, its luminescent networks cycling through subtle color shifts—amber to near-white to a pale green that makes your eyes hurt. Then it will rise (the motion itself is fluid, joint-work that seems to bend without actually bending), and walk to the next location, three feet away, and assume the exact same posture. Around it, dozens of other Listeners perform variations on this same meditation.

They are not gregarious and not solitary. They are something else—a form of collective stillness, a network that doesn't communicate because it has already *agreed*. When you move among them, they part without acknowledging your movement. They know you're there. They've always known. Their presence shifts slightly, creates spaces for you to pass through, and reforms after you've gone. They are the landscape's way of *experiencing* itself.

## The Climbers

These are the ones that trouble sleep.

They are small—perhaps the size of your hand, though size feels uncertain in a place where perspective refuses to cooperate. Their exoskeletons are chitinous, a color between slate and rust, segmented in ways that suggest more joints than mathematics should allow. They have too many legs—eight, or ten, or possibly more, depending on how you count the appendages that seem to be only partially *present* in normal space.

They climb.

The phrase doesn't capture it. The Climbers move, and the motion is always vertical, always against surfaces that appear, to any rational assessment, to be completely flat. They ascend the gentle slopes of the folded terrain with their legs at ninety-degree angles to the ground, using the *air itself* as purchase. When you focus on a single Climber, you can see how it's done—there are subtle variations in the plateau's surface that your eyes had smoothed away, tiny irregularities, almost-invisible ledges. The Climber grips these and advances upward, and the motion is both slow and rapid, both mechanical and graceful.

But when you try to see all of them—when you pull back and attempt to hold the pattern of hundreds of Climbers in your vision at once—something in your perception fragments. The pattern *exists*. You can feel its architecture, sense its intricate logic. It is clearly a coordinate system, a map written in motion, a message expressed through collective geometry. But it resists being *seen* as a whole. Your eyes can track individual Climbers, or you can sense the vast organizational beauty of the swarm, but not both simultaneously. To understand the whole pattern, you would have to become something that perceives differently than a human does. You would have to *agree* with the Stillplains.

They make sounds—clicking, chittering, a kind of hiss that emerges from spiracles you can barely identify. The sounds coordinate with their movement in ways that suggest communication or perhaps *calculation*. They're not eating the landscape. They're not building anything. They're *mapping* it, or *recording* it, or perhaps simply *proving* to it that they understand its geometry.

Sometimes—though "sometimes" is a word that means little here—a Climber will reach a particular point in its vertical journey and *stop*. It will rest there, legs locked against invisible purchase, for an interval that might be hours or minutes. Then it will retreat, descending back to the plateau's surface with the same impossible precision, and begin a different climb. These retreats are coordinated among dozens of Climbers at once, a synchronized withdrawal that suggests they've achieved something, learned something, *confirmed* something.

They leave no trace. The landscape doesn't show scars from their passage. There are no broken segments of exoskeleton, no chemical markers, nothing but the memory of their passage and the certainty that they are always climbing, always have been climbing, always will be.

## The Vast One

You've never seen it.

You probably never will, not directly, not in a way that your eyes could verify and your mind could translate into certainty. But you know it's there—a presence in the deeper folds of the terrain, something that sleeps beneath the plateau the way roots sleep beneath soil. It's *warm*. The air in the deep places where the folds are deepest holds a heat that doesn't quite match the surface temperature, a gentle warmth that suggests respiration, a slow metabolism running on a scale that might be geological.

The locals speak of it rarely and without fear. It's simply *there*, part of the landscape's slow function, no more worthy of comment than the crystal seams or the unchanging light. But sometimes, if you're still enough, if you've been here long enough that the silence is becoming your native language, you can feel its attention. Not focused on you—you're too small, too temporary. But a kind of *noticing*, the way you might become aware that a tree exists even when you're not looking directly at it. The Vast One knows the landscape it inhabits. It's part of that knowledge's mechanism.

In the evenings (which aren't visibly different from the mornings, but you feel the difference in your bones), the warmth from its resting place seems to seep upward more noticeably. The crystal deposits glow brighter, though no external light has increased. The Listeners become more still, their luminescence synchronizing subtly, as though in response to a rhythm you can't quite hear. Even the Climbers pause in their geometric ascents, clinging to their invisible ledges in a collective moment of observation.

What is it? Size suggests something massive—not in the way of mountains, perhaps, but the way of presence. Something that displaces the fabric of the place through sheer existence. The locals have no name for it, or they've forgotten the name, or the name would take too long to speak. They simply accept it the way they accept the particular shade of light, the particular temperature, the particular quality of silence.

It is old. It has been here longer than the current form of the plateau. It may have been here since before the seas came, before they left, before everything that isn't now had its ending. And it will be here after—not because it's immortal, but because *here* might not have an *after*. Here, the Vast One is as permanent as permanence allows.

## The Unnamed Things

There are others.

Things that fly without casting shadows—you've seen them, or you've seen the *absence* where they pass, a kind of negative image that briefly disrupts your perception. They move through the sky in patterns that suggest neither hunger nor curiosity, neither intention nor accident. They simply *traverse* from one horizon to another, completing some necessary trajectory, passing through the Stillplains as though passing through a medium they understand more intimately than any ground-bound creature could.

There are burrowers that move through the air as though swimming, making slow, deliberate motions with appendages that might be fins or might be hands or might be something for which you have no reference. They spiral downward and then upward again, tracing three-dimensional paths through what should be empty space, their movements leaving faint disturbances in the light—ripples in the constancy, small proofs that even changelessness can be troubled.

There are geometric things glimpsed in peripheral vision, things that seem to be simultaneously *here* and *not here*, that appear at the edge of your perception when you're not looking directly, and vanish the moment you focus your attention. You're never certain if they're animals, or artifacts, or ideas that the landscape itself is thinking.

## The People

They are the uncertainty principle made manifest.

The stone structures they've built—small huts, shelters pressed into natural alcoves, walls made from flat stones stacked without mortar—suggest permanence and intention. The tools scattered about (bone needles, worked flint, implements whose purpose you can't quite identify) suggest ongoing activity, an engaged presence. But the people themselves are almost never visible.

You feel them more than see them: breathing in the stone huts when you pass, movements in the peripheral landscape, the sense of attention directed toward you without corresponding visibility. They have learned to be part of the Stillplains' consensus, to move with its rhythms, to minimize the friction of their presence. The local records—those patterns pressed into slate, repeated methodically—suggest they've been here for generations, or perhaps for one very long generation, developing a way of living that doesn't disrupt the landscape's settled state.

When you do glimpse them, they're momentary: a figure at a distance, moving with unhurried purpose toward some task; an elder sitting beside one of the crystal seams, hand resting against it; a child (if children can exist in this place, and they do, though they're always strangely still) watching the Listeners with an expression of profound understanding. They don't seem surprised by your presence. They acknowledge you with small gestures and return to their activities. They've learned that the Stillplains tolerate newcomers, that the landscape is patient enough to encompass even disruptive species like humans.

Their language, when you hear it, is quiet—perhaps quieter than whispers, perhaps actual whispers, perhaps something else entirely. You catch fragments: words that sound ancient, words that sound like stone, words that might be names for the creatures or the landscape or concepts for which your language has no equivalent. They're not teaching you. They're not hiding from you. They're simply existing at a remove, living parallel to your presence the way all things here exist in gentle parallel.

What do they eat? The vegetation crackles when they harvest it, but barely; the crystal seams seem to sustain them, or sustain some need beyond simple nutrition. What do they want? Nothing you can identify. What do they fear? Perhaps only change. Perhaps only the possibility that the Stillplains might someday move again, that the consensus might dissolve, that they would have to *do* something other than *be*.

## The Consensus

This is the real answer.

The Stillplains of Keth aren't *inhabited* in the way normal places are inhabited. They're *maintained* in consensus—a state of mutual agreement between stone, crystal, vegetation, fauna, and people. Everything here has made a choice (or had that choice made for it, or never had the option of not choosing): to remain exactly as it is.

The Listeners click their testimony into the light. The Climbers map the geometry of stillness. The Vast One breathes beneath, confirming with each breath that it will never demand change. The flying things and the burrowers and the geometric shadows trace their necessary patterns, never altering, never developing, never *becoming* anything new. The people press their stories into stone and wait.

And the plateau itself, ancient and exhausted and finally at peace, holds all of them in its folds and asks nothing more than that they remain.

What lives in the Stillplains of Keth? Everything that has agreed to live there. Everything that has accepted that this is how the story ends: not with violence or revelation, but with a gentle, permanent, absolute stillness—not enforced, but chosen, moment after moment, by every creature that breathes there, for as long as breathing continues.

The question is whether you will agree as well.
