# The Inhabitants of Thel-Maresh

At first, you think the place is empty.

Then you learn to see.

They are not numerous, but they are present—the *residents* of this green architecture, and they move through it with the practiced ease of creatures born into constraint. The first you notice is almost certainly not the first to notice you: a human figure, slight and pale, tending to a section of the columnar plants with hands that move in repetitive, almost meditative patterns. Pruning. Checking. Adjusting nutrient-lines you can barely perceive. The figure doesn't look up when you approach, or perhaps doesn't register you as significant. The work continues. The hands move. There's a rhythm to it that matches the rhythm of the space itself—the breathing, the cooling cycles, the pulse of the system.

The person is thin in a way that suggests generations of adaptation. Not malnutrition, exactly—they move with sufficient strength—but a reduction to efficiency. Minimal frame. Maximum lung-capacity for an atmosphere that requires deep breathing. The skin has a faint luminescence to it, a greenish undertone that might be cosmetic, might be biological. You can't quite tell. They wear clothing that seems woven from plant-fiber, dyed in shades of pale green and grey. The fabric moves with them like a second skin, or like they're part of the architecture themselves. When they finally do look at you, their eyes are unremarkable except for their pupils—dilated wide in the dim-bright of the phosphorescent glow. Adapted to seeing in this light-spectrum.

"First level?" they ask, their voice thin and slightly high-pitched, as if the throat has been shaped by breathing this specific air. They don't wait for an answer. "You'll want to learn the zones before you go deeper. Everyone makes that mistake." They return to their pruning, but now they're talking, their attention half-divided. "The architecture seems random, but it's not. Each section has its own ecology. Its own requirements. You can navigate by feel—the temperature shifts, the light changes—but it's easier if someone shows you." They pause, blade suspended above a vine. "Or if you stay in your assigned zone and do your work, which is probably the intention."

There's no resentment in the statement. It's offered as fact, the way one might comment on weather.

You begin to see others, now that you're attuned to looking.

A figure moving through the undergrowth several layers back, bent double, collecting something—seed-pods, you think, or perhaps harvesting some cultivated fungus that grows on the lower-layer plants. This one moves with urgency, filling baskets woven from the same fiber as the surrounding vegetation. Their hands are stained dark with something—soil, or spore-matter, or the juices of fruits you can't identify. They're older than the first, or perhaps they simply appear older due to the curvature of their spine, the careful precision of someone whose body has learned through repetition the exact angles required to move efficiently through this confined space.

Another: a child, perhaps—it's hard to judge age in this light—suspended in a climbing harness made of vine-material, pruning the upper growth. Moving with the unselfconscious grace of someone who has never known any environment but this. The child reaches higher than seems safe, and you flinch, but they don't seem aware of danger. The harness holds. The vines are strong. This is normal. The child hums something while working, a note that seems to resonate with the ambient hum of the irrigation system. The sounds almost harmonize.

There are more than you initially thought.

As your eyes adjust—not just to the light, but to the visual patterns of the place—you begin to distinguish perhaps a dozen human figures in the visible range, each occupied with specific tasks. They move through the green with the fluidity of people who have negotiated the same space thousands of times. They know where the vines won't support weight. They know which plants will cut skin if brushed the wrong way. They know the architecture in their muscles.

But they are not native to this place, you realize. They're tending it. Maintaining it. The distinction is crucial.

One approaches you—the first person has finished their section and moved on, and this new figure seems to have been assigned, or assigned themselves, to greeting duty. They're younger, with that same luminescent undertone to their skin, but with an alertness that suggests less time spent in meditative work. Their movements are more angular, less integrated with the space.

"How long do you plan to stay?" they ask without introduction. "Your acclimation will depend on it. A few hours, we don't prepare you much. A few weeks, we teach you the systems. Longer than that—" They shrug. "You become part of the infrastructure."

The way they say it: neither threat nor invitation. Simple statement of consequence.

"You'll meet the old ones, if you stay long enough," they continue, gesturing vaguely into the deeper green. "The ones who've been here since the structure was sealed. They barely speak anymore. They just know things. They can taste when a section is out of balance—too acidic, too dry. They can feel it through their skin, through the air itself. They're not quite human anymore, but they're not quite plant either. They're something else. Something the place made."

They leave before you can ask what that means.

The ecosystem—you realize, listening more carefully—includes entities that are harder to categorize.

There are the arthropods, yes: the segmented beetles with their serial numbers, the slow-moving flies, the insects that might be engineered, might be naturally evolved to fill niches that the human-cultivators missed. But there are also creatures that seem neither quite insect nor quite something else. A thing like a translucent worm, thick as a finger, moving through the undergrowth in patterns that suggest intelligence. Or perhaps just the appearance of intelligence—a creature following a biochemical gradient, attracted to specific nutrients, nothing more. But it moves with purpose. With decision-making. It avoids obstacles. It seeks. It finds.

And then there are the ones you glimpse only in peripheral vision.

Larger shapes moving through the far-growth. Humanoid, but wrong in ways you can't quite articulate. Proportions slightly off. Movements that don't match human biomechanics. You mention this to one of the tenders, and they nod without surprise.

"Mutants," they say neutrally. "Or adaptations. Things that started human and spent enough time in the deep-zones that they changed. The environment is full of mutagenic substances—spores, chemical fertilizers in concentrations you wouldn't believe, radiation from the old backup systems that we can't quite shut down. Some people adapt to it. Some people just... change. Most of the deep-tenders are like that. They prefer it down there. The pressure, the density, the cold. It's normal to them now. They come up-level rarely. I think they might forget how to breathe regular air."

The way they say "regular air" suggests they don't quite believe in it anymore either.

The social structure, as you piece it together from observation and brief conversations, is horizontal in theory but stratified in practice. The "level-tenders"—those assigned to or comfortable with the specific zones they work in—form loose associations based on proximity and task. They meet at collection-points, share information about which sections need attention, which plants are blooming ahead of schedule, which irrigation-lines have developed leaks. There's a coordination that happens, but it's organic, emergent from repeated interaction rather than imposed by any visible hierarchy.

But there must be someone in charge, you think. Someone making decisions. Someone tracking what gets harvested, what gets replanted, what the entire vast structure is ultimately producing.

You never see them.

Perhaps they're in some administrative center you never discover. Perhaps they're one of the deep-tenders, so changed by the environment that you don't recognize them as human. Perhaps the structure has become self-managing, and the concept of "someone in charge" is outdated—the plants, through their deep integration with whatever automated systems remain, making their own decisions about growth, reproduction, resource allocation. The human inhabitants reduced to a collaborative part of the larger organism.

One of the older tenders—the one you saw harvesting seed-pods—tells you something late in a cycle, when the cold-phase is deepest and the work has slowed:

"We don't leave," they say. It takes you a moment to realize they're answering a question you never asked. "Even the ones who could. There's nothing outside anymore. This place is older than any of us. Older than our parents' parents. The outside is dead or dying or so changed that it's not the world we were born into. This—" They gesture at the pressing green, the glowing lattice-work, the cool air. "This is stable. This is life. Controlled, but real. It feeds us. It needs us. The arrangement is mutual, even if mutual means we're trapped. Or maybe especially then."

There's something in their voice that might be contentment. Might be capitulation. You can't distinguish between them.

The children born here—and you realize, with some shock, that several of the tenders are young enough to have been born after the structure was sealed—move through the growth as if it's their native landscape, which it is. They speak a dialect of whatever language the older tenders use, but with words borrowed from plant-terminology. They call each other by the zone they were born in. "Third-level," "Northern sector," "Deep-east." They have their own culture forming, their own understanding of what normal is.

One of them—a girl, maybe twelve, maybe younger—asks you, with genuine curiosity:

"What does outside smell like? Before it died?"

You realize you don't have a good answer. You try anyway, describing grass and rain and open air. She listens politely, but with the distant courtesy of someone hearing folklore about a place that has never existed for her.

"Sounds cold," she says finally. "Lonely."

The inference is that Thel-Maresh is neither of these things. And perhaps it isn't. Perhaps it's a community held together by necessity, by shared purpose, by the very fact that there's nowhere else to go. Perhaps that's better than it sounds from the outside. Perhaps the constraint is preferable to the alternative.

In the depths, the ones who have changed most extensively maintain their own routines, visible to you only in the distant-work you observe: vast sections of growth being tended with a speed and precision that suggests non-human coordination. Limbs moving in arcs that human joints shouldn't accommodate. Tasks being completed that you don't understand the purpose of. They don't interact with the upper-level tenders much, and when they do, there's a formality to it. Respect, perhaps. Or wariness. The knowledge that they've become something different.

But they're still here. Still tending. Still maintaining the structure that holds them.

And in the absolute deepest zones, where the cooling systems work their hardest and the light becomes almost obscured by density and distance, there are things you catch only the briefest glimpses of: vast, slow movements in the background, structures that might be living creatures or might be the plants themselves grown into animal-shape, entities so integrated with the architecture that you can't separate organism from environment.

Whether they're still people, or whether they've simply become part of Thel-Maresh itself, you can't determine.

But the structure persists. The light flickers in its rhythm. The air breathes with the breath of countless lungs. The work continues, performed by creatures human and half-human and no longer quite human at all, all of them bound together in the maintenance of this vast, living, closed garden.

All of them, in some way, *owned* by it. Or perhaps owners of it. The distinction, after enough generations, becomes meaningless.

They are the Thel-Mareshians now. The structure is their world, and they are its caretakers, its inhabitants, its final product. What they're growing here—in the end—might simply be themselves.
