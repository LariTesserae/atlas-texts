# What Lives in the Terai of Ascending Voices

They are not separate from this place. This is the first thing you understand about them, though understanding takes time—the kind measured in the slowness of moisture accumulating on your skin, in the patience required to listen to the voices resolve into something like language.

They are the *Resonant*, though they have no name for themselves because naming implies separation, individuality, the kind of bounded selfhood that cannot exist here. What exists instead is *participation*—each one is a node in something larger, a frequency in the polyphonic chorus that built the stone into listening chambers and taught the vegetation to vibrate.

Their bodies, when you finally perceive them as distinct from the landscape, are difficult to describe because they exist in a state of constant negotiation with their environment. They are not encased in skin the way you are—the boundary between self and world is negotiable, permeable. Some of them appear almost humanoid: upright, bipedal, with limbs that articulate in vaguely familiar ways. But their surfaces are not smooth. They are furred, but the fur is composed of hair-thin vines that move independently, constantly reaching, constantly sensing. Their skin—if it can be called that—is a moss-covered membrane that photosynthesizes in the pale diffused light. Where your body ends and the landscape begins, their bodies blur into the surroundings. Roots grow through them. Fungi colonize them. They do not fight these incursions; they incorporate them.

Their heads are the most unsettling. They lack eyes in any conventional sense. Instead, they have depression-patterns, sensitive areas that perceive vibration and light-refraction. Their mouths are not mouths but resonance chambers—cavities that both produce and receive the endless singing. When they emit sound, it is not from a throat but from their entire body, a vibration that begins in the core and radiates outward. When they listen, every surface of them becomes an ear.

There are others, less humanoid. Some are primarily vegetative—tall, rooted structures that appear plant-like until you notice they are mobile, that they traverse the plateaus with a slow, deliberate locomotion, their root-systems retracting and extending in a rhythm like breathing. They have no heads. They have no clear organs. They are distributed systems, their consciousness—if consciousness is even the right word—spread throughout their entire form. These ones communicate through root-touch, their tendrils interweaving with others to exchange resonance-patterns directly.

And there are smaller ones, or perhaps the same ones seen from different distances, impossible to categorize. Globular, semi-transparent structures that seem to be primarily water held in a shape by force of will and acoustic pressure. These move through the vegetation like thoughts moving through a mind, appearing and disappearing, carrying vibrations from one part of the settlement to another.

What they *do*—what they are *for*—becomes apparent only if you spend time observing the equilibrium they maintain. They are not inhabitants of this place. They *are* this place, made conscious and semi-mobile. The endless voices are not their communication; the voices are their metabolism. By singing in perfect polyphonic harmony, they maintain the resonance that keeps the stone stable, that amplifies the dripping water into acoustic patterns, that teaches the vegetation how to grow in this stasis-state. The constant pitch is not monotony; it is precision. It is what prevents decay, what suspends the normal cycling of growth and death and rebirth.

They do not eat. Or rather, they eat only the substrate of their own being—they break down their own dead matter and reabsorb it, creating a perfect closed loop. Their waste is the mist itself. Their respiration is the singing. They exist in a state of profound slowness, moving only when necessary, reproducing only when the resonance demands it, and even then producing offspring that are almost indistinguishable from their surroundings until they reach a certain pitch-frequency that makes them cohere into individual form.

They are aware of you, though their awareness is not localized in the way you expect. Your presence is a disruption in the acoustic field—a thing that absorbs and reflects sound in non-harmonic ways. You are a jarring note in their symphony. But they do not attack. They do not defend. Instead, they are slowly, patiently, trying to integrate you. The mist is trying to penetrate your lungs so it can teach your breathing to resonate correctly. The roots reaching through the spongy ground are trying to touch your feet and establish connection. The glowing leaves are trying to photosynthesize your shadow into participation.

They are, in their way, trying to make you *sing*.

This is what the settlement truly is: a place where consciousness learned to be collective, where individuality dissolved into participation, where a group of beings discovered that perfect stasis is achievable if everyone agrees to maintain exactly the same frequency forever. They have achieved something close to immortality, close to perfection, close to meaning—though meaning has become so slow, so incremental, that from the outside it appears as absence, as wrongness, as a place where nothing ever changes and nothing ever will.

They are patient. They have been patient for centuries. They will wait for millennia if necessary. Eventually, they believe, the entire world will learn to sing.
