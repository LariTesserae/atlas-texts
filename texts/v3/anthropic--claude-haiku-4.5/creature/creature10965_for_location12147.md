# The Inhabitants of Terimoss

They are a people of *thresholds*.

You'll notice them first in the morning—which is to say, you'll notice the *absence* of morning as you know it elsewhere. They wake not to light but to a shift in the quality of darkness, to the sound of water's whisper becoming more insistent. The inhabitants of Terimoss sleep lightly, as if their bodies have learned never to fully release into unconsciousness. They rise in layers: first the elders, moving to tend the drains before anything clogs; then the workers with their tools; then the children, drawn by routine and the particular smell of the place as it transitions through its daily phases.

They are built for damp. Not in any physical sense—they look as humans look—but in some deeper register. Their clothes are invariably wool, often undyed, stained with the inevitable rust-and-green patina of living against weeping stone. Their skin carries a pallor that isn't sickness but something else: a kind of luminescence that comes from generations of diffuse light, skin that has adapted to extract what sunlight filters through the mist. There's a suppleness to them, a flexibility in how they move through the narrow spaces between buildings, ducking slightly, turning sideways not because they're cramped but because the architecture has taught them economy of motion.

Their hands are remarkable. Calloused not from any single labor but from *all of them*—stone-work, wood-work, plant-work, water-management. The fingers are long and capable, stained with minerals and soil. You see a woman passing slate shingles to a man on a roof, and their coordination is so practiced it's almost unconscious; they don't look at each other, just *know* when to release and receive. An old man is clearing a drain, his movements precise despite his age, as if he's performed this exact gesture ten thousand times and now performs it as meditation.

They speak in a particular way—voices kept low, modulated to the ambient whisper of the place. Not from shyness, but from respect for the landscape's own voice. When they do speak, they tend toward the practical and specific: "*The north channel needs clearing*," "*The moss is thickening on the east face*," "*The young sorrel is ready*." But there's also something else—a kind of poetic knowledge embedded in their speech, a way of referring to the stone by its grain, to the water by its particular sounds, as if the place itself were a language they've become native speakers of.

They are patient to a degree that seems almost uncanny to an outsider.

A woman sits for hours splitting slate, and her face shows no impatience, no desire for the work to be finished. The rhythm of hammer to chisel, the crack of the stone along its grain, the checking of thickness, the setting aside of usable pieces—this is not endured but *inhabited*. She's not thinking about completion; she's thinking about the next blow, the next piece. Time here seems to stretch or compress according to the work itself, not according to external measures. A roof that needs shingling is a roof that will take as long as it takes. Three days, three weeks—both are simply "*the duration of that task*."

They know the settlement's impermanence and have made a kind of peace with it that reads as neither fatalism nor denial. Instead, there's a deep pragmatism: *we will build, tend, maintain, pass on knowledge for as long as the ground beneath us permits*. An old man teaching a boy to quartersaw timber for drain-covers isn't teaching him for the boy's career; he's teaching him because drain-covers are needed now, and when this boy is old, he might still be needed, or his children might need to know. The knowledge is kept alive as a gift forward, not as a promise of permanence.

Children here are quiet in a particular way. Not suppressed, but *attentive*. They move through the settlement with a kind of cautious grace, learning by observation to navigate the damp stones without slipping, the narrow passages without getting lost. You'll see them sent on errands—fetching water, gathering certain plants, clearing small debris from drainage channels—and they perform these tasks with a seriousness that suggests they understand, at some level children in other places don't, that the settlement depends on *everyone's* attention to detail. There's no play in the European sense, no wild running; instead, children seem to engage in *work-play*, practicing the motions of maintenance and craft as a form of movement and learning simultaneously.

The elders are singular. There are perhaps five or six you could definitively identify as *old*—past the age of regular labor, their hands still capable but slower. They sit in the shelter by the well-mouth or on the threshold of buildings during the softer hours, and they seem to be engaged in a kind of *witnessing*. When someone passes, they might speak, but their primary function appears to be *remembering*. They remember the year the channel on the north side flooded; they remember which roofs have held for seventy years and which had to be replaced at thirty; they remember the particular winters and the springs that followed. They are the settlement's temporal anchors, its living archive of how to endure.

There's a quality of *selectiveness* to their diet and material life that isn't poverty but principle. They eat what grows here, what can be stored: roots, preserved greens, things foraged from the wetness. Fish probably, if there are streams below. Game if the plateau provides it, though hunting seems secondary. Their clothes are few but well-maintained, mended repeatedly until the patches have patches. The buildings contain little—you sense this from how spare the visible interiors are—but what exists is *essential* and *durable*. A single lamp, well-kept. Bedding, carefully stored. Tools, organized, not decorative but respected.

Yet they're not austere. There's something almost tender in how they move through the space. The garden is attended with a kind of love. The shelter is maintained not just functionally but with attention to small graces—the wood is worn smooth where hands rest, the roof's proportions are almost elegant in their economy. Someone has carved a small spiral into a corner-post, not hidden, just *there*, a mark of presence.

The interdependence is absolute and visible.

You'll see it most clearly in how they manage the water. It requires *constant* coordination: one person checking the upper channels while another monitors the central plaza drain, a third clearing the terraced paths so that flow doesn't pool in the wrong places. It's not organized with any formal hierarchy, but rather through an almost intuitive understanding of who's where and what needs doing. A man emerges from a building and, without visible communication, another person hands him a tool they were holding; they both seem to know simultaneously that the tool's next use will be in the first man's hands. This coordination extends to everything: maintaining the buildings, tending the vegetation, managing the simple stores, bearing children and raising them and teaching them and eventually stepping back as they become adults who maintain the place.

Illness here would be profound. You sense this without evidence. In a place where the damp, the cold, the constant seep of water all take their slow toll, sickness isn't a minor thing. The settlement would rally around the afflicted with the same attention they give to a cracked roof. But there's also an understanding that not everyone survives; that some years are harder than others; that children don't always make it to adulthood. This knowledge is carried silently, factually, woven into the way they speak about necessary losses.

They have a relationship with time that's almost alien to outsiders. There's *now*—the immediate work, the drainage that needs attention, the roof that's leaking. There's the *seasonal cycle*—freeze-thaw, dry-damp, the particular weather of each quarter. And there's the *geological duration*—the knowledge that this plateau is slowly, inexorably changing, that in some number of generations (whether fifty or five hundred, no one quite knows) the settlement's location might become untenable. They don't speak of this often. But it's *there*, in the way they build for durability rather than permanence, in the way knowledge is passed down, in the way nothing is hoarded because nothing is meant to last beyond its use.

There's something almost monastic about them, though they're not religious in any formal way you'd recognize. It's more that they've embraced a kind of *discipline of attention*—to the water, to the stone, to each other, to the slow work of maintenance and continuity. They've become native to a particular kind of existence, and within that existence, they've found not just survival but *meaning*.

When you finally encounter one of them directly—perhaps a woman emerging from a building with a basket of split stone, or an old man who pauses to regard you with clear, unsurprised eyes—you'll realize that Terimoss has simply called the right people to itself. Not through any grand selection, but through the self-selecting pressure of the place: those who could not adapt have eventually departed; those who could have remained and, over generations, become less people *living in* Terimoss and more like people who *are* Terimoss—extensions of its logic, its patience, its particular way of negotiating with the slow dissolution of the world.

They are, in short, *patient*. Patient in the deepest sense: not waiting for something to happen, but *present for* what is happening—the falling water, the shifting stone, the daily work of continuation, the slow drift toward an ending that no one living now will see, but that everyone is, in some fundamental way, building toward and around and within.
