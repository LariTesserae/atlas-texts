# What Lives There

The first thing to understand is that nothing *lives* here in the traditional sense—nothing with a singular life, a clear boundary between self and environment. What inhabits the Trellis Gardens is a *tendency*. A direction. A slow, patient *becoming*.

If you must name it: it is the negotiation itself. The live argument between iron and chlorophyll, between the grid and the tangle, between the schedule of the irrigation system (still punctual, still cycling, still obeying some forgotten timer in the darkness below) and the absolute indifference of growth. The place is inhabited by the friction between these two wills.

But there are *presences*, if you look closer.

In the deepest chambers of foliage—where the light doesn't reach, where the air becomes almost liquid with humidity—there are the fungi. Not the visible fruiting bodies, though those exist too: wine-dark brackets of polypore spreading across dead wood, delicate gills of pale mushrooms erupting from the base of vines. But the true inhabitant is the mycelial network, the white filamentary consciousness that runs through every decaying board, every compacted leaf, every dark space between things. It is not one organism. It is many—networks within networks, each species following its own chemical logic, yet they form a kind of distributed awareness. The place *knows* things through fungal sensation: which boards are rotting fastest, which vines are producing the richest mulch, where water tends to pool. When the irrigation system pings and cycles, moisture spreads not randomly but along paths already established by these networks, following ancient preferences. The fungi do not decide; they suggest. The place responds.

The vines themselves are not passive. They have been here long enough to develop a kind of collective memory. They grow in patterns that echo each other—passionflower tendrils mimicking the spiral of clematis, wild grape following paths cut by older species, creating a dense manuscript written in living wood. Some of this is simple proximity, simple competition for light. But some of it is stranger. New growth often follows channels where older growth has already died—as though the plant-matter itself carries information, as though the vines can read the history written in decomposing stems and choose their paths accordingly. They are not intelligent in any human sense. But they are attentive. Responsive. They listen to the place with their root-systems, with their leaves' hunger for light. They are the memory of the Trellis Gardens, encoded in how they tangle.

The moisture itself—this is crucial—is not inert. It is a medium, yes, but also an actor. Water finds water. It pools in the warped boards, and in pooling, it creates boundary conditions, microclimates, little pockets of possibility. In the places where moisture accumulates most, certain plants thrive; in the drier pockets (relatively speaking), others establish themselves. The water-paths create geography. The seeping sound you hear is not just drainage; it is the water-system teaching the space about its own topology, carving invisible channels, carrying nutrients and spores and information from one zone to another. The water remembers where it's been. It carries the mineral taste of the old metal frames, the chemical signature of decayed leaf-matter, traces of the chemicals that once ran through the irrigation system. Nothing drinks here that doesn't drink the place's memory in the water.

The lizard you saw—that grey-backed, jerky-moving thing—is a sentinel. Rare here, yes. Its presence is indeed ceremonial, but not in the way you intuited. It is *proof*. Proof that the system has reached some threshold of stability, that something can *eat* here, can make a living from insects that themselves feed on the dense green profusion. The lizard is a signal that the place is becoming habitable—that the terrible fecundity has organized itself enough to support a creature at a higher trophic level. Its presence says: *this is working*. This anarchic tangle is producing enough surplus to feed something small and careful and furtive. When you saw it move, you saw the place confirming its own viability.

The insects—the ones you sense in the foliage but don't see—are the true workers. Aphids and whiteflies, certainly, drawn to the sap-rich vines. But also the beneficial ones: parasitic wasps, ladybugs, lacewings. They have arrived unbidden, drawn by the density and complexity. They move through the green chambers invisible, their larvae feeding on pests, their adults pollinating flowers you never even noticed blooming in the dim light. They are the hidden metabolism of the place. When a vine flowers—and some do, though their flowers are small and white and hidden deep in the tangle—it is because insects have found it, visited it, made it possible. The place is not just growing; it is *reproducing*. The insects are the mechanism by which the Trellis Gardens dreams of its own future.

There is something in the sealed-off portions—the service corridor, the corroded shelving area. You sense this rather than see it. Not a presence, precisely, but an *absence* that has become textured enough to almost be a presence. Whatever maintained this place once is gone, but the *pattern* of maintenance remains. The irrigation system still cycles. This cannot be accident. Either someone comes very rarely, on a schedule so long you'll never notice the intervention, or something—some automation, some logic—continues from habit, from the original programming. The place keeps itself alive because it was designed to keep itself alive, and that design is so embedded in its infrastructure that it persists even in the absence of the designer. You are living inside the afterimage of intention.

But perhaps most important: the place is inhabited by *time*. Not time in the human sense—the ticking of clocks, the aging of flesh. Geological time. The deep time of decomposition and growth and slow transformation. The time in which a once-orderly greenhouse becomes a semi-wild ecosystem, in which industrial metal learns to rust into a new kind of beauty, in which vines write new grammar onto the structure that was supposed to contain them. This time is not experienced; it is *lived*, moment by moment, in the wetness of the air, in the nearly-imperceptible thickening of a vine, in the slow sinking of a board under its burden of accumulated moisture and growth.

To live here is to live inside a process. There is no stable state. There is only the direction: toward greater density, greater entanglement, greater mutual dependence. The vines grow thicker. The fungi spread deeper. The water finds new paths. The insects establish new relationships. The place is *becoming*—and everything that inhabits it, from the mycelial networks to the patient cycling of the irrigation system, is an expression of that becoming.

The Trellis Gardens of Keswick Station houses not residents but *collaborators*—each element (plant, fungus, water, light, old machinery, the memory of order, the reality of chaos) engaged in an improvisation with all the others, working out what shape a living thing takes when it's built from the wreckage of human intention.
