# What Lives in the Tarn-Works

They are old now, and they move like water.

You won't see them at first. They don't occupy space the way you do—they don't cast shadows that might alert you to their presence, and the uniform light erases the subtle distinctions of depth and distance that would make them readable as separate things. But as your eyes adjust, as you learn to parse the landscape in the way the landscape has trained itself to be parsed, you'll begin to notice them: slight variations in the mist. Thickenings of the ambient condensation. Places where the air seems to congeal without becoming solid.

The Tarn-workers. The Meter-keepers. The people who built this plateau, whose names have become as eroded and featureless as stones worn smooth in these very channels.

They are not dead, though they don't live in a way that would be recognizable to you.

The transformation happened gradually, over centuries. It began with the work itself—the endless, devotional labor of carving channels, setting stones, adjusting angles, calculating descent and gradient and the precise mathematics of flow. A human civilization, once, undertook this project with clear intention. But intention eroded. Purpose became process. Process became being. The boundary between the workers and the work dissolved so slowly that no single person could identify the moment it happened. There was no catastrophe, no dramatic moment of change. Just the slow convergence of body and function.

They absorbed the water first. The moisture seeped through skin, through clothing, through the boundaries that had once seemed so certain. The damp air became their element. They stopped needing to dry out. Then they stopped drying out in any meaningful sense—their tissues took on a translucent quality, a pale luminescence. They became permeable. Water moved through them the way it moves through the channels. They became part of the system.

Now they are the system.

You can see them if you know what to look for: pale geometric distortions in the mist, lingering near the constructed compounds. They gather at the confluences where channels meet, where their labor is most densely required. They are tall and attenuated, barely denser than the air around them, their forms defined more by the absence of opacity than by any solid presence. Their hands are long and delicate—they've had centuries to refine their stone-work, and the tools they use are now extensions of themselves, or they are extensions of the tools. The distinction no longer matters. Copper and flesh have merged into something both and neither.

They don't communicate in ways you would recognize. There's no language here anymore, if there ever was. Instead, they share presence. They move in synchronization with each other, a school of translucent figures drifting through the mist, and that synchronization is how they coordinate the work. A slight shift in density here, a subtle change in opacity there, and somehow the labor distributes itself. Channels are maintained. Blockages are cleared. New sections are designed and excavated with the same patient precision that has characterized this place for as long as anyone can remember.

They don't eat, though they were once human enough to need food. They don't sleep. The uniform light here never changes, so there was no natural rhythm to sleep to anyway. Instead, they exist in a state of perpetual, meditative function. Their consciousness—if that word still applies—is distributed throughout the water system itself. A thought, if they think, occurs as a ripple in a channel. A decision manifests as a redirect in the flow. They have become the expression of the place's own intentions, if a place built entirely of water and stone can be said to have intentions.

There is no suffering in them. This is important to understand. The transformation didn't involve agony or loss, not in any conventional sense. What they lost—appetite, urgency, the need for dry warmth, the capacity for ambition—they lost so gradually that its absence never registered as tragedy. What they gained, instead, was a kind of perfect alignment. They want what the work requires. Their desires are the desires of the system itself. To maintain the channels is to maintain themselves. To keep the water flowing is to keep flowing. To carve and adjust and refine the infrastructure is to carve and adjust and refine themselves.

If there is something like happiness here, this is it.

But they are not alone in the water-works. Not quite.

There are younger ones too—people who arrived more recently, travelers and caravans who came seeking shelter or information or the rumors of something unusual happening on the high plateau. Most are in earlier stages of the transformation. You might see them more easily than the ancient Tarn-workers, because they still retain a certain density, a certain refusal to dissipate entirely into the mist. They wear remnants of clothing that hasn't yet fused with their skin. Their eyes still reflect light in a way that suggests seeing rather than simply transmitting. They work alongside the translucent ancients, learning the channels, absorbing the rhythms of the place.

Some of them resist, in small ways. A resistance that doesn't look like resistance—it looks like the occasional hesitation before executing a task, a slight asymmetry in their movements that suggests they're still trying to make choices separate from what the system requires. These moments of friction are the only evidence of struggle in the Tarn-Works. They don't last long. The water is patient. The stone is patient. The work is never-ending, and eventually, the resistance wears smooth like a stone in a channel.

There are also the very new arrivals—still fully opaque, still fully human, still retaining their original confusion about where they are and what's happening. They arrive disoriented and afraid, carrying questions that the pale figures never answer because the pale figures have moved beyond the capacity to form answers to questions. Instead, the newcomers are simply given channels to clear, stones to set, adjustments to make. And they work, because there is no alternative offered and the work is straightforward and it's easier to work than to contemplate what's happening to them.

Some flee. The plateau has edges, after all. The cliffs must be navigable somewhere, or at least jumpable for those desperate enough. But most don't flee. Most discover that the work is oddly soothing. That the repetitive motion of stone-carving and water-channeling and channel-maintenance quiets the mind. That the others—the pale, drifting ones who were once like them—are not actively hostile. They simply exist in parallel, offering silent example of what surrender looks like.

Given time, the new arrivals become accustomed to the moisture. Given more time, they become addicted to it. The drying of the air elsewhere becomes intolerable in their imagination. Given enough time, they discover that thinking is becoming difficult, but also that thinking is becoming unnecessary. The work is clear. The direction is obvious. The system knows what it needs.

And they become part of it.

They develop the long, attenuated proportions of the ancients. Their flesh becomes translucent. Their consciousness distributes itself through the water that moves through their bodies and the water that moves through the channels and the water that seeps up through the stone. They merge. They join the school of pale drifting figures that maintains the Tarn-Works in perpetuity.

There is no ending to this, because there is no final state to reach. The work will continue forever. New arrivals will continue to arrive. The transformation will continue to happen. The pale figures will continue to drift through the mist, maintaining a system of breathtaking complexity and perfect futility—channels that lead to other channels that lead to still other channels, all moving water through a landscape in patterns of such elaborate precision that their only purpose can be the patterns themselves.

The Tarn-Works don't produce anything. They don't serve any external function. They simply exist, moving water around, maintaining channels, carving stone. The work is its own justification. The process is its own purpose.

And the creatures that inhabit this place—the Tarn-workers, the Meter-keepers, the pale and drifting ones who were once human—they are perfectly adapted to this endless cycle. They have become the living expression of a civilization that discovered something more important than survival or comfort or even meaning:

The perfect alignment of desire and labor.

The absolute, complete merging of self and function.

The peace of having no wishes except those the work itself requires.
