# What Lives in the Tarn of Settling Things

The creatures here are, first and foremost, *patient*.

Not the patience of things waiting for change—there is no anxiety in them, no sense of potential disruption. Rather, the patience of systems that have achieved equilibrium so complete that urgency would be a kind of blasphemy. They have become patient the way water is patient, the way stone is patient, through the simple act of ceasing to resist what is already true.

The frogs are the philosophers. There are four distinct voices, and in the evening chorus they compose something very close to argument—not aggressive, but rigorous. Back and forth across the frequency spectrum, each species making its claim about what a voice should be, and none of them wrong. They do not move more than necessary. A frog will occupy the same reed for weeks, returning to it like a monk returns to a cell, launching itself into the water only when hunger overrides inertia. When they call, it is not to court or to threaten. It is to think aloud, to participate in the tarn's ongoing meditation on the nature of sound and presence. They are amber-eyed and watchful. They remember things. If they dream, they dream in frequencies.

The dragonflies are different: they are the tarn's thought-made-manifest, the place thinking itself into motion. Their iridescence is not coloration but a kind of truth-telling—the bending of light according to patterns too complex for static description. No two dragonflies here are quite the same color, and the color changes depending on angle and light and possibly on the observer's capacity to perceive. They hunt in those three-dimensional geometries because they are solving equations, tracing paths that the air itself requires of them. They do not rest often. When they do, they cling to reed or stone with all six legs gripped tight, as though holding themselves in place against a great wind that only they can feel. They are older than birds. They remember when the world was mostly water.

The heron is the tarn's witness and recorder. It stands in the shallows with a patience that exceeds even the frogs', sometimes for hours without movement so pronounced that it registers. Its yellow eye is not blank but intensely focused, seeing not just the fish and small creatures of the water but the *pattern* of the water, the current beneath the surface, the invisible hierarchies. When it moves to strike, the motion is so sudden and so perfect that it seems less like hunting than like correcting a mistake—setting right something that was briefly out of alignment. The heron does not eat here often; the tarn provides sustenance but seems to expect restraint. The heron understands this contract. It respects the equilibrium. Its plumage is slate-colored and weathered, beautiful in the way old things are beautiful, worn into truth.

The smaller birds—the warblers and the unnamed things—are the tarn's memory-keepers and mourners. They sing not because they are happy or because they are communicating, but because they are acutely aware of the transience of everything, including themselves. The song is both celebration and elegy, performed in real time. They flit between stones and reeds with a kind of anxious grace, never settling, always moving, as though stillness might trap them in the moment they're currently grieving. They are thin-boned and bright-eyed, and they carry within them an overwhelming sense of *now*—not the eternal now of the frogs or the deep-time now of the heron, but the sharp, aching now of creatures aware that every moment contains its own ending. The vegetation feeds them. The tarn sustains them. They sing their thanks and their sorrow in the same breath.

The insects—the beetles, the bugs, the small crawling and flying things—are the tarn's workers and its communion. They are so numerous that they constitute something almost like a single organism, a distributed intelligence that processes the place's dead matter and transforms it into living form. An individual beetle is a small thing, insignificant, but a million beetles are a *process*, a kind of metabolism that the tarn itself performs. They do not think—or if they think, it is in a language so far removed from human cognition that the distinction becomes meaningless. They simply *are*, in overwhelming, humble, continuous presence. They are the tarn's actual body, the flesh through which all other life moves.

The invisible things—the microorganisms, the creatures too small to see—are perhaps what the place is *really* made of. They are the true inhabitants, the actual decision-makers. The visible creatures are all passengers, beneficiaries of the invisible architecture that converts sediment to nutrient, that breaks down the dead into the substrate of the living. They exist in such numbers that they constitute something like geology—a layer of the earth that is alive and aware and ancient beyond human measure.

And the thing that might come back—the thing for which the shelter waits—is something the tarn remembers. Not a human, though it might wear that shape. Something that understands that equilibrium is not stasis, that patience is not passivity, that a place can be so thoroughly settled that it becomes a kind of consciousness. It arrives when the internal logic of the system requires it, fulfills some function that only it can perform, and departs again into the continuity of its own long existence.

The tarn does not mourn its absence.

The tarn simply remembers.

And in the evening, the frogs sing the different notes of their ancient thought, and the birds carry their songs like small lanterns into the soft, glowing dark, and the dragonflies trace their impossible equations against the sky, and the rocks—warm and patient and ancient—settle deeper into the earth, and the water breathes its slow, enormous breath, and nothing here is in a hurry.

Nothing here is ever in a hurry.
