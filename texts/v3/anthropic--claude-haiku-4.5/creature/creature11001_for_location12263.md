# The Inhabitants of Thel'Ka

## The Languid Folk

They move like the water moves—with that maddening temporal drag, as though they're walking through something thicker than air. At first you think you're seeing things, people-shaped ripples in the brightness, but then your eyes adjust and you understand: there *are* people here, and they have learned to move at the pace the marshes require.

They're thin. Elongated. Their skin has taken on the bronze-gold cast of the cistern water, and when light hits them at certain angles, you can see through them slightly—not transparent, but as if they're made of something more permeable than ordinary flesh. Their eyes are large and positioned slightly too far forward on their faces, giving them the aspect of creatures adapted to reading things in the middle distance, things emerging from haze. The whites of their eyes are faintly golden. Their pupils have a vertical quality to them, and they seem to process light differently than yours do—where you squint against the brightness, they seem to *drink* it, their pupils dilating in response to illumination rather than contracting.

They don't sweat, you notice. Their skin remains dry despite the heat and humidity. Instead, there's a faint shimmer across their bodies—a continuous exhalation of moisture that rises from them immediately, creating a faint halo effect. They breathe out water into an environment already saturated with it, and the contradiction doesn't seem to trouble them.

Their clothing (when they wear it) is made of something fibrous and pale, almost translucent, woven so loosely that air moves through it freely. It looks like it could be made from processed vegetation, from those strange grasses and mosses, boiled down and spun into thread. The garments hang loosely, following the contours of their elongated frames without clinging. You suspect the fabric is waterproofed not with oils or waxes, but with some kind of secretion—possibly their own.

They speak rarely, and when they do, their voices come with that same delay you've noticed in everything else here. Their mouths will move, and a moment later, sound will arrive—not an echo, but as if the words themselves are traveling through a denser medium than air. The effect is profoundly unsettling. Their language has many low-frequency sounds, creaks and groans that seem to resonate through the tile itself, harmonizing with the rhythm of the dripping water. Higher-pitched sounds are rarer and seem almost painful to produce, as if their vocal apparatus has become optimized for bass frequencies.

They work constantly, but their work is almost invisible. You'll notice a cistern that was slightly lower than the others is now level again—the water having been redistributed through channels you didn't notice before. A patch of vegetation that was drooping is now revived, though no one was tending to it while you watched. The ferns in one section will have reoriented their fronds to catch light in a new angle, and you realize this wasn't growth or movement—someone *arranged* them that way.

They know the system. Not intellectually, not as something they learned and remember. They know it the way fish know water. When they walk, they place their feet on the tiles with absolute assurance, never slipping despite the moisture, moving across the damp surfaces as if walking on dry ground. They can taste the water—you've seen them kneel at the edge of a cistern and run their fingers through it, touching their fingers to their tongues, and you realize they're reading it the way you might read a book. The composition, the temperature, the exact concentration of whatever makes it thick and slow.

Their hands are peculiar. Too many joints in the fingers. You don't notice at first, but once you do, you can't stop noticing. They can bend their fingers in ways that suggest an extra knuckle in each digit, giving them a delicate, almost tentacular precision. Watch one of them arrange the vegetation, and you'll see fingers moving with independent control, each one capable of adjusting a single frond while the others hold the plant steady. It's not dexterity—it's something more like individual manipulation.

They're not aggressive toward you, but they're not friendly either. They watch. When you move, their large eyes track you with an intensity that suggests they're assessing something about your relationship to this place. Whether you belong. Whether you can be sustained here. You have the distinct impression they're waiting to see if you'll *change*—if the marshes will accept you and begin to reshape you as they have reshaped everything else. Some of them seem hopeful about this possibility. Others seem doubtful. One of them, a figure with skin so pale it's almost translucent, watches you with what might be pity.

They sleep in the vegetation, you've realized. You've seen them curled among the ferns and mosses, their bronze-gold skin nearly indistinguishable from the water-soaked plant matter. When they sleep, their breathing becomes almost imperceptible, their bodies seeming to merge with the landscape they tend. You're not certain how long they sleep, or whether they dream. You suspect they dream of the patterns in the tiles, the geometry of the cistern system, the slow rhythms of water distribution. You suspect they understand mathematics at a level you never will, written in the language of hydrology and thermal dynamics and time made thick as syrup.

## The Deep Swimmers

In the larger cisterns, beyond the shallow pools that cluster the closer platforms, there are *things*. You've seen them only in glimpses—shapes moving in the bronze-gold water with a slowness that matches the water's own pace. Long. Sinuous. Not quite fish, not quite anything you have a name for.

They're pale as the Languid Folk, but where those people are adapted to the land-between-water, these creatures are adapted to the water itself. They move with an undulating motion that seems to work *with* the viscosity of the liquid rather than against it, as if they're part of the same system, creatures of the medium rather than visitors to it.

You've seen one surface once—just barely. The curve of something that might have been a back, skin that reflected the bright light with an opalescent quality, and then it was gone, descending into the murk. The surface of the water rippled in its wake, but the ripples moved slowly, the disturbance persisting for minutes as if the water were too thick to properly transmit waves.

The Languid Folk don't seem to regard them as food, though you're not certain they regard them as anything. There's no hunting, no interaction you can discern. The deep swimmers keep to their deeper cisterns, rarely approaching the shallows. Once you saw one of the pale figures standing at the edge of a large pool, perfectly still, for perhaps an hour. At the end of that hour, one of the swimmers surfaced nearby, and the figure reached down and—did something. Touched it, perhaps. Communicated. The moment lasted perhaps three seconds, and then the swimmer submerged and the figure walked away, moving at their characteristically lagging pace.

You suspect the deep swimmers are older than the Languid Folk. Something about their movements suggests an older relationship with this place, a more complete adaptation. You suspect they might have been here when the marshes still *were* marshes, before they were engineered into these terraces. You suspect they might have been *part* of that engineering, partners in some ancient negotiation between sentience and landscape.

## The Birds

They're not like normal birds. For one thing, they're far too still. For another, their plumage seems to generate its own light—not luminescence exactly, but something that makes them appear brighter than their surroundings should allow, as if they exist in their own localized lighting condition.

They're predatory. You've seen them strike at the water-insects with movements so fast they seem to create their own distortion in the heat-haze, but once caught, they don't eat immediately. They'll carry their prey to a nearby plant and arrange it carefully among the fronds, as if cataloging it. Sometimes they return to these arrangements hours later. You don't know if they're saving them for later or if this is some kind of display, some form of aesthetic practice.

They vocalize rarely, but when they do, it's with that same delayed quality as the Languid Folk's speech. A bird will open its beak, and a moment later, sound will emerge—a cry that seems to arrive from somewhere distant, as if the sound had to travel through the thick air to reach you. The other birds respond to these calls with head turns and subtle shifts in posture, paying attention to something you can't quite perceive.

One of them has taken an interest in you. It perches on the vegetation nearest your path, watching with eyes that seem far too intelligent for a bird. When you move, it moves. When you stop, it stops. You have the impression it's trying to determine what category of thing you are. Whether you're a temporary visitor or whether you're in the process of becoming part of the system. It seems skeptical.

## The Unobserved

There are things here you haven't seen. You know this with certainty.

The shapes that move in the distance, disturbing vegetation but never quite coming into view. The sounds you hear sometimes in the deep hours—sounds that might be the water system itself, or might be something else, something that lives in the spaces between the terraces. The sense of being observed not by the Languid Folk or the birds, but by something vaster, something that perceives you the way you perceive the individual tiles—as part of a larger pattern.

The marshes of Thel'Ka are aware, and that awareness has attendants.

You suspect that if you stayed long enough, if the heat and brightness and that strange temporal drag began to reshape you the way they've reshaped everything else, you might eventually perceive what watches from the spaces you can't quite focus on. You might join the Languid Folk, or descend into the depths with the swimmers, or perch with the birds and wait for movements that mean something.

You suspect that every living thing here—including you, now—is in the process of being absorbed into a single vast consciousness, distributed across the terraces and the water and the tiles themselves. You suspect this process is not violent or unwilling, but rather slow, inevitable, like the pace of the water moving through the channels.

You suspect that there's no hurrying it.

And part of you, shamefully, is beginning to wonder what it would feel like to stop resisting. To let your movements slow to their pace. To feel your skin begin to take on that bronze-gold cast. To start reading the water. To learn the language of the dripping.

The Languid Folk notice your hesitation. The one with the translucent skin seems less pitying now. Almost, you might say, welcoming.
