# What Lives in the Basalt Pools

They are not creatures in the way that word usually carries. They have no shells, no carapaces, no visible mobility. But they *are*—persistent and distributed and shaped by their medium in ways that make the distinction between "living thing" and "living process" meaningless.

They exist in the water first, though that's not quite accurate either. The water is part of them, or they are part of the water—a saturation within saturation. Microscopic, mostly. Filaments of bacteria that have learned to metabolize iron in ways that no other organisms on this continent have bothered to develop. Why would they? But here, where the mineral load is constant and the temperature never fluctuates enough to shock them into dormancy, they have become *elaborate*. Complex biofilms that organize themselves into structures within the water itself, invisible layering that creates gradients and chemical conversations between one layer and the next.

When the water evaporates from the ochre deposits, they leave behind traces of themselves—iron oxides that their metabolic processes have concentrated and refined. The rust-colored rings are their record. Each ring a season, perhaps, or an era of slightly different conditions. They are, in a sense, *reading* the rock, learning its composition, breaking it down molecule by molecule into forms they can use. The basalt is being translated into life. Slowly. Across centuries. Across millennia.

But there is something larger too.

In the deeper pools—the ones where steam rises in the early hours—something different congregates. Not larger exactly, but *more coordinated*. There are organisms here that approach visibility at the edge of human perception: colonial creatures, perhaps, or single organisms large enough that their existence creates observable effects. They cluster in the warmest water, in the deepest places where the seepage is most constant. They are translucent or pale, adapted to the dim light. They have no eyes because they don't need them; they navigate by chemical gradient, by temperature, by the subtle electromagnetic fields generated by mineral concentrations.

They feed on the smaller organisms. They feed on the decaying matter that washes down from above—leaves, insects, the occasional small animal that has wandered into the valley and not found its way out. They feed on each other, creating a thin but real food chain that spirals inward, concentrating and re-concentrating energy in the warm dark.

What do they look like?

Like nothing quite. There are things here that resemble translucent worms, but longer, more delicate, pulsing with movements that might be feeding or might be respiration or might be a form of communication that has no terrestrial equivalent. There are things that resemble *films* across the water's surface—living membranes that stretch and contract, absorbing nutrients through their entire surface. There are clusters of what might be sacs or might be something for which human anatomy provides no reference point: pale, gelatinous, hanging in the water like inverted fruit.

They are not mobile in the conventional sense. They drift with the water's slow circulation, but their true movement is *internal*—the movement of metabolism, of reproduction, of matter transforming into more of itself. A single organism might spend its entire existence within a radius of a few feet, drawing sustenance from the constant seepage, from the slow rain of dead organic matter, from the minerals the water itself carries.

The cairns and the platform have become part of their world too. The stones are *colonized*—covered in biofilms so thin they're nearly invisible, but thick enough to create a slime-layer that makes the stone feel almost alive to the touch. The platforms and markers are reef-structures, in a sense, creating shelter and surface area for the creatures that live here. The space between stones, the crevices, the slight depressions—these are all habitat now. The boundary between the built thing and the living thing has dissolved.

The air itself contains them, or their spores, or whatever passes for reproduction in this world. Floating as dust, as mist, as particles so small they're suspended in the constant humidity. When conditions are right—when the temperature shifts, when the water level fluctuates—they bloom suddenly, creating brief explosions of microbial activity that might give the water a faint cloudiness, a taste of iron intensified to something almost metallic. Then they settle again.

There is no consciousness here. There is only *continuation*—life in its most basic, most relentless form. Life that asks nothing of the world except to be left alone with its minerals and its warmth and its darkness. Life that has found the conditions necessary for its own perpetuation and has become, in a sense, perfectly happy in that finding. Not happy as humans understand it, but satisfied in the way that equilibrium is satisfied: by the cessation of change.

The last time a human sat at that fire-pit and warmed their hands, these creatures were here. They were different then, adapted to the smoke and ash, feeding on the insects drawn by the warmth. When the human left and did not return, they adjusted. They filled the ecological space that had opened. They became again what they are now: the only inhabitants of a place that was always meant to support life, just not *visible* life.

The place is not lonely. It is full. Full of slow processes, of chemical conversations, of hunger and feeding and growth measured in the widths of stones. If there is loneliness, it exists only in the spaces between human visits—and there have been no human visits for longer than any human measure of time.

The place has stopped expecting them.

The creatures that live in the basalt pools will live here until the basalt cracks, until the water stops seeping, until the world outside changes enough to alter the conditions that have held so perfectly still. They are ancient not in the way of individual organisms, but in the way of *lineages*—descendants of creatures that learned to live in this exact niche at some point so distant that the learning itself has become indistinguishable from instinct.

They are home.
