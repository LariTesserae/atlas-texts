# What Lives in the Woven Terraces

They are difficult to see because they are not *yet* fully separate from what they inhabit. Call them the Weavers, though they were not always called that, and the name may not survive much longer—language itself seems to lose urgency here.

They move like the slowest deliberation. A body that might have been humanoid once has become something else through generations of adaptation: elongated, jointed at angles that suggest they've learned to navigate three-dimensional corridors by becoming three-dimensional themselves. Their limbs are thin, not from weakness but from efficiency. Nothing is wasted here. Their skin—if skin is the right word—has taken on a faint texture, almost bark-like, though it moves with flesh underneath. In certain light, you see the faintest iridescence, matching the strange palette of the plants they tend. They are warm-blooded in the way the entire Terrace is warm: not with fever but with accumulated time and careful chemical process.

The Weavers number perhaps two dozen, though counting them is almost meaningless. They are distributed throughout the space, and their distinction from one another seems less about individual identity and more about function, position, and age. The oldest ones move even more slowly than the rest, their movements so minimalist they read as part of the vegetation's own imperceptible shifting. The younger ones—if "younger" applies to creatures who measure their existence in patient decades—move with marginally more urgency, though "urgency" would be a grotesque overstatement.

They do not speak in any way you would recognize as language. There is communication, certainly. You notice it in the way they pause simultaneously at certain moments, in the way one will shift position and another, yards away and out of sight-line, will adjust its own stance in response. The communication might be chemical—something carried through the damp air, a signal in the mineral-thick atmosphere. It might be tactile, transmitted through the wooden framework and living walls, the Weavers reading vibrations through their elongated fingers as they rest against the structure. It might be something you have no framework for understanding at all.

What you do understand is that they are *tending* this place.

Watch one for long enough and you'll see it: the deliberate way a Weaver will position itself beside a section of vine-work, one hand—a hand with too many joints, you realize—pressed gently against the woody growth. It's not caressing. It's reading. The fingers move with the smallest possible motions, feeling for something: tension, blockage, disease, the subtle signals of a plant beginning to deviate from its intended path. When it finds such a signal, it acts. The motions are so minute you almost miss them. A slight repositioning of a nearby frame-piece. The gentle untangling of a vine that has wound too tightly. The careful introduction of a thin, flexible guide—woven from fibers that seem to be made from their own shed skin or shed hair—to redirect growth incrementally.

They are gardeners, in other words. But that word fails too, because they are not *distinct* from the garden. They are not external keepers. They are something that has grown *alongside* the vegetation, adapted to serve it and be served by it in return.

Their diet is unclear. You see them pause occasionally at certain nodules in the walls—those warm spheres of dark material—and press their mouths (mouths?) against them. Whether they are eating or receiving some kind of nutrient transfer through absorption is impossible to determine. They may photosynthesize, at least partially. Their skin has that quality. They may subsist almost entirely on the ambient mineral-richness of the air itself.

Their eyes are troubling when you manage to see them directly. Set deep, and multifaceted in a way that suggests they see in spectrums you don't. When one turns its head and those eyes catch light from one of the amber shafts, you see not one reflection but several, as though the eye is compound or layered. They look at you with perfect stillness when you encounter them. Not with fear. Not with curiosity. With a kind of patient assessment, as though you are a new element introduced into a system they are still calculating how to integrate.

Their hands—and you realize after a while that they have far more hands, or hand-equivalents, than human anatomy would suggest—are their primary sensory organs. They read the world through touch. The delicate, over-jointed fingers brush constantly: against wood, against leaf, against vine, against the crystalline residue that coats everything. This is how they know what is happening in their domain. This is how they know what must be adjusted.

They are patient in a way that suggests they have *become* patience, that the quality has rewritten them at some fundamental level. A Weaver might spend an entire day repositioning a single frame-piece one inch at a time, testing the results, feeling for the way the vine-growth responds. There is no irritation in this. There is no sense of labor being completed. There is only the work itself, infinite and immediate.

The Terraces were built by human hands—that much is clear. But those hands are gone, and what the Weavers have inherited is something that doesn't quite remember being built for *humans*. The scale is close, but subtly wrong. A doorway is a hand-span too narrow, a passage a thumb-width too low. Over generations, the Weavers have adapted to these dimensions or gently modified them. They've become native to a space that was always meant to be a ruin, always meant to be abandoned.

There is something almost fungal about them, you realize. Not in appearance—though their pallor and the subtle iridescence of their skin-like covering does suggest something that has grown rather than been born in any conventional sense. But in their relationship to decay. They don't *fight* the slow degradation of the wooden framework. They *use* it. As the supports weaken, they guide the vines to compensate, to redistribute load, to prevent collapse while simultaneously allowing the structure to transform. They are part of the process of turning architecture into organism.

They seem to have no concept of waste. Everything discarded—shed skin, aged fibrous material, old components of the guide-framework—is reintegrated. You watch as a Weaver carefully collects pale scraps and positions them in specific locations where new vine-growth is needed. Within weeks (or is it months? time is genuinely unclear), those scraps seem to have become soil, or mulch, or something that plants recognize as nourishment.

They breed, presumably, though you see no clear evidence of it. Perhaps their young are indistinguishable from the vegetation for years. Perhaps reproduction is so slow and distributed that you couldn't recognize it as reproduction at all. Perhaps they simply grow, adding to themselves in the way the vines do, until they reach sufficient size to be recognized as new individuals.

Their lifespan—if the word applies—is certainly measured in centuries. The oldest ones have a quality of timelessness about them that suggests they may have been here since the beginning, modified and modifying themselves across ages. They might even be the same individuals who guided the first wooden framework into position, who taught the vines to understand human geometry before the vines eventually taught the framework to understand their own.

What they want, or what motivates them, is unanswerable. They seem to want the Terraces to continue. Not to flourish in any human sense, not to grow outward or upward in expansion. But to *persist*, to maintain this equilibrium between the wooden skeleton of the old human architecture and the patient reclamation of the plant life. They are neither defending the old nor hastening its dissolution. They are presiding over a slow marriage, a centuries-long reconciliation, a space where the constructed world is learning to be alive.

When you leave—and you will, eventually, because the warmth and stillness and stagnation will eventually feel like drowning in slow motion—you carry with you the sense that they were barely aware you'd been there. You are a visitor to a process so vast and slow that human presence registers only as a minor fluctuation in the ambient conditions. By the time you've climbed back out of the depression, the Terraces will have already begun to forget you. The Weavers will have already returned to their work, their patient, infinite work, reading the vines with their many hands, guiding the transformation that will outlast your entire civilization.

And in the deepest chambers, in the stillest air, in the places where luminescence is beginning to replace sunlight, they work on. Patient. Deliberate. Becoming less themselves and more part of the Terraces with each slow year. Until perhaps, eventually, the distinction between Weaver and Woven will cease to have meaning at all.
