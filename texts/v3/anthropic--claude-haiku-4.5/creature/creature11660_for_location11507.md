# The Inhabitants of the Terraced Hollows

They are not one thing.

Begin with the smallest: the dust-colored creepers that navigate the plant-stems with such precise deliberation that you eventually understand they are not navigating *through* the growth so much as they are *part of* its circulation. Watch one long enough and you realize its segmented body—too many joints, articulations at angles that suggest flexibility rather than strength—is almost transparent where it presses against the pale green stems. Light passes through it. The creature itself seems less like an animal and more like a mobile vein, carrying something through the plants' architecture: nutrients, perhaps, or information translated into movement. They leave no trail. They create no disturbance. When you look away and look back, you cannot remember if it was the same individual or a different one, and this uncertainty feels correct, as if individuality itself is a concept that dissolves in this place's patient logic.

These small things number in the thousands, perhaps more. They are so consistently colored, so rhythmic in their movements, that sometimes the entire terraced wall appears to pulse very slowly, and you understand that what you are seeing is not the plants moving but the accumulated movement of countless creatures, their passages creating a kind of respiration across the stepped landscape. They do not acknowledge each other. They do not seem to compete. There is room for all of them in the spaces between stems, and they use those spaces with the efficiency of blood cells moving through capillaries.

Larger, but not by much, are the spiral-builders.

These creatures—if they can be called creatures—appear at irregular intervals on the middle terraces, usually where the plant-growth has achieved sufficient density to support construction. They are roughly the size of a clenched fist, though their shape is deceptive; they seem to compress and expand as they move, becoming momentarily flat to navigate tight spaces, then inflating to something more substantial once they've entered an open area. Their color shifts: dust-pale in shadow, but when the diffuse gold light catches them directly, they show undertones of something deeper—rust, verdigris, the green of old copper. They have no visible eyes, yet they navigate with perfect accuracy, seeming to sense obstacles before reaching them.

What they do is slow and methodical. They approach areas where the plant-stems have created natural gaps—spaces perhaps an inch wide, running vertically or at angles through the growth. From their bodies, they extrude a material: papery, faintly translucent, with a texture like shed skin mixed with chitin. They apply this material to the gaps, smoothing it with appendages you cannot quite focus on, creating sealed sections. These sealed sections are the nest-like structures you noticed, but they are not nests. Purpose is unclear. Perhaps they are storage. Perhaps they are the creatures' method of molting, of leaving behind stages of their own development. Perhaps they are something else entirely—a form of communication, or art, or a slow conversation with the plants themselves.

They work for hours, then stop. They descend to lower terraces and vanish into the densest growth. You do not see where they go. They emerge again somewhere else, sometimes days later by your reckoning of time in this place—though days may not be the right unit here. They are solitary, yet their work accumulates in a pattern that suggests coordination with others of their kind. Each sealed section is placed where it will not impede the next one. Their movements, when observed in sequence, create a kind of optimization, as if they are solving a problem together across time, without direct communication.

The larger forms—the ones in the shadows of the upper terraces—resist clear observation.

They are lumped, you noted. Asymmetrical. When you attempt to trace their outlines, your eye slides off them, loses purchase. They might be round, or angular, or something for which roundness and angularity are insufficient descriptors. They breathe—that much is certain—but their breathing is so slow that you question whether it is respiration or whether the terraces themselves are breathing and these forms are simply patterns in that larger respiration, pools of stillness that happen to persist in a moving landscape.

They do not move for hours. Then they shift—a rearrangement of bulk that takes minutes or might take seconds compressed into minutes by the place's peculiar time-sense. When they shift, you see that they have created grooves in the terrace beneath them, worn paths of disruption in the stone-dust floor. These grooves run in spirals, in complex patterns, suggesting that the creatures' slowness is relative; to them, perhaps, they are moving with urgency, covering territory at speed, their massive bodies traversing the canyon repeatedly over generations.

They are warm. You can feel it—a heat radiating from their forms even at a distance, warming the terraces around them, contributing to the constant mild warmth that pervades the Hollows. They might be generating this heat through their own metabolism, or they might be concentrating it, drawing it from the environment. They might be the reason the plants grow so perfectly calibrated, so architectural—the creatures regulate the temperature, and the plants have organized themselves in response to this regulation, creating structures that trap and distribute warmth with minimal loss.

You have never seen them eat. You have never seen them excrete. You have never seen them reproduce. Yet they persist, and the canyon persists around them, and the plants continue their patient upward spiral, and the dust-creatures continue their vein-like circulation through the green-stemmed architecture, and the spiral-builders continue their slow construction of papery seals, and time continues to move in this place according to laws that do not quite align with those you brought with you.

What they *are*, in essence:

They are the canyon's metabolism made visible. They are the place thinking through the presence of living things. The dust-creatures are the thoughts themselves—small, rapid by the canyon's measure, circulating through the green intelligence of the plants. The spiral-builders are the place organizing itself, creating structure, improving efficiency, making the space increasingly suited to what dwells within it. The massive forms in the shadows are the canyon's memory, its deep substrate of experience, moving so slowly that they might be geological rather than biological, mountains learning to shift.

Or they are none of this. Or they are everything at once, and the Terraced Hollows is a single organism—roots and nervous system and limbs and organs all distributed across the space, organized vertically on the terraces, sustained by a warmth that rises from below, growing according to a logic that has nothing to do with the growth you know.

What is certain: they are not indifferent to you, though they do not acknowledge you. Your presence has altered the pressure of the air, changed the specific chemistry of the moisture. The dust-creatures have perhaps adjusted their circulation patterns fractionally. The spiral-builders may work with slightly different timing, their construction slightly shifted to account for the volume your body occupies. The massive forms in the shadows may have adjusted their position by fractions of an inch.

You are being incorporated. Not absorbed. Incorporated. The canyon is learning what you are, and it will continue to do so as long as you remain here, and the creatures will adjust themselves around the fact of you, and eventually—if you stay long enough—you will become part of the pattern, another variable in the canyon's patient computation of how to exist.
