# The Inhabitants of Cadence

They are few, and they do not name themselves.

There are perhaps nine of them, though the number fluctuates in ways that don't quite correspond to arrival or departure. Sometimes you count eight. Sometimes you're certain you saw a tenth moving through the ferns at the threshold of visibility. They never fully clarify the matter. It doesn't seem to matter to them whether they're eight or nine or some other number that depends on how you're counting—whether you include the one who only leaves her building at night, or the one who seems to be present in the motion of the boardwalks themselves, more felt than seen.

They are neither young nor old, or rather, they exist in a state where age has become a variable quality. Some have hair gone entirely gray, but move with a looseness in the joints that suggests they're barely forty. Others have the unwrinkled faces of people in their thirties but move with the deliberate caution of the very aged. It's as though the place has untethered them from the normal progression of time, and they've settled into whatever state they occupy with the same acceptance they bring to everything else.

Their bodies have adapted to the humidity in visible ways. Their skin has taken on a particular texture—not unhealthy, but marked by the constant work of moisture, the way fingers of people who spend their lives in water become slightly webbed at the knuckles, or the way persistent damp makes everyone's hair curl slightly regardless of its natural state. Their fingernails are strong and somewhat translucent, and they're perpetually clean in a way that suggests constant, unconscious tending. You never see them with dirt under their nails, though they work with the earth and plants constantly.

They move with an economy of gesture that's almost meditative. There's no wasted motion, but it doesn't read as efficiency—rather as a deep attunement to what movement is actually necessary. When one of them walks the boardwalk, they don't pace. They traverse. Each step is placed with consideration. Their hands, when they work with the plants or the systems, move slowly and with great attention. It's the opposite of hurrying, but somehow they accomplish remarkable amounts. You've watched one of them tend to a section of ferns for what felt like hours, pruning here, adjusting there, moving things only marginally but with absolute intentionality. When they stepped back, the sector looked entirely different—more itself, somehow.

They communicate rarely and never loudly. When they do speak, their voices are low, almost beneath the ambient hum of the place. You've never heard them argue. You've never heard them laugh, exactly, though you've heard something like it—a soft exhalation that might be laughter or might be the sound of air moving through their throats in contentment. More often they communicate in gestures, in the positioning of their bodies relative to each other, in the simple fact of their presence in the same space. One will be working on a section of boardwalk, and another will arrive and begin assisting without any discussion of what needs to be done or how.

Their clothing is uniform in its practicality but individual in its deterioration. All of them wear layers—vests over shirts, cloaks or long jackets—but these garments are old and have been repaired many times. The repairs are visible; they've made no attempt to hide them. One woman's left sleeve is visibly a different fabric from the right, a patchwork of repair that goes back years. A man's cloak is held closed with what appears to be a splice of woven reeds. Their boots are waterproofed with something that smells faintly botanical—rendered fern oil, perhaps, or something they've derived from the plants themselves. The waterproofing is imperfect, which is fine, because they don't seem to mind having damp feet. This is simply the condition of living here.

They eat together, but not at a common table. Instead, they gather in small clusters—sometimes two, sometimes three, occasionally all of them at once—in the open space between the buildings and the deeper ferns. They eat what the place provides: small fish from the pools, caught by methods you haven't witnessed but which must involve incredible patience; vegetables or tubers grown in raised beds hidden somewhere inland; bread made from some grain that doesn't look like wheat. They eat slowly, with attention. There's no conversation during eating. When they're finished, they leave no mess; the leavings simply disappear into the moss, recycled by whatever processes operate here.

Their relationship to the pumps is intimate and strange. Twice daily, one or more of them will walk to the location where the mechanical sound originates, disappear into the vegetation, and the pumps will begin their rhythmic work. The pumps themselves you've never seen, though you've tried. The path to them leads into the ferns, and after a certain point, the ferns become too dense to penetrate without damaging them. You always turn back before you reach that boundary. The inhabitants don't. They know the way—not because they're following a visible path, but because they're moving in harmony with the place itself. Their feet find purchase where there seems to be none. The ferns part for them without being parted.

What strikes you most about them is their *attentiveness*. They are constantly aware. Not vigilant, not paranoid, but genuinely present to what's happening around them. One of them will pause mid-motion to watch an insect move across the moss. Another will stand at the edge of a pool for what feels like an hour, looking down at the dark water, while you're convinced they're doing nothing—until you realize they're observing something in the water's behavior, some micro-pattern in the ripples, some micro-ecology of the surface tension that holds meaning they're reading like text.

They do maintenance work constantly, though it would be wrong to call it labor. Replacing sections of boardwalk that have settled. Tending to the ferns in ways you don't quite understand—sometimes removing dead fronds, sometimes repositioning living ones so they lean slightly differently, sometimes introducing new plants to fill in gaps. Working on the channels, adjusting their depth and direction in small ways. Maintaining the systems that remain mostly hidden. But none of it has the character of struggle. They're not fighting the place. They're not even managing it, exactly. They're communicating with it. Having a conversation with it through the medium of their hands and attention.

One of them is mute. You're certain of this because you've watched her try to speak once—a small sound, abandoned almost immediately. She makes no other attempt. She doesn't seem frustrated by this limitation. She expresses herself through gesture and positioning with such clarity that her silence feels like a choice. Or perhaps muteness came as a condition of being here, and she's made peace with it the way everyone here has made peace with the damp, the cold, the isolation, the lack of linear time.

Another one of them never seems to sleep. You've woken in the night—when sleep is difficult in the moisture and the cold—and seen them moving through the settlement, checking the boardwalks, standing by the pools, watching the mist. But they don't seem tired. It's as though they've transformed the need for sleep into some other function, some way of being present that doesn't require unconsciousness.

They show you no particular interest, which is strange until you understand that it's not disinterest. They're simply waiting to see what you'll become. Whether you'll integrate fully into the rhythm of the place, or whether you'll eventually leave, unable to tolerate the constant work of adaptation. They know already that the place will teach you if you stay long enough. They're not your guides. They're simply the proof that it's possible to remain here and become part of the functioning of the settlement itself.

They seem to communicate with the plants and animals in ways you don't quite witness. You'll see one of them standing before a section of dense ferns, and the ferns will—shift. Not dramatically, but there will be an adjustment, a settling into a new configuration, as though they've had a conversation and reached agreement. You've watched birds—gray things you've never quite identified—land on their shoulders, remain there while they work, then depart. You've never seen them feed the birds. The birds simply know they're welcome.

There's one who tends primarily to the channels. You've watched them work in the shallow water, ankle-deep sometimes, moving along the sluggish current, adjusting stones, deepening in some places and allowing shallowing in others. The water seems to accept these interventions. The channels respond. What was stagnant begins to move, infinitesimally slowly, with purpose. It's the opposite of engineering. It's like watching someone have a conversation with water about where it wants to go.

None of them appear to be in charge, yet there is an order. When something needs doing, they seem to know. When there's disagreement—and there must be disagreement sometimes, differences of opinion about how something should be maintained—it's resolved through some process you haven't witnessed. Perhaps they simply don't disagree. Perhaps the place itself is persuasive enough that consensus emerges naturally.

They observe the seasons with attention. In spring (as best you can tell time here), there's more activity around the ferns—new planting, visible tending. In summer (if summer comes here), they seem to rest more, moving slower. In autumn, they begin some kind of preparation. In winter—if winter truly comes, if the temperature drops further than the perpetual cool—they retreat indoors more often, and you hear the sound of work being done inside the buildings, the tap of tools on wood, the scrape of something being shaped.

What they're preparing for, you don't know. What the storage houses contain, they don't explain. Whether this settlement is meant to persist indefinitely or whether it's a temporary arrangement—a pocket carved out of the fens for purposes neither they nor you fully understand—remains ambiguous. They don't seem concerned about the future. They tend to the present with all their attention.

They've never asked you to leave. They've never asked you to stay. They've never asked you anything. But you're beginning to understand that your presence here is not accidental. That the place has drawn you here for a reason, and that these people—these few, these quiet, attentive people who move through the settlement like cells moving through a living body—they are what you become if you stop resisting. If you allow yourself to be educated by the damp, the mist, the constant work of adaptation and maintenance.

And you're beginning to wonder—now that your hands have started to move with the same slow intentionality, now that you can walk the boardwalks without thinking about where your feet are going, now that the sound of the pumps has begun to feel like a second heartbeat—whether you're still becoming one of them, or whether you've already started to be.
