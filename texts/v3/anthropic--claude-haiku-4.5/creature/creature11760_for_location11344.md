# The People of Keth

They are *spare*.

This is the first thing: a physical sparseness that reads as either asceticism or adaptation, and the residents themselves would likely not distinguish between the two. Their bodies are lean without being frail—there's a particular musculature to them, developed not through labor but through the constant, unforgiving negotiation with altitude and gravity. They move economically, never taking a step that doesn't accomplish something. A child learning to walk here learns early that wasted motion is wasted breath.

Their chests are deeper than lowland chests. You notice this when they breathe, when they speak—the air moves through them differently, taken in lower, held longer. They've been shaped by the thin air across generations: it's in their bone structure now, in the capacity of their lungs, in the particular set of their shoulders. Newcomers watch them breathe and understand, after a few days of headaches and nausea, what they're witnessing: people who have learned to live in a place that most bodies would reject.

They are *old*. Not individually—there are children, young people, the usual distribution of ages—but collectively, culturally. There's a quality of accumulated time in how they move through the city, in the way they greet each other with references to repairs done by grandparents, to seasons that have passed through their families for as many generations as the city's masonry. They remember things that happened before they were born as though they witnessed them. A person might say, casually, "Oh yes, when my great-grandmother's time, during the winter the river froze solid," and speak of it with the kind of detail that suggests not family story but personal recollection. The altitude might thin the blood but it seems to clarify the memory.

Their eyes are *particular*—adapted to the harsh light, to the clarity of thin air. The pupils are small, even in dim interiors, and they seem to focus on distance even when looking at something near. There's a quality of looking *through* things rather than at them, as though they're perpetually scanning the horizon, the cliff face, the sky. This makes them seem distant even in conversation, as if part of their attention remains elsewhere.

They speak *rarely*, and when they do, the words are chosen. A greeting might be a nod. A decision might be communicated with a gesture. There's no chattering in Keth—or if there is, it happens in private, behind walls. In public, in the courtyards and passages, there's a kind of communication that operates mostly beneath language: the placement of an object, the angle of a body, the raising of a hand. They understand each other through a system of signs that has developed over centuries, refined until language becomes almost redundant. A stranger finds this deeply unsettling.

When they do speak, their voices are *soft*. Not timid—there's no weakness in the sound—but modulated to the space, to the altitude where sound dissipates quickly. They've learned that you don't have to shout to be heard if you speak to the person in front of you and trust that no one else needs to know. This creates a strange intimacy in public spaces: even a conversation about the price of wool or the state of a roof feels like something private, meant only for the person receiving it.

Their hands tell a story of *work and restraint*. The people who spin wool have fingers stained with dye and callused from the wheel; the masons have hands that seem almost sculpted by stone, with nails worn short and skin that never fully comes clean. But the work is never hurried, never frantic. A hand moves to adjust a stone, and the movement is deliberate, testing, confident in its slowness. Even manual labor here has taken on the rhythm of meditation.

They are *practical to the point of austerity*. Clothing is wool from the goats, dyed in the colors of available plants and minerals: grays, pale yellows, earth reds, the blue-green of certain lichen. No decoration except what the weave itself provides, except in rare cases where someone has woven a pattern into their sleeves or hem—these are people who've lived long enough, or whose families have, to have earned the right to ornamentation. Most dress simply: a tunic, trousers, wrappings for the feet. In winter, layers. The clothes fit close enough to preserve heat, loose enough to allow movement.

There's no *vanity* here, or if there is, it's the specific vanity of a person who knows their own worth and doesn't require external confirmation. An older woman might wear her gray hair long, braided with silver wire—this is not vanity but *claim*, a statement that she has lived long enough to have accumulated something, even if it's only wire. A young man might spend extra time at the forge not because he seeks recognition but because the work itself demands it, and doing a thing properly is its own reward.

They are *suspicious of change*, but not in the way that suggests fear. Rather, they understand that change at this altitude means something different than it does below. When the river floods—and it floods sometimes, in the spring when the snowmelt comes down—the residents respond not with panic but with a kind of grim acknowledgment that the plateau has always tested them and always will. They don't fight it; they adapt, they repair, they wait. A person living in Keth learns early that you work with the landscape, not against it.

Their *children are raised to listen*. Not to obey, particularly, but to listen—to the river, to the wind, to the sound of stones settling in walls, to the particular sound a roof makes when it's about to fail. A child learns the language of the city before learning the language of words. By the time they're old enough to work, they can read the weather in the quality of light, can hear a storm coming from beyond the visible horizon, can sense the presence of ice before they see it. They are *attuned*.

They bear the *weight* of their landscape with a kind of grace that comes from having no choice and accepting that fact generations ago. There's no resentment in how they move through the narrow passages, no bitterness about the sparse resources, the thin air, the cold. There's an understanding that has calcified into acceptance: this is the place where they live, and they have learned to live here in a way that the place permits. Some might call it resignation. They would call it *wisdom*.

The elders hold *genuine authority*—not because custom dictates it but because they've simply seen more seasons, made more decisions, learned more about how the city operates. A person who has lived through seventy winters at this altitude has earned the right to speak about them. An old mason doesn't need to raise her voice to be listened to; people simply quiet themselves to hear her better.

They are, collectively, *patient in a way that the lowlands have forgotten*. Patient with stone that takes decades to shape. Patient with children who take years to learn. Patient with each other, with the slow business of maintenance and repair, with the waiting that altitude demands. This patience doesn't feel like virtue—it feels like *necessity recognized and accepted*. The city doesn't move faster than the plateau allows it to move.

There is something in them of the *stone they live among*—a quality of endurance, of erosion accepted as inevitable, of being shaped by forces larger than themselves and responding by becoming harder, more resilient, more precisely fitted to the space they occupy. They are people who have been worn by the place and, in being worn, have become more themselves.

They are *sparse*, and *patient*, and *old*, and *clear-eyed*, and *quiet*, and they will outlast the lowlands not through strength but through the simple fact that they have already learned to want so very little.
