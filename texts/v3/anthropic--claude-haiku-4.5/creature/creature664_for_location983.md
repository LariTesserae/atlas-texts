# The Inhabitants

They are slow.

Not merely in movement—though the albino lizards move with a deliberacy that borders on meditation, each step considered as if the ground itself might shift beneath them if they proceeded with haste. Not merely in metabolism—though their bodies have calibrated themselves to require almost nothing, existing on the insects that breed in the stone pools, on the algae that films the deeper water, on a caloric intake so minimal that you wonder if they're truly alive or if you're watching something caught in the process of becoming fossil. The slowness is deeper than physiology. It is temporal. These creatures have adapted themselves to the rhythm of the plateau itself, where change occurs in increments that require decades or centuries to become visible.

The lizards themselves seem half-blind. Their eyes are large, disproportionately so, adapted to extracting light from the perpetual dimness—but they orient themselves more by vibration than sight. They feel the subsurface water-movements through the stone. They sense the minute temperature variations that mark the presence of the moss-mounds. When they drink from the pools, they lower themselves with a slowness that appears almost ritualistic, as if the act itself requires reverence. Perhaps it does. Perhaps they understand something about this place that you, in your brief span here, cannot grasp. Their clear skin—nearly transparent in places, revealing the delicate architecture of veins—suggests they are creatures of pure adaptation: everything inessential stripped away, every remaining feature calibrated to survival in a place where the essential act is simply remaining.

There are more of them than you initially realize. Once you begin to notice one, you begin to see others. A movement in the moss here. A pale flash against darker stone there. They seem to maintain territories, though the boundaries are invisible to you. You might find two of them in proximity without interaction—separate, parallel, acknowledging each other's existence through some communication too subtle for your senses to register. Or perhaps they simply coexist without acknowledgment, sharing the space as the way water shares space with stone.

They die slowly too. You find one once, barely mobile, its body rigid in the way of creatures entering torpor. You don't know if it will recover or expire. It doesn't seem to matter much to the lizard. It remains where it is, breathing in barely perceptible rises of its rib-cage, waiting for warmth or death with equivalent stillness.

The spiders are stranger—more alien in the way they've adapted. These are not the spiders of warm places, quick and aggressive. The water-spiders of the deep pools are something other. They seem to have developed a relationship with slowness so complete that it approaches stasis. You watch one hunt, and the hunt takes hours. It moves across the pool-bottom in increments so minute that you can only perceive the change when you look away and then back, comparing position retrospectively. Its legs are impossibly long and thin, transparent except for the joints, which hold a kind of milky opacity. When light catches them at certain angles, they seem to refract light in ways that suggest they're not entirely material—not quite glass, not quite something else. They hunt the microorganisms, the copepods and water-mites, creatures so small they exist at the edge of visibility. The spider's hunting strategy appears to involve nothing but patience. It moves into proximity with the feeding zones, its legs splayed in a posture of such terrible delicacy that it seems certain to collapse at any moment. It doesn't. It waits. The prey, operating on their own timescale—faster than the spider's but still unhurried—eventually move within reach. The kill itself is almost anticlimactic: a motion so small that you almost miss it. The spider's legs contract minimally. Something has been caught. The slow meal begins.

The spiders seem to exist at an even lower point of the energy pyramid. They might feed once a week. Once a month. You have no way of knowing if the one you're observing is feeding or merely existing in its hunting posture, having abandoned all pretense of movement. The spider's existence suggests a philosophy: that there is no distinction between hunting and waiting, between life and patient death.

The birds—when you finally see one clearly—are smaller than you expected. It emerges from the mist on a morning when the cloud-layer is particularly dense, and it lands on one of the ancient archways with a delicacy that matches the spiders' movements. It is largely white, with patches of a gray so pale it's barely distinguishable from white. Its eyes are enormous, dark, and seem to contain an intelligence that borders on accusation—as if it knows you have been waiting for it, and it resents being observed. It moves in short hops along the archway's curve, its head cocked at precise angles. Once, it dips its beak into a pool of collected water on the stone. The motion is swift—the only swift thing you've seen in this place—but even that swiftness is relative. By the standards of birds in warmer climes, it would appear frozen.

You realize, watching it, that the bird is listening. Its stance suggests total auditory attention. It is hearing something in the mist, in the distant sound of water, in frequencies below your perception. It might be hearing other birds. It might be hearing prey. It might be hearing the elaborate music of the plateau itself—the way water moves through hidden channels, the way stone settles, the way air pressure changes across the vast slope of the landscape.

When it finally flies, it takes a long time to build altitude, even though you understand intellectually that it's moving at normal bird-speed. The plateau has infected your temporal perception. Everything here seems slow because nothing can be rushed. The bird rises into the mist and is gone, leaving you with the certainty that you will hear its call again, eventually, from some distant point in the gray.

The insects are nearly invisible until you look specifically for them. Once you begin to understand what you're seeing, the plateau reveals itself as possessed of a staggering insect population—but the insects exist at a scale so small and in such concentration in the moss that they form almost a collective organism. The mosses themselves host microbiomes: tiny crustaceans, springtails, microarthropods that live entirely within the green world of the moss-mounds. You could spend the rest of your life studying just one square meter of this landscape and still not exhaust what lives there. The insects are both abundant and invisible, numerous and insignificant, creating an entire world of food and predation and life-cycle in the space of a few centimeters.

What strikes you, finally, is that there is no apex predator here. There is no hunter that dominates the landscape. The spiders hunt the microarthropods. The lizards hunt—what? Smaller insects? Each other, rarely? The birds hunt the insects and perhaps the smallest lizards. But there is no animal that stands at the top of this pyramid, no creature that has made the plateau its domain in the way a hawk claims the skies or a snake the stones of a warmer place. The hierarchy is so flat, so distributed, that it approaches something like equality.

Perhaps the plateau itself is the apex predator. It hunts time. It devours years and produces nothing but moss and the steady seep of water.

All living things here have learned the same essential lesson: to survive on the plateau, one must surrender to its rhythms. Movement is permitted, but only in concert with the rain, with the seasons that barely change, with the downslope drift of water toward edges you cannot see. The creatures don't resist. They don't struggle. They have become part of the city's slow machine, another element in its perfected system of processing time and moisture and the barely-detectable motion of life toward death.

They are permanent because they have surrendered to impermanence.

They are alive because they have learned to move at the speed of stone.
