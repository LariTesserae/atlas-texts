# What Lives Here

The High Plateau of Lichen Bells is not a place for singular inhabitants. It is a *composition*, a symphony of small and smaller things, each playing its part in an endless, grinding music.

Start with the insects—the overwhelming majority, the true owners.

They exist in densities that defy easy counting. Collembola (springtails) form the base: tiny, segmented, living in the billions in the upper centimeters of the mat, consuming dead plant matter and exuding nitrogen compounds that the living plants desperately need. They are invisible unless you look closely, but they are everywhere. Above them move the mites—predatory, dust-sized, harvesting springtails in an economy of infinitesimal violence. Then the flies: fungus gnats by the uncounted millions, their larvae threading through the decomposing layers below. Mosquitoes in season, rising from the dark pools in clouds. Hover flies with their jeweled bodies, feeding on pollen from the rare flowers. Robber flies, predatory and fierce, taking smaller insects from the air with mechanical precision. Beetles of a thousand kinds: bark beetles in the stunted shrubs, ground beetles hunting through the mat at night, jeweled carabids that flash in what little sunlight penetrates here. Ants—several species, small, industrious, farming their aphids on the plant stems, maintaining underground cities that honeycomb through the substrate. Wasps, hunting spiders and caterpillars with the dedication of specialists. And above all, a constant drift of smaller insects so numerous they form a haze: midges, thrips, small parasitoid wasps no larger than a grain of rice, each one a predator or parasite or consumer, each one essential.

The insects are not visitors to this place. They *are* this place.

But they live in a web.

The spiders are here too, though less obvious. They are everywhere and nowhere: small jumping spiders in the moss layer, their enormous forward-facing eyes watching for movement. Sheet-web builders strung between plant stems like tiny fishing nets. Hunting spiders, lean and quick. Crab spiders in miniature, ambush predators waiting in flowers. They are so numerous that the biomass of spiders rivals the biomass of all the insects they hunt. They are silent, patient, eternal.

The larger invertebrates form visible structures.

Earthworms—not the garden variety, but native species adapted to this altitude and moisture—tunnel through the mat in the billions. Their castings are the physical structure of the soil, their burrowing the plumbing system. Without them, the substrate would become anaerobic, poisoned, dead. They are the unseen engineers.

Slugs, disturbingly large in some cases, graze on algae and lichens. Their trails cross the vegetation like silver highways. They are slow, vulnerable, but persistent. They taste the air constantly, sensing moisture and food with their whole bodies. They are essentially taste-buds with shells (or shells dissolved away) wandering a buffet.

Snails, small and spiral-shelled, occupy a similar niche but move more defensively. Their shells are thin here—there isn't enough calcium in this landscape to make thick ones. They are evolutionary compromises, necessary despite their fragility.

Centipedes, many-legged predators, hunt the soft-bodied invertebrates at night. They are fast, armored, with venomous fangs. They are the assassins of the insect world.

Isopods—pill bugs and woodlice—click and crawl through the damp substrate, breaking down dead plant matter with patient munching.

And beneath it all: fungi.

The fungal network here is more extensive than the plants themselves. Mycorrhizal fungi coat every root, trading phosphorus and nitrogen for sugars. Decomposer fungi—the true architects of nutrient cycling—break down every fallen leaf, every dead insect, every scrap of organic matter into constituent chemicals that can be reused. Some fungi are visible: the pale ghostly threads of Armillaria, the bracket fungi protruding from dead wood, the small mushrooms and toadstools that fruit after rain, dispersing spores into the thick air. But most are invisible, living as threads too fine to see individually, forming networks that connect all the plants in a hidden commerce.

The plants themselves form a gradient.

At the very bottom, pressed against the old rock, are the hardest-working: mosses and liverworts, ancient lineages that ask little and give much. They retain moisture like sponges. They fix nitrogen from the air (some species). They are the pioneers, and they never really leave—they transform into substrate, but their work continues. Above them, lichens—symbiotic partnerships between fungi and algae that can survive nearly anything. Here, they form thick crusts on the living plant-mat, coloring it orange, green, gray, white. They are patient, indifferent, almost eternal.

The ferns are the next level up: small species with fronds like lace, and a few larger varieties that reach almost to your waist. They spread by spores, by underground rhizomes, by the simple act of cutting and covering any ground they find. They love the moisture here. They are the transition between the moss-world and the world of higher plants.

The shrubs—stunted, waxy-leaved, spreading horizontally rather than climbing skyward—are the visible dominants. They are slow-growing, tough, adapted to altitude and thin air. Their thick leaves conserve moisture and protect against UV radiation. Their growth forms are determined by wind patterns, by snow-loading in winter, by the simple fact that everything here is compressed. Some are barely distinguishable from the ground cover until you walk through them. They produce small flowers—white, purple, pink—that attract the few insects specialized to drink their nectar.

And the rare taller plants: small trees, really, or large shrubs that have found conditions favorable enough to reach above the general canopy. These are keystone species—their leaf fall feeds the system, their stems provide shelter, their roots stabilize the substrate. They are relatively new to the plateau, perhaps, or simply rare. They are not welcome in the way of a planned garden, but they are tolerated by the ecosystem because they don't disrupt the balance.

Every plant here produces chemicals: compounds that deter herbivores, compounds that attract pollinators, compounds that signal stress or nutrient availability to the fungal network below. The plants are not passive. They are constantly communicating, negotiating, defending, and reproducing.

The mammals are sparse but significant.

Small rodents—voles, perhaps, or local species you can't immediately identify—burrow through the upper substrate. They are herbivorous mostly, eating plant matter and seeds. They are prey. They are also a critical link in the energy transfer upward. They exist in populations that fluctuate with food availability and predation pressure. Their feces are evidence of their abundance.

Shrews, with their metabolic furnaces burning hotter than any animal this size should, hunt insects day and night. They are aggressive, territorial, demanding. They are heard more often than seen—their chittering is part of the constant sound.

Rabbits or hares, possibly, given the size of the droppings and the stripped vegetation in certain areas. Larger prey animals require larger territories, and the plateau is finite. They move between the valley rim and the valleys beyond, possibly, using the vegetation as cover.

And the predators:

Something larger hunts at dusk and dawn. The broken bones, the scattered remains, the trampled vegetation—all point to a carnivore of size. A fox, perhaps, or a wild cat, or a canine species you're not familiar with. It moves with purpose and intelligence through the vegetation, following paths that suggest long familiarity. It is not numerous—the prey base here cannot support many large predators—but it is present, and it shapes the behavior of everything smaller.

The birds are everywhere and nowhere.

You hear them constantly: alarm calls that suggest danger, territorial songs that mark out invisible boundaries, the soft calls of mates to each other in the fog. Some are visible: a small hawk, occasionally, circling above the plateau's rim. Crows or similar birds, raucous and adaptable. Smaller songbirds in the shrubs, their purpose a mystery but their presence undeniable. Waders, possibly, around the dark pools where water collects. Ground-nesting birds that hide their nests in the dense vegetation. Migratory birds that pass through, using the plateau as a waystation. The bird species here number in the dozens; collectively, they represent a massive energy draw. They are hunters, scavengers, seed dispersers, and insect controllers. Without them, the insect populations would expand beyond current balance.

But the apex predator here is not visible, or only glimpsed in darkness:

Something large enough to hunt the rabbit-sized creatures, something smart enough to navigate the terrain, something that leaves scat and scat-marking sites. It might be a golden eagle, if one occasionally hunts here. It might be a larger canine. It might be something you have no name for. It exists in the ecosystem's hierarchy as a shadow, a pressure, a reason for other animals to be cautious. It shapes every small behavior, every moment of inattention by a prey animal, every decision to venture into open ground rather than stay under cover.

The decomposers are perhaps the most important:

Bacteria and archaea exist in concentrations almost incomprehensible—in the organic-rich substrate, a single gram of material contains billions of microorganisms. They are the ultimate consumers, the final processors of energy, the alchemists that transform death into life. They cycle carbon and nitrogen, fix phosphorus, produce methane in the anaerobic layers (which rises and enters the atmosphere, contributing to the planet's greenhouse effect). They are invisible but omnipresent, the true engineers of this place.

And then there are the humans—or were.

The people who lived here adapted to this place with a kind of pragmatic acceptance. They built small, built low, built with stone that roots couldn't easily penetrate. They harvested plants—herbs, edible leaves, medicinal fungi—without destroying the ecosystem. They hunted, certainly, but within bounds. They maintained terraces and irrigation channels because they understood that the vegetation here wanted to grow and would overwhelm them if not managed. They likely practiced transhumance, moving seasonally to follow better conditions, returning when the plateau was navigable. Their structures are being reclaimed, and they likely knew they would be. They are part of the cycling here, their knowledge and labor temporarily extracting resources before returning to the substrate.

But they are gone now, or nearly gone. Their presence is felt as absence: the terraces slowly disappearing, the structures dissolving, the knowledge lost.

---

What lives here, finally, is a system. Not individuals, not species in isolation, but a complete, self-sustaining network of interactions that spans from the rock below to the clouds above. Every organism here—from the bacteria in a cubic centimeter of soil to the predator moving in the dark—is essential. Remove one and the entire system adjusts, compensates, but is never quite the same.

This is what lives here: *completeness*. An ecosystem that has reached a stable state after eons of refinement. A place where every role is filled, every resource is cycled, every death becomes food becomes growth becomes death again, endlessly.

It doesn't need you. It doesn't want you. It will outlast you and everything you know. And that is precisely what makes it beautiful, and precisely what makes you want to leave.
