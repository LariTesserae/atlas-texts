# What Lives Here

They are the people for whom the city above has become too expensive, too fast, too bright. They are the ones who were priced out, pushed out, aged out, locked out by circumstance and paperwork and the simple arithmetic of survival. They are workers whose industries left. They are the unwell. They are the ones who couldn't quite manage the surfaces anymore.

They live in the undercroft because the undercroft does not require permission.

There is an old woman—Marta, or Martha, the name has worn smooth over years—who has occupied the same corner enclosure for so long that people forget she wasn't part of the original settlement. She tends the fungi as though they were a garden, monitoring which shelves produce the pale fruiting bodies, which areas encourage the ear-shaped brackets. She doesn't harvest them all. She's learned which ones are safe. She keeps a journal of observations in a waterlogged ledger that she wraps in oilcloth. The handwriting has degraded into something almost abstract over the years, but the record continues. She knows the undercroft's moods better than anyone. When she says a section is going to settle, people listen.

There are families. Children born here, some of them, who have never seen the city above except through stories and the memory of parents. These children have adapted in ways that are neither tragic nor triumphant—they are simply adapted. Their eyes are more sensitive to the gloom than to bright light. Their lungs manage the thick air without complaint. They play in the spaces between dwellings, games that involve quiet and stillness and an intimate knowledge of the pillars' positions. One girl—Soren, perhaps eleven—has developed an ability to navigate the undercroft in near-total darkness, counting steps and detecting air currents with an instinct that comes from living here since infancy.

There are men who maintain the remaining operational systems—the compressor, the electrical lines, the pipes that still serve some purpose. This is skilled work, though no one calls it that anymore. They move through the undercroft with authority, understanding its infrastructure as a cardiologist understands veins. One of them, Dev, maintains a workshop in an alcove where he repairs everything: tools, lamps, shoes. He has learned to work with the corrosion, not against it, building devices designed to last in damp rather than resist it. His hands are permanently stained with oil and mineral deposits.

There is a young man—Jacob—who arrived two years ago and has slowly become something like an organizer. He doesn't hold official position because no official positions exist here, but he notices when supplies run short, when someone hasn't been seen in days, when a wall shows signs of instability. He moves between clusters of dwellings, checking on people, solving small problems. He has the particular patience required to live in such density without generating conflict, and he's developed a quiet authority that comes from actual care rather than coercion.

There are people who work, still—taking jobs above in the city, jobs that pay cash or off-books or in barter. Dishwashing. Cleaning. Labor that the city needs but doesn't see. They make the journey up and down the spiraling stairs daily, their lungs adjusting to the pressure changes, their eyes struggling with the brightness above before readjusting to the gloom. Some maintain dual lives almost completely separate—a version of themselves that moves in the upper world, another that exists here.

There are people who are dying, slowly. An older man with breathing difficulties who has come here partly because the rent was nil and partly because the constant temperature removes some of the stress his heart cannot manage. A woman with a chronic infection that antibiotics don't quite cure, for whom the undercroft's wet chill is sometimes comfort and sometimes torture. They exist in a kind of stasis here—neither getting dramatically worse nor recovering, but suspended in a strange plateau that the undercroft seems to facilitate.

There are people who don't speak much. The undercroft attracts them, or they're suited to it—the quiet, the lack of demand for eye contact, the accepted understanding that everyone here carries something they don't discuss. Their presence is acknowledged. They are brought food. They are checked on. But they are not pushed to share what they're carrying.

There are people who somehow maintain hope—not the bright, loud hope of those who haven't faced serious shortage, but a different kind. They grow things in containers on their shelves: herbs that tolerate the low light, one man has even managed to cultivate mushrooms commercially, selling to restaurants above who don't ask too many questions. They maintain routines. They read to each other. They remember jokes. They plan for futures that may or may not materialize.

There are people who are angry in ways they've learned to contain. The anger doesn't leave. It becomes part of their muscle memory, their breathing patterns, the careful control with which they move through shared spaces. But it is *contained*, because the alternative—letting it detonate—would make the undercroft unlivable for everyone.

There are the ones who arrived alone and are slowly becoming part of something. There are the ones who were born in community and have never known anything else. There are the ones who move between the undercroft and the world above, translators of sorts, bringing goods and messages and news. There are the ones who will never leave. There are the ones who are waiting for an opportunity that may never come.

They are not visibly exceptional in any way. They are ordinary people who have run out of ordinary options. They are skilled and unskilled, educated and illiterate, young and old. They are people who laugh, who worry, who form attachments and grudges and alliances. They are people who live with the knowledge that their ceiling is forty feet of water-seeping stone, that the city above cannot be seen or heard, that the emergency exit they depend on is a single spiral staircase.

They are people who have learned to live in the suspended space between acceptable and unacceptable—who have accepted the unacceptable and found in it a strange kind of peace. They are people who understand, in a way the city above perhaps cannot, that infrastructure decays, that nothing is permanent, that the ground can open up and swallow you, that sometimes the only option is to go down.

They maintain. They persist. They tend the fungi and read in dim light and check on neighbors and repair broken things and raise children and work and rest and slowly, imperceptibly, become part of the stone itself—not metaphorically, but in the way that all things that stay long enough in a place become of that place.

The undercroft lives because they live in it. And they live because the undercroft—patient, warm, unchanging, accepting—provides the one thing the world above has run out of: room.
