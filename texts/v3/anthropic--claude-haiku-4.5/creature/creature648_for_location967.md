# What Lives at Keth'morn Ridge

They are **caretakers**, though the word barely holds them.

The ones you might see are few: slow-moving figures in muted clothing that matches neither the stone nor the plants but sits between them—a color like dampened ash. They move through the terraces with deliberate spacing, never hurrying, never idle. Their hands are calloused in specific patterns: fingertips worn smooth from handling vines, palms creased from gripping stone edges, the inside of one forearm always darker from the consistent pressure of carrying tools. They tend things not with urgency but with the patient attention of people who have performed the same small adjustments for so long that the work has become indistinguishable from breath. Prune here. Guide this growth. Clear that channel. Check the tension on the vine-wrap at the western ring. They leave no mess because mess would disrupt the equilibrium—the agreed-upon balance that keeps the platform functioning.

But there are **others**.

Below—in the chambers where the pumps live—there are the mechanics. You will not encounter them easily. They exist in the deep dark spaces, moving between massive cylindrical structures that hiss and drip. They are changed by their work: their hearing is strange, permanently attuned to the minute variations in the machinery's voice, able to detect a failing bearing by shifts in pitch that would be imperceptible to anyone else. Their lungs are accustomed to air thick with mineral spray and the ghost-smell of ancient rust. They navigate by feel and by the learned acoustics of those chambers, because light down there is rationed—a few oil lamps, carefully positioned, because the machinery itself generates enough heat that excessive illumination would mean excessive danger. They speak rarely, and when they do, their voices carry an odd hollow quality, shaped by decades of conversing in spaces designed to amplify vibration rather than sound. They repair. They adjust. They listen. Some of them have been assigned to the deep work their entire adult lives and have never stood on the platform's upper tiers.

There are the **plant-speakers**—a name they don't use themselves, but it's what the caretakers call the ones who understand the vegetation in ways that seem almost intuitive. These individuals move more slowly than the maintenance workers, and they examine things: leaf color, soil density in the crevices, the angle of growth, the way water collects in certain fern clusters. They make notes on thin paper that immediately begins to warp in the moisture, as if the platform is trying to erase their records as quickly as they write them. They're interested in the *pattern*, in understanding which plants thrive in which zones, which sequences of growth predict problems with water management, how the vegetation's expansion or contraction maps onto the machinery's cycles. Some of them have spent years watching a single corner of the platform, observing the slow seasonal rhythms, waiting to see if the ecosystem will ever stabilize into something other than this stretched, thin equilibrium.

And there are the **deep-dwellers**—creatures that have claimed the spaces humans have built but largely abandoned. Small, adaptive things. Insects evolved specifically for the conditions here: species found nowhere else, with exoskeletons that resist the constant dampness, antennae attuned to vibration rather than scent, life cycles synchronized to the water's seasonal flows. The birds are recent, perhaps only decades old in terms of their settlement here—grey, unremarkable species that have discovered the insects and the seeds from the flowering plants form an adequate diet. They roost in the denser vegetation, and their droppings contribute nitrogen to the soil that accumulates in the crevices, feeding the ferns. Larger animals pass through: nocturnal mammals drawn by the water, the insects, the shelter of the platform's overhanging edges. They leave traces but never stay long. This place is not quite home to them—too ordered, too mechanical, too clearly *made*.

There are also the **listeners**—a category that's harder to define. They are people who come to the platform for purposes that seem unrelated to its maintenance, though they always ask permission first. They sit in specific locations: on the higher tiers where the vibration is gentlest, near the central channels where the water-sound is clearest, in the vegetation clusters where the mist is thickest. They come for hours or sometimes days, remaining nearly motionless, attending to the platform's ambient symphony. Some keep journals. Some don't. Some seem to be here for meditation; others appear to be conducting some form of study. The caretakers don't question them as long as they respect the grooved channels and don't disturb the plants. There's an unspoken understanding that Keth'morn Ridge tolerates observation.

At the deepest level—perhaps not physically but certainly functionally—there is the **platform itself**, or at least the consciousness it might be developing. The machinery has been running so long, through such careful calibration, that it's beginning to seem less like infrastructure and more like an organism. The water follows patterns the pumps have carved into the stone. The vegetation grows in the spaces the water has eroded. The caretakers maintain the system, but increasingly, they are maintaining a thing that has its own logic, its own slow intention. The hum that never stops—it's not a sound of failure or even of striving. It's the sound of a system that has found its pace, its breathing, its rhythm. The platform persists because it has become self-correcting. Small errors cascade into adjustments automatically. Growth responds to water flow. Water flow responds to machinery. Machinery responds to human maintenance. Humans respond to the platform's needs.

**They** are all of these things. The caretakers and the mechanics and the plant-speakers. The insects and the birds and the creatures passing through. The listeners and the observers. The machinery and the stone and the vegetation locked in negotiated balance. 

Together, they are not separate from the platform—they *are* the platform, the way a forest is not distinct from its soil and air and the creatures living in it. They maintain it. They inhabit it. They allow it to exist. And increasingly, it's difficult to say which of them is keeping the others alive, or whether that question even makes sense.

The mist thickens. The pumps turn. Water flows. Things grow.

The living system breathes.
