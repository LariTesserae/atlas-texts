# What Lives at the Bergschrund Works

The marmots are the true residents here—not the researchers, not the transient herds, but the marmots. They've solved the paradox of this place: how to thrive in abundance that masquerades as scarcity.

They're stocky, golden-brown creatures, built low to the ground as if they're already halfway into the burrows they'll occupy. Their fur is dense enough to hold the mountain's perpetual dampness without becoming waterlogged, and their eyes—small, dark, positioned high on their skulls—are calibrated for a landscape where predators arrive from above. They weigh perhaps three or four kilograms each, but their presence dominates the visible ecology more thoroughly than the bharal or eagles because they're *everywhere*, or rather, everywhere and nowhere: they appear suddenly from fissures in the scree, become entirely motionless for minutes at a time (indistinguishable from rocks), then vanish as if they'd never been.

The alarm call—that sharp *creeeep*—is not a sound they produce casually. It's exhaustingly specific: a marmot stationed at an elevated lookout detects an eagle's silhouette, a porter's movement along the distant supply trail, a shift in the wind pattern that suggests weather moving in from the valleys. The call triggers a cascade: other marmots repeat it, relay it, add variations that encode directional information (*creeeep-creeeep* from the north face, single sharp *creek* from the western talus). The entire population becomes briefly synchronized, a single nervous system distributed across the scree. Then they disperse into their burrows, leaving the slope to its surface silence.

Their burrows aren't simple refuges—they're engineering projects. The soil here is barely formed; the burrow system extends deep into cracks in the granite, following fault lines that have existed since the rock cooled, billions of years ago. A marmot's burrow is thus partially predefined by geology, but also continually excavated, maintained, expanded. The entrance is typically positioned at an angle that water won't flood it during the daily melt cycles. Multiple entrances allow escape routes. The chambers deeper down are lined with the cushion plant material they clip from around the colony—it serves as insulation, and the alpine plants have zero nutritional value by the time they've been worked into burrow bedding, so it's a waste-disposal system as much as a comfort measure.

Inside those burrows, the marmots spend approximately eight months of the year in hibernation—a deeper sleep than mere winter dormancy. Their heart rate drops from three hundred beats per minute to five. Their body temperature approaches freezing. They burn through fat reserves at the most miserly rate metabolic math allows. They emerge in June, brief and frantic, because there are perhaps ninety days of food availability before the next snow arrives in earnest. In that window, they must find mates, establish territories, eat enough to build the fat reserves that will sustain them through the next winter. The abundance of newly-exposed habitat means less competition for burrow sites, which means younger marmots—animals that might have died from territorial exclusion in established populations—can establish their own colonies and survive.

But they've also learned the research station.

One or two seasons ago—the exact timeline is unclear, because nobody at the station was tracking it closely—marmots began appearing near the supply depot. The foil packets that porters drop, the stray bits of whatever meal gets consumed at the hut, the discarded or half-eaten portions that don't make it into the waste pit: these represent calories in a landscape where calories are the fundamental constraint. A marmot that discovers this abundance gains a two- to three-week advantage in its pre-hibernation feeding, which might mean the difference between surviving winter and not. And this advantage passes to offspring: the young born to mothers who've learned to scavenge at the depot are born with the learned behavior already encoded—not genetically, but imprinted in the specific maternal behavior they observe and replicate.

So the marmots here are in the process of becoming something adjacent to commensals. Not quite domesticated—they remain wild, solitary, dangerous to approach—but loosely entangled with human presence. This is evolution in real time, at the scale of a single decade.

The pikas are harder to see and harder to understand.

They're smaller than marmots—the size of a small guinea pig—with rounded ears that seem disproportionate to their skulls, giving them an appearance of permanent surprise. Where marmots are social (or at least semi-social, gathering in loose colonies), pikas are aggressively territorial. A pika occupies a specific rocky area, perhaps a hundred meters across, and defends it from all other pikas with sounds that seem too loud to come from something so small: sharp, metallic *eek-eek-eek* calls that carry across the talus. Unlike marmots, pikas don't hibernate; they remain active throughout the winter, which means their survival depends on creating hay piles—small mounds of alpine plants they clip and dry in the summer, storing them in protected crevices.

But the recently-exposed terrain presents a problem for pikas that it doesn't present for marmots. The pioneer vegetation—the cushion plants, the dwarf willow—is sparse and unpredictably distributed. A pika's hay pile needs to be large enough to sustain it for eight months, which means it must gather from a significant area. On established alpine meadows, a pika might be able to assemble a sufficient cache from the vegetation within its hundred-meter territory. Here, the same territory yields perhaps thirty percent of the required biomass.

What emerges is a strange behavioral compromise: pikas are loosening their territorial boundaries, ranging farther than they typically would, encountering other pikas more frequently than their aggressive temperament typically allows. Confrontations are increasing. Some pikas are dying in territorial disputes over resource-access routes. Others are consolidating larger territories and living with lower densities. The population here is being sorted by personality as much as by genetics: the more aggressive, territorial pikas are being filtered out; the more flexible, ranging pikas are establishing themselves.

The bharal are something else entirely: visitors, not residents.

They're mid-sized ungulates, standing maybe a meter at the shoulder, with woolly gray-brown coats (the name "blue sheep" derives from the faint blue tint their coat has when light hits it at certain angles, though this is visible more in concept than in reality). They're built for cliff terrain and have arrived here following the glacier's retreat upslope, pursuing the newly-revealed pasture. A herd of perhaps forty or fifty animals moves through the zone where the ice-face meets bare rock, occupying the steepest, least stable terrain where predators larger than eagles can't easily pursue them.

But this is a temporary occupation. The bharal didn't evolve to live at 4,800 meters on freshly-exposed granite. They originated at lower elevations and have adapted to alpine terrain, but the terrain they've adapted to is established alpine meadow—soil that's been accumulating for centuries, vegetation that's dense and predictable. Here, the pasture is ephemeral. The plants they're eating are the same pioneer species that the marmots and pikas are relying on. The grazing pressure from the bhral herds is actually slowing the vegetation colonization, trampling seedlings, destabilizing the fragile consolidation of soil that's begun to adhere to the rock.

The bharal will either learn to move downslope as the seasons change (becoming true migrants, abandoning this zone in winter) or they'll overgraze the available vegetation and starve. The population dynamic here is actually a slow-motion crash that hasn't become visible yet because the timescale is being measured in years rather than months. In another five or ten years, the herds will likely be much smaller, or they'll have learned entirely new migration patterns that take them away from this zone.

The golden eagles are predators, but predators with a specific kind of abundance available to them.

They hunt the marmots primarily, but also young pikas, occasionally the hares. An eagle stationed at the research station (researchers have noted one, possibly two, pairs nesting on cliff faces just below the 5,200-meter zone) has developed a hunting pattern that's become almost routine: circles the scree fields in the early morning when thermals are beginning to form, dives when a marmot emerges from its burrow for the brief daily surface feeding, and retrieves the kill. The kill rate appears to be perhaps one marmot every two to three days per eagle pair.

But this is unsustainable in a strange way. The marmot population is still growing (due to reduced predation pressure relative to established populations, and due to the food-subsidy from the research station), but if the eagle population increases proportionally, a feedback cycle would begin: more eagles, more predation, fewer marmots, starvation of eagles, population crash. The system here hasn't reached equilibrium; it's still in the early stages of whatever equilibrium it will eventually find.

The eagles themselves seem aware of something: they hunt with a patience that suggests they know the prey base is abundant enough to sustain them without aggressive competition. There's no visible hunger driving their behavior, no desperation in their feeding. They hunt because hunting is what they do, and because marmots are available. If the marmot population were to crash, the eagles would leave—they'd range downslope to established valleys where the populations are stable and the hunting is more predictable.

The hares are almost invisible.

Mountain hares at this elevation are in the process of shifting their coat color to white—a molt that should be triggered by day length and temperature, but which seems to be arriving earlier this year, suggesting the hares are responding to photoperiod signals that are telling them winter is approaching even though summer isn't yet complete. Their bodies are lean, built for speed rather than endurance. They occupy the margins: the areas where enough vegetation has accumulated that they can find grazing, but where their predators (eagles, the occasional snow leopard from higher elevations) have slightly more difficulty spotting them against the patchwork of light and shadow created by the emerging topography.

The hares here seem to be thriving in a quiet way. They're less visible than marmots or pikas, less dramatic than bharal, less dominant than eagles. But the population density seems stable, or perhaps even increasing. They're occupying a niche that the other herbivores aren't fully utilizing: the scattered patches of vegetation that are too small or too isolated for marmots or pikas to defend as territories, but that are sufficient for a solitary hare to subsist. They're becoming, in a sense, the baseline herbivore of this ecosystem—not the specialist, but the generalist that can survive on whatever grows, wherever it grows.

The lichen is alive in a way that's almost invisible.

Lichen is neither plant nor fungus but a partnership—algae and fungus in a relationship that's functionally indistinguishable from a single organism. On these newly-exposed granitic surfaces, the lichen is engaged in what might be the slowest, most deliberate act of ecological colonization happening on the mountain.

The colors are remarkable: orange, yellow, pale green, gray-white, and a particular shade of deep rust-red that appears only on south-facing exposures that have been warming all day. Each color represents a different species, a different partnership of fungus and algae. The orange lichen (genus Xanthoria) is nitrogen-fixing, which means it's actually improving the substrate for future plants by adding nitrogen to the bare rock. The gray-white lichens (Rhizocarpon and related genera) are among the slowest-growing organisms on Earth—some individuals on this mountain are less than a centimeter across after thirty or forty years of growth.

The lichen is doing the real work of soil formation here. It's dissolving the rock through chemical weathering, trapping particles of mineral dust, creating the tiniest pockets where bacteria can colonize. When lichen dies—which happens rarely, very slowly—it leaves behind microscopic amounts of organic matter that will eventually contribute to real soil. The process is so slow that it's nearly imperceptible, but it's also absolutely foundational: nothing else can live here until the lichen does its work.

The bacteria and fungi inhabiting the substrate are largely invisible, but they're present in vast numbers.

In the water running through the channels—in every droplet of the subglacial melt and the condensation that coats every surface—there are bacterial colonies, algae, fungal spores. Some of these organisms are thermophilic, adapted to the slight warming that occurs in the deep glacial melt as friction and pressure interact with the ice itself. Others are psychrophilic, thriving in the near-freezing water that emerges from the glacier. The bacterial populations are engaged in the basic chemistry of that allows stone to become soil: nitrogen cycling, sulfur cycling, phosphorus cycling. They're breaking down the mineral compounds in the rock into forms that plants can utilize. Without them, the plant colonization that's beginning to occur would be impossible.

Some of these bacteria are likely ancient—species that have existed in the subglacial environment for thousands of years, living in darkness and cold, feeding on the nutrients released by the slow grinding of the glacier against the rock. As the glacier retreats and the subglacial environment shrinks, these bacteria face extinction in their native habitat. But some are being carried away in the melt, deposited in the newly-exposed terrain, where they're being introduced to a sunlit world that's radically different from the one they evolved in. A few will survive and establish populations in the surface environment. Most will not.

The research station itself is inhabited by humans in a way that's almost incidental to its real function.

Two or three scientists typically occupy the station during the height of summer—they arrive for perhaps eight weeks, live in rotating shifts, monitor the glacial retreat with GPS units, collect cores of ice and rock, maintain the sensor arrays. They cook on a diesel stove that's both heating mechanism and primary heat source. They eat meals that have been dehydrated or canned, transported up the mountain by porters who themselves are seasonal workers, arriving for the brief window when conditions allow passage.

These researchers are observers, not residents. The mountain is their subject, not their habitat. But their presence has ecological consequences: the food waste that attracts marmots, the trail scars that divert water flows, the GPS marks they've hammered into the rock (which persist as reference points for future research), the data they collect which will eventually be used to predict the mountain's future trajectory across decades and centuries.

They come and go. The animals remain.

---

What lives at the Bergschrund Works is a population in flux, a community that hasn't yet cohered. The marmots are the closest thing to true residents—they're reproducing, establishing permanent populations, learning from the research station and passing that learning forward. The pikas are consolidating, finding new territorial arrangements that allow them to survive on sparse resources. The bharal are still deciding whether to stay or leave. The eagles are permanent but not numerous. The hares are increasing quietly.

The lichen is becoming itself at the pace of millimeters per decade. The bacteria are waking up to sunlight they haven't seen in millennia. The research station is measuring all of it, calibrating instruments, recording data that will quantify the mountain's retreat into numbers that universities can understand.

In another five years, this place will be almost unrecognizable—not to the eye (the visual transformation happens slowly), but to anyone tracking the specific populations, the specific ecologies, the specific human relationships. The marmots will have become something adjacent to domesticated. The pikas will have either crashed or established stable populations. The bharal will either have migrated permanently or starved. The lichen will have spread fractionally farther downslope. The research station will still be here, or it will have been abandoned.

For now, the mountain is teaching itself how to be a mountain again, and everything that lives here is learning to live in a place that's learning to exist.
