The air here is thick with a perpetual mist, not the biting, cold fog of a mountain peak, but a damp, clinging haze born from the sheer abundance of water near the surface. It weeps from the very rocks, runs in thin, dark rivulets down every exposed stone, and collects in shallow, glassy pools that mirror the bruised, perpetually overcast sky. This is a young landscape, barely formed, where the raw processes of geology are still at play. Great, unhewn blocks of stone, slick with moisture and fuzzy with nascent mosses, lie scattered as if dropped from a giant's careless hand. There are no smooth slopes or worn valleys; instead, the terrain is a jagged, emergent chaos, a pre-formation of what might one day become mountains. Boulder fields give way to improbable scree slopes that seem to be perpetually sloughing off, a low rumble of shifting stone the only dominant sound, aside from the ceaseless drip and trickle of water.

Vegetation, however, is remarkably robust, clinging tenaciously to the difficult surfaces. Not the kind of verdant, sun-drenched green one might expect, but a muted, resilient growth. Thick, leathery mosses blanket the rocks, intermingled with tough, wiry grasses that push between the stones. Small, stunted trees, their bark perpetually dark and glistening, sprout in sheltered hollows, their branches often draped with long, stringy lichens. This lushness, however, exists within a pervasive emptiness. The sheer scale of the rock formations, even though individual features are small, creates vast open spaces between them. It feels like wandering through a newly quarried quarry, or a realm still assembling itself, where the air itself feels thin and hollow despite the clinging dampness.

This emerging geological mess is nonetheless *urbanized*. Not in the sense of polished marble and organized streets, but with a stark, brutalist infrastructure born from necessity. Great, dry-stone walls, impossibly high and built from the same unworked blocks that litter the ground, crisscross the landscape. They are less fortifications and more like immense, segmented scars, guiding the scarce inhabitants and their meager possessions. Settlements, if they can be called that, are clusters of low, squat dwellings built directly into the lee of larger rock formations or within the protective embrace of these massive walls. Their construction is primitive, using rough-hewn stone and thick, compacted earth, sealed against the persistent damp. Smoke, thin and grey from fires of peat and resinous wood, rises occasionally from these hollows, contributing to the ever-present haze.

The technology is equally elemental. Tools are fashioned from stone, bone, and the occasional piece of salvaged, weathered metal – perhaps from some older, forgotten civilization that eroded away long before this current landscape began to emerge. Craftsmanship is evident in the sheer tenacity of the builders, but elegance is absent. Everything is functional, rough-hewn, designed to withstand the harsh elements and the sparse but dangerous world.

Fauna is scarce, as one might expect in such a raw and challenging environment. The most common sounds, beyond the geological murmurs and the endless water, are the cries of unseen birds of prey circling high above the mist, and the rustle of small, hardy rodents that scurry through the sparse undergrowth. Occasionally, a larger, more solitary beast – perhaps a shaggy, horned creature adapted to the cold, damp stone – might be glimpsed moving with slow, deliberate steps across a high ledge.

The light is normal, but diffuse, perpetually filtered through the thick atmospheric moisture and the high, scattered cloud cover. Shadows are soft and indistinct. The scale of the place is peculiar. While the underlying geology suggests grand mountain heights, the actual populated areas and immediate surroundings feel cramped, almost room-like, enclosed by the immense stone structures and the dense, low-lying vegetation that hugs the damp ground. It's a world that feels both vast and contained, a rough-hewn pocket carved out of a much larger, incompletely formed world.

And there is a certain strangeness here. It's not overtly magical, but there's an unsettling quality to the landscape's nascent state. The rocks seem to hum with a latent energy, the water carries a faint, metallic tang, and the air itself feels charged, as if the very act of geological formation is a slow, audible process. The inhabitants, tough and pragmatic, live in harmony with this slow, patient world, their lives dictated by the cycles of damp and dry, light and shadow, and the ever-present, echoing sounds of a world still under construction. The dynamics are glacial; the land shifts, the water flows, the vegetation grows, all at a pace that makes individual human lives seem fleeting and insignificant. They are but a brief flicker against the backdrop of emerging stone and weeping water.
