The world is a subterranean expanse, unimaginably vast and perpetually dark. What little light exists emanates not from a sun, but from the bioluminescent exhalations of the abundant fauna that teem within its confines. These creatures, ranging from microscopic, shimmering motes to colossal, slow-moving behemoths, are the sole source of illumination in a landscape sculpted by geological forces rather than the patient hand of erosion.

The terrain itself is a testament to its youth. Jagged, freshly-formed mineral structures spear upwards from the floor and hang like colossal icicles from the unseen ceiling. Basaltic spires, still radiating residual heat from their volcanic birth, pierce the dense atmosphere, their surfaces unweathered and sharp. Crystalline formations, some the size of mountains, glitter faintly in the shifting light of passing organisms, their facets still possessing the razor-sharp edges of their genesis. There is no soil, no sand, only raw, exposed rock and mineral deposits, laid bare by the planet's internal upheavals.

The atmosphere is thick, almost viscous, a dense soup of gases that muffles sound and carries a peculiar, warm, mineral tang. It’s a constant, low hum, a pervasive thrumming that seems to originate from the very rock itself, overlaid by the faint clicks, chirps, and resonant groans of the myriad life forms. The air feels heavy, saturated with unseen presences, a constant, dense presence that presses in from all sides.

Water is utterly absent. The chemistry of this world is drier than any desert, a realm of mineral interactions and energy exchanges unseen in more familiar biologies. There are no rivers to carve canyons, no oceans to reflect skies. The only interaction with a fluid medium is the dense, warm atmosphere, and the occasional, fleeting mist of biological effluvium.

Vegetation, as we understand it, is also absent. No roots anchor to the stone, no leaves unfurl to catch light. Life here exists in forms that bypass the need for photosynthesis or plant-based diets. The fauna are the primary actors, their bodies themselves the conduits of energy and chemical processes. Some organisms, sessile and sessile-like, cling to the sheer rock faces, drawing sustenance directly from the mineral substrate or the dense atmosphere. Others, mobile and predatory, hunt through the subterranean Gloom, their forms adapted to navigate the sharp, uneven terrain.

The built environment, if it can be called that, is minimal and enigmatic. Scattered across the landscape are faint, geometric etchings on some of the larger mineral formations, as if a primitive tool had been used to mark the rock. These markings are infrequent, almost accidental, and speak of solitary, perhaps even transient, activities. Occasionally, a singular, primitive structure might be found – a crude shelter built from quarried stone, or a shallow pit designed for some unknown purpose, its edges worn smooth by the passage of time, though the world itself is young. These are not the ruins of civilizations, but the scattered leavings of beings who occupy this space with a rudimentary mastery of simple tools.

The dynamism of this world is not one of change, but of constant, stable existence. The geological processes that formed it have long since settled. The atmosphere, while dense, remains in a state of equilibrium. The light, such as it is, shifts and flickers with the movements of the fauna, creating a slow, rhythmic pulse rather than a daily cycle. It is a world locked in a perpetual, unchanging present, where the warm, dark, and dense embrace of the subterranean is the only constant. There are no seasons, no weather fronts, only the slow, inexorable march of biological presence through a static, mineral underworld. The scale is immense, continental, even planetary in its void-like extent, yet the density of life and the omnipresent, alien materials create an intimate, claustrophobic feeling. It is a vast, silent, and strangely alive tomb.
