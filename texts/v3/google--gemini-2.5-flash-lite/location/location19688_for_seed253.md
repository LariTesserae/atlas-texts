The realm is a perpetually submerged world, an ocean of liquid that presses in from all sides, yet it is paradoxically a vibrant, all-encompassing ecosystem of vegetation. This is not an ocean of water on a planet, but rather a vast, biogenic sea, where "water" itself is a super-saturation of plant-derived fluids – mucilages, photosynthetic exudates, and cellular cytoplasm. The "submerged" nature is not due to gravity, but to the sheer, suffocating density of this living, liquid mass.

Terrain here is not carved by tectonics or weather, but grown. Massive, ancient, and impossibly stable plant structures form the "land." These are not mere coral reefs or kelp forests; they are monolithic formations, fused, ossified, and utterly integrated. Imagine mountains of petrified kelp, their fronds flattened and fused into sheer cliff faces stretching upwards, or vast plains of compressed algal mats, hard as limestone, polished by the slow, viscous flow around them. The erosion parameter, rated as "ancient," speaks to this immutability. These structures have been in existence for epochs, settled into a final, unchanging form, their surfaces smoothed to an almost unnerving perfection by the constant, unyielding pressure of the surrounding liquid. There are no sharp edges, no jagged peaks; only vast, undulating curves and massive, rounded domes, testament to unfathomable geological (or rather, botanical) timescales.

The "elevation" is extreme, not in the sense of height above sea level, but in the sheer verticality and depth of these submerged structures. Pillars of interlocked, skyscraper-sized lianas pierce the liquid sky, their crowns lost in the gloom above. Canyons are carved not by rivers, but by the slow, deliberate growth and decay of colossal plant organisms, their walls shimmering with faint bioluminescent algae. Valleys are vast, hollowed-out spaces within the living mass, filled with an even denser, darker ooze.

Vegetation is total, a single, interconnected, living mass. The plant life isn't just *in* the environment; it *is* the environment. There is no distinct substrate; the plant-as-substrate is the norm here. Imagine entire continents that are, in fact, colossal, single-celled plants, or vast fungal networks that have fused into landmasses. The "all-living-mass" suggests that the entire perceptible world is composed of this interconnected floral and fungal architecture, a single, gargantuan organism. Buildings, classified as "total built," are not separate structures but integral parts of this biogenic architecture. Towers are hollowed-out seed pods the size of mountains, their interiors lined with phosphorescent moss. Bridges are woven from living, calcified roots, their spans impossibly wide. Cities are vast, multi-tiered hives of organic construction, extensions of the living world itself.

The atmosphere, if it can be called that, is the same saturated, plant-derived liquid. There are no recognizable gases, only a dense, cool medium that is teeming with dissolved organic matter and microscopic life. The "cool" temperature means the liquid is chilled but not frigid, comfortable for the prevailing lifeforms.

The "darkness" is profound. Light is a luxury, a rare phenomenon. What little illumination exists comes from internal bioluminescence, emitted by symbiotic organisms or the plants themselves. Faint pulses of soft, cool light emanate from deep within the massive plant structures, casting eerie shadows and highlighting the alien contours of the landscape. This light is more of a constant, dim glow than a dynamic source, contributing to the "never changing" dynamic of the world.

Sounds are "loud," but not in the way of crashing waves or distant thunder. The soundscape is a constant, low-frequency thrumming, a deep resonance that vibrates through the dense liquid. This is the sound of unimaginable slow growth, of massive metabolic processes, of the ceaseless circulation of fluids within the living architecture. There are also occasional, deep clicks and groans, the sounds of enormous organic structures shifting almost imperceptibly over millennia. The "sparse" density of individual presences — fauna — is striking against this sonic backdrop. While the world is teeming (fauna: abundant), the individual creatures themselves are widely distributed within the vast plant matrix. Think of colossal, slow-moving leviathans that are rarely encountered, or swarms of tiny, bioluminescent organisms that drift through the liquid like cosmic dust. These creatures are not integrated into the plant life but exist *within* it, navigating the interstitial spaces.

The "weirdness" is subtle, manifesting in the impossibility of the scale and the absolute unity of the lifeforms. The feeling that an entire planet is one vast, slow-moving organism, its "mountains" and "seas" all part of the same interconnected metabolism, is profoundly alien. The notion of life so ancient and stable that it has literally built its own world, then fallen into a near-perfect equilibrium, is the core of its unusual nature.

There is no technology as we understand it. The "none" tech parameter signifies a purely biological existence. Everything that is there, from the largest structural formation to the smallest bioluminescent creature, is a product of natural (or hyper-natural) biological processes. The rules of this world are simple: exist, grow, maintain equilibrium. Time is perceived not as a sequence of events, but as a slow, cyclical tide of becoming. The world is awake, alive, and overwhelmingly present, yet utterly devoid of change. It is a monument to eternal, submerged, vegetative being.
