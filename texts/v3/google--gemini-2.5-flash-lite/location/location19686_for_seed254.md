The air itself is a heavy, warm blanket, perpetually damp and thick with the scent of decaying leaves and rich, saturated soil. It hangs in the dim light, a soupy medium where visibility drops to only a few paces. Water is not so much a feature here as it is the fundamental element. It beads on every surface, drips from unseen heights, seeps from the very ground, and collects in shallow, stagnant pools that mirror the dripping, verdant ceiling. The ground underfoot is a spongy, yielding mat of moss and fallen, waterlogged vegetation, so saturated it feels like walking on a sodden sponge.

This is not a place of distinct terrain; the ground is entirely consumed and redefined by vegetation. It’s a **vegeta-city**, a tightly packed, biomorphic urban sprawl where every structure, from vast, arching canopies to intricate, interwoven pathways, is composed of living, growing plant matter. Enormous, bulbous fungi, slick with condensation, serve as foundational pillars, their caps forming broad, flat platforms stained with algae. Towering, woody stalks, swollen with sap, twist and knot together, creating chambers and corridors. Everywhere, thick, rope-like vines, their surfaces perpetually weeping, have been woven and coaxed into dense, interwoven walls and roofs that filter the already dim light into an even more muted, emerald gloom. This is a landscape where plant growth is so aggressive and total that it has become the primary architect of everything.

The density is staggering. There is no empty space. Every niche, every crevice, every available surface is occupied by some form of life, a tapestry of mosses, ferns, lichens, and fungi so tightly packed it feels like the very air is straining against the sheer volume of living mass. Even where there are cleared areas, they are merely temporary respites before the relentless growth reclaims them, pushing new shoots and tendrils into every available void.

Within this verdant, clamoring metropolis, remnants of built structures peek through the overwhelming biomass. They are not of stone or metal, but of densely compacted, bio-engineered plant material, hardened and shaped by long-forgotten industrial processes. These are the **biotectural** edifices, the "urban" components of this world. They are layered and worn, their surfaces softened by centuries of moss growth and the constant drip of water. What might have once been broad avenues are now choked channels of flowing water or sunken passages overgrown with bioluminescent fungi that pulse with a faint, sickly green light. What were once buildings are now enormous, hollowed-out bulbs, their interiors lined with shimmering, moisture-slicked membranes, supporting entire ecosystems within their woody shells.

The atmosphere hums with a ceaseless, low-frequency thrum, a testament to the industrial past that has been so thoroughly absorbed by the present. Hidden within the thickest of the vegetative layers are the echoes of machinery—the rhythmic churning of unseen pumps, the low hiss of escaping steam, the clanking of gears still turning in the damp, dark recesses. These are not discrete machines, but integrated systems, their mechanical hearts now beating within organic bodies. Pipes, thick as a man's arm and encrusted with calcified secretions, snake through the tangled roots, carrying nutrient-rich fluids or perhaps even the recycled breath of the plant metropolis itself. The air is thick not only with moisture but with a constant, low-grade sonic saturation, a cacophony of dripping, gurgling, buzzing, and the faint, omnipresent hum of industry.

The dynamism of this place is glacial. Cycles are measured in seasons of growth and decay, in the slow creep of root systems, the patient calcification of mineral deposits. The erosion is established, not in the carving of canyons and peaks, but in the gentle wearing away of once-sharp edges by the ceaseless flow of water and the persistent embrace of vegetation. Forms settle, they become weathered by the persistent damp, and they exist in a state of patient, overwhelming balance, where the plant mass dictates the slow erosion and the slow building, all within its own ever-expanding embrace.

There is no animal life. The ecosystem is purely vegetal and fungal. The sounds are the sounds of water, of growth, of decay, and of the buried, humming industrial heart that pulses beneath it all. The dim light, pierced only by the faint bioluminescence of deep-dwelling fungi, lends an eerie, subterranean quality to the entire landscape, even though its true elevation is not significantly below the surface. It feels like a world that has been slowly, deliberately, and irrevocably consumed by its own rampant, thriving, and densely packed greenery.
