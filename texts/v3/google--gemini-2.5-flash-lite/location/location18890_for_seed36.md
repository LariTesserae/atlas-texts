The world is a greenhouse in heat. Not a constructed one, but a planetary exhalation, a vast, humid lung that has been breathing for an age and will continue to do so, indifferent to any observer. The air itself is thick, heavy with moisture that condenses on everything, beading like sweat on the broad, leathery leaves of the dominant flora. These plants are not merely present; they *are* the landscape. Imagine a forest where the trees are not individuals but a continuous, interwoven stratum. Their trunks are less like wood and more like bundled, fibrous membranes, thick as an ancient redwood but soft and yielding to the touch. They fuse together at the base, creating a colossal, undulating floor that rises and falls in slow, organic swells.

This plant-mass is the substrate, the living earth. It breathes, too, a slow, rhythmic expansion and contraction that generates a low, resonant hum, a sound lost in the pervasive quiet of the place. Within this dense green flesh, there are pockets. Not of soil and rock, but of condensed water. These are not lakes or rivers, but deep, saturated cavities within the plant-mass itself, like swollen veins. They are startlingly clear, reflecting the brilliant light that filters down from an unseen source, a light so intense it bleaches the edges of shadows. The water is cool, a welcome respite from the oppressive heat that emanates not just from the atmosphere, but from the very density of the living biomass.

The terrain is defined by the plant-building. There are no sharp geological features. No mountains, no canyons. The ground, if it can be called that, is this living tapestry. It undulates, forming shallow valleys and broader, hill-like formations. Occasionally, these vegetal mounds open, revealing the luminous, water-filled chasms within. The scale feels immense, as if this single, unified ecosystem stretches beyond the horizon, a verdant ocean under a sky of perpetual, bright day.

Fauna is a whisper. The sheer biomass of the plant life, its density and saturation, leaves little room, little energy, for anything else. You might see a slow, deliberate pulse within the plant-stuff, a ripple of movement that suggests a being navigating the inner channels. These are not animals in the conventional sense but organisms intimately connected to the plant-matrix, perhaps symbiotic or parasitic, existing as extensions of the green itself. They are rare, however, so rare you begin to doubt their existence, attributing the subtle shifts to the slow, organic expansion and contraction of the world.

The "built" elements are even more elusive. Scattered amongst the colossal plant structures are things that appear to be deliberate formations, but they are so ancient, so integrated, that their origin is lost. Perhaps petrified fungal growths, or the calcified remains of some long-vanished plant-based architect. They are smooth, geometric fragments embedded in the fibrous walls, hinting at a purpose, a past intelligence, but offering no clear form or function.

There is little erosion. The world is emerging, but not in the sense of being shaped by wind or water. It is emerging from within itself, a slow, inexorable growth. The dynamism is almost imperceptible, a timeless equilibrium. The heat is constant, the light unyielding, the dampness a pervasive presence. It is a world perpetually under construction, yet utterly stable, where life is not a collection of individual beings, but a singular, colossal entity that lives and breathes and becomes. The weirdness comes not from outward spectacle, but from this profound integration, this seamless blending of terrain, architecture, and life itself into a single, overwhelming presence. The quiet is broken only by the faint, internal hum of the living world and the occasional, soft drip of condensed moisture within the hidden water pockets.
