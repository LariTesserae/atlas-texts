# The Loomings

Below the surface, in a sunken quarter of the old district, there is a place the residents call the Loomings. It is not a cave. It is not a ruin. It is a building that has become a landscape, or a landscape that was always a building — after sufficient time, the distinction stops mattering.

The first thing you notice is the warmth. It rises from the floor, which is made of packed clay mixed with crushed brick and the fine powder of older bricks ground down by centuries of foot traffic. The warmth is gentle and constant, as if the ground itself remembers a fire. In the deepest rooms, narrow channels have been cut into the clay floor, and warm air moves through them — the work of some long-dead engineer who laid ceramic flues beneath the foundation, or perhaps simply the geometry of the chambers themselves, which pull air from the surface vents above in a way that has never been fully explained. The air is damp. It beads on the upper walls, which are too high to touch, and drips in irregular intervals from the ceiling's woven reed matting, which has been replaced so many times that it is less a single mat than a sedimentary record of reeding — layer upon layer of dried, split, and re-split river reeds, some so old they have mineralized into a pale, brittle stone that crumbles at the edges and smells of wet paper when it fractures.

The light is normal. This is the thing visitors find most unsettling. The Loomings are below the surface — you descend three sets of shallow, wide steps to enter them — and yet the light is not torchlight, not oil-lamp light, not the blue dimness of bioluminescence. It is a clean, diffuse, slightly warm daylight, the color of morning, that falls from the oculi. These are the round openings set into the high ceilings of the main chambers, perhaps a hand's-width across, fitted with lenses of what appears to be hand-blown glass, slightly irregular, slightly green. The glass is old. The glass is very old. But the light it admits is sufficient to grow things, and things do grow — though sparsely, carefully, in the way of places where space is measured in handbreadths rather than acres.

The vegetation is sparse but deliberate. Shelf-fungi with caps like polished wood grow from the joints between bricks, and the people of the Loomings do not remove them. In the highest-damp chambers, where the condensation gathers most thickly, there are hanging gardens of a sort — wire baskets suspended from the ceiling matting, filled with soil and a low, fleshy plant with broad leaves that unfurl in the warm air and close when someone's shadow passes over them. The plant is called, in the local dialect, "hearth-tongue," and it is said to have been bred for this place, for this warmth, for this particular quality of light, over so many generations that no one remembers a time when it grew anywhere else. There are perhaps two hundred baskets. They are not many. But in the Loomings, a hundred things, densely placed, is a crowd.

And the Loomings are dense.

Every surface is made. There is no natural substrate visible anywhere — no raw stone, no earth, no uncut timber. The walls are brick, the floors are clay, the ceilings are reed and ceramic, the lintels of doorways are formed from a composite of lime and animal hair and river sand that sets harder than the brick it frames. The niches in the walls — and there are niches everywhere, hundreds of them, a hand-deep, shoulder-width, placed at every height from knee to crown — are glazed with a thin slip that has aged to the color of cream. In these niches live the things of the Loomings: small clay pots, bundles of dried herbs, lumps of tallow wrapped in cloth, balls of twine, carved bone tools, split river-pebbles used as weights, and the animals.

The animals are everywhere. They are small — mostly insects, mostly beetles of a kind that is glossy black and slow-moving and which feeds on the shelf-fungi and on the accumulated dust of the reed matting. They number in the thousands. They move in visible streams across the walls, following the mortar lines, and their collective movement produces a sound that is not quite a rustle and not quite a tick, but something between — a dry, continuous, pervasive whisper that is the Loomings' baseline voice, below which all other sounds arrange themselves. There are also lizards — small, pale, with translucent skin through which you can see the slow pulse of blood — which hunt the beetles in the upper chambers and which have, over generations, lost their fear of the human residents. They watch from the ceiling matting with an expression that might be curiosity. There are doves, too, which nest in the oculi and which descend at certain hours of the day in flocks of fifteen or twenty, their wings producing a sound like a deck of cards being shuffled, and which leave behind feathers and droppings that the residents collect for the lime-mix they use to repair the walls. And there are rats, of course — there are always rats — but in the Loomings they are tolerated, even encouraged, because they eat the beetles that eat the matting, and because their digging in the clay floors is said to keep the warm-air channels clear, and because no one here is entirely sure the rats aren't doing something else, something they haven't noticed yet, something the Loomings need.

The sound level is high. It is the beetles. It is the dripping water, which falls from the ceiling matting into a thousand small clay pots and basins placed on every surface, each producing its own pitch, so that the Loomings hum with a loose, arrhythmic music — plink, plonk, plink-plink, pause, plonk — that residents learn to read the way a farmer reads the sky. A change in tempo means a shift in the surface weather. A new note means a basin has cracked or a matting has torn. The silence that follows a sudden stop in the dripping is the most feared sound in the Loomings, because it means the warm air has shifted, or the channels have clogged, or something above has changed that the residents cannot see.

The residents are human, though they are not often visible. They live in the Loomings as the beetles do, in niches — in rooms so small they are called "cups," each barely large enough to lie down in, carved into the walls at irregular intervals and furnished with a reed mat, a clay pillow, and a niche for a lamp. There are perhaps forty cups per chamber, and three chambers in the Loomings proper, and the residents move between them in patterns that seem random but are not. The Loomings are not a building that contains life. They are a building that is alive in the way a reef is alive — a structure that provides habitat, that channels water and air and light and warmth, that is worn by the creatures it houses, that is repaired by them, that has been shaped by centuries of their collective habitation into something that no longer distinguishes between the made and the grown, between the wall and the creature that lives in the wall, between the sound of water and the sound of beetles and the sound of breath.

And beneath all of this, in the deepest cup, in the smallest room, where the warm air is strongest and the light from the oculi is thinnest, there is a bowl of clay that has been there since the beginning — since before anyone can remember — and in the bowl, water. Just water. Clear, warm, still. It does not drip. It does not move. It simply is. And the residents tend it as they tend the channels and the matting and the fungi and the beetles and the lizards and the doves and the rats — not because they know why, but because it is part of the Loomings, and the Loomings are part of them, and the distinction between the two has been worn away by centuries of water falling into clay in the dark.
