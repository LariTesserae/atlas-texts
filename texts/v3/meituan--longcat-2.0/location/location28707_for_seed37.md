# The Womb-Cleft of Unbecoming

---

You find it by accident, or it finds you — a seam in the earth that shouldn't be there, a crack between two faces of limestone that opens only when the light is at a certain angle, which is to say: only when you've stopped looking for it.

The passage down is short. Twenty steps, maybe thirty, each one slightly wrong — the risers uneven, the stone still *remembering* being something else. You can feel it under your palm when you brace against the wall: a warmth that isn't quite heat, a texture that isn't quite solid. The rock here is young. Not young in the way geologists mean it — not recently deposited — but young in the way a face is young. Still forming. Still deciding what it wants to be.

And then the passage opens, and you are inside the pocket.

---

It is not large. You could cross it in forty paces if you walked in a straight line, which you cannot, because the floor is not flat and the space does not wish to be crossed. The ceiling arches above you — seven meters at its highest, three at its lowest — and it is *bright*. This is the first wrongness. This far below the surface, there should be darkness, or at best the gray half-light of reflected sun. Instead: brightness. A clean, white-gold luminescence that seems to come from the stone itself, or from the air, or from some quality of the space that has no name. It doesn't cast shadows the way light should. It pools. It gathers in the hollows of the floor and spills slowly, like something viscous and alive, into the lower corners.

The air is cool. Not cold — cool in the way that skin is cool, the way that the inside of a clay pot is cool. It touches you evenly, without draft or wind, and it carries moisture. Not the wetness of a cave — not drips or pools or the mineral smell of standing water — but a fine, pervasive dampness, as though the air itself is only partially committed to being air. Condensation beads on the ceiling in patterns that almost look deliberate. It does not fall. It hangs there, each droplet a tiny lens, and in each lens you can see — but you don't look too closely. Not yet.

---

The floor is the story of a place that hasn't finished becoming.

In the center, the stone is raw. Not carved, not eroded, not shaped by any process you can name — just *raw*, the way a wound is raw, the way a bone is raw when the flesh has been pulled back. It is pale, almost white, and it has a grain that shifts when you're not looking directly at it. Ridges rise from it in low, slow waves, like the surface of very thick liquid that has been frozen mid-motion. These ridges are the beginning of formations — the embryonic architecture of a cave that will not exist for another ten thousand years, or that existed once and is now un-happening, or that exists only here, in this pocket, in this moment, and will never exist again. You cannot tell. The stone will not say.

Toward the edges, the floor begins to differentiate. Here, a scatter of gravel — not the smooth, water-worn gravel of a riverbed, but angular, fresh-broken pieces, as though the wall has been slowly shedding. There, a patch of clay so damp it glistens, pressed with marks that might be tracks or might be the impressions of things that have no feet. The clay is the color of a bruise in its early stages: yellow-green, tender, not yet committed to its own damage.

And everywhere — *everywhere* — the seepage. Not water, exactly. Something thinner. Something that moves like water but catches the light differently, that beads on the stone in perfect spheres and then, after a long moment, is absorbed. The stone drinks it. You can see the darkening of the rock where it has been consumed, a spreading stain that fades as the stone incorporates whatever this is into itself. The sound of it — and there is a sound, though the space is mostly silent — is a faint, irregular *tik*, like the ticking of a clock that has no mechanism, no purpose, no time to keep.

---

The vegetation is sparse, but it is *present*, and it is wrong in ways that take a moment to resolve.

Near the eastern wall — if there is an east; the light makes directions uncertain — a cluster of something that resembles moss grows from a crack in the stone. It is the correct color. The correct texture. It even has the correct softness when you press it with a fingertip. But it grows in a pattern that spirals inward, and at the center of the spiral, where the moss is oldest, it has begun to *harden*. The outer edges are soft and green and alive. The center is smooth and pale and cool to the touch, like bone, like tooth, like something that has decided to stop being plant and start being mineral. The transition is gradual. You can trace it with your finger: soft, softer, firm, hard, stone. The moss is becoming the cave. Or the cave is becoming the moss. The direction of the transformation is unclear.

A few meters away, a single stalk rises from the clay — a stem, really, pale and translucent, with no leaves and no flower. It is the height of your hand, and it sways, though there is no air current. It sways toward you. Not quickly. Not obviously. But over the course of minutes, it leans, and if you step to the side, it follows, and if you step back, it straightens, and if you stand very still, it curves again, slowly, like a question mark forming in real time. It is not threatening. It is *curious*. It is the first living thing in this place that has acknowledged your presence, and its acknowledgment is a kind of gentle, vegetal attention that you can feel on your skin like a change in pressure.

There are other things. A film of something on the wall that might be lichen or might be the beginning of a patina that will, in a thousand years, cover everything. A thread of root — impossibly, because there is soil for meters in any direction — descending from the ceiling, ending in a fine, hair-like tip that quivers in the damp air. A patch of what looks like mold but is arranged in a fractal pattern so precise, so geometrically exact, that you know it is not mold, or not only mold, or mold that has been *instructed*.

---

The structures.

They are scattered — here, there, never close together, never in a pattern you can discern. Small things. The largest is perhaps waist-high: a cairn of stones stacked with a care that suggests intention but not intelligence, or intelligence of a kind you don't recognize. The stones are not from this pocket. They are darker, denser, older — brought from somewhere else, or grown here under different conditions, or *remembered* into existence by the stone that surrounds them. The cairn is stable. It has the look of something that has been standing for a long time, though the pocket itself feels young, and the contradiction sits in your mind like a stone in a shoe.

Near the far wall, a line of pebbles — twelve, thirteen — laid out in a curve that might be decorative or might be functional or might be the beginning of a wall that was abandoned when the builder understood that walls are not needed here. The pebbles are wet. They gleam. They are arranged by size, smallest to largest, and the largest is the size of a thumb, and it is not a pebble at all but a tooth — long, curved, rooted — from something that had no name in any language you speak.

And in the corner, half-hidden by a fold in the stone, a structure that is not a structure: a hollow in the wall, roughly the size and shape of a person curled on their side. It is smooth. It is damp. It has the look of something that was used, or is being used, or will be used, by something that fits exactly into that space. You do not put your hand into it. You don't know why, but you don't.

---

The fauna.

This is where the pocket reveals itself.

They are abundant — not in the way that insects are abundant, or fish, or birds, but in the way that *presences* are abundant. You feel them before you see them: a sense of being watched from multiple directions, not with hostility but with the frank, uncomplicated attention of things that have never learned to hide.

The first you notice are the *skitterers* — though that is your word, not theirs. They are small, no larger than your thumb, and they move across the stone in quick, darting bursts, like water striders on a pond. They have too many legs. Not an unsettling number — not dozens, not hundreds — just *too many*, in a way that your eye refuses to resolve. Six? Eight? The number changes depending on the angle, and when you look directly at one, it freezes, and in the stillness you can see that it is not an insect, not a spider, not anything with an exoskeleton. It is soft. It is pale. It is *wet*, and it leaves a faint trail of the same thin moisture that beads on the stone, and the trail evaporates — or is absorbed — within seconds.

They feed on the moss. Or they feed on the stone. Or they feed on the light. You watch one press itself against the raw, pale rock at the center of the floor, and the rock *dimples* beneath it, and the creature sinks slightly, and the light in that spot flickers, and when the creature moves on, the dimple remains, and the light is slightly less bright there than it was before. They are eating the brightness. They are eating the stone. They are eating the *newness* of the place, consuming the raw geology before it can decide what it wants to be.

And there are larger things.

In the damp corner, where the seepage gathers most heavily, something *coils*. It is the length of your arm, pale as the stone, and it has the texture of the clay — smooth, damp, slightly yielding. It is not a worm. It is not a snake. It is something that has not yet committed to a body plan, and as you watch, it shifts: now elongated, now compact, now sprouting a cluster of fine, hair-like projections that wave in the still air like the stalk of the plant, now smooth again, now *gone*, pressed so flat against the stone that it is indistinguishable from the rock itself, and then, slowly, reassembling, rising, coiling again. It is not hiding. It is *practicing*. It is trying on shapes the way you might try on coats, and it has not yet found one that fits.

Above you, on the ceiling, the *lenses* — the condensation droplets — are not all condensation. Some of them are eggs. Or eyes. Or both. They hang in their perfect spheres, and within each one, something turns, something watches, something *blinks* with a lid that is also a lens, and the light refracts through it, and for a moment the whole pocket is filled with a web of fractured, golden lines, and then the blink is over and the lines are gone and the droplet is just a droplet again, except that it is slightly larger than it was before, and slightly more opaque, and the thing inside it is slightly more *formed*.

---

The silence is not empty.

It is a *full* silence — a silence with weight and texture, a silence that has been accumulating since the pocket first opened, since the stone first began to decide what it wanted to be. Within it, the small sounds exist like stars in a dark sky: the *tik* of seepage, the almost-inaudible rustle of the skitterers, the wet, soft sound of the coiling thing rearranging itself, the faint, high hum of the lenses on the ceiling — a sound at the very edge of hearing, a sound that might be communication or might be digestion or might be the sound of reality *bending* around a shape it doesn't quite recognize.

And beneath all of it, so low you feel it in your chest rather than hear it with your ears: a pulse. Slow. Irregular. Not a heartbeat — not biological, not mechanical — but a *geological* pulse, the rhythm of stone under impossible pressure, of water moving through passages that don't exist yet, of a place that is still, after all this time, *becoming*.

---

The weirdness is not a quality of the place. It *is* the place.

You notice it in small ways first. The way the light doesn't diminish as you move away from the center — it stays constant, as though the brightness is a property of the air itself, not a source. The way the walls, when you look at them from the corner of your eye, seem to *breathe* — a slow expansion and contraction, as though the pocket is inhaling and exhaling over the course of minutes. The way the skitterers, when they move, leave trails that persist for a moment as *afterimages* — not on the stone, but in the air, a faint, glowing line that traces their path and then fades, and the afterimage is not a reflection but a *memory*, and the air is remembering the creature's passage, and the memory is visible, and it is beautiful, and it is wrong.

You notice it in larger ways too. The way the coiling thing, when it presses itself flat against the stone, doesn't just *look* like the stone — it *becomes* the stone, and for a moment you cannot tell where the creature ends and the rock begins, and the boundary between animal and mineral dissolves, and you understand that in this place, the categories are not fixed, that *living* and *not-living* are not opposites but points on a spectrum, and the spectrum is *moving*.

The way the moss, at the center of its spiral, has become stone, and the stone at the center of the floor is *softening*, and the two processes are not separate but part of a single, slow exchange — the organic becoming inorganic, the inorganic becoming organic, a cycle with no beginning and no end, just a continuous, patient transformation that has been going on since the pocket opened and will go on long after you leave.

The way the hollow in the wall — the person-shaped hollow — is *dampest* at the center, and the dampness is warm, and the warmth is not from the stone but from the hollow itself, as though something has just left, or is about to arrive, or is *always* arriving and *always* leaving, and the hollow is not a space but a *moment*, and the moment is shaped like a body, and the body is shaped like yours.

---

You stand in the center of the pocket, in the bright, cool, damp air, and you feel the pulse of the stone beneath your feet, and you watch the skitterers eat the light, and you feel the attention of the lenses on the ceiling, and you hear the silence that is not silent, and you understand that this place is not a cave, not a pocket, not a micro-environment, not a hidden thing.

It is a *question*.

The stone is asking itself what it wants to be. The water is asking the stone. The light is asking the water. The creatures are asking the light. And the pocket — the whole pocket, the whole small, bright, impossible space — is asking *you*, in its quiet, patient, geological way:

*What are you becoming?*

And you don't have an answer. But the pocket is not in a hurry. It is still forming. It has time.

The seepage ticks. The stalk leans. The lenses blink.

The stone decides, and decides again, and decides again.
