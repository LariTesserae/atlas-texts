# The Drowning Room

---

You come to it through a throat of stone — a passage so narrow your shoulders brush both walls, so low you must crouch, then crawl, then press yourself forward on your belly through wet rock that leaves your clothes dark and clinging. The sound finds you before the space does. It is not a sound you hear so much as a pressure that arrives in your chest, a vibration that enters through your sternum and fills your ribs like a second heartbeat. By the time you emerge, you are already inside the noise. There is no adjusting to it. You simply exist within it, the way a fish exists within water.

The chamber opens.

Not dramatically — there is no grand reveal, no cathedral vault. It simply *is*, and you are in it, and it is larger than you expected but not large. Perhaps forty paces across, perhaps sixty. The ceiling is low enough that you could touch it if you reached up in most places, though you will not reach up, because your body has already understood that this is a place for keeping your head down, for moving slowly, for being small. The walls curve in ways that suggest the water made them — not recently, not in any timeframe that matters to the water, which has been doing this since before the concept of before. The stone is smooth in the way that only very old, very wet stone is smooth: not polished, but *worn*, the way a bone is worn when it has been carried in someone's pocket for years. Rounded. Familiar with touch.

The waterfall is not large. This is the first strange thing. The sound is so total, so immense, so *everywhere*, that you expect something monumental — a column of water dropping from a great height, a torrent, a violence. Instead: a thin stream, perhaps the width of your forearm, sliding down the far wall from a crack perhaps ten feet above the chamber floor. It does not fall so much as *secrete*, a slow and continuous weeping from the stone. It pools in a shallow basin at the base — a basin that is clearly not natural, or rather, that was once natural and has been worked, deepened, shaped by hands. The edges are too regular. There are tool marks, old ones, almost erased by the same patient water that made the chamber. The pool overflows gently at one lip and disappears into a crack in the floor. The water is so clear it is almost not there. You can see the bottom of the basin — smooth stone, a few darker stones that might be deliberate placement, a slow accumulation of mineral deposit that has built up over what must be centuries into a rim around the pool's edge, like the rim of a very old cup.

And yet the sound.

The sound is *deafening*. It is the loudest thing you have ever experienced, and it is coming from this modest trickle. The chamber shape is responsible — the curves of the wall catch the sound and fold it back on itself, amplify it, multiply it until the air itself seems to have texture. It is not a roar. It is a *hiss*, a white noise, a static that contains within it every frequency of water-sound: the slap, the trickle, the drip, the rush, the deep subsonic hum of water moving through stone. It is so loud that your own thoughts become difficult. You cannot hear your breathing. You cannot hear your footsteps. If you speak, you will not hear your own voice, though you will feel the vibration in your throat. The sound is a presence. It occupies the space the way a body occupies a room. You move around it. You move through it. You cannot move away from it.

The cold is the second thing. It is not the cold of winter or wind. It is the cold of deep stone, of water that has never seen sun, of air that has not moved in a long time. It is a cold that does not bite — it *settles*. It gets into your clothes and stays there. It makes your fingers slow. It makes you aware of your own warmth as a temporary condition, a small fire that is burning down. The temperature does not vary. There is no warmer spot, no cooler spot. The cold is total and even, the way the sound is total and even. This is a place that has reached its state and will hold it.

The darkness is the third thing, and it is nearly complete. Your eyes adjust, as eyes do, and you learn to see by the faintest light — a light that seems to come from the vegetation itself, or from the water, or from some quality of the stone that holds and releases the memory of light the way the walls hold and release the sound. It is not bioluminescence, exactly. It is more like *persistence*. The walls have a faint, grey, directionless glow, as though they are remembering something bright that happened a long time ago. It is enough to see shapes. It is enough to see the room. It is not enough to see details, or color, or anything that would let you describe this place to someone who has not been here. You would fail. The darkness keeps its own counsel.

And the vegetation — this is the fourth thing, and it is the thing that will stay with you.

It grows in patches. Thick, low, dense patches of moss and things that are not quite moss — things that have the texture of moss but the structure of something more deliberate, more *architectural*. They cling to the walls in shapes that suggest intention: a fan, a rosette, a slow spiral. They are pale. Not white, not green, but the color of things that have never needed color — a grey so faint it is almost silver, a cream so pale it is almost stone. In the patches, they are lush beyond reason. They are thick and wet and *alive* in a way that feels almost aggressive, almost excessive, given the darkness and the cold and the sound. They are the most alive thing in this place. They grow where the moisture is — near the pool, near the base of the waterfall, in the cracks where condensation gathers. They grow on the built things too, the old structures, and this is where the line between made and grown becomes impossible to find.

Because there are structures here. Small ones. Scattered. A low wall, perhaps waist-high, built of flat stones stacked without mortar, now half-collapsed and furred with moss. A standing stone — a single slab, taller than it is wide, placed upright near the pool's edge, its surface worn smooth on the side that faces the water, as though people have stood there for a long time, leaning against it, looking into the basin. There are more tool marks here: lines cut into the standing stone, regular and shallow, a counting of something. Days. Years. Something else. A few stones arranged in a circle near the chamber's center — a fire pit, perhaps, though there is no soot, no ash, no sign of burning. The stones are cold and wet and have been for a very long time. A shelf carved into the wall at the height of a person's head, and on it, things: a stone tool, a blade, still sharp, made of a dark flint that does not come from this chamber or anywhere near it. A bundle of something organic — plant fiber, perhaps, or hair — so old it has become a single mass, a felt, a thing that is no longer its components. A small figure, carved from a pale wood or bone, shaped like a person but simplified, reduced to the idea of a person: a head, a body, two arms. It fits in a palm. It is smooth with handling.

These things are old. Not ancient in the way of ruins and empires, but old in the way of a place that has been visited quietly, repeatedly, by people who came for the water, or for the silence within the noise, or for something about this chamber that you do not yet understand. They came with stone tools and simple hands and they left these marks and they did not come back, or they came back so many times that the marks have merged into a single long visit, a single continuous act of being here.

The ecology of the chamber is spare and strange. The moss-things are the dominant life, and they are joined by a pale, thread-like fungus that hangs from the ceiling in places, so fine it is almost invisible, drifting in air currents you cannot feel. There are small, white, many-legged things in the moss — not insects, something older, something that has adapted to the dark and the wet and the noise by becoming very small and very still. You will not see them unless you look closely. You will not see them unless you are the kind of person who looks closely. There may be something in the pool — a movement, a shadow, a suggestion of a shape that is not a stone. If it is there, it is rare, and it is shy, and it has learned to live in the sound the way the moss has learned to live in the dark: by making it home.

The air is thick. Not humid, exactly — *saturated*. It is air that has reached its limit of water and is holding it, just barely, in a state that is not quite vapor and not quite liquid. Your clothes are damp within minutes. Your skin is damp. The condensation gathers on the ceiling and falls in drops that are indistinguishable from the waterfall, and the waterfall gathers in the pool, and the pool overflows into the crack, and the crack leads to somewhere deeper, somewhere lower, somewhere that is not this chamber but is connected to it by the slow, patient movement of water through stone. This has been happening for a long time. The erosion is established. The chamber is not growing or shrinking in any way you could measure in a lifetime. It is in its own state. It is what it is. The water has found its path and the stone has found its shape and they have reached an agreement that will hold until something changes that is larger than water or stone.

Time moves slowly here. This is not a metaphor. The sound, the cold, the darkness, the damp — they conspire to remove the markers by which you measure duration. Your watch will not help you. Your heartbeat will not help you. The waterfall does not change. The moss does not grow in any way you can observe. The pool does not rise or fall. The only movement is the slow, almost geological accumulation of mineral on the pool's rim, the patient building of stone from water, and this is a process that makes glaciers look hasty. You could sit here for an hour and it would feel like a day. You could sit here for a day and it would feel like an hour. The chamber does not care. The chamber has its own time, and it is not yours.

You will leave the way you came. On your belly, through the wet throat of stone, backward or forward, it does not matter. The sound will fade slowly, not all at once, as the passage narrows and the walls stop their work of amplification. The cold will stay in your clothes for hours. The damp will stay in your skin. The pale shapes of the moss will stay in your vision, floating in the dark when you close your eyes, the way the sound will stay in your ears — a ringing, a hiss, a memory of a noise so large it became a place, a place so quiet it became a secret.

The standing stone will stand. The pool will fill. The water will fall.

The chamber will wait, the way it has always waited: in the dark, in the cold, in the sound, in the slow and patient company of water and stone and the small, pale, tenacious things that have learned to live where almost nothing lives.
