# The Weep

---

You arrive from below, though "below" is a direction that has already begun to lose meaning.

The climb — if it was a climb — has taken you through cloud, through a belt of air so thick with moisture that your lungs learned to drink, and now you stand at the top of the world on a plateau that should not exist. The elevation is wrong. Not merely high — *wrong*. The sky here is a deep bruised indigo even though the sun is directly overhead, and the light falls at an angle that doesn't correspond to any sun you can see. The air pressure feels normal. The air pressure feels like nothing. You cannot tell if you are gasping from thinness or from heat.

The heat. God, the heat.

It sits on you like a hand. Wet, heavy, tropical — the kind of heat that makes a cradle of you, that holds you in place. At this elevation, the temperature should be killing you with cold. Instead it is a wet furnace, a mouth, a room with the door shut. The thermometer, if you had one, would read something like 42°C. The humidity is total. You are not sweating anymore; you are simply wet, the same temperature as the air, indistinguishable from the moisture that coats every surface in a living film.

And there is so much water.

---

The plateau extends outward in every direction, and it is *weeping*. That is the only word. The ground — a dark, fine-grained stone that might be basalt or might be something else entirely — is covered in a sheet of water perhaps two centimeters deep, and from every vertical surface, water emerges. It seeps from the stone itself, as though the rock is porous beyond any geology you know, as though the plateau is a sponge saturated past capacity and slowly, slowly releasing. The water is warm. It is the same temperature as the air, as your skin, as everything. It does not flow downhill so much as it *spreads*, finding its way across the stone in a network of rivulets so dense and so finely branched that the ground appears to be made of water laid over water, a fractal delta with no river at its source and no sea at its end.

The sound of this is the first thing that strikes you as impossible.

It is *deafening*. Not the roar of a waterfall or the crash of surf — this is something else. It is the sound of a million million tiny emergences, a hiss and whisper and gurgle and seep that combines into a wall of white noise so total that it erases thought. You cannot hear your own footsteps. You cannot hear your own breathing. The sound is not loud in the way that a jet engine is loud; it is loud in the way that silence is loud, in the way that a held note becomes everything. It fills the skull. It fills the world. It is the sound of water being born from stone, over and over, an infinite and unchanging birth-cry that has been going on for — how long? The erosion says: a long time. The erosion says: forever.

---

And then you see the city.

It rises from the weeping stone perhaps a kilometer ahead, and at first you think it is a forest. It is too dense, too organic, too *grown* to be architecture. But the longer you look, the more the shapes resolve into intention. Towers. Arches. Bridges spanning between towers. Long low structures that might be halls or might be walls. A grid of streets — or what were streets — now filled with the same shallow water that covers everything, so that the city appears to be standing in a mirror, a reflection of itself in warm still water.

The city is made of something that is not quite stone and not quite wood and not quite bone.

It is pale. Cream-colored, with veins of darker material running through it like grain or like marrow. It has the texture of coral — no, of *teeth* — no, of something that grew in layers, that accreted over time, that was deposited rather than built. The surfaces are smooth in the way that a shell is smooth, ridged in the way that a femur is ridged. There are no joints. No seams. No mortar. The structures rise from the ground as though they emerged from it, as though the plateau grew a city the way a reef grows a colony, and in a sense, perhaps it did.

The scale is vast. The towers rise fifty, sixty, seventy meters — not skyscrapers, but enough to create a skyline, enough to cast shadows that fall across the water and the lower structures in long dark geometries. The bridges between them are slender, impossibly slender, and they arc in curves that suggest organic growth rather than engineering. Some of them are broken. Some of them end in midair, the broken edge smooth and rounded, like a healed wound, like a bone that was fractured and then mineralized over.

There are no windows. There are openings — irregular, oval, placed without pattern — but they are not windows. They are more like pores. More like the openings in a sponge. More like something that was designed to let water through.

---

The vegetation is everywhere.

It covers the city like a skin. Like a disease. Like a second city growing over the first. Vines — thick as your arm, thick as your torso — drape from every horizontal surface, hanging in curtains that sway in a wind you cannot feel. The leaves are enormous, broad and dark green, almost black in the strange light, and they are *dripping*. Every leaf is a fountain. Water runs down the veins and drips from the tips in a constant patter that adds its own frequency to the deafening hiss. The leaves are so dense in places that they form a canopy, a secondary ceiling over the streets, and beneath them the light is green and submarine and thick.

There are flowers. They are the size of dinner plates, and they are the color of nothing you have seen — a deep, saturated magenta that seems to generate its own light, that seems to pulse with a slow internal rhythm. They grow from the vines, from the walls, from the water itself, their stems rooted in nothing, floating on the surface of the shallow flood. They do not move. Nothing moves. The petals are open to exactly the same degree on every flower, as though they were frozen in the act of blooming, as though this moment — this exact configuration of petal and stamen and dew — is the only moment that has ever existed or will ever exist.

The roots are the most extraordinary thing. They emerge from the vines and from the structures themselves, thick and pale and branching, and they descend into the water and into the stone, and where they touch the stone, the stone *accepts* them. There is no crack, no fissure. The root simply enters the rock as though the rock were water, as though the boundary between organic and mineral were a suggestion rather than a law. In places, the roots have grown so thick and so dense that they form a second architecture — a root-city over the stone-city, a lattice of living wood that reinforces and entangles and ultimately *becomes* the structures it clings to. You cannot tell where the building ends and the vine begins. You cannot tell if the vine is holding the building up or if the building is holding the vine up or if they are the same thing.

---

There are no animals.

This is the silence beneath the sound. This is the absence that makes the place feel like a cathedral after the congregation has left. There are no birds in the canopy. No insects on the flowers. No fish in the water. No lizards on the walls. No spiders in the corners. Nothing moves that is not plant or water or stone. The flowers do not sway. The vines do not creep. The water does not ripple except where it emerges from the rock, and even there, the emergence is smooth, laminar, *unnatural* — water that flows without turbulence, without chaos, as though every molecule has agreed on a direction and a speed and will not deviate.

You crouch at the edge of the water and look for your reflection, and you find it, but it is wrong. It is you, but it is you from a slightly different angle, as though the water is a mirror that is not quite flat, that curves in a way that shows you a version of yourself that is adjacent to the real one. You blink. The reflection blinks. But there is a delay — not a delay in time, but a delay in *space*, as though the reflection is coming from a few centimeters to the left of where it should be.

You look up. The city stands in its green shroud, dripping, steaming faintly in the heat, and it is beautiful, and it is terrible, and it is *still*. Not quiet — never quiet, the hiss is eternal — but still. Nothing changes. The water emerges at the same rate it has always emerged. The flowers hold their petals at the same angle they have always held them. The roots grow at a rate that is imperceptible, that may be zero, that may be a memory of growth rather than growth itself.

---

The reality breakdown announces itself slowly, the way a smell announces itself — first as a wrongness you cannot name, then as a wrongness you cannot ignore.

It is in the geometry. The city's streets, viewed from above (and you do view them from above, though you have not climbed, though you are somehow looking down from a height that shifts without transition), form a pattern that is almost a grid but not quite. The angles are wrong. The blocks are rectangles that are also parallelograms that are also something with five sides. The streets intersect at crossings that have three arms, or five, or seven, and the number seems to change depending on how directly you look at them. When you stare at a crossing, it has four arms. When you look away and back, it has six. The extra arms lead to streets that were not there before, or that were always there, or that exist only in the peripheral vision, in the corner of the eye, in the place where seeing becomes something else.

It is in the water. The water flows in the same direction everywhere — outward, away from the center of the plateau, toward the edges — but the edges are in every direction, and the center is everywhere too. The water flows away from you no matter where you stand. The water flows toward you no matter where you stand. Both of these things are true. The rivulets branch and branch and branch until they are thinner than hair, thinner than capillaries, and at that scale they do not branch anymore — they *merge*, flowing together into streams that grow larger as they approach the source, so that the water is flowing uphill, uphill in every direction, converging on a center that is also the edge, that is also you.

It is in the light. The light is normal. The light is perfectly normal. It falls from a sun that is directly overhead and casts shadows that point directly away from you, and this is true no matter which way you face, and this is true no matter where you stand, and the shadows are always the same length, and the shadows are always the same darkness, and the shadows do not move because the sun does not move because time does not move because this is a place that has found an equilibrium so perfect, so total, that change itself has been eroded away.

---

The erosion. Yes.

The stone is old. The stone is so old that it has forgotten what it was before it was stone. The surfaces are rounded, smoothed, worn to a softness that feels almost biological — like skin, like the belly of something that has been sleeping for a million years. There are no sharp edges anywhere. The towers, though they rise to impossible heights, have walls that curve gently inward, that bell outward at the base, that have the organic taper of a tree trunk or a stalagmite. The bridges are thickest in the middle and thin at the ends, like bones, like femurs, like the arches of a spine. Everything has been worn to its most efficient shape, its most *settled* shape, the shape it will hold forever because it has already been held for so long that there is no other shape it could possibly be.

And yet the city is not crumbling. The city is not decaying. The city is in a state of perfect preservation, perfect stasis, as though the erosion that shaped it has stopped — not because the forces of erosion have ceased, but because there is nothing left to erode. Every surface has been worn to the point of minimum energy, minimum exposure, minimum *possibility*. The stone cannot be worn further because it has already become the shape that wear demands. The roots cannot grow further because they have already filled every space available to them. The water cannot flow faster or slower because it has already found the exact rate at which emergence equals evaporation equals flow equals return.

This is the equilibrium. This is the frozen heart of the place. It is a system that has reached its final state, its attractor, its *end*, and it will remain in this state for as long as the world exists, which may be forever, because in a system where nothing changes, there is no entropy, no decay, no death — only this, only the eternal present, only the weep.

---

You walk into the city.

The water is ankle-deep in the streets, and it is warm, and it is clear, and you can see the stone beneath it, smooth and dark and veined with the pale traces of old roots that have been absorbed, mineralized, turned to stone themselves. The walls rise on either side, draped in vines, punctuated by those oval openings that are not windows, and through the openings you can see — what? More water. More green. More of the same. The interiors of the structures are hollow, or filled with a tangle of roots so dense that they form a solid mass, or filled with water that is perfectly still, perfectly clear, and reflects the green light from above in patterns that shift when you don't look at them directly.

The sound changes as you enter the city. The hiss is still there — it is always there, it is the background radiation of this place — but it is joined by other sounds. A deep, subsonic hum that you feel in your chest more than you hear with your ears. A high, thin whine that seems to come from the flowers, from the magenta petals that vibrate at a frequency just below visibility. A rhythmic *drip-drip-drip* from the vines overhead, each drop falling into the water with a precision that is too regular, too metronomic, too *intentional* to be natural. And beneath all of it, so low that it is more a pressure than a sound, a groan — the groan of stone under stress, the groan of roots under tension, the groan of a city that is holding itself up through sheer accumulated weight and time and the stubbornness of materials that have forgotten how to fall.

The density of the place is overwhelming. Every surface is covered. Every space is filled. The vines layer over the stone, the roots layer over the vines, the moss layers over the roots, the water layers over the moss, and the reflections layer over the water, so that you are walking through a space that is simultaneously solid and liquid, real and mirrored, here and *somewhere else*. The air is thick with spores — you can see them, golden, drifting in the light — but they do not drift randomly. They move in slow, spiraling currents that follow the geometry of the streets, that flow through the oval openings, that enter and exit the buildings in patterns that suggest respiration, that suggest the city is *breathing*, though nothing moves, though nothing changes, though the breath is a single held inhalation that has lasted since before you were born.

---

You find a plaza.

It is circular — or it is hexagonal — or it is a shape that has no name, that your eye slides off of, that your brain refuses to resolve. In the center, there is a structure that might be a fountain or might be a tree or might be a tower that has been swallowed by a tree. It rises ten meters, twenty meters, and it is covered in the pale, bone-like material of the city, and it is covered in vines, and it is covered in roots, and from its summit — if it has a summit — water emerges. Not flows. *Emerges*. It appears on the surface of the structure as though the structure is sweating, as though the structure is weeping, and it runs down the pale stone in sheets, and it drips from the leaves, and it falls into the pool at the base, and the pool overflows into the streets, and the streets carry it outward, and the outward flow is the inward flow, and the water is the same water, and it has always been the same water, and it will always be the same water.

There are no animals. There are no birds to drink from the pool. There are no insects to skate on its surface. There are no fish to move in its depths. The water is sterile, or the water is so alive with microscopic life that it has become a single organism, a single cell, a single *thought* that thinks itself over and over in the language of emergence and flow and return.

You stand at the edge of the pool and you look at your reflection and your reflection looks at you and between you and your reflection there is a distance that is not distance, a gap that is not empty, a space where the rules of the world have worn thin, where the erosion has cut through to something underneath, something that is not stone and not water and not root but is the *idea* of stone, the *idea* of water, the *idea* of root, the platonic forms of a world that has been worn down to its own blueprint.

---

This is the Weep.

It is a city that grew or was grown or accreted or dreamed itself into being on the top of a world that should be cold and dry and dead, and instead it is hot and wet and alive with a life that is only vegetable, only mineral, only water, only the slow patient chemistry of emergence and growth and stasis. It has no technology. It has no tools. It has no makers, or its makers are so long gone that they have become part of the stone, part of the water, part of the roots, and the city has continued without them, has reached its equilibrium without them, has become its own reason for being without them.

It does not change. It will not change. The water will weep from the stone forever. The vines will hold their leaves at the same angle forever. The flowers will hold their petals open forever. The roots will hold the city together forever. The sound will fill the air forever. The heat will press down forever. The light will fall at its impossible angle forever. The geometry will be wrong forever. The reflections will be displaced forever. The spores will spiral forever. The pool will overflow forever.

And you are here, in the middle of it, standing in warm water in a green-lit street in a city that is not a city, on a plateau that is not a plateau, under a sky that is not a sky, and the sound is so loud that you cannot hear yourself think, and the heat is so heavy that you cannot feel your own weight, and the wetness is so total that you cannot tell where your skin ends and the world begins, and you understand — not with your mind but with your body, with your water, with your mineral, with your vegetable — that you have entered a place that has already finished becoming itself, and that there is nothing left to do but stand in it, and be still, and weep.
