# The Weeping Reach

From a distance — if distance means anything here, if there is a from — the Reach is a sound before it is a place. A low, continuous exhalation, like the breath of something enormous and patient, something that has been breathing since before the concept of before. The sound is water, but not only water. It is water moving through stone, water moving through metal, water moving through the spaces between things that should not have spaces between them. It is the sound of a world that has never once been dry.

The darkness is not an absence. It is a presence. It has weight and texture and temperature — warm, always warm, a warmth that comes from below, from the deep geology of this high plateau where the crust is thin and the interior heat of the world presses upward like a fever that never breaks. The darkness is so complete that it has become a medium, a substance you move through, and over the millennia — over the long, slow millennia of established erosion, of water finding every crack and widening it, of stone learning to be water's patient collaborator — life has learned to see with other senses, or to abandon seeing altogether and find other ways of knowing where the edges of things are.

The plateau itself is vast. Not merely large — vast in the way that weather systems are vast, in the way that it generates its own atmospheric logic. The elevation is high enough that the air should be thin, should be cold, should be the kind of place where nothing grows and the wind scours everything to bone. But the water will not allow it. The water is everywhere, rising from below, condensing from the warm dark air, weeping from every surface in a perpetual slow rain that falls upward as often as down. The atmosphere is so saturated that it has become a kind of broth, a warm mineral soup in which everything is suspended, everything is damp, everything is in the process of dissolving and reforming simultaneously.

And in this broth, things grow.

The vegetation is lush in a way that has nothing to do with sunlight, because there is no sunlight. There has not been sunlight here for longer than the oldest stone can remember. The plants — if they are plants, if that word still applies — are chemosynthetic, feeding on the mineral-rich water, on the thermal vents that puncture the plateau like pores, on the slow chemical reactions between the warm stone and the perpetual moisture. They grow in layers, dense and interwoven, a vertical ecology that fills every available space from the ground to — well, to whatever passes for sky here, which is more darkness, more warm wet air, more of the same medium extending upward until it becomes indistinguishable from the darkness below.

The ground is not ground in any conventional sense. It is a mat of root and fungal tissue and accumulated organic matter, meters thick in places, floating on the water that pools and flows beneath it. Walking on it — if anyone walked here, if there were feet and legs and the concept of walking — would be like walking on a sponge that has been soaking for ten thousand years. It yields. It compresses. It slowly rebounds. And everywhere, through the mat, the water finds its way up, beading on every surface, running in thin films over every leaf and stem and frond, so that the entire world is lacquered in a sheen of moisture that catches what little light there is.

What little light there is.

Because there is light, after all. Not much. Not the light of stars or sun or any celestial body — those are elsewhere, or elsewhen, or elsewhat. But the world makes its own light, in small quantities, in strange spectra. Bioluminescence, yes — the fungi pulse with a dim blue-green glow, and certain of the larger organisms carry their own light in organs that have evolved over epochs of darkness. But also there is the light of industry, scattered and intermittent, the light of the built things.

The built things.

They are scattered, as the parameters say. Small structures, faint marks, partial remains, occasional artifacts. But they are not primitive. They are industrial. They are engines and factories and mechanized systems, scaled production, electrified. They rise from the wet dark landscape like the bones of some vast animal half-swallowed by the earth — smelting towers, processing plants, the hulking shapes of machines whose purpose is not immediately clear. They are old. They are very old. They have been here longer than the current ecology, longer than the established erosion patterns, longer than the warm water that now seeps through their foundations and drips from their rusted catwalks. They were built by something — someone — and then they were abandoned, or their builders were absorbed, or they simply continued operating long after the concept of an operator became irrelevant.

And they still operate.

This is the strangeness. This is the weirdness that permeates everything here, that makes the familiar industrial shapes into something uncanny. The factories still run. The engines still turn. The electrified systems still carry current through cables that have been overgrown with chemosynthetic vines, through conduits that have been colonized by organisms that feed on the electromagnetic fields. The production continues, but what is being produced has changed. The machines were built to process ore, perhaps, or to manufacture some component, some part of some larger machine. But the inputs have changed. The ore is gone, or has been replaced by something else — by the mineral-rich water, by the organic matter that accumulates in the warm dark, by the bodies of the fauna that die and are drawn into the intake systems by currents and conveyor belts that still move, still function, still perform their ancient programmed motions.

The fauna is abundant. It moves through the dense vegetation, through the water, through the structures themselves. It is adapted to the darkness, to the warmth, to the wet. It is strange — not alien, not exactly, but strange in the way that deep-cave life is strange, in the way that abyssal life is strange, in the way that life becomes strange when it has been evolving in isolation for longer than most species exist. There are things with too many legs and things with no legs at all. There are things that are transparent, their organs visible through skin like wet paper. There are things that are colonial, that exist as networks of smaller organisms functioning as a single entity, spreading through the root-mat like a slow-motion explosion of coordinated life. There are things that have incorporated the industrial materials into their bodies — that have grown around pipes and gears and electrical conduits, that have made the machines part of their anatomy, that draw energy from the still-functioning systems and in turn maintain those systems with their movements, their secretions, their slow biological processes.

The sound is loud. It is the sound of water, always water, the constant percussion of droplets falling from every surface, the rush of water through channels natural and artificial, the deep subsonic hum of the earth's heat moving through stone. But it is also the sound of the machines — the rhythmic clank of pistons, the whir of turbines, the crackle of electrical discharge, the groan of metal under stress. And it is the sound of the fauna — the clicks and chirps and low-frequency rumbles of communication, the rustle of bodies moving through dense vegetation, the splash of things entering and leaving the water, the occasional sharp report of something breaking, something being caught, something being consumed.

The dynamic is active. Everything is in motion. The water cycles — rising from below, condensing from the air, flowing across the surface, seeping back down through the root-mat and the fractured stone. The biological cycles — growth, consumption, death, decomposition, regrowth. The industrial cycles — the machines processing, producing, breaking down, being repaired by the organisms that have learned to maintain them. The geological cycles — the slow uplift of the plateau, the continued erosion by water that has been eroding since before the machines were built, the thermal vents opening and closing as the deep heat finds new paths to the surface.

And through it all, the darkness. The warm, wet, dense darkness that is not an absence but a medium, a substance, a condition of existence. The darkness in which the scattered industrial structures glow with their own dim light, in which the bioluminescent organisms pulse and flicker, in which the water catches and refracts every photon and scatters it into a diffuse, directionless illumination that reveals nothing clearly but suggests everything.

This is the Weeping Reach. It is high and vast and old. It is wet and warm and dark. It is alive in ways that are strange and dense and loud. It is industrial and organic and geological all at once, all intertwined, all in the process of becoming something that none of its components — not the stone, not the water, not the machines, not the life — could have predicted or intended.

It weeps. It has always been weeping. It will weep long after the last machine has ground to a halt, long after the last organism has been consumed and recycled into the root-mat, long after the concept of weeping has lost all meaning. The water will continue to rise from below, to condense from the warm dark air, to find its way through every crack and channel and conduit, to shape the stone and feed the life and carry the dissolved minerals in an endless slow circulation that is the closest thing this place has to a heartbeat.

And in the darkness, in the wet, in the dense and teeming and strange and loud and active darkness, the world continues. Not as it was. Not as it was intended. But as it is. As it has become. As it is still, always, in the process of becoming.
