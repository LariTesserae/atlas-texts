# The Mossworks

---

From a distance, you think you are looking at a mountain that has learned to breathe.

The plateau sits high — high enough that the air has a thinness to it, a coolness that sits on your skin like a damp cloth even in full sun. And the sun *is* full. The light here is extraordinary: high-altitude brightness, undiffused by lowland haze, striking every surface with a clean, almost surgical clarity. Water catches it everywhere. The whole plateau gleams.

You hear it before you understand what you're seeing. A roar — not the clean roar of a waterfall, but something thicker, more textured, a sound with *layers* to it. It is the sound of enormous volumes of water being forced through constricted spaces, through channels and conduits and the gaps between stone. It is the sound of machinery that never stopped. It is the sound of living things growing so densely and so fast that the air itself seems to shudder with their expansion. These sounds are not separate. They have merged into one continuous, deafening voice that the plateau speaks without pause, day and night, a roar so constant that after an hour you stop hearing it as sound and start feeling it as pressure — a weight against your eardrums, a vibration in your sternum, a hum in your teeth.

---

Closer now.

The rock here is young. Not old rock, not the smooth, rounded, patient stone of ancient mountains — this is *new* geology, raw and still deciding what it wants to be. The plateau is a shelf of recently uplifted sedimentary stone, and you can see the strata laid bare in long, exposed walls: bands of grey and rust-red and pale ochre, tilted at angles that speak of violent vertical forces not long past. The stone is sharp-edged. It fractures in sheets and wedges. There are surfaces here that still carry the tool-marks of tectonic splitting — clean faces where the rock has only recently been broken open, as if the mountain itself is a construction site, still under assembly. Water seeps from every joint and fissure in these fresh faces. The rocks weep. The whole plateau is damp to the touch, sheen of moisture on every surface, and where the sun hits the wet stone it produces a brightness that is almost painful — a million tiny mirrors catching and throwing the high, hard light.

This water is the key to everything.

The plateau catches moisture from the air itself — clouds drag across its upper surface daily, and the condensation alone provides a constant supply of fresh water that beads and runs and pools in every depression. But there is more: somewhere beneath the surface, an aquifer is being forced upward through the newly fractured stone, and it emerges everywhere, unpredictably, finding paths through the raw geology like blood finding capillaries in new tissue. The water does not yet have rivers. It does not yet have streambeds. It is still in the process of *deciding* where to go. It sheets across flat rock in thin, glassy films. It drips from overhangs. It pools in angular basins that have not yet been worn smooth. It funnels into cracks and disappears, resurfaces, disappears again. The entire plateau is a wetland in the process of organizing itself — a drainage system still embryonic, still dreaming of the rivers it will one day become.

---

And into this raw, wet, bright stone, the plants have come.

Not gently. Not gradually. Not as a thin skin of lichen or a modest coat of moss. The vegetation here is *total*. It is the dominant substance of the place. It has colonized every surface, filled every gap, turned the entire plateau into a living architecture of green.

But — and this is the strange thing — it is not dense in the way you expect. The density is *sparse*. There are vast open spaces here, clearings where the raw stone is exposed and the water runs freely in bright, shallow sheets. The vegetation does not form a continuous mat; it forms *structures*. Walls. Colonnades. Arches. Canopies. The plants grow in concentrated masses separated by open air and bare rock, and the effect is less like a forest and more like a city — a city built entirely of living things.

The dominant species — if you can call it a single species — is a kind of enormous, woody-stemmed moss. Not the soft, cushiony moss of lowland forests. This is structural moss. Its stems are rigid, fibrous, as thick as your wrist at the base, and they grow in tight vertical bundles that, over time, fuse together into solid walls of living tissue. These walls rise three, four, five meters high, and they are everywhere — running in long, curving lines across the plateau like the streets of some organic metropolis. They are the architecture of the place. They form corridors and chambers, enclosed spaces that are open to the sky, vaulted passages where the stems have arched over and woven themselves together into living ceilings.

Between the walls: open stone. Open water. Bright light falling in shafts through gaps in the canopy above, illuminating the wet rock in patches of intense, almost white illumination that contrast sharply with the deep green shadow of the moss-walls. The air in these open spaces is cool and heavy with moisture — your clothes are damp within minutes, your skin beaded with condensation that is equal parts atmospheric and dripped from above.

Other plants fill the spaces between and within the moss-architecture. Ferns of enormous size — tree-fern analogs, though they are not trees — rise from cracks in the stone, their fronds spreading in great, fountain-like cascades of green that arch over the water-channels. Their trunks are thick with moisture, furred with smaller mosses and liverworts, and where the fern-trunks lean against the moss-walls, the two have begun to merge, the fern sending adventitious roots into the moss-wall and the moss sending upshoots along the fern-trunk, until it is impossible to tell where one plant ends and the other begins. Epiphytes coat every horizontal surface — hanging gardens of small, succulent-leaved plants that drip water from their overlapping foliage, creating a constant, localized rain beneath every ledge and overhang.

And then there are the *structures*.

---

They are scattered — sparse, like the vegetation itself, sparse like the fauna that is almost entirely absent. You will walk for twenty minutes through moss-corridors and fern-gardens and across bright, wet, open stone, and you will see nothing that was made by hands. Then you will turn a corner, pass through a gap in a wall of living green, and find yourself standing before it.

A machine.

Not a ruin. Not a relic. A *machine* — still operating, still performing whatever function it was built to perform, though that function is now deeply strange.

The first one you find is a pumping station. You can tell what it was because the housing is still legible — a long, low industrial shed with a peaked roof, built from corrugated metal and concrete, painted (once) in a color that might have been red but is now so thoroughly coated in living moss that it appears to be a structure *made* of moss, as if the moss-wall simply decided to become a building. The corrugated metal is still there beneath the growth, and when the wind shifts you can hear it — a deep, rhythmic clanking from inside the structure, the sound of a pump still pushing water through pipes that are still, somewhere, connected to something. The sound is enormous. It is one of the sources of the plateau's deafening voice — this great, slow, mechanical heartbeat, *klang-klang-klang*, felt in the chest more than heard, a sound that has been repeating for so long that the moss has grown *around* it, shaped itself to it, incorporated the vibration into its own growth patterns. The moss on the walls of the pumping station is thicker where the vibration is strongest, as if the plant has learned to harvest the energy of the machine's motion.

Water pours from the station's outflow pipes in a constant, heavy stream — not a dramatic jet but a thick, continuous flow that spills across the bare stone and immediately begins its slow process of finding a path downhill. The water is cold. The pipes are wrapped in moss and fern-roots so thickly that they look like living columns, and where the roots have found gaps in the metal they have entered the pipes themselves, so that the water flowing out is threaded with fine root-hairs that wave in the current like cilia.

There are other structures, scattered across the plateau at wide intervals. A generator station, its turbines still spinning, producing electricity that feeds into a network of cables that run along the ground — cables that have been swallowed by moss so completely that they appear as raised, linear ridges in the vegetation, like veins carrying not blood but current, a faint electrical hum running through the living walls that contain them. A water-treatment facility, its settling tanks now filled with a living soup of algae and aquatic plants, the water still flowing in and out through channels that the plants have widened and reshaped, so that the industrial process and the ecological process have merged into a single hybrid system that is doing — something. Purifying water, perhaps. Or simply moving it. Or both. Or neither. The system has outlived its original purpose and found a new one that no human language has a word for.

A watchtower, its metal ladder still climbable if you don't mind the moss that coats each rung in a thick, damp, spongy grip, leading up to a platform at the top where you can see the whole plateau spread out below you — and from here, the pattern becomes visible. The moss-walls are not random. They follow the water. They channel it. They form a vast, branching network of living conduits that direct the flow of water across the plateau in patterns that are partly natural and partly designed — the original engineers' channels now maintained and extended by the plants themselves, the moss growing preferentially along the water's edge, building itself up into walls that deepen and direct the flow. The entire plateau is a hydraulic system, half-built and half-grown, and from the watchtower you can see it: a dendritic pattern of water and green spreading across the bright wet stone like a circuit board, like a leaf's venation, like the drainage pattern of a river delta compressed into a single plateau, the water caught in the act of organizing itself into channels that will, in a thousand years, become streams, and in ten thousand, rivers, and in a hundred thousand, valleys cut deep into the young stone — but for now, *emerging*, still embryonic, still deciding, the water running in thin sheets and shallow rills that the moss-walls guide and shape with the patience of geology.

---

The light. I need to say more about the light.

At this elevation, under this frequently clouded sky, the light has a peculiar quality. When the clouds part — which they do, suddenly, with a speed that feels almost theatrical — the brightness is *staggering*. The wet stone flashes. The water in every channel and pool becomes a mirror throwing light upward. The moss-walls, damp-slicked, gleam with a deep, saturated green that seems to produce its own luminescence. The fern-fronds become translucent, backlit, their cellular structure visible as a fine filigree of veins. The condensation in the air catches the light and produces a constant, shifting display of small rainbows — not dramatic arcs but subtle, localized prismatic effects, glints of color at the edge of vision that vanish when you turn to look at them.

When the clouds close again, the light does not so much dim as *thicken* — it becomes diffuse, directionless, a cool grey-white brightness that seems to come from everywhere at once, and in this light the plateau becomes a different place: softer, quieter (though never actually quiet — the roar of water and machines never stops), the greens deeper and more mysterious, the shadows in the moss-corridors becoming caverns of dark, damp, textured richness where small details emerge — the fine structure of moss leaves, the slow drip of water from a fern-frond tip, the glint of a wet stone, the rare, startling sight of a small, pale, blind-eyed creature — something between a salamander and a slug — moving with infinite patience across the surface of a moss-wall, one of the few animals hardy enough to make a life in this loud, wet, bright, plant-dominated world.

---

Time moves slowly here.

The moss grows, but it grows *slowly* — you cannot see it, cannot perceive its motion. The water carves the stone, but so slowly that the stone's edges remain sharp. The machines rust, but the rust is incorporated into the moss's structure, becoming mineral nutrition, so that the machine's decay feeds the moss's growth in a cycle that nearly balances. The clouds come and go. The light shifts. The water runs. The machines clank. The moss grows. The stone, imperceptibly, begins to wear.

Nothing is dramatic. Nothing is sudden. Everything is patient, slow, continuous, and very, very loud.

You stand on the bright wet stone of a high plateau, surrounded by walls of living green, and you listen to the sound of a world that is building itself — water, plant, stone, and machine, all working together in a process so slow and so vast that it makes your own life feel like a single breath, drawn and released in the brief, bright, roaring space between one moment and the next.
