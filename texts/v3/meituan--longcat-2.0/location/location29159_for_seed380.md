# The Underworks

You descend.

Not far — perhaps the height of a cathedral laid on its side — but the quality of air changes immediately. It thickens. The temperature drops four degrees in a single step, and your skin registers the shift before your mind does: this is a place that has never known sun.

---

The ceiling is low. Not oppressively so — you can stand, most of you — but low enough that you are always aware of the stone above you, close enough to touch. It is weeping. Not dramatically, not in streams, but in a fine, perpetual exhalation of moisture that beads on every surface and runs in slow, branching threads toward the floor. The stone is a dark limestone, pocked and rounded, and the water has been working on it for a very long time. The shapes it has made are smooth, organic — long tongues of flowstone, shallow basins carved into the floor, channels that direct the water with what looks almost like intention. The erosion here is old. Settled. The water and the stone have reached their agreement, and what you see is the architecture of that compromise: curves instead of edges, hollows instead of fractures, everything sanded down to its most patient form.

The floor is a series of broad, shallow steps — not natural, not entirely. They have been cut, once, by tools, but the water has been at them since, and the corners are gone now, and the surfaces are glazed with a film of mineral deposit that catches what little light there is and throws it back as a dull, greenish sheen. You walk carefully. The steps are slick. They have been slick for longer than anyone alive.

---

This is a town. Was a town. Is a town still, depending on what you think a town requires.

The structures rise from the floor in clusters of three and four — low buildings with thick walls, built from the same limestone as the ceiling, as if they grew from it, or sank into it, or the distinction stopped mattering at some point. Their roofs are flat, and water pools on them in shallow films, and the walls weep as the ceiling does, and everything is damp, everything glistens. Between the buildings, narrow lanes run in patterns that suggest planning — once. Now the lanes have softened. Their edges blur. Flowstone reaches out from the walls like slow hands, and in places the buildings and the cave have merged so completely that you cannot tell where construction ends and geology begins. A doorway is half-blocked by a column of deposited minerals, cream-white and ridged like a stack of plates. A window has become a basin, and the basin has become a pool, and the pool has become a habitat.

The power is still on. This is the first strange thing you notice, though you will notice stranger.

Thin cables run along the ceiling in loose loops, and from them, at irregular intervals, hang bulbs — not glass, some polymer that has yellowed and gone cloudy with age. They emit a light that is less illumination than suggestion: a dim, amber murmur that pushes the darkness back to perhaps three meters and no further. Most of them still work. A few flicker, and the flicker is not electrical — it is something moving in front of them, something small and quick, a beetle or a millipede navigating the cable by touch, its legs finding purchase on the polymer sheath, and as it passes, the light swells and dims, swells and dims, like breathing.

---

The machinery is the bones of the place.

It sits in the open, in the lanes, in the buildings, in the spaces between — great iron frames rusted to the color of old blood, gear assemblies the size of dinner tables, flywheels that are still, pumps that are not. The pumps are the reason the water behaves. They sit in low housings at the edges of the space, four of them, and they run. They run at a speed that is barely perceptible — you can hear them more than see them, a low, sub-audible hum that you feel in your sternum before your ears, a vibration that lives just below the threshold of sound. They move water from one part of the underworks to another, cycling it through channels in the floor, up through pipes in the walls, and back again. The system is closed. The water has been cycling for — no one knows. Long enough that it has developed its own ecology.

The gears are stationary but not still. Things live in them. The gaps between the teeth are wide enough for small bodies, and small bodies have found them. You can see, in the dim amber light, the glint of eyes — dozens of them, low to the surface, watching. Crustaceans, pale and translucent, no longer than your little finger. They have colonized the machinery entirely. They move through the gear trains like water through a streambed, finding the paths of least resistance, the channels and hollows, the places where moisture collects. They are the color of the stone, or the color of the water, or the color of nothing at all — a pale, almost luminous white that makes them visible in the dark the way dust is visible in a shaft of light. There are thousands. Tens of thousands. They are the dominant life here, and they have arranged themselves around the machinery the way coral arranges itself around a reef — not on it, but *with* it, so that the gears and the creatures have become a single system, a living mechanism.

The millipedes move along the cables. The beetles — large, slow, armored — navigate the floor in deliberate paths, wearing trails in the mineral deposit, and the trails branch and intersect and form a network that mirrors the lane system above, as if the beetles have built their own town on the town's floor, a smaller, slower, more patient version. And in the pools — the window-basins, the door-depressions, the places where the pumps deliver water and the water has nowhere to go — there are things that are not quite fish and not quite worms, pale ribbon-bodies that undulate in the shallow water, feeding on the mineral film, on the algae that grows in the amber light, on the dead.

---

Sparse things grow. A moss, dark green and fine as hair, clings to the north-facing walls in patches no larger than a hand. It grows slowly — so slowly that you could watch it for an hour and see nothing, and for a year and see almost nothing — but it is alive, and it is the only green in the place, and it matters more than its size suggests. Lichen crusts the higher surfaces in pale plates, and in the wettest corners, where the pumps deliver a constant trickle, a fungus has established itself: small, pale caps on thin stems, clusters of them, and they glow. Faintly. A biological phosphorescence that is not bright enough to read by but is bright enough to navigate by, and the creatures navigate by it, and you navigate by it, and it is the only light in the place that was not put there by hands.

The air smells of stone and water and iron and something else — something organic and deep, like the inside of a root cellar, like the breath of a place that has been closed for a long time but is not dead. The humidity is near total. Your clothes are damp within minutes. Your skin is damp. The water is on you, around you, above you, below you. It drips. It runs. It condenses on the bulbs and makes them haloed. It pools in the hollows of your collarbones if you stand still.

---

It is mostly quiet.

The pumps hum below hearing. The water drips at a rate of perhaps one drop every four seconds, and the drops land in different places — stone, metal, water, flesh — and each place has its own note, and the notes arrive in a slow, irregular rhythm that is not quite random and not quite patterned, and after a while you stop hearing it as sound and start hearing it as texture, the way you hear your own heartbeat when you are lying very still in a very quiet room.

The beetles click. Not loudly. A small, dry sound, like a fingernail on wood, and it comes from everywhere and nowhere, and you cannot locate the source, and this is the second strange thing: the sound is *distributed*. It moves. It is not one beetle but many, and they are not communicating, not coordinating, but the effect is the same as if they were — a sound that walks through the underworks like a presence, pausing here, arriving there, never still.

And then there is the other sound. The third strange thing.

At intervals — long intervals, minutes apart — there is a deep, resonant tone that comes from below the floor, below the pumps, below the stone. It is low. It is felt more than heard. It lasts for perhaps three seconds and then stops, and in the silence after it, the dripping seems louder, and the beetles seem louder, and everything seems more itself, as if the tone has reminded the place of what it is.

No one knows what causes it. The pumps are not the right frequency. The water is not the right medium. The stone is not the right shape. But it happens, and it has been happening, and it will go on happening, and the crustaceans do not react to it, and the beetles do not react to it, and this means either that they are accustomed to it or that it is not a sound to them — that it is something else entirely, something that travels through the earth the way a thought travels through a mind, and what you hear is only the part of it that happens to intersect with air.

---

The place is small. You can walk its length in perhaps ten minutes, its width in four. It is bounded on all sides by stone, and the stone is wet, and the water comes from the stone, and the stone is the water's home as much as the town is yours. It is a room. A grove. A hamlet. A single feature area. It has edges, and the edges are soft, and beyond them the cave continues, but no one goes there, or no one goes there and returns, or the distinction is irrelevant because there is nothing beyond the edges that is not already here.

The light does not reach the walls. The amber bulbs pool their glow in the center of the space, and at the margins, the darkness is complete, and the darkness is wet, and things move in it — you can hear them, the small clicks and rustles of bodies navigating stone — but you cannot see them, and this is good. This is as it should be. The dark has its own life, and it is not yours.

---

Time moves slowly here. This is the last thing you notice, and it is the thing that stays with you after you leave.

The place is not static. It is changing. The water is still working on the stone, the stone is still settling into new shapes, the crustaceans are still moving through the gears, the moss is still growing at its imperceptible pace, the fungus is still glowing, the pumps are still pumping, the tone is still sounding from somewhere below. But all of this is happening at a rate that is — generous. Patient. The place is not waiting for anything. It is not in transition. It is not becoming something else. It has arrived at what it is, and what it is will endure, and the endurance is not a victory or a defeat but simply the state of things, the way water is wet and stone is hard and the dark is dark, and the beetles click their slow clicks, and the water drips its slow drips, and the pumps hum their slow hum, and the tone sounds, and the silence after the tone is full of everything that lives here, everything that has found its niche in the wet and the dim and the cool and the quiet, and the place is complete, and the place is strange, and the place is at peace.
