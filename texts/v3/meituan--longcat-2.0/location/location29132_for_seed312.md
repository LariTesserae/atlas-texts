# The High Plateau of Vethis

The plateau is so vast that it generates its own weather.

You feel the elevation before you see it — in the thinness of breath, the way sound carries differently, the particular quality of cold that has nothing to do with wind and everything to do with the simple absence of the atmosphere's lower warmth. The air here is thin but heavy with moisture, a paradox that defines everything. It is cold enough that your exhaled breath hangs in clouds, yet damp enough that the moisture never quite falls as rain. Instead it condenses — on stone, on fur, on the walls of the cities — a perpetual sheen of wetness that makes everything glisten under the diffuse, even light.

The light itself is strange for such altitude. At this elevation, you would expect harsh, unfiltered sun, the kind that burns and blinds. But the plateau's own atmosphere — thick with fog, with the exhalations of its cities, with the moisture that rises from ten thousand seeps and streams — diffuses everything into a soft, directionless glow. Shadows are gentle. The sky is a pale, luminous grey-white, like the inside of a shell. You can look at the horizon without squinting. The light reveals everything without drama.

And what it reveals is stone. Young stone.

The plateau is geologically fresh — recently uplifted, recently exposed, its features still sharp in a way that older landscapes have forgotten. The peaks are jagged, angular, their edges not yet softened by the patient work of erosion. The valleys are steep-sided and narrow, carved by water that is still learning its paths. The rock faces are clean, almost raw, showing their strata like open pages — bands of dark basalt, pale granite, rust-red iron deposits, all laid bare. There is very little soil. What exists is thin, mineral, scraped into crevices and hollows by wind and the occasional flow of water. The stone dominates. It is the substrate, the building material, the foundation of every living thing's existence.

The dampness finds the stone everywhere. Water seeps from a thousand fissures, not in streams but in slow, persistent weeping. The rock faces glisten. Moss and lichen — the plateau's primary vegetation — cling to every surface that holds moisture, creating a patchwork of dark green, grey-green, orange, and black across the otherwise bare stone. The vegetation is sparse in the traditional sense — there are no trees, no grasses, no flowering plants to speak of — but in another sense it is everywhere, a living skin over the rock, visible only when you look closely. Lichens spread in slow, fractal patterns across boulders. Moss fills every crack and hollow, swelling with retained water, soft and dark and cold to the touch. In the lowest valleys, where water collects, there are thick cushions of moss that you can sink your hand into, and occasionally a hardy sedge or rush pushing up through the wet.

The dampness creates corridors of richer life across the plateau. Streams — narrow, cold, clear — thread through the valleys, fed by condensation and the occasional rain. Along these waterways, the vegetation thickens. Mosses grow taller, darker. Small invertebrates — hardy, cold-adapted — move through the wet layers. Birds gather here, the plateau's primary fauna, nesting in the cliffs above the streams and feeding on the insects that hatch in the damp. The streams are the plateau's arteries, and along them, life concentrates.

But the fauna is not limited to the streams. The plateau supports vast herds of grazing animals — large, shaggy, broad-hooved creatures that move across the stone in dense, slow-moving groups. They are lichen-eaters, primarily, their digestive systems adapted to extract nutrition from the thin carpet of vegetation that covers the rock. They move constantly, covering enormous distances across the plateau, their hooves clicking on the stone in a sound like distant, irregular rain. They are well-adapted to the cold — thick coats, layers of fat, a metabolism tuned to efficiency. They move slowly, conserving energy, their breath rising in clouds.

The herds are dense. Where one walks, a hundred follow. They fill the wider valleys, spread across the lower slopes, their dark forms visible against the pale stone from great distances. They are the plateau's most visible life, its most audible presence — the lowing of the herd carries across the stone, a deep, resonant sound that echoes off the cliff faces and fills the valleys with a low, continuous hum.

Predators follow the herds. Large, solitary cats — or something like cats — that move through the rocks with a fluid, patient grace. They are rarely seen, but their signs are everywhere: the remains of kills, the scratch-marks on stone, the occasional glimpse of a shape moving along a ridge, low and purposeful. Smaller predators — fox-like, weasel-like — hunt the birds and rodents that cluster around the streams and the cities.

And the cities. The cities are everywhere.

They cling to the mountainsides, fill the wider valleys, cluster around the streams and seeps. They are built from the plateau's own stone — the same dark basalt, the same pale granite — and from a distance they are nearly invisible, blending into the rock face so completely that you can stare at a mountainside for minutes before realizing that what you took for natural stone is actually a city, its walls and towers and terraces carved from and built onto the living rock.

The cities are dense. This is the first thing that strikes you up close — the compression, the layering, the way every inch of space is used. Buildings share walls. Rooftops are streets. Stairs — always stairs, carved into the stone, worn smooth by generations of feet — connect level after level, rising from the valley floor to the cliff top in a continuous, winding ascent. The passages between buildings are narrow, sometimes so narrow that you must turn sideways to pass. The ceilings are low. The walls are thick stone, cold to the touch, perpetually damp.

The construction is primitive but sophisticated. The people of the plateau work stone with stone — hammerstones, chisels, abrasives — and with simple copper tools, the metal cold-hammered and edge-ground. There is no mortar. The stones are fitted together with a precision that comes from generations of accumulated knowledge, each piece shaped to its place, the joints so tight that you cannot slide a blade between them. The walls lean slightly inward, a technique that distributes weight and resists the plateau's occasional tremors. Doorways are low and narrow, retaining heat. Windows are small, fitted with thin sheets of mica or alabaster that admit the diffuse light while keeping out the cold.

The cities grow slowly. A new room is added when a family needs one — a matter of months of careful stone-work, the blocks quarried from the cliff face below the city, hauled up by hand and rope, fitted into place with the same precision as the original construction. The cities are layered with time. The oldest sections, at the base, are worn smooth, the stone dark with age and moisture. The newest sections, at the top, are still sharp-edged, their fresh-cut surfaces pale and clean. You can read a city's history in its stone — the dark, smooth base giving way to progressively fresher, sharper construction as you climb.

The infrastructure is dense but low-tech. Water is collected from condensation — stone channels carved into the cliff faces, directing the slow seep of moisture into cisterns and basins. The cisterns are everywhere, built into the walls, the floors, the very structure of the cities. Water drips constantly — from the channels, from the ceilings, from the walls themselves. The sound of dripping water is the cities' constant accompaniment, a irregular percussion that echoes through the narrow passages. In the mornings, when the temperature drops and condensation peaks, the dripping intensifies to a steady patter, and the channels run with a thin, clear flow.

Heat comes from fire — small, efficient fires burning dried animal dung and the sparse woody shrubs that grow in the deepest valleys. The fires are built in stone-lined pits in the center of rooms, the smoke rising through chimneys carved into the walls. The smoke is thick, acrid, and it stains the stone black over time. The cities are connected by roads — narrow, stone-paved paths that wind along the valley floors and climb the passes between peaks. The roads are ancient, worn into grooves by the passage of countless feet and hooves. They are the plateau's circulatory system, carrying trade, news, and the slow migration of peoples between cities. The distances are vast. A journey between two cities might take days or weeks, depending on the terrain and the weather. Travelers move in small groups, carrying their goods on their backs or on the backs of pack animals — smaller, sure-footed relatives of the herd animals, adapted to the steep, narrow paths.

The plateau's scale is difficult to comprehend. It stretches to the horizon in every direction — a vast, high, cold expanse of stone and fog and slow-moving life. The cities are scattered across it like stars in a dim sky, each one a dense cluster of human presence in an otherwise empty landscape. Between them, the plateau is wild — the herds moving across the stone, the predators following, the birds circling above the cliffs, the fog rolling through the valleys in slow, silent waves.

The fog is a constant presence. It forms in the evenings as the temperature drops and the moisture in the air condenses, filling the valleys with a dense, cold mist that reduces visibility to a few body-lengths. The fog muffles sound, softens edges, transforms the landscape into a world of grey and white and dark stone. In the mornings, the fog lifts slowly, retreating up the valley walls in thin, wispy strands, revealing the plateau piece by piece — first the highest peaks, then the ridges, then the valleys, then the cities, emerging from the mist like something being born.

The quiet is the plateau's most pervasive quality. The cold air, the sparse vegetation, the stone construction — all absorb and muffle sound. The cities are quiet places. Footsteps on stone, the drip of water, the distant lowing of a herd, the call of a bird — these are the sounds that define the soundscape, each one distinct and clear in the general hush. Voices carry poorly in the narrow passages, absorbed by the damp stone. The plateau's people speak quietly, a habit born of environment. Loud sounds are rare, startling, quickly swallowed by the stone.

The slow dynamic of the plateau permeates everything. The cities grow by the addition of a single room in a year. The herds migrate on seasonal cycles that take them across the plateau over the course of months. The fog rolls in and lifts on a daily rhythm that has not changed in living memory. The stone itself is still rising — the plateau is still being pushed upward by the tectonic forces that created it, though so slowly that it is imperceptible in a human lifetime. The people measure their lives against these slow rhythms. They build for generations they will not see. They herd animals whose migration routes were established before their grandparents' grandparents were born. They collect water from channels that have been dripping for centuries.

The plateau endures. It is a place of patience, of accumulation, of the slow, persistent work of water on stone and stone on stone and life on life. The cities grow. The herds move. The fog rolls in and lifts. The water drips. The lichens spread their slow, fractal patterns across the bare rock. And the plateau, vast and cold and damp and sharp-edged and ancient and young all at once, continues its long, slow rise into the thin, bright air.
