# The Lungs of the Green Continent

---

It begins with the wet.

Not rain. Not standing water. Not any single thing you could name and point to. The wet is a condition of the air itself — a warm, enveloping saturation that makes every surface glisten, that beads on your skin the moment you arrive, that fogs your breath even though the temperature would not seem to warrant it. The air is approximately ninety-seven percent water vapor by volume, and the remaining three percent is oxygen, nitrogen, and a faint sharpness that might be ozone or might be something the plants are exhaling. You cannot tell. The distinction has ceased to matter here.

Beneath your feet — if you are standing, if there is a "you" and a "standing" — the ground is not ground. It is a mat. A woven, layered, compressed mat of living and semi-living plant tissue, somewhere between two and eleven meters thick, and it extends in every direction to the limit of vision and then past that, past the curvature of the horizon, past the haze, past the haze beyond the haze. The mat is warm to the touch. Not hot. Warm the way a body is warm. You press your hand into it and it yields slightly, then resists, and if you hold your hand there long enough you can feel something that is almost — almost — a pulse.

But nothing is pulsing. Nothing moves. That is the first rule of this place, and it is absolute.

---

The darkness is not total. It is a darkness like the inside of a greenhouse at midnight — not the absence of light but the presence of something that light has happened to. There is a very faint luminescence that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere: a deep green so dark it is almost black, a bioluminescence generated by the mat itself, or by the upper canopy, or by some chemical process in the constant condensation that sheets and beads across every surface. The light is sufficient to see by, after a fashion. You can see shapes. You can see depth. You can see the architecture.

The architecture.

This is where you begin to understand that the word "landscape" is not quite right, and the word "garden" is catastrophically wrong, and the word "forest" is almost offensive in its inadequacy. What stands around you — what rises from the mat in every direction, what fills the space from ankle-height to a ceiling somewhere in the murk above — is a living industrial infrastructure. There are columns. There are columns the width of redwoods and the height of cathedral spires, and they are not trees. They are hollow. They are ribbed internally with spiraling channels that you can see where a column has sheared cleanly — and the sheared edges are sharp, almost machined, the tissue pale and dense and smelling faintly of copper — and through those channels, fluids are moving. Not quickly. Not slowly. At a rate. At a precise and unvarying rate that has not changed in any measurable period and will not change in any measurable period to come.

The fluids are the wet. The wet is the blood of the system. It rises through the columns by some mechanism that is not quite capillary action and not quite pumping and not quite anything in the standard taxonomy of biological fluid transport — it moves because the columns are structured in such a way that movement is the path of least resistance, and the path of least resistance has been optimized over — how long? There is no record. The columns are young. Geologically, biologically, in every sense that matters, they are young. They have the freshness of something that emerged fully formed, that did not evolve so much as *arrive*. The sheared edge of the column is white and damp and there is no scar tissue. It has not begun to heal because there is nothing to heal from. The damage is not an injury. It is simply a new fact about the shape of the column, and the column accepts it the way a river accepts a boulder.

---

You walk. The mat compresses and does not rebound. Your footprint remains — a shallow depression that fills immediately with condensation, so that the mat records your passage in a chain of small, still pools that will persist until the water finds its way back into the vascular system, which it will, at the same rate at which everything else happens here.

Around the columns there is understory, but that word implies a hierarchy that does not exist. There is no overstory and understory. There is only *system*. Flat, overlapping plates of photosynthetic — no. Not photosynthetic. There is no sun. Chemosynthetic? Electrosynthetic? The plates are dark and broad and they grow flush against the columns and against each other, and they are threaded with filaments that carry — current. Electrical current. You can feel it if you press your tongue to the surface: a faint, flat, sour tingle, like a nine-volt battery but deeper, more sustained, as if the voltage is not a stored charge but a flow, a current being generated and consumed in the same instant, the same molecule, the same metabolic breath.

The plates are warm. They are all warm. The warmth is not uniform — there is a gradient of perhaps four degrees between the surface of the mat and the upper reaches of the columns, where the air is fractionally cooler — but the gradient is fixed. It has the quality of something that was established at the beginning and has not deviated since. There is no weather here in the way that word is usually understood. There are no fronts, no pressure systems, no convection cells. The air moves, but it moves the way fluid moves through a pipe: laminar, predictable, in paths that were determined by the architecture and that have not shifted and will not shift. The warm air rises along the columns and the cool air descends between them and this exchange has been continuous and unvarying for as long as the system has existed, which is — long enough for the mat to reach continental scale, but not long enough for a single column to show wear.

---

Scale.

This is the thing that takes the longest to comprehend, because it is the thing that the body rejects first. Your eyes tell you that you are in a grove. A stand of large organisms in a damp lowland. Your eyes are lying, or rather, your eyes are telling you a truth that is six orders of magnitude too small. The columns you see are not the largest structures in this place. They are the *smallest visible* structures. Above you, hidden in the permanent haze, there are forms so large that they do not register as forms — they register as *conditions*. A curvature in the haze that might be a wall. A darkening of the darkness that might be a surface the size of a mountain range, covered in the same plates and filaments and vascular channels, operating on the same principles but at a scale where the word "organism" begins to feel inadequate and the word "geology" begins to feel more appropriate. The continent is not covered by the system. The continent *is* the system. The mat is the crust. The columns are — protrusions. Outcroppings. The visible expression of something that extends down into the planetary substrate and up into the lower atmosphere and laterally in every direction to the edges of the tectonic plate, which is the only boundary the system recognizes.

The haze is not weather. It is exhalation. The system breathes — not in and out, but in a continuous, balanced exchange: drawing in atmospheric gases at the margins, cycling them through processes that are industrial in their precision and biological in their medium, and exhaling warmth and moisture and that faint ozone sharpness at the center. The haze is permanent. It has a structure — layers, densities, regions of slightly different opacity that you can learn to read the way a sailor reads clouds, except that these formations do not move, do not build, do not dissipate. They are fixed features of the sky, as permanent as the columns, as permanent as the mat. There is a river of slightly clearer air that runs from north-northeast to south-southwest, about two hundred meters up, and it has run that path since the haze formed. It will run that path until the haze does not, which is to say: it will not.

---

The silence.

It is not total. There is sound here, but it is sound at the threshold of perception — sound as a physical phenomenon rather than an auditory experience. If you press your ear to a column you can hear the fluid moving inside it: a deep, continuous, subsonic hum that you feel in your mastoid bone more than you hear with your cochlea. The frequency is approximately seven hertz. It does not vary. It is the same in every column, at every point along every column, and it has been the same for — again, the system is young, but it has been the same for the entirety of its existence, which is the only timeframe that matters in a place where nothing changes.

The condensation makes sound, but so faintly and so uniformly that the brain edits it out. A droplet forms at the tip of a plate. It grows. It falls — a distance of perhaps three centimeters — and strikes the mat, and the impact produces a pressure wave that your ear interprets, if you are paying very close attention, as a single soft *tik*. But the droplets are falling everywhere, at every moment, at the same rate, from the same height, and their individual *tiks* merge into a sound that is not a sound but a texture: a continuous, high-frequency, nearly uniform hiss that is the acoustic equivalent of the bioluminescent haze. It is the sound of a system in equilibrium. It is the sound of a machine that is running perfectly. It is the sound of a living thing that is not alive in any way that implies change, growth, decay, or death.

---

You find — after walking for what might be hours or might be days, because the light does not change and your circadian rhythm is the only clock and your circadian rhythm is unreliable in the warm dark — you find a place where the mat has been opened. Not torn. Not eroded. *Opened*. A clean, rectangular section, perhaps two meters by three, has been lifted — or has grown in such a way that it can be lifted — and beneath it is a chamber. The chamber is ribbed with the same spiraling channels you saw in the sheared column, but here they are exposed, accessible, and you can see that they are not simple tubes. They are structures within structures: concentric layers of tissue, each with a different opacity, each with a different warmth, each carrying a different fluid. The fluids are colorless, or nearly so — one has a faint amber tint, one is perfectly clear, one is so dark it seems to absorb the bioluminescent light. They move at different rates through their respective layers, and where the layers are adjacent, there is a membrane, and across the membrane, something is being exchanged. Heat. Charge. Molecules. The chamber is a reaction vessel. It is a factory floor. It is a metabolism scaled up to the size of a room, and it is not built — it grew, it emerged, it *is*, in the same way that a liver is, in the same way that a root nodule is, except that it is made of plant tissue and it is performing chemistry that no plant has any reason to perform, and it has been performing it at the same rate, with the same inputs, producing the same outputs, for the entire duration of its existence, which has been sufficient for it to reach the size of a room but not sufficient for it to show any sign of wear, or use, or the faintest departure from its original specifications.

You could reach in. You could touch the membrane. You could take a sample of the amber fluid or the dark fluid or the clear fluid. You could do this. The system would not react. The system does not react. That is the second rule.

---

The youngness of the place is perhaps the most unsettling thing about it, more unsettling than the scale, more unsettling than the silence, more unsettling than the vast industrial metabolism running in perfect equilibrium in the warm dark. Because youngness implies a beginning. And a beginning implies a before. And a before implies a change — the biggest change of all, the change from not-existing to existing, from nothing to this — and the system is so profoundly, so deeply, so completely *unchanging* that the idea of its beginning is almost a logical contradiction. It is a place that must have been born but shows no evidence of birth. No growth rings. No developmental stages. No succession. No pioneer species giving way to climax community. It arrived at completeness. It emerged at scale. One moment there was not a continent-spanning industrial metabolism made of plant tissue operating in warm darkness at the bottom of a waterlogged atmosphere, and the next moment there was, and it has been running perfectly ever since, and it will run perfectly forever, or at least until the sun goes out, and possibly not even then, because the system does not appear to need the sun.

---

There are no animals. There are no insects in the mat, no worms in the tissue, no birds in the columns, no fish in the amber fluid. There are no decomposers because there is nothing to decompose. There are no predators because there is no prey. There is no competition because there is no scarcity. There is no sex because there is no reproduction — the system does not reproduce. It does not grow. It does not expand. It simply *is*, at the scale it is, in the configuration it is, running the processes it runs, producing the warmth and the wet and the seven-hertz hum and the *tik-tik-tik* of condensation and the faint green-black light, and it has been doing this since it began and it will do this until it ends, and there is no evidence that it will end, and there is no evidence that it began, and the only evidence that it is not eternal is the sharpness of its edges, the freshness of its tissue, the clean fact of its youth, which is the one thing about it that suggests it is subject to time at all.

---

You are alone. You are the only animal in a system that has no place for animals, in a world that is not a world but a single, vast, perfectly balanced process, in a darkness that is not empty but full — full of warmth and wet and the smell of copper and the taste of ozone and the sound of seven hertz and the feeling, always, beneath your feet, of standing on something that is alive and aware and utterly, profoundly indifferent to your presence.

You are not the first to have been here. There is no sign that anyone has been here. The mat holds your footprints and your footprints fill with water and the water will return to the system and your footprints will close — not heal, *close*, the way a system closes around a perturbation, absorbing it, integrating it, returning to equilibrium — and there will be no record of your passage, no mark, no scar, no story.

The system hums.

The condensation falls.

The warm wet darkness holds everything, and changes nothing, and has always been this way, and will always be this way, and is — for now, for the only moment that matters, for the eternal present tense of a world that has never known a past or imagined a future — *complete*.
