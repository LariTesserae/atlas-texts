# The Hollows of Yr

---

You come down into it through a crack that wasn't there yesterday.

The ground simply opened — a seam maybe two meters wide, running straight for forty meters before turning at an angle that geometry cannot justify, then terminating in a rough amphitheater where the earth has peeled back like the rind of some impossible fruit. Below: a space. Not a cave. Not a chamber. Something more deliberate than either, though nothing here was deliberate.

The air that rises from the opening is cool and entirely dry. Not the dryness of heat — there is no heat here, has never been — but a fundamental absence. Your throat notices first. Then your eyes. The moisture leaves them within minutes, and you learn to blink more, to carry your own wetness with you like a small animal carries its shell.

---

## The Substrate

What you stand on — what everything stands on — is a pale mineral, somewhere between chalk and fused salt, though it is neither. It is cool to the touch even when the rare sun finds the crack above and pours light down in a dusty column. The surface is ridged in fine concentric patterns, like the growth rings of something, except that the rings radiate from multiple centers and intersect and overlap, and where they meet the ridges sharpen to edges that will cut a careless hand.

This is the skin of the Hollows. Below it: more of the same, compressed, layered, going down farther than anyone has tested. The substance is young. You can tell because nothing has weathered it. Every ridge is crisp. Every edge is sharp. The faces of every formation rise at angles that erosion has not yet softened. This place has not existed long enough to be worn.

How long? Long enough for what grows here to grow. Not long enough for anything to die of old age. That is all anyone knows.

---

## The Structures

They rise from the substrate in clusters.

You will call them towers, though they are not towers. You will call them walls, though they are not walls. You will use these words because you have no others, and because the structures present themselves with an arrangement that reads, against all evidence, as intended. They are close together in some places — close enough that you must turn sideways to pass between them — and absent in others, leaving broad floors of the pale mineral open to the narrow sky above. The spacing is irregular. The spacing is not random. It is the spacing of things that grew where conditions permitted and did not grow where conditions did not.

The tallest rise perhaps fifteen meters. They are tapered, ridged, hollow. Their walls — and they do have walls, with interior spaces and apertures — are thin enough to transmit the dim light, which gives them a quality of being lit from within even though the light comes from outside and passes through. The material is the same pale mineral as the substrate but denser, with a faint translucence at the thinnest points, like bone held up to a candle.

Some of the structures are layered. A large formation will have a core of smaller formations, and those in turn will have grown from a base that is itself a cluster of still smaller forms, so that the whole thing reads as a neighborhood — a block — a quarter of something that might, if you squint and abandon rigor, be called a city.

There are no tools here. There have never been tools here. The structures are accretions. Deposits. They grew from solution, from vapor, from some mineral-rich exhalation that rose through fissures in the substrate and crystallized on contact with the cool dry air. Layer on layer, year on year, the formations built themselves. The regularity of their growth — the fact that some of them grew at similar rates, in similar directions, producing the uncanny impression of streets and alleys and plazas — is a property of the underlying chemistry, which is not uniform but which is also not random. The substrate breathes. It exhales mineral-rich air in pulses. The pulses follow patterns that correspond to nothing anyone has been able to measure: not temperature, not pressure, not the movements of any body in the sky above.

---

## The Silence

There is no wind in the Hollows. The air moves, but it moves slowly, without friction against itself, and it makes no sound. Your footsteps make no sound. Your breathing is shockingly loud by comparison — a rasp and a hush that seems indecent in this context, like a shout in a cathedral.

The structures themselves are silent. They do not creak. They do not groan. When they grow — and they are growing, you can see it if you stay long enough, a faint white line of new mineral advancing along an edge like a very slow frost — they do not make the smallest sound. The substrate does not crack or settle audibly. Even when a formation collapses — which happens, rarely, a thin wall buckling inward with what should be a crash but is instead a soft displacement of air, a puff of pale dust, and then stillness — the silence simply reasserts itself, as if the sound were absorbed by the mineral itself, taken in, made part of the structure of the place.

---

## The Light

It is always dim. The crack above is narrow, and the sun, even at its zenith, sends down only a diffuse glow that illuminates nothing directly. The translucence of the structures helps — they catch what light there is and distribute it, faintly, through their walls, so that the spaces between them are never fully dark. It is like being underwater in clear, sunlit water at a depth where the blue has begun to thicken but has not yet become black.

At night the darkness is more complete but not absolute. The structures retain something — a faint luminescence, mineral phosphorescence, the slow release of energy absorbed during the day — so that they glow, barely, like the dial of a watch in a dark room. You can see the shapes of them. You cannot see their edges. The ridges disappear. The sharp features become suggestions.

---

## The Sparse Life

There are organisms here. Not many.

The walls of the structures are stained in places with something that is not mineral — a faint discoloration, pale green-grey, that follows the ridges and collects in the crevices where moisture might condense if there were moisture to condense. It is a living film. It metabolizes the mineral surface itself, slowly, excreting something that becomes part of the structure, so that the growth of the formations and the growth of the film are entangled — neither quite primary, neither quite symbiotic, neither quite parasitic. The film has no name. It has no cells in the sense that your cells are cells. It is organized, but its organization is not the organization of biology as you know it. It is something else. Something that works here.

On the open floors of the substrate, where the pale mineral is exposed and unshadowed, there are tufts of something that resembles vegetation only in that it is vertical, rooted, and photosynthetic. They are not plants. They are mineral structures that have incorporated the film into their architecture, so that they stand up — thin, brittle, branching — like sculptures of ferns made from the same pale substance as everything else, but with a faint green-grey tint and a quality of flexibility that the larger formations lack. They sway, sometimes, when the air moves. They make no sound. They grow perhaps a centimeter in a year. Some of them are as tall as your knee. Some are no thicker than a thread. They are sparse — you can walk for minutes between them — but they are not absent. The Hollows are not sterile.

---

## The Rare Fauna

Once, a visitor saw something move in the deep space between two large formations. It was low to the surface. It was fast. It was the color of the substrate — pale, ridged — and it moved with a fluidity that no mineral structure possesses, a rippling locomotion that suggested many small contact points with the ground, lifting and placing and lifting again. It was perhaps thirty centimeters long. It did not have a head, or if it did, the head was indistinguishable from the rest of it. It vanished into an aperture in the base of a formation and did not emerge.

No one has seen it since. No one has seen anything like it. But the tufts of mineral-vegetation are sometimes found disturbed — pressed flat, or broken, or arranged in small piles — in ways that suggest something moves through the Hollows in the dark hours, something that interacts with the structures but does not consume them in any way that can be measured.

---

## The Strangeness

Here is what is strange, and you should not skip this part:

The structures grow. You accept this. It is unusual but not impossible — mineral accretions are known elsewhere in the world, though not like this, not with this organization, not with this pace.

But the structures also *change*.

Not only growth. Change. A formation that had three apertures on your first visit has four on your second, and the fourth is not new — it is weathered, ridged, as old as the others, as if it had always been there. A passage between two walls that was narrow enough to force you sideways is, on your return, wide enough to walk through abreast, and the walls show no sign of having been widened — the surface is continuous, unbroken, as if the passage had always been that wide. The concentric rings on the substrate shift. Not dramatically. Not quickly. But they shift, the centers migrating by centimeters, the patterns reorganizing themselves in ways that are subtle and pervasive and impossible.

The Hollows are young. The Hollows are changing. The Hollows are, in some sense that no one has been able to articulate, *becoming*.

What they are becoming is not clear. The direction of the change — if it has a direction — is toward greater complexity, greater organization, greater resemblance to something that was planned. The structures are more regular now than they were a decade ago. The spacing is more suggestive. The layering is more pronounced. The apertures are more numerous and more uniform in size.

Nothing here has tools. Nothing here has intention. Nothing here has biology in any recognizable sense.

And yet.

---

## The Rules

Stay close to the crack. The Hollows are not large — you can cross the visible extent in twenty minutes at a careful walk — but the formations create a maze of deep shadows and similar-looking corridors, and it is possible to become turned around. The silence makes it worse. Without echoes, without the sound of your own footsteps bouncing back, you cannot gauge distances. The formations all look alike. The dim light flattens depth. You walk what you believe is a straight line and find yourself back where you started.

Do not touch the walls more than necessary. The edges are sharp, and the mineral dust that flakes from them when disturbed hangs in the air for a long time in the still atmosphere, and when it settles on your skin it draws moisture from it with a faint, persistent thirst.

Do not stay after dark. Not because of danger — nothing here is aggressive, nothing here is predatory in any way that has been observed — but because the change is more visible in the phosphorescent glow of night, and there is something in watching the structures shift that unsettles the mind in a way that is difficult to recover from. People who have spent a night in the Hollows report a feeling afterward that the place is watching them. This is not true. The place is not sentient. The place is not aware.

But it is not inert, either.

---

You climb back up through the crack. The seam has narrowed by a finger's width since you descended. It is closing, or another crack is opening somewhere else, or the substrate is reorganizing itself in ways that will make the Hollows accessible again in a year, or a decade, or never.

Behind you, in the cool dry dark, the structures continue their slow, silent becoming.
