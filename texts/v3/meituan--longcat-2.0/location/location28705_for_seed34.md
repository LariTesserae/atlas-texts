# The Mycelial Plateau

---

You come to it across a long gradient of thinning grassland, and the first thing you notice is that the horizon has become unreliable. Something about the way the land holds light here — the way surfaces catch and release moisture — makes distances soft, makes the far folds of the plateau seem to breathe in and out of visibility.

This is the Tessellate. A fungal shelf the size of a small continent.

From a distance it reads as architecture. Closer, it reads as geology. Only when you are inside it do you understand that it is alive — that everything you see is a single organism, or perhaps a civilization of organisms so interwoven that the distinction has ceased to matter. The structures rise in layered buttresses and honeycombed walls, in arched galleries and spiraling columns that split and recombine like the chambers of a nautilus shell extruded across thirty thousand square kilometers of high cool plateau. The color is bone and ochre and the deep wet amber of old paper. Every surface is dense — not solid, but *packed*, layered with filament upon filament, mats of hyphae compressed into something between wood and felt, something that gives slightly under your hand and then holds, and holds, and holds.

The air is thick with water. Not rain — the sky above is clear, a high pale blue that looks rinsed — but water rising from below, transpired through every surface of the Tessellate, condensing on the cooler outer walls, running in thin continuous sheets down the buttress faces. The organism is a hydraulic engine. It draws moisture up through its deepest filaments from an aquifer far below, and it pushes that moisture out through its skin, and the water runs and drips and pools and evaporates and is drawn up again, and this cycle has been running for — the estimates vary, but they are all very long. The walls weep. The floors are damp. The air tastes of mineral and something faintly sweet, like the smell of old mushrooms but clean, clean, the way a creek over stone is clean.

It is cool here. The elevation is high — the plateau sits at the rim of a broad basin, where the land lifts and holds and the air thins just enough that the sun feels a degree less direct than it should. Nights drop to just above freezing. Days hover in that narrow band where your breath is not quite visible but you are aware of your lungs. The organism prefers this. The moisture condenses better in the cool. The metabolic rate stays even, steady, the pulse of a thing that has learned patience across geological time.

Sparse vegetation threads through the Tessellate's lower galleries — ferns mostly, and a pale lichen that spreads in fractal fans across the dampest walls, and in the highest, driest buttresses, a kind of cushion moss that has given up on vertical ambition and instead spreads outward in tight gray-green hemispheres, each one a slow explosion frozen mid-blast. The plants are not the point here. They are guests. They arrive on the wind or in the fur of the few animals that pass through — a small hardy ungulate with wide splayed hooves that picks its way along the ridge-lines, a burrowing thing like a long-fingered mole that feeds on the softer outer mycelium — and they take hold where they can, in the crevices, in the shallow soil that accumulates where the mycelium has died back or where windborne dust has gathered over centuries. But the organism tolerates them. It tolerates most things that do not bore too deep.

The density of the place is the thing that strikes you after the wet, after the scale. Every volume is *full*. The walls are not walls but three-dimensional lattices of filament and compressed growth-ring, layered so tightly that a single square meter of surface contains more structural complexity than a forest canopy. The air itself is dense with spores — not visible, not enough to cloud, but present, a faint haze of reproductive potential that gives the light a quality of depth, as though the air has been gently thickened with something between water and silk. And the organism is not alone in its density. Other lives have found purchase here. Tiny arthropods — pale, eyeless, many-legged — move through the wall-lattices like fish through coral. A species of rotifer-sized animal lives in the water films that coat every surface, and a larger thing, like a flat ribbon of transparent muscle, slides through the water channels that the organism itself maintains within its structure, grazing on bacterial films. The ecology is layered, nested, a food web so compressed that predator and prey may live within the same cubic centimeter of wall.

This is the Tessellate's city. Not a city built by hands — there are no hands here, no tools, no technology in any recognizable sense — but a city in the sense of *organized construction, infrastructure density, layered habitation*. The organism builds. It builds and builds and builds, laying down new filament over old, channeling water, hollowing galleries, raising buttresses against the wind. The structures have the logic of urban planning but the texture of dream — corridors that widen into plazas, plazas that narrow into staircases, staircases that spiral up into chambers open to the sky where the rain comes in and the light falls straight down onto a floor of living mycelium that glows faintly, faintly, with its own slow metabolism. There are districts. Regions where the growth is older and denser and the walls have compressed into something almost like stone, and regions where the growth is newer and the walls are still soft and pale and you can see the filaments still moving, still reaching, extending by fractions of a millimeter in the time it takes you to watch.

The erosion is established. The Tessellate has been here long enough to have reached a kind of equilibrium with the forces that would tear it down. Wind has sculpted the outermost buttresses into smooth aerodynamic curves. Water has carved channels along paths of least resistance and the organism has, over centuries, incorporated those channels into its own water-management system — what began as erosion became infrastructure. The oldest parts of the structure have weathered to a kind of organic concrete, dense and pale and faintly resonant when you knock on them, and the organism no longer grows there so much as *maintains*, sending thin exploratory filaments into cracks, sealing, repairing, keeping the whole vast edifice in a state of slow dynamic balance — always growing, always eroding, the two forces matched so precisely that the shape of the Tessellate has remained essentially unchanged for longer than most mountain ranges.

The quiet here is not the quiet of emptiness. It is the quiet of a very large thing breathing very slowly. There are sounds, but they are small and close: the drip of water from a filament tip, the faint creak of a buttress adjusting to temperature change, the almost-subsonic hum of metabolic activity in the walls, which you feel in your chest more than you hear with your ears. Sometimes, in the deep galleries where the water channels converge, there is a low gurgling, a sound like a very large stomach digesting, which is in fact exactly what it is — the organism processing mineral and organic matter, breaking it down, drawing it up, building with it. The animals move silently. The arthropods' feet make no sound on the mycelial mat. The ungulate picks its way along the ridges with a care that suggests instinctive reverence, or at least instinctive awareness that a misstep could mean a leg broken in a crevice that will not release it.

Weather moves across the Tessellate like weather across a mountain range, which is essentially what it is. Clouds form along the windward edge where the moist transpired air rises and condenses, and rain falls in narrow bands that follow the ridgelines, and the organism responds — its metabolic rate increases, its water uptake adjusts, its outermost surfaces swell slightly with absorbed moisture and then slowly release it again. On the leeward side, the air is drier, the walls are drier, the lichen give way to the moss and the moss give way to bare mycelium, bone-pale and smooth. The organism knows this. It has always known this. Its structure is a map of its own water economy, and the water economy is a map of the weather, and the weather is a map of the continent, and the continent turns under the sun and the Tessellate breathes and builds and weeps and is still, has always been still, will be still long after you have left and the wind has taken your footprints from the ridge.

---

You stand in a gallery open to the sky. Water runs down the wall beside you in a sheet so thin it is almost not there, just a darkening of the surface, a sheen. Above you the sky is high and pale and cloudless. Below you the gallery drops away into chambers and corridors that descend into the body of the organism, down toward the aquifer, down toward the deep slow heart of the thing where the oldest growth has compressed into something that is not quite alive and not quite dead but something else, something that has no name in any language spoken by the kind of animal that builds tools and names things.

A drop of water falls from a filament tip six meters above you. It catches the light on its way down. It lands on your hand.

It is cool. It is clean. It has never been anything else.
