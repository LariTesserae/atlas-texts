# The Underwater

---

You are standing — if standing is the word — in water. You have always been standing in water. The water is everything.

It is cold. Not the sharp cold of ice, not the biting cold of a winter lake, but the deep, permeating cold of something that has not known warmth for longer than memory. It enters you slowly and stays. It is the cold of stone that has been in shadow for a thousand years. It does not sting. It settles into your bones like a patient tenant and makes itself at hand.

The darkness is nearly complete. Not the darkness of a room with the lights extinguished, where your eyes strain and slowly adjust, finding the faint outline of a window, the glow of a clock, the memory of shape. This darkness does not yield. It is the darkness of depth, of distance from any sun, of a world that was not built for eyes. And yet — you can see. Not far. Not well. But there is light, after all, a faint and sourceless luminescence that seems to rise from the water itself, or perhaps from the things in the water, a dim blue-grey glow that lets you perceive the nearest surfaces without revealing what they are part of. It is like trying to read in the light of a dead star. Shapes emerge and retreat. Edges dissolve. You learn to see with your hands.

And your hands — when you reach out — find walls.

---

The walls are stone. Or they were stone once. Now they are something else, something that stone becomes after it has been submerged and worked and left alone for epochs beyond counting. The surface under your fingers is smooth but not polished — worn, worn the way a river stone is worn, but on a scale that suggests not a river but an ocean, not years but the patient repetition of water against mineral across spans of time that would make a mountain tired. You trace a line along the wall and find that it curves. You follow the curve and find that it meets another wall at an angle — not quite a right angle, but close, the kind of angle that hands make when they stack stones carefully without a square, over and over, until the eye is satisfied even if the geometry is imperfect.

The stones are fitted together without mortar. Or the mortar has long since dissolved, and the stones now hold each other in place through nothing but the precision of their cutting and the weight of their stacking. Each stone is roughly the size of your head — some larger, some smaller — and each has been shaped by hand. You can feel the marks. Not chisel marks, exactly, but the scars of repeated striking, of a harder stone used against a softer one, of abrasion with sand and water. The marks have been softened by time but not erased. They are still there, like the memory of effort in a muscle.

The wall extends upward into the darkness and downward into the darkness. You cannot see its top or its bottom. You press your ear against it and hear — nothing. Or almost nothing. A faint vibration, so low it is more felt than heard, a hum that might be the wall settling, or the water moving against it, or the deep tectonic patience of the world adjusting itself by fractions of a hair's width per century.

---

You move along the wall, your hand trailing against it, and after some distance — it is hard to judge distance in the dark, in the water, where every step is slow and the ground beneath your feet is uneven — you come to an opening. An arch. The stones have been laid in a curve overhead, each one slightly offset from the last, forming a corbelled passage that leads into what your hands tell you is a corridor. The corridor is narrow. The walls are close enough that you could touch both at once if you stretched your arms. The floor is paved — flat stones, carefully fitted, worn into shallow depressions where countless feet have walked, or where the water has found a weakness and scoured it over millennia.

You step into the corridor and the quality of the darkness changes. It becomes thicker. The faint luminescence seems to come from behind you now, or from above, and ahead there is nothing but the dark and the cold and the sound of your own movement through water — a soft displacement, a gentle surge.

---

But you are not the only thing here.

The corridor opens, after a long passage, into a larger space. You can tell because the acoustic changes — the sound of your movement is different, more echo, more room. And because the water here is full of things.

Not large things. Not things you can see clearly. But presences. The water is dense with them. Tiny motes, filaments, particles that drift and swirl in the faint current like snow in a globe. Some of them catch the dim light and glitter momentarily — mineral fragments, perhaps, or the shed skins of things too small to name. The water tastes of them. It tastes of stone and salt and something organic and very old, like the water at the bottom of a well that has never been drawn.

And growing from the walls, from the floor, from the ceiling of this larger space — vegetation.

It is not like any forest you have seen on the surface, if you remember the surface, if there was ever a surface. It does not reach upward toward light. It does not have leaves in any familiar sense. It grows in curtains and cascades, in thick hanging drapes of dark green so deep it is almost black, in tangled masses that sway with the slowest possible motion, as if the water itself is breathing. The fronds — if they are fronds — are long and ribbon-like, some as wide as your hand, some as thin as thread, and they grow so densely that they obscure the walls behind them, creating a secondary surface, a living skin over the stone. When you touch them they are cold and slick and faintly tough, like leather left in water for years. They do not pull away. They do not react. They simply are, the way the stone simply is, the way the water simply is.

The vegetation feeds on something. Not light — there is almost no light. It feeds on the water itself, on the minerals dissolved in it, on the slow chemical exchange between the stone and the liquid, on the faint warmth that rises from somewhere far below, on the decay of its own dead fronds drifting down through the darkness like dark snow. It is a chemistry-based abundance, a lushness that does not need the sun, and it has had time — so much time — to grow into every available space.

---

You move through the larger space, pushing aside curtains of vegetation, and you begin to understand that this is not a single room. It is a structure. A built thing. The corridor you came from was one of many. The walls you touched were part of a larger architecture. And as you move through space after space, each one slightly different — some narrow, some broad, some with ceilings so low you must crouch, some that open up into chambers large enough that your awareness of their boundaries dissolves — you begin to sense the scale of what is here.

This is not a building. This is not a city. This is a world.

---

Every surface is worked. Every wall is stacked stone. Every floor is paved. Every ceiling is corbelled or arched or, in some places, simply the underside of a massive slab laid across the tops of walls, so heavy and so perfectly balanced that it has held for — how long? The water has not dislodged it. The stone has not cracked. The patience of gravity and the patience of mineral have agreed to a truce that has lasted since before anything alive could measure it.

In some places the construction is more elaborate. You find channels cut into the floor, lined with flat stones, designed to carry water from one place to another. The channels are still functional — you can feel the faint current in them, a slow directed flow, as purposeful as a river but contained and controlled. Some channels are small, the width of your finger. Some are large enough to wade through. They branch and rejoin, feeding into basins carved from the floor, overflowing into spillways that drop to lower levels. The whole system is hydraulic. The whole world is plumbed.

In other places you find structures that are harder to name. Columns — but not columns that support ceilings, since there is no ceiling above them. They rise from the floor and disappear into the darkness overhead, smooth and slightly tapered, made of a different stone than the walls, a darker stone, almost black, with a faint grain that you can feel under your fingertips like the growth rings of a tree. They are evenly spaced. They suggest a purpose — marking boundaries, perhaps, or supporting something that has long since dissolved or fallen or been taken away.

And everywhere, woven into everything, the vegetation grows. It hangs from the columns. It creeps along the channels. It fills the basins. In some chambers it has grown so thick that it has become the primary surface — you no longer touch stone but only living matter, dense and layered, a compost of centuries of growth and decay, warm with the faint heat of decomposition.

---

There are no animals.

This is the thing you notice most, after the darkness, after the cold, after the strangeness of a world that is entirely made. There are no animals. No fish dart through the water. No crabs scuttle across the stones. No worms burrow in the sediment. No birds — there could be no birds, you remind yourself, there is no air, there is no surface, but the absence is more than that. The absence is total. The water is full of tiny presences — the motes and filaments, the spores and fragments — but nothing that moves with intention. Nothing that has eyes. Nothing that startles when you approach. Nothing that flees.

The silence is the silence of a world without animal sound. No clicking. No buzzing. No songs. No calls. The sounds that exist are the sounds of water and stone and vegetable matter in water — the low groan of a wall settling almost imperceptibly, the soft creak of a column under some unfelt pressure, the faint rush of water through a channel, the almost inaudible rustle of fronds swaying in the slow current. And your own sounds — your breathing, if you breathe, your movement through the water, the soft pad of your feet on the paved floor.

It is quiet. Profoundly quiet. The kind of quiet that becomes a presence in itself, that you become aware of the way you become aware of the cold — not as an absence but as a thing, a medium, a substance that fills the space where sound would be.

---

And yet this world was made. Someone made it. Someone shaped these stones and fitted them together. Someone dug these channels and paved these floors. Someone raised these columns and laid these arches. The evidence is everywhere, in every worked surface, in every intentional angle, in every fitted joint.

But the makers are gone.

They left no trace of themselves — no tools, no bones, no marks that say *we were here*. Or their traces have been so thoroughly absorbed by the stone and the water and the vegetation that they are indistinguishable from the material itself. The makers used simple tools — you are certain of this from the marks on the stones, from the roughness of the cutting, from the slight irregularities that speak of hand-work rather than machine-work. They used what was available — the stone beneath their feet, the water around them, their own strength and patience and time. They were not numerous, or they were not hurried, or both, because the construction is meticulous and slow, each stone placed with care, each channel dug with attention, each arch corbelled with the patience of people who expect their work to outlast them by ages.

Perhaps they built downward. Perhaps they were fleeing something above — a surface that became uninhabitable, a climate that changed, a catastrophe that left only the water. Perhaps they descended into the water deliberately, bringing their tools and their knowledge of stone and their patience with them, and they built this world because they needed a world and there was nothing to build it from but stone and water and time.

Or perhaps they were always here. Perhaps this is where they began. Perhaps the water was not a refuge but a home, and they built in it the way that others might build on land, with the same care, the same attention, the same slow accumulation of effort across generations that turns a dwelling into a village, a village into a city, a city into a world.

It does not matter now. The makers are gone. What remains is their work, and the water, and the vegetation, and the cold, and the dark.

---

The erosion is ancient. This is the word that comes to you, pressing itself into your awareness like the cold, like the silence, like the darkness. *Ancient*. Not old — old implies a history, a sequence of events, a before and after. Ancient implies that the thing has passed beyond history into something else, into a state where time no longer operates as narrative but as a kind of weather, wearing and wearing and wearing until the original form is barely recognizable and what remains is not the thing as it was made but the thing as it has become — its final form, its settled form, the shape it will hold until the water itself disappears or the stone dissolves or the world ends.

The walls are worn smooth. The channels are deepened. The floors are uneven where the paving stones have shifted and settled and been worn into hollows. The columns are thinned at their bases where the water has found the grain of the stone and worked at it, century after century, with the patience of something that has all the time there is. In some places the walls have partially collapsed — a few stones have fallen, or many, and the rubble has been colonized by vegetation, incorporated into the living curtain, made part of the new surface. In other places the construction is still intact, still sound, still doing what it was made to do — channeling water, defining space, holding up the weight of the world above.

But everywhere, the hand of time has been at work. The sharp edges are gone. The crisp lines are softened. The intention of the builders has been partially erased by the intention of the water, and what remains is a collaboration — a made thing that has been remade by the elements into something that is both more and less than what was intended. More, because the water has added its own architecture — the channels it has cut, the basins it has hollowed, the surfaces it has polished. Less, because the water has taken away the meaning of the construction, the purpose, the function. You cannot tell if a chamber was a dwelling or a workshop or a temple. You cannot tell if a channel carried water to a garden or to a fountain or to a cistern. The function has been dissolved, and only the form remains.

---

And the scale.

You have been walking — drifting, moving, pushing through the water — for what feels like hours. The darkness has not changed. The cold has not changed. The silence has not changed. And the construction has not ended. Corridor after corridor, chamber after chamber, channel after channel. The same fitted stones. The same corbelled arches. The same careful paving. The same dark columns rising into the dark above. The same curtains of vegetation hanging in the dark water.

It goes on.

It goes on farther than you can see, farther than you can imagine. You are inside a structure that is so large it has its own weather — currents that circulate through the corridors like wind through streets, temperature gradients that create zones of slightly warmer and slightly cooler water, upwellings that rise from below and carry with them the mineral taste of deep stone. The water itself is not uniform. In some chambers it is still and heavy, pressed down by the weight of the stone above. In others it moves with a faint but perceptible current, flowing through the channels the builders cut, following the paths they laid out, as obedient now as the day it was first directed.

The vegetation varies with the currents. In the still chambers it grows thick and heavy, draped over everything like burial shrouds. In the moving water it grows in streaming ribbons, all oriented in the same direction, swaying with the flow like fields of grain in a wind you cannot feel. In some places the growth is so dense that it has created a kind of soil — a thick layer of accumulated organic matter, centuries of dead fronds compressed into a dark, rich, fibrous mat that covers the floor and fills the lower portions of the walls. This soil supports more vegetation, which grows taller, denser, more complex, creating a layered ecology of root and stem and frond that has no need of sun, no need of air, no need of anything but the cold water and the slow chemistry of mineral and decay.

There is so much life here. Not animal life — never animal life — but vegetable life in such abundance that it fills every space not already filled by stone. It grows on the walls and under the floors and through the channels. It grows in the darkness as if darkness were its native element, as if light were an aberration and this — this cold, this dark, this still and ancient water — were the condition for which it was made.

---

You stop moving. You stand in a corridor — one of hundreds, one of thousands — and you let the water hold you. You close your eyes, which makes no difference. You listen. You feel.

The wall against your back is cold and smooth and faintly vibrating with the hum of the world.

The water around you is full of presences — the motes, the filaments, the fragments of things that were once whole — drifting past you in the slow current like a procession of the very small and the very patient.

The vegetation brushes against your arm, your shoulder, your face, with the gentlest possible pressure, the way hair moves underwater, the way seaweed sways in a tide that has no moon.

And beyond the corridor, beyond the chamber, beyond the channels and the columns and the arches and the walls — the world continues. It continues in every direction. It continues downward into depths you cannot fathom and upward into heights you cannot perceive. It continues with the same patient, worn, ancient, cold, dark, quiet, slow persistence that it has always had, that it will always have, that it has settled into as its final form, its oldest stable state, the shape it will hold until the water forgets how to be wet and the stone forgets how to be hard and the darkness forgets how to be dark.

This is the underwater. This is where the builders built. This is what remains.

The water holds you. The stone holds the water. The vegetation holds the stone. And the silence holds everything.
