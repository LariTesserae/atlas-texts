# The Rootworks

---

You arrive at the edge of a continent.

Not the surface — there is no surface here, not anymore, perhaps not ever. You are *below*, deep below, in a place the world above has forgotten or never knew. The air is cool, almost painfully so, carrying the scent of cut stone and something green — not grass, not leaf, but the interior of something alive, the way a split stem smells, vegetal and mineral at once.

The ground beneath you is not rock. Not exactly.

It is *wood* — or what wood becomes when it has been growing for a thousand years in the dark, when it has never known wind or rain or the cycle of seasons, when it has only known pressure and depth and the slow patient chemistry of a closed system. It is pale, faintly luminescent, a bone-white that catches what light exists and holds it. The grain runs in vast waves, like the contoured lines of a topographic map drawn at continental scale, and if you press your palm flat against it you can feel a pulse — slow, deep, arrhythmic, the heartbeat of something so large it has forgotten it has a body.

This is the substrate. Everything here grows from it, is it, returns to it.

---

## The Architecture of Living

The city — and it is a city, dense and layered and loud with the hum of purpose — does not sit on the ground. It *is* the ground, or rather the ground became the city, or the city grew the ground, and no one is sure of the direction of causality anymore.

Buildings are trunks. Not shaped like trunks — they *are* trunks, vertical columns of this pale woody material, rising ten, twenty, forty meters, their walls still alive, still growing, ringed with the slow calloused ridges of annual growth rings that here mean something different, mean *age* not in years but in *decades* of continuous habitation. Between the trunks, bridges have been trained — actual branches, guided and grafted, their living tissue coaxed into horizontal spans that connect one column to the next at various heights, forming a three-dimensional lattice of habitation that looks, from a distance, like the inside of a ribcage. The analogy is not lost on anyone.

Light comes from the walls themselves. A soft, steady, photosynthetic glow — not bright, not dim, that quality of light that makes you forget you're underground because it *is* daylight, filtered through living tissue, the bioluminescence of a plant that found another way. It doesn't flicker. It doesn't vary. The circadian rhythm here is civic, not biological: the light dims on a schedule maintained by the municipal authority, a slow fade over two hours, a slow brightening over two hours, and in between there is only the constant hum.

---

## What Grows

Vegetation — if you can call it that, if you can use that word for the totality of the living — is everywhere. Not *on* things. It *is* things. The streets are paved with a dense mat of pale rhizomes, interlocked so tightly they form a surface almost smooth, springy underfoot, warm — warmer than the air, radiating that deep metabolic heat of growth without purpose, of a plant that grows because growing is what it does. The facades of the buildings are furred with a fine, hairlike moss that is not moss, that is in fact the root-hair of the same organism that forms the trunks, and it sways in imperceptible currents of air, reaching, testing, *feeling*.

There are no gardens. Gardens imply a separation between the cultivated and the wild, and here there is no such thing. The plant does not stop at the property line. It does not recognize property. It grows through the walls, across the ceilings, between the floorboards. Maintenance crews go out each morning with short blades — not to cut the plant back, that would be futile, like trying to hold back the tide — but to *prune* it, to guide it, to persuade it in the direction it was already going. The relationship is not adversarial. The city and the plant are the same thing, and the city knows it.

In the gaps between buildings, in the spaces where the canopy has not yet closed, there are groves — if you can call them that — of smaller growth: fronds, tendrils, structures that have no name in any surface language, spiraling forms that catch the bioluminescent light and scatter it in patterns that shift as the growth shifts, as the slow pulse of the organism moves through them. The air here is thicker, more humid, and the green smell intensifies until it becomes almost sweet, almost narcotic, and you find yourself breathing deeper without meaning to.

---

## The Engines

The city is not only alive. It is *industrial*.

At the center of the settlement — if "center" has meaning in a three-dimensional lattice — there are engines. Great ones. They run on *sap*, on the slow pressurized exudate of the organism itself, a thick, amber, viscous fluid that flows through channels in the walls like blood through veins, and the engines tap into those channels, draw off the pressure, convert it into motion. The machinery is brass and iron and steel, bolted into the living wood, and where metal meets plant there is a seal of something organic — a gum, a resin, a coagulated interface that blurs the line between the manufactured and the grown.

The sound is the hum. It is always there, beneath everything, a low-frequency vibration that you feel in your teeth before you hear it. The engines run constantly. They power the lifts — vertical shafts where platforms rise and fall on counterweights of living wood, their movement smooth and slow and organic, like breathing. They power the presses, the lathes, the looms, the vast industrial looms that weave the hair-moss into fabric, that press the wood-pulp into paper, that extract the sap and refine it into fuel and medicine and something like wine, something that tastes of deep earth and slow time.

There are smokestacks. They rise above the canopy — if there is a canopy, if there is an above — and they vent something that is not smoke but *spore*, a fine particulate mist that the organism sheds as a byproduct of its metabolism, and the spore drifts down through the groves and the streets and settles on everything, a pale dust that smells faintly of yeast and crushed stems, and the maintenance crews sweep it each morning, and each morning it returns.

---

## The Quiet

It is, mostly, quiet.

The hum is not noise. It is *atmosphere*. The silence beneath the hum is real silence — the absence of wind, of rain, of the ten thousand surface sounds that the ear doesn't notice until they're gone. Footsteps on the rhizome pavement are soft, muffled. Voices carry strangely in the three-dimensional space, bouncing off the trunks and the bridges and the living walls, arriving from unexpected directions. When someone speaks, the sound is clear but close, intimate, as if the air itself has absorbed the distant sounds and left only the near.

The animals — and there are animals, many of them — move quietly. They have evolved, or been bred, or simply adapted, to a world without echo and without weather. Small things, mostly: pale arthropods with too many legs, that scuttle along the walls in the spaces between the moss; furred things, warm-blooded, that nest in the hollows of the trunks and emerge at the dim-hours to forage in the groves; larger things, rare, that move through the upper reaches of the lattice, visible only as a shifting of shadows, a displacement of light, a sense of something vast and slow and *present* that watches and is watched.

The animals do not fear the city. The city does not fear the animals. They are part of the same system, the same metabolism, the same slow cycle of growth and decay and growth.

---

## The Strange

And it *is* strange. You feel it before you can name it.

It is the way the light comes from the walls and not from the sky, and yet it is *normal* light, and your eyes accept it, and that acceptance is the first wrongness. It is the way the buildings grow — you can watch them grow, if you are patient, if you place a mark on the wall and wait a week and find the mark has moved, the wood has expanded around it, the building has become larger without anyone adding anything to it. It is the way the ground pulses, that deep arrhythmic beat, and how after a few days you stop noticing it, and then one night you wake in the dark and realize you have been sleeping in time with it, that your own heart has synced to the pulse of the organism, that you are part of its rhythm now.

It is the way the city is *not finished*. Not abandoned — unfinished. There are places where the trunks have not yet risen, where the bridges have not yet been trained, where the rhizome pavement gives way to bare raw wood, the pale exposed tissue of the organism, still damp, still growing, the grain still shifting, still deciding what it will become. These are the edges of the city, the frontier, and they are *new* — not old, not ancient, but actively emerging, actively becoming, the geology of this place still raw, still embryonic, still in the act of forming itself.

And in these new places, the plant grows *faster*, visibly faster, and the air is warmer and the green smell is stronger and the light is brighter, and the animals gather there — not in fear, not in curiosity, but in something like *anticipation*, as if they know what is coming before it arrives, as if the future is a scent on the air and they can taste it.

---

## The Rule

There is one rule in the Rootworks, and it is not written anywhere because it does not need to be:

*You do not cut deep.*

The surface wood, the outer growth, the moss and the fronds and the hair-roots — these can be pruned, shaped, guided. The organism does not mind. It is how it communicates, how it responds to being touched. But the deep wood, the heartwood, the channels where the sap runs thick and slow — these are not to be breached. Not because the organism would retaliate, though perhaps it would, in its slow vegetal way, but because the deep wood is the *structure*. It is the skeleton of the world. Cut it, and the light fails. Cut it, and the pulse stutters. Cut it, and the engines fall silent, and the city holds its breath, and in the silence you can hear, for the first time, the true depth of the quiet that surrounds you, the vast mineral darkness that waits below the living, the void from which the plant grew and to which, in time, everything returns.

So the city grows outward, not upward. It extends. It extends. It extends into the dark, following the organism's own slow advance, building as it goes, a frontier of living industry pushing into raw geology, into the emerging, into the not-yet-formed, and behind it the old city settles and the pulse steadies and the light holds, constant and warm and strange, the light of a world that has never seen the sun and does not need to, that has found its own way to be alive, and keeps finding it, and will keep finding it, long after the surface has forgotten what light was for.
