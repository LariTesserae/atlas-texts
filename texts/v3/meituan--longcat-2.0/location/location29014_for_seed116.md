# The Cistern at the Bottom of the World

---

You are inside a lung.

Not a biological lung — though it breathes. Not a mechanical lung — though it pumps. It is something that was built so long ago that the distinction has become meaningless, a chamber at the deepest point of a flooded world, where the ocean has found a way in and never found a way out.

The space is small. That is the first thing. You could cross it in forty steps, fifty if you are careful. The ceiling — if it is a ceiling — is a low dome of dark metal, green-black, sweating. The walls curve. Everything curves. There are no corners left. Whatever angles the builders intended have been sanded away by ten thousand years of warm, mineral-heavy water moving through this place in slow, patient circuits. The surfaces are smooth as river stone. Smooth as bone. You can run your hand along the wall and feel nothing but the gentle undulation of a surface that has forgotten it was ever welded, ever riveted, ever *made*.

And yet it was made. That is the second thing. This is not a cave. The metal is real — you can find places where the erosion has bitten deep enough to reveal the layered structure of it, the strata of fabrication, the ghost-grain of cast alloy. There are channels cut into the floor, perfectly semicircular, worn to a polish. There are the stumps of machines. There are rails. There is, in the center of the chamber, a column — a thick, rounded pillar that rises from floor to ceiling like a trunk, like a stalactite-stalagmite marriage, and it is covered in a thick, living fur of something that is not quite moss and not quite mold and not quite coral.

The water is everywhere. Not crashing, not surging — *present*. The chamber is fully submerged, has been since before memory, and the water is warm. Blood-warm. It moves in a slow, continuous circulation, entering through a series of tall, narrow slots near the floor — you can feel the current pulling gently at your ankles — and exiting through a single dark aperture in the ceiling, a throat, a drain, a mouth. The water is not clear. It carries a fine suspension of particulate, a permanent haze, a luminous fog that catches the light and holds it, so that the air — the water — the medium — glows with a diffuse, directionless amber. Normal light, the parameter said, and it is true: there are no spotlights, no dramatic shafts, no bioluminescent theatrics. Just a warm, even, everywhere-glow, as if the water itself has a low wattage. As if the world has a filament.

The light comes from the walls. Not from fixtures — the fixtures are long gone, or long buried under accretion — but from the walls themselves, which have developed, over the millennia, a skin. A patina. A living, photosynthetic film that feeds on the warm water and the mineral exhalations of the metal and glows, faintly, steadily, the color of old honey. In places the film is thick and lush, a velvet of deep green and copper and black, and in places it has been scraped away by the current or by the passage of bodies, and the bare metal shows through like a scar, like a memory of industry.

Because this was a factory. You can feel it in the bones of the place. The column in the center — it was a reactor housing, or a press, or a vat. The channels in the floor carried coolant, or waste, or product. The slots in the walls were intake filters, and you can still see the ghost of the mesh, a mineralized lattice, a fossil of engineering. The scale of the machinery was human, or human-adjacent: the column is just wide enough to wrap your arms around, the channels are knee-deep, the ceiling is low enough that a tall person would stoop. This was a place where people worked. Where they stood and sweated and monitored and maintained. And then the water came, and they left, or they didn't leave, and the water stayed.

The life here is abundant but not dense. That is the paradox you have to hold. There is so much of it, and yet so much empty space between each instance. The chamber is mostly open water — vast, warm, glowing emptiness — and then, suddenly, a cluster. A thicket. A *colony*.

The vegetation — if you can call it that — grows in scattered gardens. Not on the floor, not on the walls directly, but on the *ruins*. On the stumps of machines, on the rails, on the column. It is lush where it is lush: heavy, dripping, overgrown, a tangle of fronds and filaments and bulbous, translucent pods that pulse faintly with their own slow rhythm. The fronds are long — two feet, three feet — and they move in the current like hair, like sea-grass, like the fingers of a dreaming hand. They are deep green, almost black, and they are studded with tiny, bright points of color: orange, white, a startling, electric blue. These are not flowers. These are animals.

The fauna is abundant. It is everywhere, and it is nowhere. You will swim — because you are swimming, you are always swimming here, the water is everything — through a long stretch of empty, glowing, silent water, and you will begin to think you are alone, and then you will round the column and find a *city*.

A city of crabs. Or crab-like things. Or things that were crabs once and have been something else for a long time. They are small — the size of your thumb, the size of your fist — and they are everywhere on the column's surface, moving slowly, deliberately, tending the fronds, scraping the film, carrying their young in the cups of their shells. Their shells are the color of the metal: dark, green-black, iridescent. They have too many legs, or too few legs, or legs that split and rejoin in ways that are not quite right. They are strange. They are *so* strange. They watch you with eyes that are not eyes, with organs that are not organs, and they do not flee. They have never had a reason to flee. Nothing comes here. Nothing leaves.

There are other things. Eels — or eel-things — that live in the channels, that slide through the warm water with a sound like a whispered conversation. Jellies that are not jellies, that have structure, that have *bones*, tiny cartilaginous lattices that you can see through their transparent bells, and they pulse through the water in slow, stately processions, trailing tentacles that are studded with the same bright points of color, the same tiny, living jewels. Fish that are not fish. Things with too many fins. Things with no fins at all, that move by some other principle, some undulation of the body that is not quite swimming, not quite crawling, not quite anything you have a name for.

And the sound. The sound is *loud*. Not the sound of water — the water is silent, or nearly silent, a low, constant, bone-deep hum that you feel more than hear. The sound is the sound of the machines. The machines are still running.

You don't understand this at first. You think it's the current, you think it's the water moving through the slots, you think it's the column, vibrating at some resonant frequency. But it's not. It's the machines. The machines are still running, have been running, will be running. The pumps — somewhere, in some adjacent chamber, in some deeper level of this buried, flooded, forgotten factory — the pumps are still pumping. You can feel them in the rhythm of the current, in the slow, steady, tidal pulse of the water entering and leaving the chamber. You can hear them in the clank and groan of the column, which is not a column anymore but a machine, a still-functioning machine, its purpose lost but its motion intact. It vibrates. It hums. It *works*. It does something to the water — heats it, mineralizes it, charges it with some energy that the film and the fronds and the crabs and the eels and the not-jellies and the not-fish all feed on, all depend on, all *are*.

The factory is still producing. It does not know what it is producing. It does not know that the world above is gone, or changed, or irrelevant. It knows only the work. The warm water comes in. The warm water goes out. The column turns, or pulses, or processes. The film grows. The fronds grow. The crabs tend. The eels slide. The jellies drift. The cycle continues.

This is the ancient stable state. This is the end of erosion, the end of change, the place where everything has been worn down to its final, simplest, most durable form and then *stayed there*. The metal is as smooth as it will ever be. The machines are as simple as they will ever be — stripped of their casings, their controls, their interfaces, reduced to their core function, their essential motion. The life is as adapted as it will ever be — perfectly suited to this one chamber, this one warm current, this one endless, humming, glowing, submerged moment.

You are inside a lung at the bottom of the world, and it is breathing, and it has been breathing since before you were born, and it will be breathing after you are gone, and the crabs will tend their fronds, and the eels will slide through their channels, and the column will turn, and the water will glow, and the sound — the deep, industrial, mechanical, biological, geological sound — will go on.

And on.

And on.
