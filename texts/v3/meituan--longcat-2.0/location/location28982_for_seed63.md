# The Throat

---

You are inside a cathedral-sized cavity, roughly oval, perhaps two hundred meters along its longest axis and half that across. The ceiling vaults overhead in a shape that suggests neither natural cave nor deliberate architecture but something that has settled into its own necessity over a time beyond counting.

The air is hot. Not the dry heat of open desert — this is a close, dense heat, the heat of breath held too long in a small room. It presses against your skin with a faint weight. You taste minerals on it: sulfur in trace amounts, something metallic and sharp, something else that has no name in any human tongue. There is no moisture. Your lips crack within minutes. Your eyes dry and sting. The heat does not fluctuate — it is the temperature of the place, constant as stone, as old as the roots of whatever mountain this chamber hides beneath.

The light is near-absent. The darkness is not the darkness of a room with the lights off, which always holds the memory of light, the suggestion that a switch exists somewhere and brightness could return. This is a darkness that has never been otherwise. There are no photons here to spare. What faint perception you have comes from the walls themselves — not light, exactly, but a response to your presence. The surface of the rock — if it is rock — dimples with points of dull amber warmth where your body heat touches it, like the points of pressure on a sleeping animal's skin when you lay your hand against it. These points fade slowly, reluctantly, as though the wall is considering whether to remember you.

The walls. The walls are where the strangeness begins.

They are not stone. Or they are stone, but stone that has been convinced — over geological ages, over epochs of steady heat and no interruption — to do something else. The surface is covered entirely, floor to ceiling and around every contour, with a dense growth of what you might call organisms if you are being generous or minerals if you are being precise. They are both. Neither.

Each one is a rosette roughly the size of an open hand, though some are smaller and a few — the oldest, set deep in crevices where the wall curves inward — are as wide as a dinner plate. Their form is radial: filaments or spines or fingers that extend outward from a central nub, each one curved slightly, all of them curving in the same direction, so that every rosette has a handedness, a spiral. The filaments are rigid but not brittle. They have a faint flex to them, like keratin, like horn. Their color in your body-heat-glow is a deep amber-brown, almost the color of old amber itself, with a faint translucency at the tips where the filaments are newest and thinnest.

They are packed against one another. There is no bare wall visible anywhere. Each rosette touches its neighbors, filament-tip to filament-tip, and where they touch there is a slight fusion — the tips have grown into one another over time, forming a continuous surface, a membrane, a skin. The entire chamber is lined with this skin. It is the wall and it is alive and it is one thing and it is many things and it does not care about the distinction.

The floor is the same, thicker, the rosettes here larger and flatter, pressed down by their own weight and time into something like cobblestones. Walking on them — if you are here, if you are walking — gives slightly under each step, a faint compressibility that is deeply unsettling. Not soft. Not yielding. Just aware. Each footfall sends a ripple of warmth-glow outward through the filaments, a wave of amber that propagates for several meters before fading, and you realize that the floor is communicating your presence to itself in a language of heat.

There is no water anywhere. There has never been water here. The chemistry of this place is built on silicates and sulfides and metals — iron, copper, manganese — and the rosettes metabolize the slow oxidation of the rock itself, pulling energy from the interface between mineral and mineral, from the infinitesimal electron exchanges that happen when certain crystals press against others under heat. They are, in a sense, eating the mountain. They are eating the heat. They are eating the gradient between what is more and what is less. Their metabolism is so slow that a single rosette might live for ten thousand years, but there are millions of them, and their combined exhalation — a faint off-gas of hydrogen sulfide and something like methane but not quite — is what gives the air its weight.

They are the fauna. 2.9 out of 3. Overwhelming. They are everywhere, they are the place, and they are alive in a way that does not map onto any kingdom of life you know. They do not move, or they move so slowly that their movement is measured in the growth of their own filaments — a millimeter per year, perhaps, as they extend outward and upward, always toward the heat source, always toward the center of the chamber where the rock is warmest. Predator and prey do not exist here. Instead there is competition for surface area, for thermal gradient, for the slight advantage of a crevice where geothermal warmth concentrates. The rosettes that find these spots grow faster, extend further, shade their neighbors. The shaded ones grow slower, their filaments thinning, their central nubs dimming. It is an ecosystem of pure position, pure placement, and it has been running for so long that every available surface is occupied and the entire system has reached a state of equilibrium so stable it might as well be peace.

The scattered structures. Here and there, rising from the floor or descending from the ceiling, are formations that resemble architecture. A cluster of rosettes, growing over millennia in a thermal column, has built a column of its own — a vertical stack of fused bodies, eight meters tall, slightly tapered, fluted with the spiral patterns of a thousand individual rosettes that grew and died and were incorporated into the structure. It stands near the center of the chamber like a pillar in a nave. Another has formed near the eastern curve — a low wall, waist-high, that runs for fifteen meters before dissolving back into the general floor. It is not a wall. It is a reef. It is a succession of rosettes that happened to find a favorable band of warmth and grew along it, and the result looks like a bench, a pew, a shelf, a foundation. There are perhaps a dozen of these formations in the chamber, none of them built, all of them grown, and each one creates a space around it — a niche, a corner, a threshold — that feels architectural without being so.

The silence. The place is mostly quiet. The rosettes make no sound in their metabolism — no bubbling, no clicking, no hum. What you hear, if you listen, is the sound of heat: a faint, high, almost subliminal tone that might be the chamber itself vibrating at some frequency below the threshold of ordinary hearing. It is the sound of rock under thermal stress, of the mountain adjusting by microns to the slow exhalation of the earth below. Occasionally — very occasionally — there is a crack, sharp and singular, like a knuckle popping, as a filament somewhere deep in the wall finally breaks under the accumulated strain of its own growth. The crack echoes once and is swallowed by the density of the chamber, absorbed by the million soft surfaces of the rosettes, which take the vibration and convert it to heat and add it to the whole.

The dynamics. The place is active. Not in the way of weather or predation, but in the way of a system that is still processing, still running its cycles. Thermal plumes rise from below and the rosettes nearest them brighten — literally brighten, a faint visible glow, a deep red-orange that is the only light in the place, the only light there has ever been. These glow-spots shift over hours, over days, as the plumes wander, and the rosettes respond by redirecting their growth, turning their spirals by degrees, following the heat the way plants follow the sun. The chamber breathes. The air pressure rises and falls by small amounts as the geothermal below exhales and inhales on a cycle that seems to be roughly tidal — some deep rhythm, some pulse in the planet's interior, translated through rock into a slow respiratory motion of the whole cavity.

The weirdness. It accumulates. It is the weirdness of a place that is entirely alive and entirely unaware of you. It is the weirdness of finding structure without intention — a cathedral-shaped space with pillar-like formations and bench-like reefs and a ceiling that vaults like a ribcage, none of it built, all of it grown, the whole thing an accident of chemistry and heat and time that has produced something that looks like a place of worship. It is the weirdness of the filaments under your fingertips, which are warm and smooth and faintly ridged and which, when you pull your hand away, leave a pattern of glowing points on their surface that mirrors the whorls of your fingerprints, as though they have read you and are holding the memory of your touch in their slow, thermal language.

You are in the belly of something. Not a creature — there is no creature here, no brain, no nerve, no intention. But a body. A body made of millions of small slow lives that together constitute a single thermal organism, a distributed entity that metabolizes rock and exhales gas and maintains a steady state in the deep dark, and has been doing so for longer than your species has existed, and will continue doing so long after.

The rosettes nearest you glow faintly where your warmth touches them. The floor records your weight and holds it. The air accepts your breath and is unchanged. The pillar-reef at the center of the chamber stands in its nave of living stone and does not mean anything by it.

It is very hot. It is very dark. It is very quiet.

It has always been exactly like this.
