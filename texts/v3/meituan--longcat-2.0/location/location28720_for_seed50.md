# The Throat of the Unfinished Fall

There is a place where the water has not yet decided what it is.

It hangs — not falls, *hangs* — in a vertical column so vast that the mind refuses to hold its scale, yet the space you occupy is no larger than a room. A pocket. A bubble of air trapped in the moment before the waterfall commits to being a waterfall. The water is everywhere: a wall of it to the north, a curtain to the east, a slow grinding sheet to the south that hasn't yet learned to fall. It presses against the air like a held breath. The sound is not a roar. A roar implies distance, implies a source you can point to. This is closer to the sound of the world's own blood moving — a pressure in the chest, a vibration in the teeth, a frequency that makes the bones of your inner ear feel like tuning forks struck against the edge of a continent. You do not hear it. You *are* heard by it.

The rock around you is wrong. Not wrong in the way of broken things — wrong in the way of *unfinished* things. The walls are raw, not weathered. There are no smooth curves here, no patient rounding of stone by millennia of flow. Instead: edges. Fresh edges. Edges that still have *intent*. The rock looks like it was shaped by something that began the work and then stopped, or by something that is shaping it still, in this moment, so slowly that the change is invisible but so recently that the stone has not yet forgotten being something else. In places, the surface has a waxy, almost organic quality — like scar tissue, like the skin over a healing wound. In other places, it is sharp enough to draw blood, and the blood, when it falls, is caught by the mist before it can strike the ground and is carried upward, which is the first strange thing.

The second strange thing is the light.

It is normal light. That is the strangeness. You are inside a waterfall, inside a cloud, inside a column of water so dense that it should be dark, should be green-black, should be the color of deep places. But the light is the light of a clear afternoon. It comes from everywhere and nowhere. It does not cast shadows correctly. Your hand, held up, is illuminated from the left and the right simultaneously, and the shadow it casts is not behind it but *beneath* it, as if the ground itself were the light source, or as if the light were passing through you from below, filtered through stone that has not yet decided whether it is opaque.

The air is cool. Not cold — cool. The kind of cool that makes you aware of your own warmth, that makes the boundary between your skin and the world feel like a negotiation. The mist does not wet you. It *almost* wets you. It hovers at the threshold of contact, a film of moisture that clings to your clothes and hair and eyelashes without ever quite becoming water. You are damp the way a dream is damp — the memory of wetness, the idea of it, without the substance.

There are structures here. Scattered. Small. They might be altars, or they might be the remains of something that was never finished being built. They are made of the same raw stone as the walls, but shaped — deliberately, though the deliberation feels ancient and also recent, as if the hands that shaped them were working in a time that does not map onto any calendar you know. The shapes are simple: a low platform, a half-arch that leads nowhere, a series of shallow basins carved into the floor that hold water which does not evaporate and does not overflow, that simply *is*, at a level that never changes. The craftsmanship is primitive but not crude. There is a care to it. A patience. The kind of patience that belongs to someone who has all the time in the world and none of it at all.

In one of the basins, something moves.

It is not a fish. It is not anything you have a word for. It is small — the size of your thumb — and it is the color of the raw stone, and it moves in a way that suggests it is not swimming in the water but *moving the water around itself*, as if the water were its body and the body were something it wore only when necessary. It has no eyes that you can see. It has no mouth. It is there, and then it is not, and the water in the basin is still, and you are not sure if you saw it or if the light did something to the stone that made it look like life.

The third strange thing is the sound beneath the sound.

Under the deafening pressure of the water, there is another sound. Quieter. Slower. It is the sound of the rock growing. Not cracking — *growing*. A low, almost subsonic hum, like a note played on an instrument the size of a mountain, a note so low that you feel it in your marrow before you hear it in your ears. It is the sound of geology in its embryonic stage, of stone that is still deciding what kind of stone it wants to be. It is the sound of *becoming*, and it is the most unsettling sound you have ever heard, because it implies that the ground beneath your feet is not a given, not a foundation, but a process — ongoing, unfinished, and not guaranteed to hold.

The vegetation is sparse. A kind of moss, or something like moss, grows in the crevices where the raw stone meets the mist. It is pale — not green, but a color that green becomes when it has never seen direct sun, a color like the memory of chlorophyll, like the idea of photosynthesis without the execution. It does not grow toward the light. It grows toward the sound. You can see it, if you look closely: the tiny filaments leaning, almost imperceptibly, toward the walls, toward the hum, toward the place where the rock is still becoming.

There is a fourth strange thing, but you do not notice it until you try to leave.

The way out is the way you came in. You are certain of this. You entered from the east, through a gap in the curtain of water, and the gap is still there — you can see it, a darker space in the wall of falling water, a place where the mist thins and the light from outside (normal light, afternoon light, the light of a world that has finished becoming) filters through. You walk toward it. You reach it. You step through.

You are still in the room.

The gap is behind you now. You turn. The gap is in front of you. You turn again. The gap is behind you. The room has not moved. The water has not moved. The light has not changed. But the exit is no longer where it was, or it is always where you are not, or it exists only when you are not looking for it.

The water continues its patient, deafening work. The rock continues its slow, unfinished becoming. The thing in the basin, if it was ever there, does not reappear. The moss leans toward the walls. The blood you drew from your hand is still somewhere in the mist, still rising, still not falling.

You are in a place that is not yet a place. A pocket of almost-existence, suspended in the throat of a waterfall that has not yet learned to fall, at an elevation so high that the air should be thin but isn't, in a space so small that it should feel claustrophobic but doesn't, because the walls are still deciding how far apart they want to be.

The sound is everything. The sound is the world, still being made.
