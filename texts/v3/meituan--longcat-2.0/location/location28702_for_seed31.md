# The Weeping Plateau

You come up through the last of the cloud layer and the world opens.

The plateau is not large — perhaps two hours walking across its widest axis — but it feels immense because of the sky. The elevation puts you above most weather. Below, the clouds move in slow rivers, but up here the air is still and warm, almost thick, carrying a fine mist that never quite becomes rain and never quite stops. Your skin is damp within minutes. Your clothes take on the weight of water.

The rock is dark. Not black — a deep, wet charcoal, the color of iron that has been left in the rain. It is young rock, freshly broken from some deeper uplift, and it has not yet learned to be smooth. Every surface is angular. The plateau's edge drops away in clean fractures, geometric and sharp, as if the land were cleaved by a blade rather than worn by time. There are no rounded boulders here, no softened corners. The stone looks like it was broken yesterday.

And it weeps.

Water seeps from every joint and fissure in the rock. Not flowing — seeping. The stone is so saturated that moisture beads on vertical faces, runs in thin sheets down cliff sides, pools in every depression and hollow. The ground underfoot is firm but wet, a dark crust of mineral and moss that gives slightly, like stepping on a sponge that has been wrung out. In the low places, shallow pools form — not ponds, not lakes, just flat expanses of still water that mirror the sky so perfectly they look like holes cut in the world. You cannot always tell where the rock ends and the water begins.

The warmth is strange at this height. It comes from below — a deep, steady heat that rises through the stone and keeps the air temperate even where it should be cold. The water that seeps from the rock is warm to the touch. Not hot. Just warm, like blood. It steams faintly in the still air, so that the whole plateau exists in a low haze of its own breath.

---

The vegetation grows in patches. Not scattered — *clustered*. Dense, lush colonies of moss and fern and low, waxy-leaved plants that seem to thrive on the warm, wet stone. They grow in the crevices, in the sheltered angles where two rock faces meet, in the lee of the structures. The moss is extraordinarily green, almost luminous, a green that seems to generate its own light in the grey air. The ferns are tall and arching, their fronds heavy with moisture, each tip carrying a bead of water that catches and holds the light.

But between the patches — bare rock. Long stretches of bare, wet, dark stone, clean and open. The vegetation does not spread. It holds its territory and stops, as if it has reached an agreement with the mineral world. The effect is of islands: green archipelagos in a dark sea, each one dense and self-contained, each one separated from the next by twenty or thirty paces of open stone.

There are no trees. Nothing with a trunk, nothing that reaches for the sky. Everything grows low and close to the rock, as if the wind — though you cannot feel it — might punish anything that rises.

---

The settlement sits near the plateau's center.

It is not large. Perhaps forty structures, built from the same dark stone as the plateau itself, so that the buildings seem to grow from the ground rather than rest upon it. The construction is simple but deliberate. Walls of fitted stone, dry-stacked without mortar, the joints tight enough that you cannot fit a blade between them. Roofs of flat stone slabs, supported by thicker walls, low and heavy. The buildings are single-story, broad rather than tall, with small openings for doors and smaller openings for windows. Some have no windows at all.

The layout is organized. Not a grid — something more organic, but clearly intentional. The structures are arranged along paths that follow the natural contours of the plateau, curving around the larger rock formations, bending to avoid the deepest pools. The paths themselves are paved with flat stones, fitted together with the same care as the walls. In places, the paths cross the shallow pools on low stone bridges — simple arches, no railings, just enough width for a single person to walk across.

There are no roads. No carts. No signs of animal husbandry — no pens, no troughs, no harnesses. The settlement was built by hand, for hands. The doorways are sized for people. The paths are sized for people. Everything speaks of a community that carried what it needed on its back and built what it could from what was beneath its feet.

The tools that built this place were simple. You can see it in the stone — the surfaces are dressed but not polished, shaped with percussion rather than abrasion. There are marks: small, regular pits where the stone was struck and flaked. No metal. No fine edges. Just patient, repeated impact, the work of hands and stone on stone.

Some of the structures have fallen. Not collapsed — *settled*. Walls that have shifted a few degrees off vertical, roofs that have sagged but not broken, doorways that are now slightly too narrow because the frame has moved. But the stone is sound. The joints still hold. The buildings are not decaying. They are simply... resting. Finding their final position.

---

The silence is the first thing you notice when you stop moving.

Not quiet. Silence. The absence of sound rather than the presence of soft sound.

There are no birds. No insects. No rustling of small bodies in the undergrowth. The ferns do not sway. The moss does not whisper. The water does not lap or trickle or drip — it seeps, it pools, it steams, but it makes no sound. The air does not move. There is no wind in the grass because there is no grass, and no wind in the ferns because the ferns are still, and no wind in the air because the air is warm and heavy and does not circulate.

The only sound is your own. Your footsteps on the wet stone. Your breathing. The faint creak of your clothing as you move. These sounds are shockingly loud in the stillness, sharp and close, as if the world has been muted and only you remain audible.

You stand in the center of the settlement and listen, and the silence listens back.

---

The light is even. Not bright, not dim — a flat, diffuse illumination that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere. The cloud layer below reflects light upward, and the mist within the air scatters it, so that there are no shadows. No sharp contrasts. Everything is visible, everything is equally lit, and nothing casts a dark enough shadow to hide in. The world is fully, evenly, relentlessly visible.

The sky above is pale. Not overcast — there are no clouds up here, just a high, pale vault of atmosphere, the blue faded to something closer to white. The sun is somewhere in it, but you cannot point to it. The light has no source. It simply is.

---

And nothing changes.

This is the thing that takes longest to understand, because at first you do not think to look for it. But after an hour, or two, or three — after you have walked the paths and crossed the bridges and touched the weeping walls and stood in the doorways of the empty structures — you begin to notice.

The water seeps at the same rate. The same beads of moisture on the same rock faces. The same depth in the same pools. The steam rises in the same slow curtains. The moss is the same height, the same color, the same density. The fern fronds hang at the same angle. The shifted walls have not shifted further. The sagged roofs have not sagged more.

Nothing is growing. Nothing is eroding. Nothing is settling, or drying, or warming, or cooling. The warmth is the same warmth it was an hour ago. The silence is the same silence. The light is the same light.

You could leave and return — if there were a leaving, if there were a returning — and find everything exactly as it was. The water weeping from the stone. The green islands in their dark sea. The low stone buildings with their small doors and their patient, settled walls. The silence. The stillness. The warm, wet, even light.

The plateau does not change because it has already become what it is. It is not waiting. It is not in transition. It is not young in the sense of *becoming* — it is young in the sense of *recently arrived*, fully formed, complete. The rock was broken and the water found its paths and the moss found its crevices and the people built their walls and then — equilibrium. A state of perfect, stable, unchanging balance.

The water will weep forever, or what passes for forever in a place where time has no visible effect. The moss will hold its green. The stone will keep its sharp edges. The buildings will stand in their slight, settled tilts. The silence will hold.

You are the only thing on the plateau that moves. You are the only thing that changes. And standing there, in the warm mist, on the dark stone, surrounded by the green islands and the still pools and the low stone walls, you understand that this is not a place that was abandoned.

It is a place that was *finished*.
