# The Weep

A cavity no larger than a chapel, buried in the mountain's last good bone before the peak dissolves into wind-scoured granite.

The walls are weeping. Not water — nothing here is water. A thin mineral brine, heavy with zinc and copper salts, seeps from hairline fractures in the rock face, threading down through centuries of accumulated crust. The deposits have grown into something that demands to be called foliage. Pale green fronds of malachite, each one no wider than a hair, have built themselves upward in dense overlapping curtains, layered so thick the original stone is entirely forgotten behind them. In places they've achieved the complexity of ferns — branching, re-branching, each tip terminating in a droplet of concentrated solution that hangs and does not fall, surface tension holding it in a lens that catches the only light this place receives: a faint chemical luminescence from deep within the walls, blue-green, the color of deep-sea things that have never known the sun.

The air is cool and tastes of metal. It does not move. It has not moved in a long time. The chamber has reached its own atmosphere — saturated, balanced, sealed by the sheer density of its own growth. Every surface is occupied. The malachite fronds pack the space so tightly that passing a hand through them requires parting them like a bead curtain, and they do not spring back. They hold the shape you leave. The floor — if there is a floor — is a compressed mat of older growth, generations deep, crusted over, walking on packed chalk.

The loud part is the dripping. Each fracture weeps at its own rhythm, and the sound is enormous in this small space. Mineral drops strike mineral surfaces and ring at a high pitch, a constant chime, layered and overlapping, never synchronizing, never resolving. It sounds like a workshop that forgot to close. And in a way, that's what this is — the scattered remnants of something built: a rusted conduit here, a collapsed scaffold there, iron bolts consumed by verdigris until they are more plant than metal. Someone once piped solutions into this cavity. Someone once tended something. Now the system runs itself, or seems to, though nothing here changes. The growths are still. The drips fall at their measured pace. The light holds its level. The air does not circulate. The chamber is in a state of completed process, every reaction balanced against its product, every surface satisfied. It has been this way long enough that the word *equilibrium* feels like a living thing, like the room's actual name.

There is no animal life. There is no rot, no fungi, no bacterial film. The chemistry here does not permit it. What grows is mineral only, crystalline and sharp and beautiful in the way that dangerous things sometimes are — the malachite dust is toxic to touch, and the air in concentration would sear soft tissue. But there is nothing soft here. Nothing has come to disturb the balance. The pocket holds itself, complete, sealed at its upper end by a fall of native rock, sealed below by its own weight of accumulated growth, a world the size of a room that has perfected its own small extinction and called it peace.
