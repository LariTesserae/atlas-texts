# The Suspended Works

You arrive at the edge of a vastness.

The plateau has no name that anyone speaks aloud — not because it is forbidden, but because the name keeps changing in the mouth, rearranging its own syllables like a machine that hasn't decided what it is yet. The locals, if you can call them that, call it simply *the Height* or *the Hum*, and they say these words with the flat affect of people describing the weather in a place where the weather has been the same for eleven thousand years.

And it has. That is the first wrong thing.

---

**The air** is cold and wet and *thick* — not with humidity exactly, but with the feeling of having just missed something. The atmosphere is saturated. Every surface is weeping. The rocks glisten with a film so thin it has no depth, and when you touch a boulder your hand comes away damp but the boulder is not wetter where you touched it; it is uniformly, impossibly damp, as though wetness is a property of the material itself and not something that happened to it. Condensation forms on your eyelashes. Your breath does not mist — the air is too close to your temperature for that — but it *joins*, seamlessly, as though the boundary between your lungs and the world has been negotiated away.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It seeps from joints in the basalt, pools in depressions that have no business holding water on a plateau this high, and runs in thin sheets down faces of rock that were clearly — *geologically* — cut recently. The erosion is young. The landscape looks *fresh*, like a wound that has healed just enough to form skin but not enough to lose the tenderness. Spurs and arêtes, knife-edged ridges, gullies with sharp V-shaped profiles — this is terrain that hasn't decided what it wants to be. The water hasn't had time to soften it. The water is working on it, right now, but *slowly*, so slowly, as though the plateau is resisting its own aging.

The sound of water is constant. Not dramatic — no waterfalls, no rivers — but the low, omnipresent *seep and trickle and drip* of ten million small surrenders. Rock giving up its hold on dryness. The sound is a texture more than a melody, and it underlies everything, a basal note in a chord that never resolves.

---

**The vegetation** is where the wrongness begins to *veer*.

It is lush. Absurdly, improbably lush for a cold plateau at this altitude. Ferns with fronds the width of a man's arm uncurl from every crevice. Moss — but not the timid moss of temperate forests, not the polite green fuzz that suggests a healthy ecosystem — this is *aggressive* moss, architectural moss, moss that builds in columns and buttresses and vaulted chambers, that covers boulders so completely you cannot find the stone beneath, that hangs from ledges in curtains three meters deep and sways in a wind you cannot feel. Liverworts spread across wet rock in sheets of such saturated green they appear to be *glowing*, as though lit from below. Horsetails rise in dense stands, their jointed stems clicking against each other in the faintest air movement with a sound like distant construction — like someone, far away, assembling something out of metal rods.

But there are no flowers. No seeds. No fruit. No insects to pollinate them even if they existed.

No insects at all.

No birds. No worms in the soil — and there *is* soil, dark and rich and smelling of something between petrochemical and loam. No spiders in the moss. No bacteria that you can detect, though the plants are clearly doing *something* metabolically because they are growing, visibly, perceptibly, you can almost see the fronds unfurling if you watch long enough. But the growth is strange. It doesn't spread. It doesn't colonize new ground. It simply *thickens*, in place, like a recording of growth played on loop, the same frond extending and extending and extending without ever reaching the next rock.

The ecology is a closed loop with no consumers. No decomposers. The plants feed on water and light and the minerals in the basalt, and they grow, and they do not die, and they do not reproduce, and they do not change. They are *lush* the way a painting of lushness is lush — total, saturated, and fundamentally still.

---

**The city** is the second wrong thing, and it is enormous.

It sprawls across the plateau in a configuration that suggests planning but not *design* — as though someone laid out the infrastructure first and then built whatever was needed wherever the pipes and conduits happened to terminate. Towers of riveted iron and poured concrete rise in clusters, connected by elevated walkways and enclosed bridges and pipes of every diameter, from capillaries to arteries, and all of them are *full*. You can hear the fluid moving inside them — not water, not oil, something thicker, something that moves with a peristaltic rhythm, as though the pipes themselves are swallowing.

The buildings are industrial. Smokestacks — hundreds of them, thousands — rise in irregular ranks, and they are *working*. They exhale. But what they exhale is not smoke. It is vapor, clean and white and smelling faintly of ozone and hot metal, and it rises in columns that do not dissipate so much as *accumulate*, forming a permanent overcast layer at about two hundred meters that diffuses the light into a flat, shadowless, directionless glow. The light is normal in the sense that you can see clearly, but it is wrong in the sense that it comes from everywhere and nowhere, and nothing casts a shadow, and you cannot find the sun even though you know it must be up there somewhere, behind the Works' own weather.

Because the Works *has* its own weather. The plateau is vast enough — hundreds of kilometers across, maybe more, the edges lost in haze and distance — that the thermal output of the city generates its own microclimate. The smokestacks produce the overcast. The overcast produces the condensation. The condensation feeds the moss and the ferns and the seeping rocks. The plants produce — something. Oxygen, presumably, though the air doesn't taste oxygen-rich. It tastes *used*. Recycled. Like breathing in a room that has been breathed in before.

The machinery is loud. Not the grinding, clanging loudness of a factory floor — this is deeper, more pervasive, a *tonal* loudness, a chord of industrial sound that fills the air the way the moss fills the crevices. The hum of turbines. The rhythmic compression of pistons in engines the size of buildings. The whine of electrical transformers, thousands of them, stepping power up and down across a grid that has no visible source and no visible destination. The sound is so constant that it becomes a kind of silence — you stop hearing it the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat, except that if you leave the city and return, you notice it again, and you realize it was *always there*, that the silence you remembered was just your brain editing it out.

And the city is *empty*.

Not abandoned. *Empty*. There is a difference. An abandoned city has the residue of departure — doors left open, tools set down mid-task, the archaeology of sudden leaving. This city has none of that. The machines run. The pipes flow. The smokestacks exhale. The lights — there are lights, electric lights, arc lamps and incandescent bulbs in fixtures of brass and iron — are on, and they illuminate corridors and rooms and vast open floors of machinery that are *pristine*. No dust. No rust. No wear. The concrete is not cracked. The iron is not corroded. The glass in the windows — and there are windows, thousands of windows, in buildings that clearly were designed for occupants — is not broken and not dirty and not clean. It simply *is*, in a state of perfect, unmaintaining maintenance.

There are no people. There have never been people, or if there were, they left no trace, or they left traces so subtle that they have been absorbed into the Works' own aesthetic. The city was built for human scale — the doorways are the right height, the stairs the right depth, the railings the right thickness for a hand — but there is no *humanity* in it. No graffiti. No wear patterns on the floors. No preferred paths worn into the concrete. The city is a body without a ghost, a machine that operates itself, a factory that produces —

What does it produce?

This is the question that the plateau does not answer. The machinery runs. The pipes flow. The smokestacks exhale. The grid hums. But there is no product. No raw materials arriving. No finished goods leaving. The Works takes in water and air and light and basalt and it *processes* them, and the output is — more Works. More pipes. More towers. More moss. More of the same, endlessly, in a cycle that has no beginning and no end and no purpose that you can identify.

---

**The stillness** is the third wrong thing, and it is the worst.

Because nothing changes. Not slowly — *not at all*. The water seeps at the same rate it seeped yesterday and the day before and the day before that, for millennia. The moss grows at the same rate, thickening in place, never spreading, never dying. The machines run at the same speed, the same rhythm, the same pitch. The smokestacks exhale the same volume of vapor. The overcast sits at the same altitude. The light has the same quality. The temperature is the same — cold, but not bitterly so, a steady 4°C that does not vary by so much as a tenth of a degree, day or night, season or year.

There are no seasons. The plateau is too high for the lowland weather systems to reach, and the Works' own climate is too stable to generate variation. There is no wind, or rather there is a wind — you can feel it on your face, a steady 8 km/h from the northwest — but it is the *same wind*, the same speed, the same direction, the same temperature, the same humidity, always. The ferns do not sway differently. The moss curtains do not shift. The vapor from the smokestacks rises in the same columns, and the overcast does not thicken or thin, and the light does not brighten or dim.

Time passes. You can feel it passing — your body ages, your watch ticks, your thoughts move forward — but the plateau does not participate. It is in equilibrium. A frozen equilibrium, a dynamic stasis, a system that has found its lowest energy state and *stayed there*, not because it cannot change but because it has *already changed into everything it is going to be* and the change is complete and the result is *this*: a cold, wet, loud, green, empty, industrial plateau that runs forever and produces nothing and needs no one and will not, cannot, *must not* be disturbed.

---

You stand at the edge of the Works and you listen to the hum and the seep and the click of horsetails, and you look out over the vastness — the towers and the pipes and the moss-covered ridges and the seeping rock and the flat white sky — and you understand, with a certainty that sits in your chest like a stone, that you are not the first person to stand here and you will not be the last, and that the plateau will be exactly the same when you leave as it was when you arrived, and that this is not a comfort.

The moss is very green. The water is very wet. The machines are very loud.

Nothing moves that is not already moving. Nothing changes that is not already changed.

The Works works.
