# The Hushplate

---

You arrive, if arriving is the word, on a high shelf of black stone that extends in every direction until it doesn't.

The elevation is not something you feel in your knees — you feel it in the silence that should exist but doesn't. The air is thin and carries almost nothing. Cold sits on your skin like a second skin, dry and mineral. You are above the weather, but not above the water: moisture beads on every surface in a slow, perpetual sweat, as though the rock itself is feverish. It does not run. It does not pool. It simply is — a gloss, a sheen, a constant damp that never dries and never accumulates. Touch a boulder and your fingers come away dark and wet. Touch it again an hour later and the same. The water arrives from nowhere you can identify. There are no clouds here, or rather, there is one cloud, low and flat and impossibly far below you, a grey sea that fills the world beneath the shelf's edge and reflects nothing back.

The light is wrong. Not dark, not bright — dim in the way a room is dim when someone has placed a cloth over a lamp. The sky is the color of old pewter, directionless, sourceless. There is no sun you can find, no point of origin for the illumination. It comes from everywhere and therefore from nowhere, and it never changes. Shadows exist but they are shallow, reluctant, as though the world can't commit to having a dark side.

And the sound — the sound is the first thing that tells you this place is broken.

It is loud. Not the loudness of a waterfall or a storm but the loudness of a single sustained tone, a drone, low and resonant, that seems to rise from the stone itself. It is not coming from any direction. It is simply here, the way gravity is here. Stand still and it fills you. Move and it does not change. Cover your ears and you feel it in your teeth, in the fluid of your eyes, in the hollow spaces behind your sternum. It is not unpleasant, exactly. It is the sound of a world holding its breath and never letting go.

---

The stone is old. That is the first honest thing about it. Whatever else is happening here — and much else is happening — the erosion tells a story of deep time. The plateau has been carved by wind that no longer varies in speed or direction into long, sweeping channels and hollows, smooth as the inside of a bowl. In places the rock has been worn into arches and thin blades that should collapse but don't. There is a rhythm to the weathering, a pattern repeated across the shelf: a shallow depression here, a knife-ridge there, a basin, a bridge, a basin again, as though some patient intelligence once ran its fingers across wet clay and then left the clay to cure forever.

The water collects in these depressions in thin, black mirrors. They do not ripple. The drone does not disturb them. In them you can see the sky, or rather you can see something that resembles the sky but isn't quite — the colors are shifted, the proportions off by just enough to make your hindbrain itch. One pool, roughly thirty paces from the first structure, reflects a version of the plateau that is not the one you're standing on. The rocks in the reflection are shaped differently. There is a tree in the reflection, a single dark shape with branches like a nervous system. There are no trees here. There have never been trees here. You look away and when you look back the tree is still there, patient, dripping something that isn't water.

---

Sparse. The vegetation is sparse in the way that stars are sparse in a dark sky — each one an event. A low, grey-green lichen clings to the north sides of boulders in patches no larger than your hand, and it has the texture of felt, and it is cold to the touch even when everything else is merely cold, as though it carries its own private winter. In the deeper erosion channels, where the stone cups moisture against its body, there grows something that is not quite moss and not quite fungus, a thing that spreads in thin filaments and puts up occasional stalks topped with translucent beads. The beads are the size of peppercorns. They are slightly warm. If you hold one to the drone, the drone changes — just for a moment, just in that one bead — into something almost like a voice. Then you blink and it is the drone again and the bead has gone cold and you are not sure you heard anything at all.

There is one area, roughly in the center of the accessible shelf, where the filaments have formed a mat about four meters across and perhaps two centimeters thick. It is the greenest thing in this world. It is the only thing that grows in a direction — up — and it does so with a purposefulness that the stone around it lacks. Touch it and it does not move. But you have the sense that it notices.

---

The structures.

They are scattered. Six that you can find, though the number changes depending on where you stand and whether you count the half-structure that may be natural or may be the beginning of something that stopped. They are built of the local stone, dry-fitted, without mortar or tool marks. They are small — most are barely tall enough to kneel inside. Their walls are thick and their interiors are dark and they are empty. Not empty as in unused: empty as in *hollowed*. The insides have been smoothed to a concave finish, as though something needed a place to sit that fit its body exactly, and the body was round.

The structures have no doorways. You enter them through openings in the roof, or through gaps where a stone has shifted, or — in one case — through an opening that exists on one side of the building but not the other, so that the structure is simultaneously sealed and open depending on where you stand. Inside, the drone is louder. Inside, the air is warmer by a degree you can't measure but can feel. Inside, the walls are damp with condensation that has no source, and if you press your ear to them you can hear, beneath the drone, a second drone, lower, slower, as though the building itself is breathing on a timescale of hours.

There are marks on the floors. Not writing. Not symbols. Impressions — shallow, curved, repeated — as though something heavy and curved has been set down and picked up and set down again, thousands of times, in the same places, until the stone itself learned the shape and kept it.

No one has lived here for a long time. No one has lived here recently. The distinction may not be meaningful.

---

The fauna.

You see nothing for a long time. The emptiness is the dominant fact of the landscape — the vast, low, wind-carved shelf stretching to edges you can't quite resolve, the thin air, the drone, the stillness of the black pools. The density of life is near zero. You could stand for an hour and see nothing move except the bead-plants turning, slowly, toward something you cannot identify.

Then you see the tracks.

They are in the damp stone of a shallow basin, and they are not tracks so much as *slight discolorations* — darker lines where the moisture has been disturbed, forming patterns that might be footprints if the thing that made them had three toes, or four, or seven, or a number that changes depending on which print you count. They lead from the edge of the basin to the center, where they stop. Not gradually. Not with a fading-out. They simply cease, as though whatever made them learned to be something that doesn't touch the ground.

You find, near the third structure, a single feather. It is long, longer than your forearm, and it is the color of the sky — that pewter, that non-color — and it is damp, and when you hold it up to the light you can see that its structure is wrong. The barbs don't connect to a central shaft. They connect to each other, in a mesh, a weave, a net with no entry point and no exit. It is not a feather. It is a piece of something that decided to look like a feather. It is warm. It weighs almost nothing. When you set it down it does not fall; it settles, as though the air is thicker near the ground, as though gravity increases by degrees the closer you get to the stone.

You do not see the animal. You may never see the animal. But sometimes, at the very edge of vision, something moves — not quickly, not slowly, but *reluctantly*, as though motion is a concession it makes only when absolutely necessary. And the drone shifts, just slightly, in its direction.

---

The sky does not change. The light does not change. The drone does not change. The water does not change. This is the deepest strangeness of the place, deeper than the pools that reflect other worlds, deeper than the buildings with doors that exist and don't exist: *nothing here is moving toward anything*. The landscape has achieved a state of permanent equilibrium, not because it is at rest but because it has passed through every possible change and come out the other side into a condition of total, frozen balance. The wind that carved the stone is still blowing — you can see its work — but it is blowing at the same speed it has always blown, in the same direction, and it will continue to do so without variation until the stone is dust and the dust is atoms and the atoms are whatever comes after atoms. The water weeps and does not flow. The lichen grows and does not spread. The drone sounds and does not resolve. The pools reflect and do not ripple.

Time is not stopped here. Time is *satisfied*. It has found a configuration it likes and it is keeping it.

You realize, eventually, that you are not cold anymore. The air has not warmed. You have simply arrived at the same temperature as everything else. You are equilibrating. The damp has found its way into your clothing, your skin, the spaces between your thoughts. You are becoming part of the balance.

The feather, wherever you left it, is gone. Or it is where you left it but it is also in your pocket. Or it was never there. The drone does not comment.

---

Somewhere far below, the cloud sea breathes. Somewhere far above, the pewter light holds steady. And here, on the shelf, in the damp and the drone and the thin green filaments and the empty round buildings and the tracks that stop and start for reasons that have nothing to do with you, the world continues to not-change, perfectly, forever, in a state of broken and absolute equilibrium.

You can stay. Many things have stayed. The stone remembers their shapes.

You can leave. The edge of the shelf is where you found it.

The drone does not care either way.
