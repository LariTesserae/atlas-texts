# The Warm Drown

---

The first thing is the warmth of the water. Then the weight of it. Then the sound.

You are standing—if standing is the word—in water to your chest. The water is not clear. It carries a fine suspension, a perpetual particulate haze that turns every beam of light into a pale column and every shadow into a wall. Visibility extends perhaps thirty meters in the clearest direction, and at that distance the world does not end so much as give up. The water simply eats the light and returns nothing. Somewhere beyond that threshold, things continue. You can hear them.

The warmth is wrong for the depth. You can feel that immediately. It is not sun-warmed surface water; it is the warmth of something generating heat from below, a geothermal persistence that has nowhere to dissipate because the volume is too great and the source too constant. The temperature sits at that precise point where the body stops fighting the water and starts to accept it, which is its own kind of danger. Your fingers prune. Your skin softens. The water finds its way into the seams of your clothing, the spaces between joints, the creases of your eyes. It presses against your eardrums with a constant, muffling insistence, and through that pressure come the sounds.

The sounds.

---

They arrive not as individual noises but as a single compound roar that your brain slowly, reluctantly, begins to parse. At the lowest register: a vibration you feel before you hear it, a frequency that makes the water shimmer against your collarbone and resonates in the fluid of your inner teeth. Above that: the rhythmic concussion of something large and mechanical, a cycle of pressure waves that arrives every four seconds like the breathing of a sleeping animal—*whom. Whom. Whom.*—each pulse accompanied by a brief upward surge in temperature, a pulse of warmer water rising from below. Higher still: the hiss of gas escaping through narrow apertures, the groan of metal under torsional stress, the crack and collapse of mineral formations too new and too brittle to hold their own weight. And over everything, a sound like a factory at full production: the clatter of conveyors, the ring of impact, the whine of something spinning very fast in a medium that resists it.

It is deafening. It is also, after a few minutes, almost legible. The sound has a structure. It is not chaos. It is a process running at tremendous speed, and the noise is the exhaust of that process, venting into the water in every frequency simultaneously.

---

The floor beneath your feet is sharp.

Not sharp like broken glass—sharp like *new*. The rock here has the unfinished quality of something recently formed: basalt shelves with edges that have not yet learned to round themselves, mineral deposits that jut in crystalline blades from the substrate, channels cut by currents that have not yet committed to a single path and so have cut several, a braided network of shallow grooves that redirect and merge and redirect again with a frequency that suggests the floor itself is still deciding what shape it wants to be. In places the rock has cracked along fresh fault lines, and from these seams rise plumes of superheated water, milky with dissolved metals—iron, copper, manganese, others—that precipitate on contact with the cooler ambient water and build chimneys. The chimneys grow rapidly, inches per hour, fragile towers of oxide and sulfide that reach toward nothing and collapse under their own weight and are replaced by new ones before the fragments have settled. The cycle is fast enough to watch. The floor is building itself and unbuilding itself simultaneously, a landscape caught in the act of becoming.

And from this floor, from every surface, from the chimneys and the cracks and the flat basalt shelves and the broken remnants of everything that has ever been built here, the vegetation rises.

---

It is not like kelp. It is not like coral. It is not like any terrestrial plant, though it has a verticality that invites the comparison, a reaching quality, a need to occupy space above the floor. The dominant forms are columnar—thick stalks, some as wide as your arm, some as wide as your torso, rising three or four meters before branching into a canopy that interlocks with its neighbors so thoroughly that the space above you is a continuous ceiling of growth, a lattice of living material through which the dim light filters in patterns that shift with the current.

The color is wrong. Not wrong like disease—wrong like *preference*. The plants have incorporated metals from the water into their tissues, and the metals have given them pigments that no chlorophyll-based organism should wear. Rust-red. Verdigris-blue. A deep, bruised purple-black that might be manganese or might be something the periodic table does not have a name for. The stalks are heavy with accumulated metal, dense enough that when the current pushes them they resist, bending slowly, and when the current relents they do not spring back—they hold their new shape, permanently deformed, and new growth continues from the bent position, so that the older columns are twisted into helical curves, metal-fatigued spirals that groan when the pressure changes.

Other forms grow between and among the columns. Mats of filamentous growth that spread across every available surface like a skin, sealing rock and metal and the detritus of dead columns under a living carpet that is warm to the touch—warmer than the water, as if the biological process itself generates heat. Curtain-like fronds that hang from the canopy and sway in the current, their edges serrated with crystalline deposits that rattle faintly against each other, adding a high, glassy note to the general roar. Bulbous growths that cling to the columns like tumors, translucent, pulsing with a slow internal rhythm that matches—or perhaps drives—the four-second thermal pulse of the deeper machinery.

There are no leaves. There are no flowers. There are no seeds that you can see. The vegetation propagates by fragmentation: pieces break off, drift in the current, lodge against a surface, and begin to grow. The water is full of these fragments—a slow blizzard of living particulate drifting downward and outward, each piece a potential colony, each surface a potential substrate. The density is extraordinary. Every square meter of floor, every vertical face, every structure, every chimney—everything that holds still long enough is colonized. The vegetation grows over itself, through itself, around itself, building up in layers so thick that the original surface is not merely covered but *entombed*, buried under centimeters of accumulated biological growth that is itself being buried by newer growth that is being buried by newer growth still.

And there are no animals. None. No fish in the water column. No crustaceans in the sediment. No worms, no snails, no drifting larvae, no parasites on the plants, no grazers, no predators. The water is warm and rich with organic matter and dissolved nutrients and it is *empty* of anything that swims or crawls or burrows. The vegetation exists without pollinators, without herbivores, without symbionts. It grows and dies and is replaced by its own offspring and the cycle continues in perfect, eerie solitude. The lushness is the lushness of a garden tended by no one, growing for no one, a biological abundance that serves no animal purpose and is witnessed by no animal eye. It is beautiful. It is deeply, fundamentally lonely.

---

The structures appear one at a time, separated by distances of fifty or a hundred meters, each one an island of industrial geometry in the organic tangle. They are not ruins. This is the thing that takes time to understand. They are active.

The first one you reach is a tower. Steel, or something that was steel before the vegetation began its slow conversion of it—the metal is thick with biological growth, coated in a centimeter-thrust of filamentous mat and metal-accumulating crust, but beneath that living sheath the structure is sound. It rises twelve meters from the floor, a vertical column of riveted plate, and at its summit it vents something: a steady, roaring stream of bubbles that rise through the water column and break against the vegetation ceiling with a sound like applause. The bubbles are warm. They carry a mineral load that precipitates in the cooler water above, building a slow halo of deposits on the underside of the canopy, a stalactite mirror to the chimneys below.

The tower has no visible power source. It has no controls. It has no markings that survive the biological overgrowth. But it is running. You can feel it—the four-second pulse that you have been feeling since you arrived, the *whom whom whom* that comes up through the floor and through the water and through the structure and into your bones. The tower is part of whatever process drives that pulse. It may be a pump. It may be a vent. It may be something that was designed to do one thing and has, in the absence of its operators, found a new function, or has had a new function imposed on it by the vegetation that has grown into and through its mechanisms, the roots and filaments threading through gearboxes and circuit boards and hydraulic lines, the biological and the mechanical so thoroughly intertwined that the distinction has become academic.

The second structure is a platform. Concrete and rebar, partially collapsed, tilted at a seven-degree angle toward the east. The concrete is dissolving—the warm water is slightly acidic, and it has been eating the platform for years, decades, the surface soft and pitted, the rebar exposed and corroded to half its original diameter. But the corrosion products have not been wasted. A species of vegetation has colonized the rebar specifically, a vivid arterial red, its filaments wrapped so tightly around the metal that removing them would require removing the rust as well. The plants are eating the platform and the platform is feeding the plants and the plants are holding the platform together. The structure is being destroyed and maintained by the same process.

On the platform: machinery. Tanks with walls thick enough to withstand pressure. Conveyors that move—actually move, right now, at this moment, carrying a load of fragmented organic matter from one end of the platform to the other, where it drops into a hopper that feeds something below. Pipes that carry fluids at different temperatures, some warm enough to have stimulated a halo of accelerated growth around them, others cold enough to have killed the vegetation in a bare circle, the dead material sloughing off in slow flakes. Indicator lights. Red. Amber. Green. Still blinking, still cycling through their patterns, powered by a source that has outlasted every assumption about battery life or generator fuel.

What is being produced here? The question is reasonable and the answer is not available. Something enters the tanks. Something is transformed. Something leaves through the pipes. The process has a logic—the conveyors move at a consistent speed, the pumps cycle on a regular schedule, the lights indicate states that imply a sequence—but the logic does not resolve into purpose. It is a factory that has forgotten what it makes, or a factory that is making something you do not have the framework to recognize as a product.

---

The current changes.

It does this without warning. The water that has been pressing steadily from the north shifts—not gradually but all at once, a rotation of ninety degrees that sends the curtain-fronds swinging and the columnar vegetation groaning against its own weight and the particulate haze reorganizing into new patterns of flow. With the current change comes a temperature fluctuation: a surge of warmer water from below, carrying a new load of dissolved minerals, and then a surge of cooler water from somewhere to the east, and then both at once, the two currents meeting and churning in a zone of turbulence that extends upward from the floor to the canopy.

In the turbulence, things move. Fragments of vegetation, broken free by the shifting current, tumble through the water in a slow cascade. Mineral fragments from a chimney that has just collapsed. Particulate that has been stirred up from the floor, a cloud of sediment that was settled and is now airborne, if airborne is the word for suspended in water. And—something else. A shape. Large, moving against the current with a purposefulness that the fragments do not have.

It is not an animal. There are no animals here. It is—

It is difficult to see. The particulate is thick in the turbulence and the light is dim and the shape is moving fast, but what you can make out is this: a mass of vegetation, roughly spherical, approximately two meters in diameter, rolling along the floor against the current, propelled by—what? Not by the current, obviously. Not by any visible mechanism. It is moving under its own power, or something is moving it, and as it rolls it sheds fragments that immediately begin to grow, trailing a wake of new vegetation like a comet's tail of green and red and verdigris.

You watch it until the current shifts again and the turbulence subsides and the haze resettles and the shape is gone, absorbed back into the murk.

---

The light has no source. Or rather, it has too many sources, none of them sufficient, all of them partial. There is a faint bioluminescence in the vegetation—a dim greenish glow that pulses with the thermal cycle, brightening slightly at each *whom* and fading in between, so that the entire landscape breathes with light, a slow rhythmic dimming and brightening that is visible only when you close your eyes and then open them again and catch the transition. There is a chemical luminescence in the vent plumes: orange and yellow, the colors of excited mineral ions, casting upward illumination that reaches the underside of the canopy and makes it glow like a lantern shade. There is the industrial lighting: harsh white beams from fixtures mounted on the structures, some still functional, some flickering, some broken and dark, all of them illuminating the particulate haze in long pale columns that swing slowly as the fixtures sway in the current, sweeping the landscape like searchlights in fog.

The combined effect is a dim, sourceless illumination that seems to come from the water itself, a glow without direction, a light that fills the space without revealing it. You can see what is close. You can sense what is distant. The middle ground is a murk of partial information, shapes that might be columns or might be structures or might be the rolling vegetation-mass returning for another pass, and you cannot tell, and the uncertainty is not temporary, it is the permanent condition of being here.

---

Time passes. The current shifts again. A chimney collapses somewhere to the south—you hear it, a prolonged crackling crash that sends a pressure wave through the water and a surge of warm mineral-laden fluid that the vegetation responds to immediately, the nearest columns bending toward the heat, the filamentous mats thickening visibly, the bulbous growths pulsing faster. Something on the platform cycles: a valve opens, a pipe disgorges a cloud of dark fluid that the current catches and disperses, staining the water for a hundred meters in every direction before the particulate haze absorbs it and the stain becomes the haze becomes the stain.

The sound does not stop. The sound has not stopped. The sound is the constant against which every other variable is measured, the roar that encompasses the mechanical and the biological and the geological, the pumps and the groaning vegetation and the collapsing chimneys and the grinding of the conveyors and the hiss of the vents and the deep subsonic pulse that you feel in your sternum and your teeth and the fluid of your eyes. It is the sound of a process that is too large to stop and too old to remember why it started and too integrated to be disassembled into its component parts, a process that has become the environment and the environment has become the process and the two are now the same thing.

---

You move through it. The vegetation parts around you and closes behind you. The water carries you and resists you in equal measure. The floor shifts under your feet—new rock, sharp rock, rock that is still deciding its final shape. The sound presses against you from every direction. The light dims and brightens with the thermal pulse. The warmth seeps deeper into your body with every minute.

And the place continues. The tower vents. The platform processes. The vegetation grows. The chimneys build and collapse and build. The current shifts and shifts and shifts again, each shift rewriting the local conditions, each shift triggering a cascade of biological and mechanical responses that trigger further shifts, a feedback loop of environment and process and growth and dissolution that has been running for longer than you can determine and shows no sign of reaching equilibrium.

It is young. It is very old. It is forming. It is dissolving. It is a factory. It is a garden. It is a drowned landscape. It is a landscape that has become the water and the water has become the landscape and the distinction was always arbitrary and is now irrelevant.

The sound is everywhere. The warmth is everywhere. The green and red and verdigris growth is everywhere, growing on everything, growing through everything, growing into the spaces between things and filling them with life that is not animal and not mineral and not mechanical but somehow, impossibly, all three at once.

You are standing in the warm drown. You are standing in the roar. You are standing in a place that is making itself and unmaking itself at the same speed, in the same motion, with the same sound, and the sound is deafening, and the sound is the sound of everything happening at once, and the sound does not stop.
