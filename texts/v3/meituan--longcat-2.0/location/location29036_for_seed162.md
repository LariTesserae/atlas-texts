# The Uplands of Unmaking

---

You come upon it gradually, or perhaps you have always been inside it and only now begin to notice.

The first thing is the warmth — not the thin, bright warmth of altitude, but a dense, close warmth, the warmth of something breathing. The air at this elevation should bite. Instead it clings. It has weight and texture, like the air inside a greenhouse, or the air above a engine that has been running for a long time and has warmed the room around it. You can feel it on your skin: damp, faintly electric, carrying a smell that is mineral and green and something else — something like hot metal, like ozone, like the inside of a machine that has no casing.

The ground beneath you is young. That is the only word for it. The rock has not learned to be old yet. It has not been rounded or softened or taught the patience of erosion. It is sharp — freshly fractured, freshly uplifted, freshly *here*. The strata are visible in the exposed faces of ridges that rise around you like the ribs of something enormous and half-born: bands of dark basalt, pale quartzite, rust-red ironstone, all tilted at angles that suggest the earth shrugged and never finished settling. In places the rock is so new it still weeps — thin films of mineral-laden water seeping from fissures, not flowing so much as *exuding*, as though the stone itself is damp from being recently formed. The water does not gather into streams. It darkens the surface, evaporates, returns. A cycle measured in minutes rather than seasons.

And yet — the vegetation.

It should not be here. The elevation is too high, the rock too fresh, the soil too thin. But the vegetation does not seem to have received this information, or has decided to ignore it. It is everywhere. Not the sparse, wind-clipped vegetation you would expect at this altitude, but something *lush* — dense, layered, almost aggressive in its abundance. Ferns with fronds the size of your arm unfurl from cracks in the basalt. Mosses — if they are mosses — cover every surface in deep, saturated greens and blacks, thick as upholstery, springing slightly under your step. Higher up, things that might be trees but are not quite trees rise in tight clusters: slender, pale-barked, their canopies so interlocked that the light filtering through is not green but a dim, submarine amber, as though you are already underwater.

The canopy is the reason for the dimness. It is not a single canopy but many — layer upon layer, each one catching what little light penetrates the one above, so that by the time it reaches the ground the light is not so much *reduced* as *digested*. It has been processed. It arrives warm and diffuse and directionless, without shadows, without glare, a light that seems to emanate from the air itself rather than from any source above. You cannot find the sun. You are not sure there is a sun. The light is simply *here*, the way heat is simply here, the way the dampness is simply here — a property of the place, not a condition imposed from outside.

The density is the next thing you notice. Not just the density of the vegetation, but a density of *presence*. The air feels occupied. Every cubic meter of space seems to contain more than it should — more organisms, more processes, more *happening*. The ferns are not just growing; they are growing visibly, or almost visibly, their uncurling fronds moving with a speed that is just below the threshold of perception, so that you cannot quite catch them in the act but you know, the way you know someone is watching you, that movement is occurring. The mosses are not just photosynthesizing; they are *doing something* — a faint, rhythmic pulse in their surfaces, a slow contraction and expansion, as though the entire carpet of them is breathing. Insects — if they are insects — move through the understory in dense, swirling clouds, their bodies catching the dim light like sparks from a forge, their paths forming patterns that are almost geometric, almost purposeful, almost *meaningful*, before dissolving into chaos and reforming again.

And beneath all of this, or within all of this, or *as* all of this: the industry.

There are no structures. No buildings, no roads, no smokestacks, no machines in any form you would recognize. And yet the place is *industrial*. You can feel it in the rhythm of things — a rhythm that is not natural, or not only natural, but *mechanical*. The pulse of the mosses has a regularity that suggests a cycle, a process, a production line. The insects move in patterns that suggest logistics, supply chains, the movement of materials from one place to another. The water that seeps from the rock and evaporates and returns is not a hydrological cycle but a *cooling system*. The warmth is not geothermal but *waste heat*. The entire landscape is functioning as a factory — not a factory that was built, but a factory that *grew*, or *emerged*, or *became*, its processes distributed across every surface, every organism, every seeping fissure and pulsing moss-bed and swirling cloud of sparks.

The rock itself participates. In places, the exposed strata have a quality that is not quite natural — a regularity to the banding, a precision to the angles, that suggests *machining*. The quartzite faces are too smooth. The ironstone layers are too uniform. And in certain lights — or in certain moments, because the light here is not constant but *fluctuates*, dimming and brightening in slow waves that seem to correspond to nothing — you can see patterns in the rock that look like circuitry, like the traces on a printed board, like the layout of a factory floor rendered in mineral form. They are not carved. They are not etched. They are simply *there*, as though the rock crystallized this way, as though the geology of this place is also its technology.

The fauna is abundant, but it is not fauna as you understand it. The things that move through the understory — the spark-insects, the things that might be lizards but move with a mechanical precision that lizards do not possess, the larger shapes that you glimpse in the deeper dimness, moving slowly, heavily, like gantry cranes — they are not separate from the landscape. They are *components* of it. They do not eat, or if they do, what they eat is not organic matter but *process* — they consume the outputs of one cycle and produce the inputs for another. They are not alive, or not only alive. They are *functional*. They have roles. The spark-insects distribute something — heat, charge, information — through the canopy. The lizard-things patrol the moss-beds, their movements maintaining the pulse, keeping the rhythm regular. The large shapes in the deep dimness are doing something you cannot see, something that requires their mass and their slowness, something foundational.

And the quiet. The profound, almost absolute quiet. Despite the density, despite the abundance, despite the constant churn of processes and movements and cycles, the place is *silent*. Or nearly silent. There is a sound, but it is below the threshold of hearing — a vibration in the ground, in the air, in your bones. It is the sound of the factory running, the sound of the engine turning over, the sound of *production*, but it is too low or too high or too *other* to be heard as sound. You feel it as a pressure, a presence, a sense that the air itself is under tension, like the air before a storm that never comes, or the air inside a machine that is running just below its critical threshold.

The turbulence is the thing that holds it all together, or the thing that threatens to tear it apart — you cannot tell which. The landscape is not stable. It is not *unstable*, exactly, but it is *active* in a way that landscapes are not supposed to be. Ridges shift. Not quickly — not in any way you could watch — but between one moment and the next, the geometry of the place is slightly different. A peak that was to your left is now to your left and also slightly behind you. A valley that was deep is now shallow, or has moved, or has been *replaced* by a different valley with different proportions. The vegetation grows and dies and grows again in cycles that are too fast, too compressed, as though the forest is iterating on itself, trying configurations, discarding them, trying again. The spark-insects swarm and disperse and swarm in patterns that are not random but not predictable — chaotic in the mathematical sense, deterministic but unrepeatable, a turbulence that is not destructive but *generative*, each swirl producing new configurations, new arrangements, new possibilities.

This is the reality breakdown. It is not dramatic. It is not a tearing or a rupture or a visible seam in the fabric of things. It is simply that the rules are *local*. They apply here, in this particular volume of space, and they may not apply ten meters away, or they may apply differently, or they may apply in a way that contradicts what they were doing a moment ago. Distance is not reliable. Direction is not reliable. The relationship between cause and effect is not reliable — you will see the spark-insects swarm *before* the moss-pulse intensifies, and you will understand, without being able to explain how, that the swarm is the *cause* of the pulse, even though it happens first, even though causality is supposed to work the other way around. Time here is not a river but a *factory floor* — it moves in cycles, in batches, in production runs, and sometimes it loops, and sometimes it skips, and sometimes it runs in parallel, two or three or seven timelines occupying the same space, visible as faint overlays, ghost-images of what is happening and what has happened and what might happen, all present simultaneously, all equally real.

The dampness is part of this. The water that seeps and evaporates and returns is not just water. It is a *medium*. It carries information, or charge, or something for which there is no word — the thing that the factory processes, the thing that the factory produces. When it evaporates from the rock, it takes something with it — a pattern, a configuration, a *state* — and when it condenses on the canopy above, it deposits it, and the canopy uses it, and the canopy grows, and the canopy produces something new, which the mosses process, which the insects distribute, which the large shapes in the deep dimness consume, and the cycle begins again. The water is the conveyor belt. The dampness is the factory floor, wet with production.

You stand in the dim, warm, dense, quiet, churning vastness of it, and you understand — not with your mind but with your body, with your skin, with the bones that feel the subsonic hum — that this place is not a landscape. It is a *process*. It is not a place where things happen. It is a place that *is* things happening. It has no history, or its history is measured in cycles rather than years. It has no builders, or it built itself, or it is still building itself, or the distinction between builder and building does not apply here. It is young — geologically, ontologically, *actually* young — and it is vast, and it is warm, and it is damp, and it is dim, and it is quiet, and it is *working*.

You do not know what it is making.

You are not sure it knows either.

But the rhythm is steady, and the production continues, and the spark-insects swarm in their chaotic, generative patterns, and the mosses pulse, and the rock weeps its mineral water, and the large shapes move in the deep dimness, doing whatever it is they do, and the whole vast, impossible, untouched, industrial, organic, young, warm, damp, dim, quiet, turbulent, reality-breaking place turns over and over and over, like an engine, like a world, like a factory that has no product because it *is* the product, because the process is the point, because the making is the thing being made.

You stand in it.

You breathe it.

You are, for this moment, part of its production line.
