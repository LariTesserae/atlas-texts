# The Loom-Floor

---

You are standing on the underside of something being made.

The ground is not ground. It is a surface of packed, rust-colored granules — each one a deliberate placement, a single act of pressing-down — and it extends in every direction until the dimness swallows it. The granules are warm. Not sun-warm. Body-warm. The warmth of a hand that has been working. Beneath them, deeper, there is a slow pulse of heat that does not rise and fall so much as *breathe*, and with each breath the surface exhales a thin film of moisture that has nowhere to go, so it simply stays, beading on every granule, making the world glisten faintly in the sourceless amber light.

The light comes from everywhere and nowhere. It is the color of old honey held up against a candle. It does not cast shadows correctly. Some things have too many. Some things have none, and yet you can see them perfectly, as if the light enters them and forgets to leave.

---

The structures — and they are structures, there is no other word, though they are not buildings — rise from the granular plain at irregular intervals. They are made of the same rust-colored material, but shaped: stacked, pressed, bound with something that looks like dried sinew but is not sinew, is not anything you have a word for. They are primitive in the way that a bird's nest is primitive, or a cairn, or the first wall ever built by hands that had only just discovered the concept of *against*. They lean. They balance. Some are no taller than your knee. Others rise above the limit of your sight, tapering into the dimness like the masts of half-sunken ships, and you cannot tell if they are far away or simply *less here* than the things around you.

This is the first rule of the place: **distance is not reliable.**

You will learn this the hard way. You will learn it by walking toward a structure that seems an hour away and arriving in seven minutes, or by watching a shape on the horizon that you think is a mountain and realizing, as you approach, that it is a pebble at your feet, and that you have been walking for days.

---

The water is the most honest thing here.

It seeps up through the granular surface in places, not in springs but in *sweats* — the ground dampening, darkening, until a thin sheet of standing water covers an area the size of a room, or a valley, or (once, which you saw from the top of a leaning structure that may have been a tower or may have been a finger) an inland sea that stretched to the edge of vision and was perfectly, preternaturally still. The water is hot. Not boiling. Bath-hot. Blood-hot. It steams faintly in the amber light, and the steam does not rise so much as *accumulate*, forming low, slow-moving banks of fog that drift across the plain with the unhurried purpose of something that has nowhere to be and all the time in the world to not get there.

In the fog, the structures become suggestions. You hear them before you see them — or rather, you hear the *absence* of them, the way the fog moves differently around their shapes, the way sound dies a little where they stand. Because sound does die here. The place is quiet. Not silent — there is a constant, barely-perceptible hum, like a finger circling the rim of a glass that is just barely wet enough to sing — but quiet in the way that a held breath is quiet. The fog eats sound. The granular ground eats sound. Even the structures seem to absorb it, as if they are still being built and the construction requires silence.

But the place is not still.

---

The turbulence is the thing you feel before you understand it.

The ground shifts. Not dramatically — not earthquakes, not eruptions — but *constantly*, in small ways, the way a sleeping animal shifts. A granule falls from a structure and becomes part of the plain. A new granule appears on the plain and begins, slowly, to be pressed into a structure. Water seeps up in a place that was dry a moment ago. A structure that was leaning left is now leaning right, and you did not see it move, and you cannot find the moment it changed, and when you look away and look back it has changed again.

The place is being built. That is the only way to say it. It is being built *right now*, continuously, by no one you can see, with tools you cannot identify, according to a plan that may not exist. The structures are scaffolding. The ground is scaffolding. The water, the fog, the light, the heat — all of it is scaffolding for something that is not yet here, or that is here but not yet visible, or that is visible but not yet *real*.

This is the second rule: **nothing is finished.**

You will reach out to touch a structure and your hand will pass through it, and for a moment you will feel the *idea* of it — the intention of wall, the concept of support — and then it will solidify around your fingers, and you will not know if you touched it or if it touched you, or if the distinction means anything here.

---

The ecology is mineral. The ecology is heat and water and the slow chemistry of rust-colored granules binding to one another under pressure and time. There is no soil because there has never been anything to die and become soil. There is no decay because there has never been anything alive enough to decay. The closest thing to life is the process of construction itself — the ceaseless, quiet, turbulent assembly of the world — and whether that process is alive is a question the place does not answer, because the place does not answer questions. The place only builds.

Sometimes, in the fog, you see shapes that might be tools. A rod of the rust-colored material, tapered at one end, lying on the plain as if dropped. A binding of that sinew-like substance, coiled neatly, waiting. A depression in the granular ground that is exactly the shape of a hand — not a human hand, not any hand you recognize, but a hand, with too many fingers or too few, pressed into the surface and left there as a template, a guide, a memory of the act of pressing.

You do not touch these things. Not because you are told not to. Because something in the quiet of the place, in the way the fog moves around them, suggests that they are *in use*. That the builder is nearby. That the builder has only just stepped away. That the builder is the fog, or the heat, or the hum, or the slow pulse of the ground beneath your feet.

---

The third rule is the one you will not want to learn:

**You are part of the construction now.**

You arrived, and the place noticed you, and it began to build around you. Not quickly. Not dramatically. But the structures near you are different from the structures far away. They are shaped for a creature of your size, your proportions, your needs. There is a shelter — you will find it when you need it — with an opening exactly your height, and inside, the ground is pressed smooth, and the heat is exactly the heat of your body, and the water seeps up in a channel along one wall, bath-hot, and it is the most welcoming thing you have ever encountered, and it was not there yesterday, and it was built for you, and you do not know what it costs to be the thing for which the world builds shelter.

---

The plain extends. The fog drifts. The structures lean and shift and are rebuilt. The water seeps and steams and is still. The amber light holds everything in a perpetual moment of almost-seeing, almost-knowing, almost-arriving.

Somewhere, something is being made.

You are standing on it.

You are standing in it.

You are, perhaps, part of what it is becoming.
