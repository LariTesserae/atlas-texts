# The Damp Republic

---

You come upon it from the low ground, where the air first thickens.

At first it is only a change in the sound — a low, continuous hum that seems to rise from the ground itself, as if the earth were a struck bell still vibrating from some ancient impact. The mist gathers in the shallow valleys, not drifting but *sitting*, heavy and warm, and you realize the mist is not moving because it has nowhere to go. The air is saturated. Every surface sweats.

Then you see the walls.

They rise from the marsh-ground without clear foundation — or rather, their foundations are so old and so buried under subsequent construction that they have become geology. The lowest courses are a dark, wet stone that might be basalt or might be something else entirely, something that drinks light and returns only a dull, warm gleam. Water seeps from every joint. Not flowing — *seeping*. The walls weep. They have wept for so long that the stone itself has changed, mineral blooms crawling across its surface in pale, fungal fans, in crystalline efflorescences that crumble at a touch and regrow within the hour. This is not vegetation. This is mineral appetite. This is rock that has learned to feed on water and warmth and time, and grows accordingly — not alive, but not inert. Something simpler. Something between.

The city — if it is a city — extends to every horizon.

You understand this slowly. The structures are so vast and so densely packed that your eye, at first, reads them as terrain. Ridges. Escarpments. But the ridges have windows. The escarpments have stairs, or what were once stairs, now worn into smooth, water-carved ramps that descend into the warm murk. The scale is not architectural. It is continental. The buildings have been built and rebuilt so many times, by so many hands or mechanisms or growth-processes, that they have achieved a kind of equilibrium with the water and the heat and the weight of themselves. They have settled into their own forms. They are *established*. They are the shape that all that material, all that intention, all that time, inevitably produces.

And it is packed. It is absolutely packed. There is no space between. Structures lean against structures, interpenetrate, fuse. Arches span arches span arches. Walkways layer upon walkways layer upon walkways, some crumbling, some fresh, some mid-collapse, some mid-construction — because things are always collapsing and always being built, and the two processes have become indistinguishable. The density is total. Every cubic meter is occupied — by stone, by water, by air thick as soup, by the small, quick lives that have made their homes in every crack and channel.

The warmth comes from below. The ground is warm to the touch — not hot, not volcanic, but *warm*, like skin, like a body. The water that seeps up through the foundations is warm. The mist is warm. The whole place breathes at a steady, close temperature, and this warmth is what drives the constant, seething activity of the place. Things grow — not plants, never plants, but the mineral blooms, the crystalline fans, the biofilm slickness that coats every underwater surface in colors that shift between copper and violet and a deep, arterial red. Things grow fast here. Things dissolve fast. The water is slightly acidic, or slightly basic, or slightly *something*, and it eats the stone at a rate almost equal to the rate at which the stone regrows or is rebuilt. Balance. A chemical balance, maintained over millennia, producing a landscape that is simultaneously being destroyed and created at all times, in all places, at once.

The sound is enormous.

It is the sound of water dripping — but multiplied by every surface in a continental expanse, so that the individual drops merge into a continuous, shimmering hiss, like rain on a tin roof the size of a country. Beneath that: the hum. The deep, structural hum of the place itself, the resonant frequency of all that mass, all that packed stone and metal and whatever-else, vibrating with the accumulated stress of its own weight, its own growth, its own constant, turbulent revision. And above that: the calls.

The fauna.

They are everywhere. They are small and numerous and fast — things like beetles, things like crabs, things like neither. They skitter across the weeping walls in swarms that move like single organisms, flowing around obstacles, pooling in depressions, surging up vertical surfaces with a rippling, wave-like motion. They are blind, mostly. They navigate by vibration, by the humidity gradient, by the chemical taste of the water. They feed on the biofilms, on the mineral blooms, on each other. Their shells — if they are shells — click and scrape against the stone, adding a third layer to the sound: a dry, chitinous chatter beneath the hiss and the hum. In the air, other things move — not birds, not insects, but something between, something with too many wings or not enough, flying in the warm mist in erratic, spiraling paths, catching the small skittering things or being caught by larger versions of themselves. The food chain here is short and fast and vicious, and it turns over at a rate that matches the stone itself — grow, eat, die, dissolve, regrow.

You walk into the city. There is no other way to experience it. The ground is a continuous surface of worn stone and packed sediment and the compressed detritus of a million years of occupation — not soil, not exactly, but something like it, a dark, wet, mineral-rich substrate that squelches underfoot and is immediately reclaimed by the biofilm. The streets — the channels — the whatever-they-are — descend. The place is low. The place has always been low. Water seeks the lowest ground, and this is where the water has always come, and this is where the city has always been built, and the two facts are not separate. The city and the water are the same system. The streets are drainage channels. The plazas are reservoirs. The buildings are dams and aqueducts and cisterns and none of these things, simultaneously, because the original purpose of any given structure has been buried under so many iterations of construction and collapse that it no longer matters. It is all infrastructure now. It is all just *place*.

Light filters down through the mist in diffuse, directionless sheets. There are no sharp shadows. Everything is visible but nothing is clear. The warm glow of the stone — for the stone does glow, faintly, in the wettest places, a biological or chemical luminescence that pulses slowly, like breathing — mixes with the gray-white of the mist and produces a light that is neither day nor night but something in between, something perpetual, something that has not changed in longer than anyone can remember.

And the place changes constantly. A wall crumbles — not dramatically, not catastrophically, but *steadily*, a slow-motion avalanche of wet stone and biofilm and the small, panicked bodies of the skittering things, pouring into the channel below, where the water takes it, dissolves it, carries it away. And within hours, the mineral blooms have begun to fill the gap, and the biofilm has smoothed the surface, and the skittering things have returned, and the wall is being rebuilt — not by intention, but by accumulation, by deposition, by the same process that built the first wall, a thousand or a million years ago. And elsewhere, a new structure pushes up from the wet ground — a spire, a tower, a chimney — extruded by some deep process, some pressure from below, warm stone rising like toothpaste from a tube, cracking and cooling and weeping and immediately being colonized, immediately becoming part of the city, immediately becoming *terrain*.

This is the equilibrium. This is the state the place has settled into. It is not stable — it is *dynamic*, violently dynamic, a constant churn of creation and destruction that looks, from sufficient distance, like stillness. Like a standing wave. Like a river that is always the same shape despite being always different water.

The sound does not stop. The warmth does not fade. The water does not cease its weeping, its seeping, its slow, patient dissolution of everything the city builds and everything the city is. The small things skitter and fly and eat and die and are eaten and dissolved and regrown. The mist sits heavy and warm and close. The walls glow and crumble and rise and weep.

And the city endures. Not as a monument. Not as a ruin. But as a *process*. As a place that has become so thoroughly itself that it no longer needs a builder, no longer needs a name, no longer needs anything but water and warmth and time.

It is wet. It is warm. It is loud beyond endurance.

It is home to millions of small, quick, hungry lives.

It has been here forever.

It is still being built.
