# The Drowned City of Vael Morath

---

You stand on a causeway of fused basalt, and the city rises around you — not built so much as *grown*, or perhaps *exhaled* — from a plain of shallow, warm water that stretches in every direction to a horizon that curves just slightly too much, as though the world here is a little smaller than it should be, or you are a little larger.

The water is everywhere. It is the ground. It is the air's weight. It sits in a sheet perhaps ankle-deep across the open plazas, deeper in the channels between structures — waist-deep, chest-deep — and it is warm, body-warm, the temperature of blood or breath, and it steams faintly where it meets the darker stone of the buildings, so that the lower city is always half-hidden in a low, drifting fog that smells of iron and hot mineral salts and something older, something like the inside of a shell.

The buildings themselves are tall and dense, packed so closely that their walls nearly touch overhead, forming corridors and canyon-streets through which the fog moves like a slow river. They are made of a dark, glassy stone — not quite obsidian, not quite basalt — that has been worked but not carved, *persuaded* into shape, as though the material were once liquid and was convinced to stop flowing at the right moment. The surfaces are smooth and slightly warm to the touch, and they weep. Every wall, every pillar, every overhang is beaded with moisture that does not fall so much as *migrate* — sliding in slow, silver threads down to the water below, where it joins the general shallow sea with a sound like a thousand whispered consonants.

There is no plant life. Nothing green. Nothing soft. The city is mineral and metal and water and heat, and the only color comes from the stone itself — deep grays and blacks and the occasional vein of rust-red or sulfur-yellow where mineral deposits have been left to bloom in the damp air. In places, the walls have developed a patina of crystalline growths — not biological, not quite geological — thin, branching structures of salt and metal oxide that spread across the dark stone like frost, like circuitry, like the ghost of a forest remembered by something that never saw one.

The city is vast. You cannot see its edges. The structures rise and fall in a skyline that suggests districts, neighborhoods, zones of different character — here a cluster of tall, narrow towers connected by arched bridges at multiple heights; there a broad, low complex of interlocking domes, their surfaces dimpled with condensation; further on, something that might be a factory or a temple or both, its walls pierced by hundreds of small, round openings from which steam issues in slow, rhythmic pulses, like breathing.

And the fauna. The fauna is *overwhelming*.

They are in the water first — the shallow, warm water that covers everything. Things with too many legs, or no legs at all, or legs that are also mouths. Things that are flat and wide and undulate just beneath the surface, their backs breaking the water like the hulls of tiny, living boats. Things that cluster in the thousands in the deeper channels, their bodies translucent or iridescent or simply *dark*, moving in coordinated waves that suggest intelligence or instinct or something between. The water is never still. It is always *stirred*.

And they are on the walls. Things that cling. Things that crawl. Things with shells like ceramic, like enamel, like the caps of old teeth. They move slowly — everything here moves slowly — leaving trails of moisture or slime or something more viscous, and where they have passed in great numbers over long years, the stone has been worn into channels and grooves, so that the walls themselves record the patterns of their movement, a kind of writing in negative space.

And they are in the air. Not flying, exactly — *drifting*. Things with membranes, things with filaments, things that are more gas than solid, buoyed by the warm, humid air, trailing tendrils that catch the light from the city's few sources — the glow of heated metal in the factory-districts, the bioluminescent patches on certain walls where colonies of something have been *encouraged* to grow, the occasional flare of a vent releasing pressure from somewhere deep below. The air is thick with them. You breathe them in. You feel them settle on your skin, your lips, the surface of your eyes, and they are warm and slightly damp and they do not seem to mean you harm, but there are so *many*.

The sound is constant. The water moving. The steam venting. The slow, grinding shift of structures settling into the warm mud below. The clicking and scraping and wet, soft percussion of ten thousand — a hundred thousand — small bodies moving against stone, against each other, against the water. And beneath it all, a low hum, almost subsonic, that you feel in your chest more than you hear — the sound of the city's engines, or its heart, or the deep geological process that keeps the water warm and the air thick and the whole vast, slow, wet, living machine turning over and over in its own patient rhythm.

Because the city is slow. That is the thing you understand after you have been here for a time. Nothing hurries. The water rises and falls on a cycle that might be tidal or might be something else — a breathing, a pulse — but it takes hours to notice the change. The creatures move at the speed of growth, of crystallization, of erosion. The buildings themselves seem to be *settling*, slowly, incrementally, finding their final positions in the warm mud, and the people — yes, there are people, or something like people, tall and slow-moving and damp-skinned, who navigate the causeways and channels with the unhurried confidence of those who have never known urgency — the people move as though time here is a different substance, thicker, more viscous, more forgiving.

The light is normal. That is perhaps the strangest thing. After the warmth and the wet and the overwhelming *presence* of so much life in a place with no plants, no soil, no sun-driven ecology — the light is simply *there*, diffuse and even, coming from no single source, as though the air itself has a faint luminescence, or as though the sky, wherever it is above the fog and the steam and the drifting things, is simply a uniform, gray-white brightness that asks nothing and gives everything and never changes.

You are high up. You understand this now. The city sits on a plateau, or a mountain, or a structure so large it functions as geography, and below — far below, beyond the edges of the city where the buildings thin and the water deepens — there is a drop. A cliff. An edge. And beyond that edge, more water, more fog, more of the same warm, shallow, living sea, stretching out and out and out to a horizon that curves wrong, that suggests you are inside something, or on top of something, or that the world here is not a sphere but a bowl, and you are somewhere near its rim, looking out across the interior of a vast, warm, wet, slowly turning thing that is not quite a planet and not quite a machine and not quite alive but is, undeniably, *inhabited*.

The city of Vael Morath. The drowned city. The living city. The city that breathes steam and weeps mineral water and hums with the sound of ten million small lives moving slowly through their slow, warm, wet, patient days.

You take a step forward on the causeway. The water accepts your foot with a soft, warm pressure. Something small and many-legged skitters across the surface ahead of you, its legs dimpling the water into a brief, radiating pattern of rings. Above, a drifting thing trails a filament that catches on your shoulder, holds for a moment, then releases.

The city continues. The city has always continued. The city will continue long after you have learned its rhythms, or left, or been absorbed into its warm, slow, mineral patience.

The water steams. The walls weep. The engines hum.

Welcome to Vael Morath.
