# The Gorge of Unfinished Making

---

You arrive at the lip of it before you know you've arrived.

The ground simply stops. Not a cliff — cliffs have a geology you can read, a story of pressure and time. This is a wound. The rock face drops away at an angle that feels wrong, too steep and too clean, as if the mountain was opened yesterday by something with a very sharp tool. The edges are still bright. Not weathered. Not rounded. The fracture surfaces catch what little light exists and throw it back in flat, mineral glints — feldspar, mica, something glassy and black that might be obsidian or might be something that hasn't decided what it is yet.

The gorge is narrow. Forty feet across at the top, less than twenty at the bottom, and the bottom is eight hundred feet down. You know this the way you know the dimensions of a room — by the way sound moves, by the way the air changes. It is a space with walls. A space with a ceiling, if you count the perpetual haze of steam and mist that bands across the upper third like a low cloud that has forgotten how to leave.

It is dark. Not night-dark — there is no night here, no day either, just a permanent grey dimness, the light of a heavily overcast afternoon compressed into a space that doesn't deserve it. What light enters from above is diffuse, directionless, filtered through the steam and the canopy that overhangs both lips. It does not reach the bottom. The bottom makes its own light, or rather its own anti-light — a deep, warm darkness that seems to absorb what little illumination falls into it, broken only by the occasional orange pulse of a thermal vent, the brief blue-white flash of mineral fluorescence where fresh rock meets water.

The water is everywhere and nowhere. It does not flow — not in any riverine sense. It seeps. It condenses on the gorge walls in sheets so thin they're more like sweat than streams, and where the walls are warmest, where the thermal veins run closest to the surface, the water doesn't even condense — it simply hangs in the air as a permanent mist, a suspension so fine it behaves like a gas. You breathe it. It tastes of iron and sulfur and something else, something alkaline and clean, like the smell of a just-struck match extended into a flavor. Pools gather in the irregularities of the rock floor — shallow, perfectly still, their surfaces tensioned with mineral films that shift color as you move: oil-slick rainbows, copper greens, the deep red of oxidized iron. Some of the pools are warm enough to steam. Some are cool enough to shock. The difference between them can be a matter of inches.

The vegetation is the thing that makes no sense until it makes perfect sense.

It grows from the walls. Not from soil — there is no soil here, not yet, not in a place this young. It grows from the rock itself, or rather from the film of moisture and mineral and microscopic life that coats the rock. Mosses first — thick, saturated, the green so deep it's almost black in the low light, growing in cushions and mats that hang from every ledge and projection like the pelts of drowned animals. The moss is the substrate. Everything else grows from the moss, or through it, or in it.

Ferns. Dozens of species, if they are species — some are barely more than a single frond unfurling from a crack, others are massive, prehistoric things with trunks like gnarled hands and canopies that spread six, eight feet across, their fronds dripping with condensation that falls in a constant, irregular patter. The ferns are the architecture of this place. They fill the middle distance, layer upon layer, their fronds interlocking so densely that the air between them is a separate climate — more humid, more still, warmer. You could not walk through this. You would have to push, and the push would be met with resistance that feels almost structural, as if the plants are holding the space together.

And above the ferns, below the ferns, between the ferns: liverworts, hornworts, filmy ferns so thin they are translucent, their leaves a single cell thick, glowing faintly green when backlit by the thermal flashes. Clubmosses that are not mosses at all but something older, their stems creeping across every surface in dense, interlocking mats. Horsetails — not the modest garden varieties but thick-stemmed, bamboo-like things that rattle in the updrafts from below. And everywhere, everywhere, the fungi. Not the fruiting bodies you'd recognize — no mushrooms, no brackets — but the mycelia, the underground (or in this case, under-moss) networks, visible as white and yellow and pale orange threads that lace through the moss like veins through tissue, binding it, feeding it, connecting every living thing in the gorge to every other living thing in a web so dense it has its own topography.

The sound is the first thing that overwhelms you, and the last thing you'll forget.

It is not one sound. It is a hundred sounds that have learned to occupy the same space without resolving into noise. The hiss of steam from a hundred vents, each at a different pressure, a different temperature, a different mineral content, so that each hiss has its own pitch and timbre — some are high and thin, some are low and wet, some are almost musical, a clear sustained note that rises and falls as the pressure behind it fluctuates. The drip of condensation, but not the gentle drip of a cave — this is the drip of a space so saturated that water is falling almost continuously from every surface, a patter so dense it becomes a texture, a constant granular hiss like radio static, like rain on a tin roof, like the sound of a crowd breathing.

And beneath it all, the deep sound. The sound of the mountain still being made. A low, sub-audible rumble that you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears — the sound of rock under stress, of tectonic forces still actively pushing this gorge open, of the earth's crust adjusting to the new absence where the rock used to be. It is not an earthquake. It is the sound of an earthquake that never quite arrives, a continuous low-grade seismic murmur that rises and falls on a cycle of minutes, sometimes hours. When it rises, the steam vents hiss louder, the water flows faster, the ferns tremble on their rock-anchored roots. When it falls, the gorge seems to hold its breath, and in the brief silence you can hear the individual drops, the individual hisses, the individual creak of a fern frond adjusting its weight.

The air moves. It is not wind — wind implies weather, implies a sky. This is convection. The thermal vents at the bottom heat the air, and the air rises, and as it rises it cools, and as it cools it drops its moisture, and the moisture condenses on every surface, and the air sinks again at the walls, and rises again at the center, and the whole gorge is a single continuous circulation cell, a convection engine running on geothermal heat, moving air and water and spores and mineral particles in a loop that has been running since the gorge opened and will run until it closes. The air at the bottom is warm and wet and smells of sulfur and hot stone. The air at the top is cool and wet and smells of moss and ozone. The air in between is everything at once, and it is never still.

There are no animals. This is not a place that has been abandoned by animals — it is a place that has not yet been found by them. The gorge is too young, too isolated, too extreme. Insects have not arrived. Birds do not fly over it — the thermal updrafts are too strong, the air too turbulent, the space too narrow and too dark. The only life is plant life, fungal life, microbial life, and it is enough. More than enough. It is a world unto itself, a closed loop of growth and decay and growth again, the dead moss becoming the substrate for the living moss, the fallen fern decomposing into the mineral film that feeds the next fern, the whole gorge a single organism in the sense that a coral reef is a single organism — not one thing, but one system, one process, one continuous act of making.

And that is the word for this place, the word that keeps presenting itself: *making*. Not made. Not finished. Not in a state of being but in a state of becoming. The rock is still fracturing — you can see the fresh cracks, the new fissures, the places where the wall has opened in the last days or weeks, exposing rock that has never seen air, never been weathered, its surface still bright with the memory of the pressure that formed it. The water is still finding its paths, still cutting its channels, still dissolving and depositing and dissolving again, building and destroying with the same motion. The plants are still colonizing, still sending their roots into new cracks, still dying back in the places where the thermal output has shifted and the temperature has changed by a few degrees. The fungi are still networking, still connecting, still building their web of nutrient exchange that is the gorge's true circulatory system.

It is a factory. That is the other word. Not a factory in the sense of buildings and machines and production lines, but a factory in the sense of a place where raw materials are processed into something else, where energy is applied to matter and matter is transformed. The raw materials are rock and water and heat and air. The energy is geothermal, is gravitational, is chemical. The products are soil, are biomass, are the slow accumulation of organic matter that will, in a thousand years, in ten thousand years, become the deep humus of a forest floor. The gorge is making a forest. It is making it from scratch, from bare rock, and it is doing it with an intensity and a speed that is almost violent, the growth so dense and so rapid that you can almost see it if you watch long enough — a frond uncurling, a moss cushion expanding, a crack in the rock widening by a fraction of a millimeter as a root pushes into it.

The scale is human. That is the strange thing. Eight hundred feet deep, forty feet across — these are not the dimensions of a canyon, not the dimensions of a landscape feature that dwarfs you. These are the dimensions of a building. A cathedral, as the prompt suggests, or a very deep room. You can see the other wall. You can see the bottom. You can see the individual plants, the individual rocks, the individual drops of water. It is a space you could know, if you lived here long enough. A space you could learn the way you learn the rooms of a house — this corner is warmer, this ledge is drier, this pool is deeper than it looks, this crack in the wall will hiss at you if you put your ear to it and wait for the seismic cycle to peak.

But you do not live here. You are visiting. And the gorge is not interested in you. It is interested in what it is doing — the making, the processing, the continuous transformation of rock into life, of heat into motion, of water into everything. It is loud and dark and wet and warm and cold and dense and alive and utterly, utterly indifferent to your presence, the way a furnace is indifferent to the person who opens its door, the way a river is indifferent to the stone it is wearing down.

You stand at the lip and you look down into it, and the steam rises to meet you, and the sound rises to meet you, and the smell rises to meet you, and for a moment you understand that you are looking at something very old and very new at the same time — the oldest process on earth, the making of a living world from an inert one, happening right now, right here, in a space no larger than a cathedral, in a darkness that has never known sun, in a noise that has never known silence.

The gorge is not finished. The gorge will not be finished for a very long time.

But it is working. God, it is working.
