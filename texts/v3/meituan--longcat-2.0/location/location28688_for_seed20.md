# The Throat

---

You hear it before you understand that you're hearing anything at all.

The sound is not a sound. It is a pressure, a full-body immersion in vibration so total that your bones become tuning forks and your thoughts arrive already half-dissolved, syllables breaking apart before they can cohere into meaning. It is the roar of water doing something water should not be able to do — not falling, not flowing, but *insisting*. A continuous, deafening exhalation from the earth's interior, so loud that silence becomes a theoretical concept, something you remember the way you remember a dream upon waking: with the certainty that it happened but no ability to reconstruct its shape.

The place is small. That is the first thing you understand once your ears stop trying to close. It is bounded — a bowl, a throat, a cupped hand of stone perhaps two hundred meters across, perched at an elevation that your lungs register as *too high* even as the warm, thick air contradicts every expectation of altitude. The walls rise around you in sharp, unweathered ridges. This stone is young. Not young as a mountain is young — not the mere millions of years of a recent upthrust — but young the way a wound is young. The fractures are still bright. The edges have not yet learned to be soft. Some of the rock faces gleam with a wet, pinkish freshness, like meat exposed beneath skin, and when you touch them (if you touch them) they are warm. Not sun-warm. Blood-warm. The warmth of something that has only recently been inside something else.

And the water.

The water is everywhere and it is wrong.

It does not pool. It does not cascade. It *stands* — columns and curtains and hovering sheets of it, rising from fissures in the stone floor in defiance of every law that water has ever agreed to obey. Some of the columns are as thick as ancient trees, perfectly vertical, trembling with internal motion but holding their shape with a surface tension that no chemistry can explain. Others are thin as wire, braiding and unbraiding in midair, weaving patterns that almost resolve into language before dissolving. Between them, the air is thick with suspended droplets that do not fall but drift in slow, purposeful currents, as if the atmosphere itself has become a liquid medium and the boundary between water and air has been negotiated away.

The light is dim. It comes from below — from the water itself, which glows with a faint, amber phosphorescence, the color of old honey or infected tissue. It is enough to see by, barely, and it makes everything look submerged even when it isn't, because the light moves the way light moves when it has to pass through something denser than air. Shadows pool in the crevices between stones. The suspended droplets catch the glow and hold it, so that the entire bowl is filled with a slow, drifting constellation of dim amber points, like bioluminescence, like a sky full of dying stars.

The warmth is close and wet. It clings. The temperature sits at that precise threshold where the body stops trying to regulate and simply surrenders — not hot enough to cook, not cool enough to refresh, just a steady, enveloping warmth that makes the air feel like a membrane you are always on the wrong side of. Your skin is never dry. Your clothes, if you are wearing clothes, hang heavy and useless. The water in the air condenses on every surface and does not evaporate. Everything is slick. Everything is growing a thin, warm film of moisture that catches the amber light and makes the stone look alive, look *secreted*.

And the place is changing.

This is the thing. This is the thing that your mind keeps trying to look away from and cannot.

The columns of water shift. Not randomly — or not only randomly. They lean toward each other and merge, and where they merge the water does not splash or spray but *knits*, surface binding to surface with a quiet, organic certainty, forming new shapes that hold for a moment — a span, an arch, a shape that might be a ribcage or might be a doorway — before the whole structure shivers and collapses back into its component streams. The fissures in the floor open and close. Not like mouths — that metaphor is too intentional, too biological — but like the stone is breathing, and the water is its breath, and the rhythm is not quite regular enough to predict but not quite irregular enough to dismiss. New spires of rock are emerging from the floor even as you watch, pushing up through the standing water with a slow, grinding patience, their surfaces still slick with the mineral-rich fluid that birthed them. They are sharp. They are *new*. In a place where erosion has not yet learned its name, every edge is a recent event.

The sound shifts with the changes. It is always loud — it is *always* loud, a roar that has no peak and no valley, a wall of noise so constant that it becomes a kind of silence, a baseline against which smaller sounds register as absences, as holes in the fabric. But within that wall, there are textures. A deep, subsonic pulse that you feel in your chest when a new fissure opens. A high, glassy singing when two water-columns braid together. A wet, tearing sound — like fabric, like flesh — when a rock spire breaks the surface. And sometimes, in the spaces between, a sound that might be a voice, or might be the memory of a voice, or might be the place itself trying to form a word with a mouth made of water and stone.

There is life here, but it is sparse and it is strange.

In the crevices where the stone is oldest — which is to say, where the stone is only days or weeks rather than hours — there are growths. Not plants, exactly. Not lichen. Something that occupies the same ecological niche but is made of different stuff: thin, filamentous structures that cling to the rock in patterns that look almost like script, almost like circuitry, almost like the branching of capillaries seen through skin. They are the color of the amber light, or they produce it — it is impossible to tell. They do not move, but they are not still. They are *growing*, visibly, perceptibly, extending their filaments into new crevices with a slow, patient urgency, and where they grow, the stone darkens and softens, as if they are not colonizing it but *digesting* it.

There are animals, or things that might be animals. You see them rarely — a flicker at the edge of vision, a shape that resolves into water and then resolves into something else and then resolves into water again. They are small. They move through the standing water-columns as if the water is air and the air is water, and they leave no wake, no ripple, no evidence of passage except a brief, local dimming of the amber light, as if they are drinking it as they move. You never see one clearly. You see the *idea* of one — the suggestion of a body, the implication of motion — and then it is gone, and the water closes behind it without a seam.

And there are the remains.

Not many. Not obvious. But if you look — if you let your eyes adjust to the dim, amber, water-thick light and stop trying to see what you expect to see — you begin to notice them. Shapes in the stone that are too regular to be natural. A series of depressions in the floor of the bowl, arranged in a pattern that might be a foundation, might be a floor plan, might be the ghost of a room that existed before the water came, or that the water is slowly un-building, dissolving back into its component intentions. A fragment of something that might be ceramic, might be bone, might be a material that has no name, embedded in the fresh stone of a new spire like an inclusion in amber — a thing that was here before the stone, or that the stone grew around, or that the stone *remembered*.

No tools. No devices. No technology in any form you would recognize. But the marks are there — faint, partial, scattered — the way a word is still visible on a page after the ink has been washed away, the impression remaining in the fiber of the paper. Someone was here. Something was here. It built, or it arranged, or it *intended*, and the place has not yet finished forgetting.

The weirdness is not a quality of the place. It *is* the place.

Reality here is not broken — that implies a whole thing that has been damaged. Reality here is *thin*. Permeable. The water does not obey gravity because gravity is a suggestion here, a local custom that the water has not agreed to observe. The stone grows because the distinction between liquid and solid is a spectrum, not a boundary, and the place is somewhere in the middle of that spectrum, leaning toward solid but not committed. The light comes from the water because the water is not entirely water — it is something that has not yet decided what it is, and in its indecision it glows, the way a thought glows in the moment before it becomes a word.

Time is thin here too. You feel it in the way the place changes — not gradually, not in the slow, geological patience of erosion and growth, but in lurches, in stutters, in moments where the whole bowl seems to *skip*, like a stone across water, and when it lands again something is different. A column that was on the left is now on the right. A fissure that was open is closed. The filamentous growths have extended six inches in a moment that felt like a breath. The rock spires are taller. The water is louder. Or quieter. Or the same. You cannot tell. Your memory of what the place was a moment ago is already softening, already being overwritten by what it is now, and you understand — with a calm, warm, water-thick certainty — that the place is not changing over time.

The place is changing *instead* of time.

And you are in it. And the sound is total. And the water is standing in columns that should not stand, and the stone is growing that should not grow, and the light is coming from the wrong direction, and the warmth is the warmth of a body, and the place is small, and the place is high, and the place is new, and the place is old, and the place is *here*, and it is *now*, and it is *always*, and the water is singing in a voice that is almost yours, and the stone is warm beneath your hands, and the amber light is dim, and the sound is deafening, and the place is becoming, and the place is becoming, and the place is becoming.
