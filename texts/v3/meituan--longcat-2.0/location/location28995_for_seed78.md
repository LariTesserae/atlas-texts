# The Towerfields

---

You arrive at the edge of the world and find it is not an edge at all — it is a beginning.

The ground beneath your feet is raw. Not weathered, not rounded, not patient. The stone here is freshly broken, as if the planet cracked open only yesterday and hasn't yet learned to hide its wounds. Ridges of dark metamorphic rock rise in knife-sharp arêtes, their faces glittering with mineral fractures that catch what little light exists and scatter it into brief, cold stars. There is no soil to speak of — only scree, only angular rubble, only the exposed and unapologetic bones of the earth pushed upward by forces that are still, geologically, happening. You stand on a plateau that has no business being this high, this sharp, this new. The air is thin enough to notice. Cold enough to hurt.

And it is dark.

Not night-dark — the sun still exists, somewhere above the perpetual cloud-ceiling that sits at about twice your height, a dense, slow-moving blanket of vapor that diffuses all light into a uniform, directionless grey so dim you could read by it only if the letters were large and your eyes were patient. The cloud layer never breaks. It never fully rains. It simply *sits*, and from its underside, moisture condenses in fat, cold droplets that fall at irregular intervals — not rain, not mist, but something between: a sporadic, heavy dew that soaks everything it touches and then stops, as if the sky forgot what it was doing. The stone is always damp. The air tastes of wet rock and ozone and something faintly organic, like the breath of a greenhouse.

But there are no greenhouses. There is no green.

There is, instead, the Towers.

---

They rise from the plateau at irregular intervals — vast, columnar masses of living material, each one a single organism or a colony so integrated it might as well be one. They are not trees. They are not fungi. They are not anything with a name in any language you know, though they perform the function of all three. Each Tower is a pillar of densely packed plant-matter, rising anywhere from thirty to several hundred meters into the cloud-layer, and they are the only significant vertical features on the entire plateau for distances that would take days to cross. Between them: nothing. Bare rock. Scree. The occasional shallow pool of condensate, black and still, reflecting the grey ceiling like a mirror laid flat on the ground. The emptiness between Towers is so complete, so total, that standing in the middle of it you feel less like you are in a landscape and more like you are in a room — a room with no walls, no ceiling, no floor except stone, and a silence so profound it has texture.

Except it is not silent.

The wind moves through the Towerfields constantly, and because the Towers are spaced so far apart, the wind has room to *build*. It accelerates through the gaps, funnels between rock ridges, and when it strikes the Towers themselves, they *sing*. Each Tower is hollow, or chambered, or fibrous enough to resonate — and the wind plays them like instruments. The sound is enormous. A low, throbbing hum that you feel in your chest before you hear it with your ears, overlaid with higher whistles, moans, and occasional sharp cracks as internal structures shift or ice fractures within the chambers. The Towers are loud. The wind is loud. The rock itself creaks and groans as temperature fluctuations — small but real, the difference between cloud-thick and cloud-thin — cause expansion and contraction. You cannot whisper here. You must shout to be heard ten meters away. The world is a constant, roaring chord.

And yet, in the lee of each Tower, there is a pocket of stillness. A zone of relative calm where the wind parts around the living column and leaves a quiet wake, like the eddy behind a ship's hull. These are the places where life concentrates.

---

The Towers are the ecosystem. There is no other.

Their surfaces are covered in a dense, interlocking mat of vegetation so thick it functions as a substrate — a living soil, if you will, though it grows on vertical surfaces and horizontal ones alike. Moss-analogues in deep burgundy and black, not green (the light is wrong for chlorophyll here; these organisms run on something else, some chemosynthetic or radiotrophic process that feeds on the mineral richness of the stone and the faint, strange energy that percolates up from deep below). Lichen-analogues that spread in fractal plates, their edges curling upward like pages of a book left in humidity. Vine-like structures that drape from upper branches — because the Towers do branch, eventually, sending out horizontal limbs that interlock with neighboring Towers when they're close enough, forming living bridges across the void. Where two Towers have grown together, the space between them becomes a three-dimensional maze of woven plant-matter, a hanging garden suspended over emptiness, and this is where the density of life becomes almost overwhelming: a thick, wet, warm tangle of growth that traps heat and moisture and creates its own internal weather.

Because the Towers make their own weather. Each one is a water-harvesting engine. The cloud-layer condenses on their upper surfaces, and the plant-matter wicks the moisture downward through capillary action, distributing it through the entire structure. Water drips from every surface. It pools in internal chambers. It runs down the exterior in thin, continuous sheets that freeze in the cold and form glaze-ice sculptures — translucent, amber-tinted, shaped by the wind into forms that look almost deliberate. The Towers *leak*. They weep. They sweat. And this moisture, combined with the heat generated by the slow decomposition of their own dead interior layers (the Towers grow from the outside, die from the inside, and their hollow cores are filled with a warm, composting mulch that never fully freezes), creates a microclimate around each one that is dramatically different from the bare plateau.

Near the base of a Tower, the temperature rises. Not to warm — never to warm — but to merely *cold* rather than *bitter*. The air is thick with humidity. The ground, where the Tower's root-analogues have broken down the rock into something approaching soil, is soft and yielding. And the life here is *dense*.

---

The fauna of the Towerfields is abundant, but almost all of it is concentrated around the Towers. The bare plateau between them is nearly lifeless — a few extremophile microbes in the rock fractures, perhaps, and the occasional hardy arthropod-analogue scuttling between stones. But at the Towers, the biomass is staggering.

Small creatures — things like six-legged furred grubs, things like segmented worms with too many eyes, things like beetles the size of your hand with carapaces that glow faintly blue — swarm over the Tower surfaces in numbers that make the living substrate appear to move, to breathe, to ripple. They feed on the moss-analogues, on each other, on the warm compost that seeps from cracks in the Tower's bark-like outer skin. Larger animals move among them: grazers the size of sheep, low-slung and wide, with broad, flat teeth and tongues like squeegees, scraping the living mat from the Tower's surface in long, methodical strokes. They are prey. The predators are fast, low, and numerous — pack-hunters with too many legs and a chittering, echolocating call that cuts through the wind-noise like a knife. They hunt in the damp stillness at the Tower's base, where the sound of the wind is muffled and their prey's hearing is confused by the humidity.

And there are flyers. Gliders, mostly — membranous wings, long bodies, navigating the airspace between Towers with a precision that suggests they can read the wind the way a sailor reads currents. They nest in the upper branches, where the Towers enter the cloud-layer, and their droppings — rich, acidic, constant — have over centuries created a secondary ecosystem on the Tower surfaces: a layer of nutrient-dependent organisms that exist only because the flyers exist, a living scum of pink and orange that glows faintly in the dark and smells like ammonia and overripe fruit.

The animals are loud. They have to be. The wind takes everything. So they evolved to be heard: deep, resonant calls that carry through the stone itself (many of them have specialized limbs or body parts that drum against the rock, using the plateau as a communication medium), and high, piercing cries that cut through the air-noise. At any given moment, the soundscape around a Tower is a layered cacophony — the deep hum of the Tower itself, the wind, the chittering of insects, the calls of grazers, the hunting cries of predators, the wing-beats of flyers, and beneath it all, a constant, wet, organic sound: the sound of things growing, eating, dying, decomposing, all at once, all the time.

---

The built things are here, but they are old and few.

Scattered across the plateau, at distances that suggest their builders traveled far and wide but never settled densely, there are structures. Small ones. Low walls of stacked stone, roofless and open to the elements. Platforms carved directly into the rock, their edges still sharp — they haven't been here long enough to erode. A few upright posts, petrified or preserved by the cold, that might have been part of something larger or might have been complete in themselves: markers, perhaps, or the frames of shelters long since stripped of their covering. The tech is simple. Stone on stone. No mortar. No metal. The kind of construction that says *we were here, we needed shelter, we used what we had.*

Who built them is unclear. The structures are empty. Some are partially buried by scree. One, near the base of a large Tower, has been incorporated into the living mat — the moss-analogue has grown over and through it, using the wall as a substrate, and the boundary between built and grown has become impossible to determine. Whether the builders are gone, or dead, or simply elsewhere on the vast plateau, there is no way to know. The structures are too few and too scattered to suggest a civilization. They suggest, instead, a people who passed through. Who used the Towers as waypoints in an otherwise featureless landscape. Who sheltered in their lee and moved on.

There are marks, too, on the Towers themselves. Not natural. Deliberate cuts in the outer bark, arranged in patterns that might be writing or might be decoration or might be functional — channels to direct water flow, perhaps, or notches for climbing. The cuts are old enough to have been partially healed over by new growth, but not so old that they've disappeared entirely. Someone shaped these Towers, at least a little. Someone lived with them, on them, in them.

---

The strangeness of this place is not in any single feature but in the way they combine.

The light is wrong. Not just dim — *wrong*. The cloud-layer diffuses everything, but there are moments, brief and unpredictable, when the light seems to come from below rather than above, as if the plateau itself is luminous. The moss-analogues may be contributing to this; many of them bioluminesce faintly, a cold blue-green glow that is invisible in the daytime (such as it is) but becomes the primary light source when the cloud-thickens and the world dims further. At its darkest, the Towerfields are not black but *blue* — a deep, pulsing, living blue, emanating from every surface, every creature, every drop of water that has passed through organic matter. The glow is not bright enough to read by, but it is bright enough to navigate by, and the animals have adapted to it: many have eyes that are enormous, or eyes that are barely functional, navigating instead by sound, by vibration, by sensitivity to the electrical fields generated by the Towers' internal processes.

Because the Towers generate electricity. Not much. Not lightning. But a constant, low-level current that runs through their root systems and into the rock beneath, a byproduct of the chemical processes that allow them to extract energy from the mineral substrate. You can feel it if you press your hand to the ground near a Tower's base: a faint tingle, a vibration just below the threshold of perception. The animals feel it too. Some of them use it to navigate, the way a shark uses electroreception. Some of them are drawn to it, the way a moth is drawn to flame. The grazers orient their bodies along the current's flow lines when they rest. The predators hunt perpendicular to them, using the current as a grid.

And the current is not constant. It pulses. Slowly, irregularly, in patterns that don't correspond to any obvious external stimulus — not temperature, not moisture, not the presence of animals. The Towers have a rhythm, and it is not one that matches anything else in the environment. It is as if they are communicating with each other, or with something deeper, something below the plateau, something that the freshly exposed rock has only recently revealed and that the Towers have only recently learned to tap into.

The wind carries sounds between Towers that shouldn't be audible at those distances. A predator's call from a Tower five kilometers away arrives not as an echo but as a clear, distinct signal, as if the rock itself is a conductor and the air above it is irrelevant. Animals respond to these distant sounds as if they were local. The ecosystem is connected across vast distances by a communication network that runs through the stone, and the creatures of the Towerfields are all, in their own ways, listening.

---

This is a young place. The rock is sharp. The Towers are still growing — fast, by the standards of anything this size, adding centimeters per year to their height, meters per decade. The ecosystem is still assembling itself: new species appear (or rather, become noticeable) on timescales of centuries, evolving or migrating or emerging from some deep refugium that the uplift has exposed. The weather is still finding its patterns. The wind still surprises itself.

It is a place that has not yet decided what it is.

And it is vast. The plateau extends to the horizon in every direction, and the horizon is not the gentle curve of a world but a sharp, jagged line of peaks and ridges that suggests the plateau is not flat but *fractured* — a high, broken tableland, its surface a maze of sharp valleys and knife-edge ridges, its Towers rising from every high point like the spines of some enormous, sleeping creature. The scale is continental. The weather is local — each Tower creates its own microclimate, and the spaces between them are a patchwork of wind-shadows and wind-tunnels, of damp stillness and desiccating gale. You can stand in warm, wet calm and see, a kilometer away, a storm of ice crystals blasting across an exposed ridge, and between the two: nothing. A wall of wind. A boundary as sharp as a knife-cut.

The Towerfields are not a single place. They are a thousand places, a million places, each one defined by its proximity to a Tower and its distance from the next, each one a world unto itself, each one connected to the others by the stone beneath and the wind above and the slow, pulsing current that runs through everything like a heartbeat.

It is dark. It is cold. It is loud. It is alive.

It is still becoming.
