# The Thermal Gardens of Unn

You descend through a throat of rust-eaten steel — a service ladder, its rungs worn to half their original thickness, bolted into a wall of fused scoria that sweats continuously — and emerge into a space the size of a village square, though it feels larger, because the ceiling has lost itself in haze.

The air is close. Wet-bath heat. The kind that sits on the skin and does not move. It tastes of iron and something sweet-rot underneath, like fruit left too long in a warm room, though there is no fruit here.

---

The chamber is roughly oval, perhaps a hundred meters along its longest axis, though distances are difficult to judge because the walls are not walls so much as *accumulations*. Over decades — centuries, possibly — mineral-laden water has wept through the porous rock overhead and deposited its load in slow, stalactitic curtains that hang in folds and rivulets, building outward in layers the color of old bone, of sulfur-yellow, of a green so deep it reads almost black. These formations are dense. They crowd the space. They branch and re-branch, narrowing the floor into a network of paths no wider than a body's breadth, and in places they meet overhead, creating vaulted tunnels that seem almost architectural, almost intentional, though they grew without intention, which is a different kind of architecture.

The vegetation lives in these formations. Or perhaps the formations are the vegetation — the boundary has blurred. What grows here is not plant life as you know it, or not only plant life. Thick, rubbery ropes of something like moss hang in curtains from the dripping ceiling, deep emerald at the tips shading to ochre at the bases where they have been longest in contact with the mineral crusts. Between and through these, ferns. Not the delicate ferns of temperate forests, but coarse, leathery things with fronds as wide as your hand, some curled tight as fists where the air is cooler, others spread wide in the warmer zones near the geothermal vents. Their color is saturated, almost artificial-looking — the green of old paint, of oxidized copper. They grow in mats over every surface, swallowing the original rock, the original industrial structure, until you cannot always tell what was built and what has merely accreted.

There are still structures. You find them when you press through the fern-curtains: a row of pressure gauges, their glass faces clouded with condensation, needles frozen at readings that may or may not mean anything anymore. A control panel, its buttons furred with moss, a few still faintly warm when you press your palm to them. A pipe, waist-thick, that runs along the floor for thirty meters before disappearing into a curtain of mineral deposit, its surface flaking in scales the size of dinner plates. Scattered throughout — *scattered* is the word, because they are few and far between and always half-concealed — are the remnants of some industrial function. Machinery whose purpose you can guess at but not name. The guts of engines, their gears locked in mineral calcite, their pistons seized. Once this place was worked. Once there were people here, doing something that required the heat, or using the heat to do something else.

Now there is no one.

---

The heat comes from below. You feel it through your boots before you understand its source. The floor is warm, uneven, ridged — a landscape of sinter deposits built up over decades from mineral-rich water that rises through cracks in the deep rock and pools in shallow basins before draining away through fissures you cannot see. Some of these pools are still, their surfaces mirror-still and dark, reflecting the dim amber glow of the bioluminescent growths that cling to the ceiling — yes, there is light, but it is dim, sourceless, a faint phosphorescence that comes in patches, enough to see by if you let your eyes adjust, enough to make the steam rising from the warm pools look like it is breathing. Other pools are active. They bubble. Not violently — this is not a geyser field — but with a steady, gurgling persistence, and the water that surfaces is hot enough to keep a thin haze of vapor clinging to the air just above it, so that moving through the chamber means moving through veils of warm mist that part and reform behind you.

The loudness is the water. Not the bubbling — that is secondary. The primary sound is the *weeping*. Water moving through the rock above, finding paths through the porous ceiling, emerging in hundreds of tiny streams and droplets, each one finding its own trajectory down through the hanging vegetation, striking the mineral crusts, the metal remnants, the pools, the stone. Individually they are nothing. Together they produce a continuous, sibilant roar, like a crowd breathing, like static, like the sound of a space that is being slowly, slowly unmade. It is not unpleasant. After a while it becomes a kind of silence.

---

There are no animals. You understand this before you understand it consciously — there is no skittering, no buzz, no ripple in the pools. The heat is part of it. The chemistry is part of it. The water that drips from above is not clean water; it carries dissolved minerals in concentrations that would sting an open wound, and the air has a faint acridity at the back of the throat. Nothing with blood lives here. Nothing with chitin. The only motion is the slow, slow growth of the mineral curtains, the slow, slow unfurling of the fern fronds — you cannot watch it happen, but if you leave and return, the frond has opened, the curtain has extended by a millimeter, the pool has changed shape. The dynamic is geological. The timescale is not yours.

But there is strangeness beyond the mere absence of fauna, beyond the merging of organic and inorganic. The strangeness is in the *patterning*. The mineral curtains do not hang randomly. They tend — subtly, but observably — toward the shapes of the machinery beneath them. The moss grows in geometries that echo the rivet patterns on the old pipes. The bioluminescent patches on the ceiling seem to cluster where the rock above is thinnest, as if they are responding to some influence from the surface world — pressure, or magnetism, or something stranger that has no name in your physics. And occasionally, when you are standing very still, the sound of the weeping seems to organize itself into something almost rhythmic, almost like a pulse, almost like a heartbeat, though you know — you *know* — that it is only water finding its way through stone, only the patient, mindless work of gravity and heat and time.

Almost.
