# The Plateau of Becoming

---

You arrive from below.

The passage up through the rock narrows and then suddenly doesn't — your hands find open air, your knees come down on a surface that is warm, almost uncomfortably so, and when you look up the sky is wrong. Not dark the way night is dark. Dark the way the inside of a closed fist is dark. A darkness that has texture, that presses down, that seems to have weight and temperature of its own. The warmth comes from beneath you. The plateau radiates.

You are very high. You know this not because you can see the drop — you cannot, the darkness swallows the edges — but because the air has thinned to a whisper. Each breath is a conscious act. The atmosphere here is present but barely, a ghost of atmosphere, enough to carry a faint hiss of venting gas from somewhere to your left but not enough to carry your own voice more than a few feet. When you speak, the words die in your mouth. When you step, the crunch of your foot on the surface is a private sound, a secret between you and the ground.

The ground.

It is not stable. Not in the way you understand stability. It does not shake or tremble — there is no earthquake here, no dramatic rupture. Instead it *breathes*. A slow, almost imperceptible rise and fall, as though the entire plateau is the skin of something vast and warm and only half-solid. The surface under your palms is rough, freshly fractured, dark mineral — something between basalt and something denser, something that doesn't have a name in any geology you know. It is black but not the black of absence. The black of *concentration*. The black of matter that has been compressed and heated and compressed again until it has become something more than rock, something that sits at the edge of mineral and doesn't quite know what it wants to be.

And it is new. That is the first thing that strikes you once your eyes adjust to the darkness — everything here is *new*. The fractures are sharp. The edges are knife-clean. There is no weathering, no smoothing, no patient work of wind or water (there is no water, there has never been water, the concept of water here is as foreign as the concept of silence is foreign to a storm). This surface has not been touched by erosion. It has only just emerged. It is still deciding what it is.

---

The plateau extends.

In the near-darkness you can make out its character in pieces, assembling the whole from fragments the way you might assemble a room from touch alone. The ground slopes gently upward to your right, gently downward to your left, and in both directions it is broken by formations that rise from the surface like — 

Like buildings.

That is the word your mind reaches for, and it is wrong, but it is the closest word. They are structures in the sense that they have verticality, intentionality of shape, a relationship to the ground that suggests *rising* rather than *accumulating*. But they are not built. Nothing was built here. Nothing was designed. These are mineral extrusions, chimneys of crystallized gas and pressure, walls of deposited mineral that have grown upward from the plateau surface the way teeth grow from a jaw — blindly, chemically, according to rules that have nothing to do with purpose and everything to do with the deep grammar of heat and pressure and time.

Some are tall and narrow — spires, columns, fingers of dark crystal reaching up into the black sky. They are not uniform. They twist slightly, lean slightly, branch at their tops into delicate fractal crowns that catch what little light exists and hold it, faintly, like phosphorescence. Others are broad and low — platforms, mesas, wide flat-topped extrusions that rise ten or twenty feet from the plateau floor and then stop, their surfaces level and smooth, their edges sharp as broken glass. Still others are hollow — arches, tubes, open frameworks of mineral that frame the darkness beyond them like doorways leading nowhere.

They are scattered. The density of them is low — vast stretches of open plateau between each cluster, each solitary spire, each wall. You can stand in the center of a clearing a hundred yards across and see the formations at its edges like a distant skyline, dark shapes against darker sky, and the space between you and them is empty, raw, freshly exposed mineral surface that still holds the warmth of its formation.

And it is growing.

---

You notice it first as a sound — or rather, as the absence of sound where sound should be. A spire to your right, perhaps forty feet tall, is *making* something. At its tip, a slow accretion of material is occurring. You cannot see it happen — it is too slow, too fine — but you can see the evidence. The tip of the spire is slightly brighter than its shaft, slightly rougher in texture, and as you watch (and you do watch, because there is nothing else to do in this darkness, because the stillness and the silence demand that you pay attention to the only thing that moves) you realize the tip is *different* than it was a moment ago. A new layer. A new skin. The spire is growing by fractions of millimeters, building itself from the gas that vents from its core, mineral crystallizing out of vapor the way frost crystallizes on a windowpane, except this frost is dark and warm and grows not downward with gravity but upward against it.

Elsewhere, a wall is crumbling. Not dramatically — not in a collapse or a crash — but in a slow, almost gentle shedding of material. Flakes of mineral the size of your hand detach from its face and fall to the plateau floor, where they lie like shed skin. The wall is losing itself even as the spire gains itself, and between them the plateau floor is littered with the debris of this constant making and unmaking — shards, flakes, fragments of dark crystal that crunch softly underfoot and that will, in time, be reabsorbed, reincorporated, drawn back into the cycle.

This is the turbulence. Not the turbulence of wind or water or storm, but the turbulence of *process*. The plateau is in a state of constant, chaotic becoming. It is not stable. It will never be stable. It is a system caught in the act of forming, a geology that has not yet decided what it wants to be, and the energy that drives this indecision is immense — you can feel it in the warmth of the ground, see it in the faint glow that emanates from the deepest fractures in the surface, hear it (barely, almost not at all) in the subsonic hum that you feel in your chest more than you hear with your ears.

The plateau is alive with creation. Not alive in the biological sense — there is no biology here, no cells, no metabolism, no life in any sense that a biologist would recognize. But alive in the way that a fire is alive, in the way that a crystal growing in a supersaturated solution is alive — a process consuming energy and producing form, a pattern that perpetuates itself, a system far from equilibrium that maintains its distance through constant, restless, turbulent activity.

---

The darkness is not total.

This is important. If it were total, you would see nothing, and the plateau would be a place of pure sensation — warmth, texture, sound, the feeling of the ground beneath you. But there is light, and it comes from below.

The fractures in the plateau surface glow. Faintly — so faintly that your eyes must be fully adapted, so faintly that you might mistake the glow for a trick of perception, a retinal ghost, a phosphene born of darkness and wishful thinking. But it is real. A deep, warm, red-orange light seeps from the cracks in the rock, the color of cooling magma, the color of heat without flame, the color of a world that is still warm from its own formation. It is not bright enough to illuminate — not really. It is bright enough to *suggest*. To give shape to the nearest formations, to cast faint shadows that point in directions you cannot quite determine, to create a dim, directionless luminescence that makes the darkness feel less like absence and more like a medium — something you are submerged in, something that has its own properties, its own depth.

The glow pulses. Slowly, irregularly, in patterns that seem almost but not quite rhythmic. The plateau is breathing light the way it breathes surface — in and out, rise and fall, a cycle of pressure and release that has no fixed period and no discernible cause. Sometimes the glow brightens enough to reveal formations a hundred yards distant — a cluster of spires, a broad platform, a wall that stretches in both directions like the foundation of something vast. Sometimes it dims to nearly nothing, and you are left in a darkness so complete that you cannot see your own hands, and the only evidence that the plateau exists at all is the warmth under your feet and the faint, faint sound of the world making itself.

---

You walk.

There is no path. There is no destination. There is only the plateau, extending in all directions, and the formations scattered across it like the pieces of a game whose rules you don't know. You walk because the warmth of the ground is more bearable than the stillness of standing, because the crunch of mineral underfoot is the only sound in a world that has almost none, because movement is the only way to understand a place that is itself nothing but movement.

The formations change as you move through them. Near the passage you climbed through, they are small — knee-high extrusions, low walls, shallow depressions in the surface that might be the beginnings of formations or the endings of them. Further out, they grow. The spires reach higher. The platforms widen. The walls extend further in both directions, and some of them connect, forming partial enclosures, spaces that are almost rooms, almost courtyards, almost the rooms and courtyards of a city that was never planned and never built but that grows anyway, blindly, according to the logic of mineral deposition and gas venting and the deep thermal rhythms of the plateau's interior.

And they are strange. Not strange in the way that a foreign country is strange — unfamiliar but comprehensible. Strange in the way that a dream is strange — familiar shapes assembled in unfamiliar ways, patterns that seem to mean something but don't, or mean something you can't quite grasp. A spire that branches at its top into a shape that looks almost like a hand. A wall that curves in a spiral, its surface covered in fine crystalline ridges that catch the faint glow and throw it back in patterns that seem almost like writing. A platform whose surface is perfectly flat and perfectly circular, as though it were machined, as though something with intention and tools had shaped it — but there are no tools here, no intention, only the blind chemistry of mineral growth, and the circularity is a coincidence, a trick of the process, a pattern that means nothing and looks like it means everything.

This is the weirdness. Not the weirdness of the alien or the supernatural, but the weirdness of *emergence* — the way that simple rules, iterated enough times, produce complexity that looks like design. The plateau is not designed. Nothing here was made by anyone or anything. But it looks made. It looks *built*. The formations cluster in ways that suggest streets and avenues. The walls align in ways that suggest property lines and boundaries. The platforms rise in ways that suggest foundations, stages, altars. And none of it means anything. None of it was intended. It is all process, all chemistry, all the blind and relentless creativity of a world that is still in the act of becoming itself.

---

The silence is not empty.

It is full of the sounds that the plateau makes — sounds so small, so quiet, so far below the threshold of ordinary hearing that you perceive them not as sounds but as *sensations*. The subsonic hum of the ground. The almost-inaudible hiss of gas venting from a fracture somewhere to the north. The faint, faint tick of mineral crystallizing on the tip of a spire — a sound so small that you feel it in your teeth more than you hear it with your ears. The occasional soft *tock* of a flake of mineral detaching from a wall and falling to the ground, a sound like a clock ticking in a room three houses away.

And sometimes, less often, a sound that is louder. A crack. A groan. A deep, resonant *boom* that comes from somewhere below the surface and that you feel in your chest and your bones and the soles of your feet. These are the sounds of the plateau restructuring itself — a new fracture opening, a formation collapsing, a chamber below the surface emptying or filling. They are not frequent. They are not predictable. They arrive without warning and without echo (the thin air swallows echoes whole) and they leave behind a changed landscape — a new wall where there was none, a spire toppled, a platform split in two, a fresh glow of heat from a new wound in the surface.

The plateau is always changing. Always. The formation you passed an hour ago is not the formation you would pass now — it has grown, or shed, or cracked, or leaned. The ground you walked over is not the ground that is there now — it has risen, or fallen, or fractured, or smoothed. The plateau is a place in constant flux, a landscape that is never the same twice, a world that is always in the act of becoming something other than what it was.

---

You stop.

You find a platform — broad, flat, warm — and you sit on it, and you look out over the plateau in the faint glow of its own making, and you try to understand what you are seeing.

A world without water. Without life. Without light, in any meaningful sense. Without sound, in any meaningful sense. Without stability, without permanence, without anything that you would recognize as familiar or comfortable or safe.

And yet.

The formations rise around you like the architecture of a city that is building itself. The ground breathes beneath you with a warmth that is almost biological. The darkness is not empty but full — full of process, full of change, full of the restless, turbulent, chaotic energy of a world that has not yet decided what it wants to be. The silence is not empty but full of the small sounds of creation — the tick of crystal, the hiss of gas, the groan of rock under stress, the deep subsonic hum of a planet's interior expressing itself through the thin skin of its surface.

This is a place that is not finished. That will never be finished. That exists in a permanent state of *almost* — almost formed, almost stable, almost knowable. It is a landscape in the act of emerging from the raw material of itself, and the emergence is not gentle or gradual but turbulent, chaotic, violent in its patience, relentless in its slowness.

You sit on your platform in the warm dark and you watch the plateau make itself, and unmake itself, and make itself again, and you understand that you are not looking at a place.

You are looking at a process.

And the process is not done.

The process is never done.
