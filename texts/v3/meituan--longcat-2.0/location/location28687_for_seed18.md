# The Resonance Spire

From below — if there were a below, if the cloud layer didn't swallow everything beneath the midline — the Spire would look like a single vertical scratch against the sky, a dark needle drawn upward from the summit of the summit. But you are not below. You are here, standing on the observation platform at its crown, and the Spire is not a needle. It is a hollow.

The platform is perhaps forty feet across. That is the whole of the inhabited world. Beyond its railing, the rock face drops away in every direction — not jagged, not dramatic, but smooth, almost polished, curving outward slightly as it descends like the wall of a bell. The stone is dark and faintly warm to the touch, not from the sun, which is a pale diffuse disc somewhere above the haze, but from within. Something deep in the Spire is running, has been running, will continue to run, and the warmth seeps upward through the built substrate like blood through skin.

Everything here is made. The platform underfoot is a grillwork of iron, painted long ago with a compound that has since cracked into a topography of its own — tiny ridges and valleys of oxidized coating, each one hosting its own garden of moss. The railing is brass, gone green, and the green is furred with something that is not quite lichen, that pulses almost imperceptibly with a rhythm you feel in your fingertips when you grip the bar. The walls that rise from the platform's edge are riveted steel, plate upon plate, and the seams between them have been caulked with a material that has since turned the color of old bone and the texture of cork. It is in these seams that the life began.

You hear it before you see it: the sound. It is not loud in the way that a machine is loud, or a waterfall, or a storm. It is loud in the way that a cathedral is loud when it is empty — a sound that is less a noise than a condition of the air, a standing pressure, a tone that has been sounding so long it has become the same thing as silence. The Deafening, the old workers called it, if there were workers, if this was ever a place of work. A low, vast hum that is the sum of ten thousand smaller sounds — the tick of expanding metal, the drip of condensate, the creak of the Spire swaying almost imperceptibly in the high wind, the rush of water through pipes that run the structure's entire interior height, the chittering and singing and calling of the things that live here. All of it merging into one continuous chord that you feel in your chest, in your teeth, in the spaces behind your eyes.

And then you look over the railing, and you see the Garden.

The Spire is hollow. That is the first thing. The walls rise from the platform, yes, but they rise around an emptiness — a vertical chamber, circular, perhaps two hundred feet across, descending downward into a warm haze. The scale is wrong. The Spire is small from the outside, a hamlet, a single feature, but inside it opens into a volume that belongs to a different order of magnitude. The walls of this interior chamber are not smooth. They are layered, buttressed, bracketed, and festooned with structure — catwalks, pipe runs, housing units, cable trays, tanks, vats, the rusted carcasses of machinery whose purpose has been forgotten or was never known. Every surface is a shelf, and every shelf is a terrace, and every terrace is a jungle.

The vegetation is lush in a way that seems aggressive, almost muscular. It does not merely grow — it constructs. Thick ropey vines as wide as your thigh wrap around structural members and compress them slowly, over decades, into new shapes. Ferns the size of armchairs erupt from joints in the pipework, their fronds heavy with moisture, dripping steadily into the space below. Mosses of every conceivable green — emerald, jade, viridian, a deep blue-green that seems to hold its own light — cover every horizontal surface in cushions so thick they have become soil, and in that soil other things grow, and in the canopy of those things, still other things. The air is warm and thick with humidity. Condensation forms on every surface and runs in rivulets down the walls, feeding the growth, and the growth feeds the condensation by transpiring, and the cycle is so old and so stable that it has become its own weather system. It rains inside the Spire. A fine, perpetual mist falls from the middle distances, catching the light from the bioluminescent panels — yes, panels, rectangular, industrial, bolted to the walls at intervals — that provide the normal, slightly amber illumination by which all of this is visible.

The water is everywhere and nowhere. It does not pool on the platform (the grillwork sees to that), but it drips, it runs, it hangs in the air. The pipework that lines the walls is a circulatory system, and it is still, after all this time, functional. Water enters somewhere far below — drawn up from the cloud layer, or from a spring in the rock that may not be rock at all, that may be foundation, that may be the Spire's own buried root system — and it rises through the structure, branching and rebranching, feeding the vegetation, feeding the machinery, feeding the air itself. Some of the pipes have burst, or been burst, and from these ruptures water jets horizontally into the chamber, freezing into arcs of spray that catch the light and create, if you stand at the right angle, a permanent rainbow that hangs in the middle air like a fixture.

The fauna is abundant, but you have to learn to see it. At first the Garden appears to be only plants — a green world, a vertical world of leaves and stems and fronds. But then something moves in the periphery, and you realize that the Garden is full of eyes. Lizards — or things that are lizard-shaped but are not, quite, lizards — bask on the warm pipe runs, their scales the exact color of oxidized copper. Beetles the size of your thumb navigate the moss meadows on business of their own, their carapaces glossy black, clicking faintly as they move through the undergrowth. Birds — there are birds, somehow, at this elevation — nest in the housing units of dead machinery, their songs thin and bright against the Deafening, barely audible but present, a counterpoint. And there are others. Things that live in the water. Things that live in the walls. Things that may be plants that move or animals that have forgotten to be animals, that root themselves in the moss and wait, with the patience of geology, for something to come within reach.

The erosion is established. This is not a place that is falling apart. This is a place that has found its shape. The Spire was built — by whom, for what, the questions have no answers that satisfy — and the Spire has been weathering, and the weathering has been building, and the building has been weathering, and after enough time the two processes have become indistinguishable. The iron is rusting, yes, but the rust is holding the moss, and the moss is holding the water, and the water is feeding the roots, and the roots are cracking the iron, and the cycle is in balance. The machinery is still running, some of it — you can hear it in the Deafening, the deeper notes, the rhythmic pulses — but it is running at a pace so slow and so steady that it has become geological. A gear turns. It has been turning. It will continue to turn. The vibration of its turning is what keeps the condensation from settling in certain places, what keeps the air circulating, what keeps the whole system from stagnating. The tech is industrial in the way that a fossil is industrial — it is the compressed remnant of a process so vast and so sustained that it has become part of the landscape.

And the landscape is strange. Not alien — the warmth is mammalian, the water is water, the green is green — but arranged in a way that unsettles. The bioluminescent panels still glow, but their light has been colonized by the algae that grows on their surfaces, so the light that reaches the Garden is filtered through a living scrim, green-gold, dappled, moving slightly as the algae shift. The machinery is shaped like bodies — housings like ribcages, pipe runs like spines, cable bundles like nerves — and the vegetation has grown into and through these body-shapes until it is impossible to tell where the built ends and the grown begins. A tank has burst open and from its interior a tree has grown, or something tree-shaped, its bark the color of steel, its leaves broad and waxy, its roots visible through the torn metal like veins through skin. The tree bears fruit. The fruit is round and heavy and the color of rust, and when it falls — slowly, so slowly, tumbling through the middle air of the chamber — it strikes a catwalk somewhere far below with a sound like a bell, and the bell-sound is absorbed into the Deafening, and the Deafening continues.

You stand on the platform. The air is warm and damp and smells of iron and green things and ozone and something sweeter underneath, something almost floral, almost like fermentation. The Deafening holds you. The Garden descends below you into haze and green shadow and the sound of water falling into water. Above you, the walls of the Spire rise another twenty feet and then stop, and beyond them is the sky — pale, diffuse, enormous — and the wind, and the thin cold air of extreme elevation, and the sense that you are standing at the top of something that has no top, that the Spire continues upward in some direction you cannot perceive, that the elevation is not just a measurement but a condition, a state of being, a place where the world has been stretched thin.

A bird lands on the railing beside you. It is small and copper-colored and it regards you with an eye that is entirely black and entirely still. It sings — one note, high, clear, a needle of sound inserted into the fabric of the Deafening — and then it is gone, dropping over the railing and falling, or flying, into the green depths below, and you watch it go until it is indistinguishable from the leaves.

The gear turns. The water drips. The Garden grows.

The Spire stands.
