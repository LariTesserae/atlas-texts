# The Hum

It smells of wet iron and honey. There is no sky. You are inside a low-ceilinged chamber, sealed, warm, alive — roughly twenty meters across and shaped like a depressed dome, though the dome is not stone. It is something older, more like a fused membrane that has hardened into curves. Condensation beads everywhere. The humidity is so dense it breathes you back. Warm — like the inside of a body. This is a low place, sunken, half-buried in the red earth, and the earth presses in on all sides.

And yet the space is **packed**. There is almost no void. Every surface is occupied. The walls are thick with creeping moss — not green, but a deep bruised purple-blue, bioluminescent, giving off a faint pulsing light that syncs with something you cannot see. You hear it before you understand it. A low hum. A slow, arrhythmic mechanical breathing. Something industrial was built here, once, and something is still running. A belt or piston buried somewhere deeper. It runs without stopping. The vibrations travel through the floor. You feel it in your sternum.

The center of the room holds a great structure: not a machine, not a tree, but both. A thick trunk rises from a tangle of black pipes that knot and loop like industrial serpents. The trunk is bark-brown but covered in cracks that glow faintly amber — sap, or oil, or something that has learned to behave like both. It has branches. The branches are not branches. They are conduits — copper tubes, rubber hoses, and organic shoots all intertwined — that spread against the ceiling membrane like roots against the underside of a stone, searching for a light that is not there. And from these branches drip droplets. A slow, steady dripping. The sound is the first music.

Small creatures live here. In the crevices of the bark, in the loose seams between the pipes, in the dark water that pools in the shallow depressions on the floor — beetles with glassy shells that reflect the blue light, tiny translucent crabs that move sideways through the wet moss, and somewhere in the pipes, a constant skittering of smaller things — larvae or wires or both. They feed on the moss, on the residue from the humming machine, on the warm condensation. An ecosystem of maintenance. No one thing here is wild. Everything is domestic to the machine.

There is a smell of deep earth mixed with ozone. And underneath the hum — a ticking. Metal expanding in heat. Or a clock. Or a heart. The place is alive, but it is an active life — restless, ongoing, building and dissolving in the same motion. The erosion has not yet had time to soften these features. The pipes are still sharp. The bark still bleeds. The blue moss still pulses like it's counting something.

You are not the first to find this. In one corner, barely visible in the organic tangle, is the remains of a chair, a lantern, a logbook — the scattered fragments of a human presence. The logbook has swollen with moisture, its pages grown thick as leaves, moss growing through its spine. A meal tray. A sink. But no human. And the moss grows thicker toward the corner as if it has been fed.

You stay. You listen. The hum shifts slightly. The crabs click in the dark. The great trunk sweats its glowing sap, and the sap runs down into the pipes and is carried away, somewhere deeper, to whatever is breathing in the dark below.

And you realize: the chamber is growing. The membrane ceiling has new bulges. Fresh root-like structures push at the walls from the outside, or maybe the pipes push at the membrane from the inside. The chamber is expanding its lungs. The floor sinks imperceptibly. The place is not finished with you yet.
