# The Lithic Garden

---

You arrive on silence.

Not the silence of emptiness — the silence of a place so full that sound has been crowded out. Or perhaps absorbed. Or perhaps it was never invited in the first way. The air is still and warm, thick as held breath, and it carries no moisture at all. Your lips crack within minutes. Your eyes feel the dryness before anything else — a faint sanding with each blink, as though the atmosphere itself is mineral.

The ground is pale. Not white, not grey, but the color of raw calcium, of crushed bone, of something that has never known a sea. It stretches in every direction, flat to the horizon, and the horizon is very far away — so far that the curvature of this place becomes apparent, a gentle bowing of the land that suggests you are standing on something continental, something that could hold weather systems the size of old countries. The sky above is a deep, untroubled blue, lit by a sun that seems normal in every respect except that its light falls on a world with no water to catch it, no rivers to glitter, no lakes to hold the sky's reflection. The light simply lands and stays, absorbed into pale stone, and the warmth accumulates.

This is a low place. You can feel it — not in your ears, but in the way the air sits, heavy and uncompressed, as though the atmosphere has pooled here like an invisible fluid. There are no mountains. There are no valleys. The land is a plain so vast and so flat that your sense of scale begins to distort within the first hour. Objects at the middle distance — and there are objects, many of them — seem to shimmer not from heat haze but from the sheer difficulty of judging distance across an unbroken surface.

And there are objects. So many objects.

---

The first thing you mistake for geology.

Rising from the pale ground at irregular intervals are formations that look, at a distance, like weathered rock pillars — tapered, ridged, leaning slightly as though shaped by ancient wind. But there is no wind. And these are not rock. As you approach the nearest one, perhaps two hundred meters away, you see that its surface is not weathered but *textured* — covered in fine, overlapping scales, each one the size of a fingernail, each one a slightly different shade of cream and ochre and pale rust. The scales are not mineral. They are not dead. They shift, almost imperceptibly, as you watch. The whole formation is breathing.

It is a structure. It is alive. It is both.

This is the architecture of the place — not built in any sense you would recognize, but *grown*, and grown with such density and intention that the result is indistinguishable from a city. The formations cluster together, dozens of them, hundreds, their bases merging into continuous ridges of the same scaled material, their upper reaches branching and rebranching into delicate, coral-like filigree. Between them, the ground is not visible. Every surface is covered — by the scaled material, by something else that looks like dried moss but crunches faintly underfoot and releases a smell like hot stone, by low, spreading growths that resemble lichen but pulse with a slow, rhythmic contraction, as though something beneath them is circulating.

The density is extraordinary. There is no bare ground. There is no gap. The vegetation — if you can call it that, this dry, mineral-fed, waterless profusion of living things — covers everything in layers. The lowest layer is a crust, thin and brittle, that breaks under your weight and immediately begins to re-form, new material accreting from the air itself, or from the ground, or from some process you cannot see. Above that, the moss-analogues and lichen-analogues spread in interlocking mats, their colors shifting from pale gold to deep amber depending on the angle of the light. Above those, taller growths rise — some like dried ferns, some like the fans of desert plants, some like nothing on Earth, structures of interlocking mineral filaments that hold their shape without water, without cellulose, without any structural logic you can identify. They are rigid and light, and when you brush against one, it does not sway. It *rings* — a single, clear, bell-like tone that hangs in the silent air for a long moment and then is gone.

The sound does not echo. The sound is swallowed.

---

You walk deeper into the city — because it is a city, you realize now, though no hand built it. The formations are not random. They are arranged. They cluster around central spaces that function as plazas, their walls curving inward to create sheltered areas where the air is still warmer, where the density of growth is even higher, where the ground-level crust gives way to something softer, something that yields underfoot like packed earth but is not earth, has never been earth, is some organic-mineral composite that has been walked on for a long time. Paths exist. Not carved, not paved, but *worn* — the vegetation grows differently along them, lower and denser, as though responding to the passage of bodies. The paths branch and intersect with a logic that suggests planning, or at least pattern, and they connect the larger formations the way streets connect buildings.

And there are bodies.

The fauna reveals itself slowly, because it is quiet and because it is the same color as everything else. The first one you see is motionless on the wall of a formation, and you mistake it for a growth, a knob of the scaled material that has developed differently. Then it moves — a slow, deliberate rotation of its entire body, like a lizard adjusting to the sun — and you see that it is an animal. It is the size of a cat. Its body is flat and roughly circular, covered in the same scaled material as the formation it clings to, and it has no visible eyes, no visible mouth, no features at all except a faint radial segmentation that suggests it could fold itself into a tighter shape if it wanted to. It does not want to. It clings. It is warm to the touch when you reach out — warmer than the air, warmer than the stone — and it does not flinch. It has no predators here, or if it does, it has no fear of you.

There are more of them. Every surface has them. They cling and they rotate and they are utterly silent. Some are larger — the size of dogs, the size of sheep — and these move along the paths with a slow, gliding gait, their flat bodies undulating in a way that suggests many small legs or a single muscular foot. They do not interact with each other. They do not interact with you. They move with purpose, following the paths, entering the larger formations through openings at their bases, and you understand that these formations are not just structures. They are *dwellings*. They are inhabited. The animals live inside them, tend them, perhaps grow them — the relationship between the fauna and the architecture is so seamless that you cannot tell where one ends and the other begins.

---

The formations themselves are not inert. This becomes clear as you stand in one of the central plazas and simply watch. The walls are moving. Not quickly — not in any way that would startle you — but moving. The scales on the surface are slowly cycling, new ones emerging from below, old ones detaching and falling to the ground where they are immediately absorbed by the crust-layer vegetation. The formations are growing. They are growing *now*, in real time, adding material at a rate visible to a patient observer. A ridge that was smooth an hour ago has developed a new branch. A surface that was flat has begun to curve inward, creating a new alcove, a new room. The city is under construction. The city has always been under construction. The city will never be finished.

This is the erosion parameter — not erosion in the familiar sense of wearing down, but its opposite: *accretion*, the building-up, the coming-into-being. The land here is not old. It is not weathered. It is *emerging*. The ground itself is young, still soft in places, still developing its crust, still accreting from whatever process feeds it. In the distance — far distance, where the pale plain meets the blue sky — you can see areas where the ground has not yet formed, where the surface is a haze of suspended particles, a fog of raw material that has not yet decided to be solid. The land is growing outward. The continent is extending itself. And the city grows with it, the formations pushing up from the new ground as soon as it is firm enough to support them, the vegetation colonizing the fresh surface within hours, the fauna following, the paths appearing, the whole cycle repeating.

---

The warmth is constant. It comes from below — you can feel it through your feet, a steady, gentle heat that rises from the ground and keeps the air at a temperature that is comfortable, almost balmy, with none of the extremes you would expect from a world without water to moderate its climate. The heat is the engine. It drives the accretion, feeds the vegetation, powers the slow metabolism of the fauna. The creatures here do not eat — or if they eat, they eat the ground itself, drawing sustenance from the mineral substrate the way plants draw it from soil. The vegetation does not photosynthesize — or if it does, it uses a process that has nothing to do with chlorophyll, drawing energy from the heat, from the mineral chemistry, from the slow exhalations of the growing land. The whole ecosystem runs on geology. The whole ecosystem *is* geology, animated.

And it is active. The city breathes. The formations grow. The fauna moves along its paths with the slow regularity of blood cells in a vein. The vegetation pulses and accretes and sheds and re-forms. The ground extends itself. The air, dry and still and warm, carries a faint mineral taste — calcium, silica, something metallic — and a faint mineral smell, like hot stone after rain, except there has never been rain, there will never be rain, and the smell is not memory but the present reality of a world that has never known water and does not miss it.

---

The silence is the last thing you fully apprehend, because it takes time to realize how complete it is. There is no wind noise. There is no animal vocalization. There is no rustle of leaves — the vegetation is too dry, too mineral, too still for that. The only sounds are the ones you make: your footsteps on the crust, your breathing, the faint ring of a filament when you accidentally break one. And even these sounds seem muted, as though the air itself is slightly absorbent, as though the dense, packed, saturated landscape swallows acoustic energy the way it swallows everything else — completely, efficiently, without echo.

You stand in the plaza. The formations rise around you, breathing, growing, inhabited. The fauna glides past on its silent errands. The vegetation covers every surface in its layered, interlocking, mineral profusion. The ground extends to the horizon in every direction, pale and flat and warm, still becoming, still emerging, still deciding what it will be.

The city grows.

The continent grows.

The silence holds.
