# The Handed Fen

Everything here has been placed.

Not recently — there is no *recently* here, no *lately*, no temporal gradient to speak of. The things that are here were put, and they remain in the posture of having just been put, and they will remain so. The wetness on every surface is not condensation, not seepage, not the sweat of the ground, but something closer to *givenness* — as though each stone and wooden strut and earthen berm was installed weeping, and has not yet stopped, and will not.

The ground is low. You feel it in the way sound refuses to travel, in the way your own footfall returns to you flat and un-echoed. It is a landscape of horizontals: channels, causeways, dikes, all laid out in a geometry that is almost regular but never quite — the kind of pattern a person makes when they are trying to follow a rule they half-remember, or one that was explained to them in a language they didn't fully speak. The channels carry water that is not flowing. It is *present* in them. It stays. The water has the quality of something poured and left — still, reflective, trembling only when something disturbs it directly, and even then the trembling is brief and local and does not propagate.

The materials are stone, wood, packed earth, and something that might be fired clay or might be something else — a reddish-brown material that holds its edges too cleanly, as though it were carved rather than formed, but no tool marks are visible on it. The stones are fitted without mortar. The wood is whole, unbarked in places, and in other places shaved to a pale smoothness that seems to precess slowly when you are not looking directly at it — but when you turn, it is still. Everything is built. There is no bare ground that has not been shaped, no surface that was not deliberately finished or deliberately left. Even the mud between the stones has the quality of having been *smoothed*.

Vegetation grows from the joints. It is lush in the way that a single, dense clump of reeds is lush — concentrated, exuberant, absurd in its greenness against the pale stone and the still water. But it is sparse in its distribution. Clusters of growth appear at intervals along the channels, each one a self-contained riot of fronds and tendrils and small waxy flowers, and between them there are long stretches of open water and empty stone where nothing grows at all. The lushness and the sparseness coexist without contradiction. The plants do not spread. They do not colonize. They remain where they are, which is to say, where they were placed.

The air is hot and wet and very still. The heat has a weight to it, a density, and it presses against your skin with a kind of intentionality — not malevolent, not benevolent, just *present*, the way a held hand is present. The light is extraordinary. It comes from everywhere and nowhere, a diffuse, shadowless brilliance that seems to emanate from the air itself, or from the water, or from the pale stones — you cannot find the source. There are no shadows. Or rather, there are shadows, but they fall in directions that do not correspond to any single light source, and they do not move. A stone causeway casts a shadow that points north; a wooden strut casts one that points southeast; a cluster of reeds casts three, in three different directions, all equally dark, all equally still. The light does not change. The shadows do not lengthen. The brightness is constant and total and slightly wrong, like a room where someone has turned on every lamp and then forgotten to leave.

Animals move through this place. You will see them at a distance — shapes in the channels, ripples that are not from wind, a rustling in the reed-clusters that is too deliberate to be vegetation shifting. They are present in abundance, but they keep their distance, or the place keeps distance for them. A bird — or something like a bird, with a beak that is too long and joints that bend in one too many places — will stand on a stone at the far end of a channel and watch you with an eye that reflects no light. It will not approach. It will not flee. It will stand, and watch, and when you look away and look back, it will be gone, or it will have been joined by others, or there will be fewer of them than you remember. The fauna is real. The fauna is here. The fauna does not care about your accounting of it.

The quiet is the most unsettling thing. Not silence — there is the faintest possible sound of water moving somewhere, or being somewhere, a liquidity without motion. But no birdsong, no insect hum, no creak of wood or clink of stone. The quiet has texture. It is a quiet that has been *made*, deliberately, the way the channels were made and the dikes were made and the light was made. Someone or something wanted it to have no noise, and so it has none, and the absence is a presence, and the presence is shaped.

There are structures. Dwellings, perhaps, or storehouses, or something else — low, broad buildings of the same clean-edged red-brown material, their walls so precisely fitted that you cannot slide a blade between them. Their doors are open. Their interiors are dark in a way that the bright exterior cannot explain — the darkness begins at the threshold and does not yield to the light, as though it were a substance with weight and surface tension. You can enter. Nothing prevents you. But the threshold feels like a decision that cannot be unmade, and the darkness inside is not empty. It is *full* of something you cannot see, and that something is not moving, and it is not still. It is in a state for which there is no word.

This is a place that has been built by hand. Every stone was carried. Every dike was packed. Every channel was dug with tools that left no metal marks, only the smooth, slightly organic striations of something like bone or very hard wood worked against earth. It is a place that was built by people who did not have much — no complex metallurgy, no wheels, no pulleys, no machines — but who had *time*, or who had *patience*, or who had something else that functioned like patience but was not patience, something more like *insistence*. They built everything. They built the watercourses and the light-shadows and the silence. They built the animals, or the idea of the animals, or the spaces where animals would later be built. They built the heat and the wetness and the low, flat horizon that goes on and on, past the edge of what you can see, past the edge of what you can conceive.

And then they stopped.

Or they didn't stop. Or stopping is not something that applies here. The place is in a state of completion that is also a state of suspension. It is finished and it is frozen and it is *new*. The stones are still sharp. The wood is still pale. The water is still weeping from the joints as though the builders just turned their backs for a moment and will return any second to adjust a stone or smooth a channel or plant another clump of reeds in the precise spot where reeds are supposed to grow.

But they will not return. Or they are already here, and you cannot see them. Or they are the ones watching from the far ends of the channels, with eyes that reflect no light and beaks that bend in too many places, and they are waiting to see what you do.

The fen goes on. The channels branch and rejoin and branch again, always at those almost-regular intervals, always leading nowhere in particular and everywhere in general. The light stays bright. The shadows stay wrong. The water stays still and wet and given. And the silence is the shape of a hand held out, palm up, waiting for you to place something in it.
