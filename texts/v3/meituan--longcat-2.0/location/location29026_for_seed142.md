# The Stillworks of Keth-Azhar

---

You stand on a plain that has forgotten the meaning of change.

The ground beneath you is a dark mineral composite — something betweenite andite, something that has been ground down so thoroughly by time that it has achieved a kind of mineral silence. It is not sand. Sand still has edges, still shifts, still remembers the rock it came from. This is finer than that. This is the dust of dust, the powder of powder, the final residue of a world that has been eroding since before erosion had a name. It lies in a thin, even layer over a surface that is almost perfectly flat — not flat like a floor, flat like a limit. Flat like the answer to a question that took a billion years to ask. The elevation is high. You can feel it in the thinness of the air, in the way sound dies without traveling far, in the strange clarity of the darkness — because it is dark here, profoundly dark, a darkness that has weight and texture, that presses against your eyes like velvet made of nothing.

The warmth comes from below. Not from any sun — there is no sun here, or if there is, it is so distant or so dim that it has become a theoretical concept, a rumor of photons. The warmth rises from the deep mineral body of the plateau itself, a geothermal seep so diffuse and so ancient that it has become indistinguishable from the temperature of the rock. It is not hot. It is the warmth of a body that has been dead for hours but has not yet cooled. It is the warmth of a machine that has been running so long that the heat it produces is no longer a byproduct but a property, like color or mass. The air is warm and still and dry — so dry that it feels like a solid, like you are moving through a warm, dark, mineral-flavored glass.

---

And then you see the structures.

They are not buildings. They are not ruins. They are something else — something that occupies the space between architecture and geology, between the made and the grown. They rise from the flat mineral plain at irregular intervals, spaced far enough apart that each one exists in its own pool of isolation. They are industrial in character — you can see that in their bones, in the way they are assembled from repeated units, in the regularity of their proportions, in the faint suggestion of function. But the function is gone now, or rather, the function has been completed so thoroughly that the structures have become their own purpose. They are factories that have finished producing. They are engines that have run their last cycle and have settled into a state of perfect, permanent rest.

The material is a dark metal — or something that was once metal and has since become something else. It has the color of iron but the texture ofite, and it is covered in a fine layer of the same mineral dust that covers everything here, so that the structures seem to be slowly returning to the plain, or the plain is slowly claiming them, or they were never separate from it to begin with. Some of them are tall — towers or chimneys or silos, their tops lost in the darkness above. Some are low and wide, spreading across the ground like sleeping animals. Some are frameworks — lattices of dark metal that once held something, pipes or cables or conveyors, but the things they held are gone now, and the frameworks remain, empty and perfect, like the skeletons of ideas.

The strangeness of them is not in their shape. Their shapes are rational, industrial, almost mundane. The strangeness is in their stillness. They do not rust. They do not decay. They do not shift or settle or groan. They are in a state of perfect equilibrium with the plain, with the air, with the warmth, with the darkness. They have reached their final form, and they will hold it forever. There is something deeply unsettling about this — not frightening, exactly, but wrong in a way that is hard to name. It is the wrongness of a photograph that has started to feel more real than the place it depicts. It is the wrongness of a word repeated so many times that it loses its meaning and becomes a sound. These structures are *finished*, and there is something terrible about finished things.

---

The plain extends in all directions, and the structures thin out as you move toward the edges of the settlement — if it can be called a settlement. It is more like a scattering, a handful of industrial seeds thrown across the dark mineral flatness and left to grow into whatever they were going to grow into. Between them, there is nothing. The plain. The dust. The darkness. The warmth. The silence.

The silence is not empty. It is full — full of the absence of sound, which is itself a kind of presence. You can hear your own breathing, your own heartbeat, the small sounds of your body moving through the warm, dry air. And occasionally, very occasionally, you can hear something else — a faint, high, crystalline ringing, like a glass rod being tapped, coming from one of the structures. It does not repeat. It does not vary. It simply occurs, at long intervals, as if the metal itself is remembering something. As if the structure is dreaming.

---

There is life here, but it is rare and strange and it does not behave the way life is supposed to behave.

Once, you see something moving across the plain — a small, low shape, dark against the dark ground, moving with a slow, deliberate precision that suggests purpose but not urgency. It is about the size of a hand, and it has too many legs, or perhaps no legs at all — it is hard to tell in the darkness. It seems to be made of the same mineral composite as the plain itself, or perhaps it is the plain itself, temporarily animated, a small piece of the mineral world that has decided to go somewhere. It moves in a straight line, and then it stops, and then it is still, and then it is indistinguishable from the ground again, and you are not sure if it was ever there at all.

Once, you see something that might be a fungus growing at the base of one of the structures — a pale, branching form, like a tiny tree made of salt orite, reaching up from the mineral dust toward the dark metal. But it is not a fungus. It is a mineral growth, a crystal formation that happens to look like life, or life that happens to look like a crystal formation. It is warm to the touch, and it vibrates faintly, as if something inside it is humming.

Once, you see something in the distance — a shape that is too large to be a structure and too still to be an animal, standing on the plain like a monument or a warning. You walk toward it for a long time, and it does not get any closer, and eventually you stop walking, and it is still there, in the distance, unchanged, and you understand that it is not a thing but a place — a place where the plain has risen up into a shape that means nothing and will never mean anything and will never stop being there.

---

The sky — if it is a sky — is a darkness so complete that it makes the ground seem bright by comparison. There are no stars. There is no moon. There is no light source of any kind, and yet you can see — not well, not clearly, but enough. The darkness here is not the absence of light. It is the presence of something else, something that allows vision without illumination, that lets you perceive the shapes of things without revealing their details. It is like seeing with your memory instead of your eyes. It is like the darkness is showing you what it thinks you want to see, and what it thinks you want to see is the plain, and the structures, and the dust, and the warmth, and nothing else.

---

This is Keth-Azhar. The Stillworks. The place where industry came to rest. The place where the machines finished their work and lay down and became part of the world they had been built to change. It is high and dry and warm and dark and old — so old that it has passed through age and come out the other side into something that is not youth but is not age either. It is permanence. It is the state that things reach when they have been changing for so long that they have finally, finally stopped.

Nothing will happen here. Nothing is happening here. Nothing has ever happened here, or everything has already happened here, and the difference between those two statements is a difference that Keth-Azhar has long since ceased to recognize.

The dust settles. The warmth rises. The structures stand. The silence holds.

And somewhere, far away, something rings once, like a glass bell, and then is still.
