# The Hum

---

You are standing on nothing. You are suspended in it.

The liquid is everywhere — not falling, not rising, just *there*, the way air is there in a room, except this is denser, colder, and so clear that the concept of visibility barely applies. You can see for kilometers. You can see until the geometry of distance itself begins to feel theoretical. The liquid is perhaps a water-ammonia solution, perhaps something with no Earth analogue — it has the weight of oil and the transparency of glass, and it sits at a temperature that numbs exposed skin within seconds. Not frozen. Just cold enough that you understand, viscerally, that warmth is not a property of this place. Warmth is something that happens elsewhere.

Above you — and there is an above, because the light comes from there — a star hangs motionless in the black. Not a sun you would recognize. Smaller, whiter, fixed in the sky like a nail. The planet does not rotate, or rotates so slowly that the distinction is academic. The light is constant, shadowless in the way that overcast skies are shadowless, except there is no sky. There is only the liquid and the light moving through it, and the light has been moving through it for longer than your species has had eyes. The illumination is strange in its evenness — no sunbeams, no dappling, just a cool, directionless glow that makes everything look like a photograph taken in a studio. Normal light. Unchanging light. The same light that was here a million years ago, falling at the same angle, casting the same non-shadows over the same non-moving things.

Because nothing moves. That is the first thing you truly understand, deeper than the cold, deeper than the clarity: *nothing here has ever moved, or ever will.*

The liquid is still. Not calm — still. There is no current because there is no temperature gradient to drive one. There are no tides because the moons, if they ever existed, have long since departed or fallen. There are no waves because there is no wind, because wind requires pressure differentials, and pressure here is uniform across every cubic meter of this world-sized ocean. The liquid has reached equilibrium with itself, with the rock below, with the light above, and it will remain in this equilibrium until the star dies — if the star dies. Perhaps it won't. Perhaps it too is part of the stasis, burning at a constant rate in a universe that has decided, in this one particular place, to simply *stop.*

---

And yet there is sound.

The sound is the first thing you perceived and the last thing you will ever perceive here, and it is the reason you know this place is not merely dead. It is a hum. A deep, layered, *deafening* hum that fills the liquid the way the liquid fills the world — completely, without gap or silence. It is not loud in the way a scream is loud. It is loud in the way gravity is loud: a constant, physical presence that you feel in your teeth, in your sternum, in the fluid of your inner ear. The hum has layers. There is a fundamental tone below the threshold of pitch — you feel it as pressure, as a vibration in your organs. Above it, a mid-range drone that could be machinery or could be geology or could be something else entirely. And above that, a high, thin whine that never wavers, never pulses, never rises or falls. It is the sound of a system in perfect resonance. It is the sound of a bell that was struck once, at the beginning of time, and has not yet stopped ringing.

The hum does not echo. Echoes require variation — a sound, then its reflection, then silence between. There is no silence here. The hum is omnipresent, identical at every point in the ocean, and so there are no echoes, no reverberations, no acoustic shadows. The sound simply *is*, the way the liquid simply *is*, and you exist inside it the way a fly exists inside amber.

---

Below you, the ocean floor. And above you, the ocean floor, because there is enough liquid here that the bottom is merely a concept in that direction — down — and it resolves itself, after kilometers of empty clarity, into terrain.

The terrain is sharp. That is the word that comes to mind, and it comes with force: *sharp.* Ridges like knife edges. Peaks like broken glass. Cliffs that drop in sheer, unweathered faces into darkness below. There are no rounded hills here, no smoothed riverbeds, no sand. The rock — and it is rock, dark and dense, something like basalt but with a glassy, vitreous sheen — looks as though it was formed yesterday. The features are tectonic, volcanic, born of processes that shoved the crust upward into spires and fins and blade-like ridges that would slice anything that moved against them. But nothing moves against them. Nothing has ever moved against them. The rock sits in the liquid the way a bone sits in the body — structural, inert, and untouched by the fluid that surrounds it.

Here and there, the rock is broken by vents — not hydrothermal vents in the Earth sense, because there is no heat differential to drive them, but simply *openings*, dark holes in the cliff faces that lead into the planet's interior. They do not emit fluid. They do not emit anything. They are holes in the rock, sharp-edged, geometrically precise, and they are silent except for the hum, which is louder near them, as though the holes are amplifying it, or producing it, or are in fact the instruments through which the hum is played.

---

You are not alone in the liquid.

There are things here. They are abundant — that is, they are everywhere, in the sense that the hum is everywhere — but the distances between them are so vast that you could travel for hours and see only one, or two, hanging in the clear darkness like specimens in a jar. The density of presence in this ocean is almost zero. Almost void. But not quite.

The creatures — and they are creatures, they have the *shape* of creatures, they have the *posture* of creatures — are acoustic. That is the only word that fits, though it is not precise. They are patterns in the hum. They are standing waves in the liquid that have, through some accident of resonance and pressure, taken on forms that resemble life. Some are elongated, serpentine, with tapered bodies that could be eels or could be currents made visible. Some are radial — spherical clusters of filaments that extend outward like sea urchins or jellyfish bells, except the filaments are not solid, they are zones of slightly different density in the liquid, visible only because they refract the light differently, bending the glow into faint rainbows at their edges. Some are geometric — perfect spirals, helices, fractal branches — that have no analogue in any biology but look, unmistakably, like *animals.* Like something that grew.

They do not move. They are locked in the standing wave patterns of the hum, suspended at nodes and antinodes, their shapes maintained by the constant vibration of the liquid itself. If the hum were to stop — it will not stop — they would dissolve. They would become uniform liquid, indistinguishable from the ocean around them. As it is, they persist. They have the posture of swimming, many of them — mouths open, fins spread, bodies curved in the attitude of forward motion — but they are not swimming. They are frozen in the act of swimming, the way a photograph is frozen, except the photograph has been developing for ten million years and will continue to develop for ten million more, and the image never changes.

They are not alive, exactly. But they are not not-alive. They occupy a category that this place has invented for itself, a category for which no word exists in any language you know. They are *persistent acoustic morphologies.* They are what happens when sound is loud enough and constant enough and the liquid is still enough to let the sound build structures out of itself. They are the hum's children. They are abundant. They are everywhere. And they are perfectly, eternally still.

---

And then there are the structures.

You see one, eventually. After hours of swimming — or floating, or existing — in the clear cold liquid, past the motionless acoustic creatures and the knife-sharp ridges and the dark vents, you see something that was *made.*

It is a factory.

Not a ruin of a factory. Not the remains of a factory. A factory — or the shape of one, the *idea* of one, rendered in metal and ceramic and materials you cannot identify. It sits on a ridge of dark glass-rock, anchored by pylons that were driven into the stone at some point in the deep past, and it is intact. Corroded, yes — the liquid has not been kind to the metal, and the surfaces are rough with oxidation — but intact. The walls stand. The roof holds.
