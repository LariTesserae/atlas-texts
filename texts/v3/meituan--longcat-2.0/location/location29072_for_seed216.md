# The Lattice

The first thing is the quiet.

Not the quiet of an empty room — the quiet of a place that has *eaten* its own sound. A silence with texture, with weight, like standing inside a closed fist. You become aware of your own breathing, and then you become aware that your breathing is too loud, and then you become aware that the silence is not empty but *full* — full of something that absorbs vibration the way dark water absorbs light.

Then the scale announces itself.

You are standing on something so large that its curvature is imperceptible, so vast that the far edges dissolve into a dim haze that might be distance or might be the limits of what your eyes can process. The ground beneath you is not ground. It is a surface of interwoven plant-fibers, pale and fibrous, like the inside of a gourd magnified to the width of a river valley. It gives slightly under your weight. It is warm.

---

This is the Lattice.

The name is a sound that means *the place where the growing and the making are the same act*. It sits at a height that should kill you with cold — the air is thin enough that you feel it in your lungs, in the slight ache behind your eyes — but the cold never comes. The warmth rises from below, from the plant matter itself, from the slow metabolic heat of something so enormous that its biology generates a climate. The air is thick with moisture, warm moisture, the kind that condenses on every surface and never quite falls as rain. It weeps. Every surface weeps. The fibers beneath your feet are slick with it. The structures above you — and there are structures above you, always structures above you — hang with beads of water that catch the dim light and hold it, so that the whole world glitters faintly, like the inside of a chandelier made of living tissue.

The light is dim. It comes from everywhere and nowhere — a diffuse, directionless glow that seems to emanate from the plant matter itself, or from the mist, or from the air. It is not sunlight. It is the light of a place that has been growing for so long that it has begun to generate its own illumination: a faint, greenish-gold phosphorescence that is just enough to see by, just enough to distinguish the near structures from the far ones, just enough to cast shadows that don't quite fall in the right directions.

Because the shadows are wrong.

This is the first sign that the rules here are not the rules you know. The light comes from everywhere, so shadows should be soft and diffuse, but they are sharp — and they point in different directions from the same object, as if the light is coming from multiple sources that you cannot see, or as if the objects themselves are casting shadows that don't correspond to their shapes. A structure that is round in three dimensions casts a shadow that is angular, faceted, like a crystal. A hanging vine casts a shadow that is too long, that stretches across a gap that should be too wide for any shadow to cross, and the shadow's end is not where the vine's end is.

It is somewhere else. It is pointing at something you can't see.

---

The structures rise — or extend, or *occur* — in every direction. They are not trees. They are not buildings. They are architectures of living wood and vine and fibrous membrane, woven and bent and grown into forms that serve as walls, as floors, as ceilings, as bridges across the vast gaps between one mass of growth and the next. They were shaped by hand. You can see the marks — shallow cuts in the bark, smooth grooves where a blade has been drawn along the grain, the characteristic bend of a branch that was tied and held for years until it set in a new direction. The tools were stone and bone and hardened plant-resin. The hands that made them are gone. But the shapes remain, and the plants continue to grow in the patterns that were set for them, and new growth is still shaped, slowly, by hands that work with the same patient pressure, the same bone tools, the same knowledge passed down through a lineage so long it has become a kind of instinct.

This is the thing that takes time to understand: the structures are mostly *hollow*. The density of the place is an illusion created by the abundance of plant matter. The architectures are lattices, frameworks, woven screens of living material. They are enormous — some of them hundreds of meters across — but they are mostly empty space. You can see through them. You can see the next structure, and the next, and the next, each one separated from the others by a gap of open air so wide that light itself seems to get lost in the crossing. The gaps are not empty — they are full of mist, of condensation, of the warm wet air that hangs between the structures like breath held in a mouth. But they are *open*. The space between presences is vast. The world is a series of thin, intricate surfaces separated by voids.

And the voids are where the wrongness lives.

---

The distances are wrong. The gap between two structures that you estimated at a hundred meters is, when you cross it, three hundred meters. Or thirty. The measurement changes depending on when you make it, or how, or whether you are moving toward or away from the thing you are measuring. The structures themselves are wrong — their interiors are larger than their exteriors, or their exteriors contain spaces that their interiors do not, or they have rooms that exist only when you are not looking for them and disappear when you try to find them again.

Time is wrong. The dripping water sometimes falls upward. The growth-rings on a cut branch show patterns that don't correspond to any seasonal cycle — or that correspond to too many seasonal cycles, layered on top of each other like transparencies, each one a different version of the same year. The creatures you see sometimes move in ways that don't respect the continuity of motion. They are here, and then they are there, and the space between here and there is not a path they traveled but a gap in your perception, or a gap in the world.

The plant matter itself is wrong, in the way that all living things are wrong when you look at them closely enough, but more so. The fibers have a grain that changes direction when you're not looking. The cells — visible to the naked eye in some of the more translucent growths — have too many walls, or too few, or walls that are in the wrong places. The chloroplasts, if that's what they are, move in patterns that suggest they are not converting light into energy but doing something else. Storing it. Transmitting it. Using it to hold the structure of reality in place.

Because that is what the plants are doing. They are not just growing. They are not just being shaped. They are *holding the world together*.

---

The fauna is abundant, but you don't see it at first. You feel it. A slight vibration in the surface beneath you, transmitted through the plant-fiber from somewhere far away. A shift in the mist that might be air currents or might be something moving through it. Then you see them — or you see the signs of them.

They are small, most of them. Creatures that live in the lattice-spaces of the structures, that eat the plant matter or eat each other, that move through the hollow architectures like blood cells through capillaries. They are silent. Their feet — if they have feet — are soft, padded, designed to make no sound on the fibrous surfaces. Their calls, if they have calls, are ultrasonic or subsonic or simply absent. They communicate through touch, through chemical signals, through the vibration of the plant matter itself. You see one — a small, pale thing, like a lizard crossed with a moth, its body translucent enough that you can see the slow pulse of its heart — and it sees you, and it freezes, and the silence deepens.

There are larger things. You glimpse them in the far gaps between structures — shapes that move slowly through the mist, too large to be seen clearly, their outlines blurred by distance and condensation. They might be animals. They might be something else. They move with a deliberateness that suggests intelligence, or hunger, or patience, or all three. They do not make sound. The mist swallows their movement. The plant matter absorbs their vibration. The silence holds.

The air is thick with spores, with pollen, with the microscopic reproductive material of a thousand thousand plant species that exist nowhere else. The air is alive. Breathing it is like drinking a thin soup of organic matter. Your lungs filter it, but you can feel the warmth of it, the wetness, the faint tingle of foreign biology encountering your own. The plants here do not distinguish between themselves and their environment. They grow on each other, through each other, around each other. A single structure might be composed of dozens of different species, all intertwined, all growing together, all shaped by the same patient hands into a single architectural form. The boundaries between individual plants are lost. The boundaries between species are lost. There is only the growth, and the shaping, and the slow, warm, wet equilibrium that has been reached between them.

---

The equilibrium is the erosion — the settled form, the long-existed state. The structures are not growing wildly. They are not collapsing. They are in balance: the upward push of growth matched by the downward pull of gravity and decay, the outward expansion matched by the inward pressure of the shaping hands, the accumulation of new plant matter matched by the slow composting of the old. The surfaces are weathered — not by wind, for there is little wind, but by water, by time, by the patient chemistry of life and death occurring on the same surfaces. The plant matter is smooth where water has flowed over it for centuries, rough where new growth is emerging, pocked and channeled where decay has set in and been arrested by some mineral deposit or resinous secretion. The textures tell the history of the place — not in years or centuries but in growth-rings, in layers of accumulated and eroded material, in the slow stratigraphy of a living architecture.

And the shapers — the hands that have been working with bone and stone and resin, that have been bending and cutting and weaving the living wood for longer than memory — they know this. They have always known this. Their work is not construction. It is *maintenance*. It is the ongoing, patient, never-ending act of keeping the world from coming apart. Every branch that is bent into place, every vine that is woven into the lattice, every cut that is made to redirect growth — it is all repair work. It is all holding the fabric of this place together against the forces that would unravel it.

You see them, eventually. Or you see one. A figure, human or human-sized or approximately human-shaped, working on a structure in the middle distance. It is using a tool — a blade of polished stone, lashed to a handle of bone — to make a precise cut in a living branch. The branch is as thick as your torso. The cut is shallow, deliberate, angled to direct the flow of sap and the direction of future growth. The figure works slowly, patiently, with the focus of someone performing a task that has been performed a million times before and will be performed a million times again. It does not acknowledge you. It may not be able to see you. It may be working in a slightly different version of this place, one that overlaps with yours at this point, at this moment, where the plant matter is thick enough to support both.

---

The mist drifts. The water drips. The dim light pulses faintly, like a heartbeat, like the slow respiration of something so large that it includes you, includes the structures, includes the gaps, includes the silence. The small pale creature watches you from its perch on a woven branch, its translucent body catching the light, its heart visible, beating, steady, patient, alive.

You are inside the garden. You are inside the architecture. You are inside the organism. You are inside the maintenance. The world is being held together by hands and by growth and by time and by the patient, silent, warm, wet, dim, vast, hollow, abundant, ancient, impossible act of continuing to exist.

And it is very, very quiet.
