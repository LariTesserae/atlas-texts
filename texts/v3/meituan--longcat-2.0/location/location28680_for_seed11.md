# The Plateau of Worn Gods

---

You arrive from below, always from below, because nothing sits higher than this. The ascent takes days — not because the distance is so great, but because the air thins in a way that feels personal, as though the atmosphere has decided you are not worth carrying. Your lungs learn to work differently. Your thoughts slow and then, strangely, sharpen, as if the mind must choose between oxygen and speed and has chosen speed.

And then the ground levels.

Not gently. Not with the soft rounding of a hilltop, but with an abruptness that feels like a decision. One step you are climbing, the next you are standing on a surface so flat and so vast that your eyes refuse it. They want to find the tilt, the curve, the fall-away. There is none. The ground extends in every direction with the patience of something that has been flat for longer than your species has had eyes.

This is the plateau. It is the top of what was once a mountain range so ancient that the mountains themselves have been forgotten — not by history, but by geology. The rock remembers, but only in its grain, in the compressed schist that still carries the ghost-strata of peaks that stood a billion years ago. Those peaks are gone. What remains is their root, their basement, the deep stone that was once buried under miles of elevation that has since been lifted off and scattered to the seas of a world that was young when these mountains were old.

The surface is bare. Not desert-bare — there is no sand, no dust, no loose material of any kind. The wind took it. The wind took it millions of years ago and has not stopped taking since. What remains is the bone-stone itself, exposed, scoured to a smoothness that is not quite polish and not quite natural. It is the smoothness of something that has been caressed by air for a duration that makes the concept of erosion feel inadequate. This is not erosion. This is *unveiling*. The stone has been unveiled.

And yet it is not featureless. The plateau is carved — not by water, not recently, but by water so long ago that the channels it cut have themselves been softened into anonymity. You walk through shallow depressions that might be riverbeds or might be the memory of riverbeds. The stone dips and rises in gentle undulations, like a sea frozen mid-swell, and in these undulations the light does strange things, pooling in the low spots, glaring off the high, so that the ground seems to breathe with luminance even when the sky is flat and grey.

---

The sound arrives before you do.

You hear it from the ascent — a low, continuous roar that you mistake for wind through rock, which is exactly what it is, except that the wind never stops and the rock never stops answering. The plateau is a sounding board. Its flatness, its hardness, its vast unbroken expanse — these are not qualities of silence. They are qualities of *amplification*. The wind comes from the west, always from the west, driven by pressure systems that form over an ocean so distant it might as well be theoretical, and when it hits the plateau's edge it does not stop. It rises. It spills over the lip of the ancient mountain-root and spreads across the flat stone like water over a weir, and it *screams*.

Not metaphorically. The sound is a physical thing, a wall of it, a constant assault that makes conversation impossible at ten feet and thought difficult at any range. It is the sound of air being forced across a surface too large to flow smoothly, so it breaks into eddies and vortices and standing waves that howl and moan and, at certain velocities, produce a tone so low it is felt in the sternum rather than heard. The plateau has a voice. It is not a pleasant voice. It is the voice of something that has been shouting for a hundred million years and has forgotten how to stop.

And beneath the wind, or within it, or woven through it like a thread through a tapestry: the sound of water. Not flowing. Not falling. *Dripping*. The plateau is damp. It should not be — it is high, it is windy, it is exposed to a sun that punishes — but the stone holds water the way old bone holds marrow, in its deepest structure, and it weeps. Condensation forms on the undersides of rock lips and in the carved channels, and it gathers, and it falls, and each drop strikes stone with a clarity that should be lost in the wind but is not. The drops are sharp. They cut through the roar like needles through cloth. You hear them individually — *tik, tik, tik* — and then the wind takes the sound and stretches it, and the next drop arrives, and the next, and the plateau becomes a percussion instrument played by no one, a rhythm that is almost regular, almost intentional, almost language.

---

The sky here is not the sky you know.

It is larger. The elevation has pushed you above the thickest part of the atmosphere, and the dome of the sky extends overhead with a curvature that feels exaggerated, as though you are standing in a shallow bowl looking up at the rim. The light is normal in quality — the sun is the sun, the clouds are clouds — but the *quantity* is different. There is more of it. It arrives from more directions, scattered by the thin air, reflected off the pale stone, so that shadows are soft and indistinct and everything is lit from everywhere at once. There are no sharp edges here. No hard lines. The world is rendered in a perpetual soft-focus that makes distances impossible to judge and gives the plateau the quality of a dream about itself.

And the weather — the weather is *wrong*.

Not wrong in the sense of impossible. Wrong in the sense of *too much*. The plateau generates its own meteorology. The stone heats in the sun and the heat rises and the rising air pulls moisture from the damp rock and the moisture condenses and the condensation forms clouds that did not exist thirty seconds ago, and the clouds build and darken and release rain that falls for exactly as long as it takes to wet the stone and then stops, and the sun returns, and the water steams off the rock, and the steam rises, and the cycle begins again. You can watch it happen. You can stand in sunshine and see, a mile away, a column of rain falling from a cloud that is visibly growing, its base dark and ragged, its top still white and innocent, and you can watch the rain advance across the stone toward you, a grey curtain moving with terrible purpose, and then it hits you and the world becomes water and noise and the cold shock of drops driven horizontal by wind, and then it passes, and the sun is back, and the stone is steaming, and the cloud is dissolving, and the sky is blue, and the wind is screaming, and the drops are falling in the channels, *tik, tik, tik*, and the whole thing begins again.

This is the turbulence. This is the dynamic. The plateau is not stable. It is not calm. It is a place of constant, violent, *rapid* change — not the slow change of geology but the fast change of weather, of light, of sound, of temperature. The air temperature can swing twenty degrees in an hour. The wind can double in speed and reverse direction. Fog can roll in from nowhere — not drifting, *rolling*, a wall of white that moves across the stone like something alive, swallowing the world, reducing visibility to arm's length, filling every sound with a muffled deadness that is somehow worse than the roar, and then it lifts, and the world returns, and the stone is wet, and the drops are falling, and the wind is screaming, and the sun is doing something impossible with the light again.

---

And in the hollows — in the carved channels and the shallow depressions and the places where the stone dips low enough to hold moisture for more than an hour — there is life.

Not scattered life. Not the sparse, tough, desperate life you would expect on a wind-scoured plateau at the roof of the world. *Lush* life. Life that has no business being here and is here anyway, growing with a density and a vigor that feels almost aggressive. The hollows are filled with green so deep it is nearly black, with plants that have evolved — or been shaped, or have shaped themselves — to survive the wind by growing low and dense, matting themselves over the stone like living upholstery. Ferns that should not exist at this elevation unfurl fronds the size of your hand. Mosses that have no name build cushions inches deep, saturated with water, springy underfoot, and when you step on them they release a scent that is green and old and faintly sweet, the smell of growth itself, of chlorophyll and cellulose and the patient chemistry of photosynthesis.

In the deepest hollows, where the channels converge and the water lingers longest, the vegetation builds upward. Not trees — nothing so ambitious, nothing so tall that the wind can lever it loose — but shrubs, thick and gnarled, their branches woven together by centuries of wind-driven growth into a lattice so dense it is nearly solid. These are the *thickets*, and they are the only shelter on the plateau, and they are strange. The wind does not penetrate them. Step inside a thicket and the roar drops to a murmur, and the light filters green through the lattice of branches, and the air is warm and still and humid, and the sound of dripping water is everywhere, and for a moment — just a moment — you are somewhere else entirely. Somewhere safe. Somewhere that makes sense.

But the thickets are small. They are islands. Between them, the plateau extends in every direction, bare and flat and loud and empty, and the distance between one thicket and the next can be miles, and the walking between them is exposed and exhausting and the wind never stops and the sun never stops and the weather never stops changing, and you understand, standing in the green silence of a thicket with the roar just a wall of sound away, that the plateau is mostly absence. Mostly void. Mostly nothing.

The density of life here is an illusion created by compression. The plateau has the same total biomass as a forest, perhaps, but the forest spreads it across every square meter while the plateau concentrates it into pockets and leaves the rest bare. The result is a landscape of extremes: the thicket and the void, the green and the grey, the silence and the roar. You are never in between. You are always in one or the other, and the transition is always sudden.

---

There are structures.

Not many. Not enough to suggest a civilization, or even a settlement. But they are there, scattered across the plateau with a distribution that seems random until you realize it is not — they are always near the thickets, always in the lee of a rock lip or at the bottom of a channel, always positioned to take advantage of whatever shelter the plateau offers. They are small. They are old. They are made of stone — not carved stone, not dressed stone, but fitted stone, rocks selected and stacked with a care that suggests purpose but not sophistication. The walls are low, waist-high in most places, and they enclose spaces no larger than a room. Some have roofs — slabs of flat stone propped on thicker slabs, creating cavities just large enough to crawl into. Some are open to the sky, their walls broken or incomplete, as though the builders ran out of time or stone or will.

The tech is primitive. There is no metal. There is no mortar. There is stone and there is bone — the bones of animals you cannot identify, long and curved, built into the walls as structural elements or decorative ones, it is impossible to say. There are tools, sometimes, left inside the structures or scattered nearby: hand-axes of flaked obsidian, grinding stones worn concave by use, needles of bone with eyes so small they must have been drilled with something harder than stone. The craftsmanship is careful but not refined. These are the tools of people who had time but not resources, who could work stone and bone because they had nothing else, who built small because the wind would not allow them to build large, who lived in the thickets' shadow and ate what the thickets provided and left behind these small, sad, enduring marks of their presence.

Are they still here? The people? You do not see them. You see signs — a footprint in the moss that is too fresh to be old, a tool that has been recently used, its edge still sharp, a fire-blackened stone in the lee of a wall that still smells of smoke. But the people themselves are absent, or hidden, or so few that the odds of encountering one are small. The plateau is vast and they are rare and the thickets are many, and perhaps they move between them, following the water, following the weather, following something you cannot see. Perhaps they are watching you now from the green darkness of a thicket, their eyes reflecting the light, their bodies still, their silence a choice.

---

The animals are rare too, but they are here.

You see them at the edges of things — at the margins of thickets, at the rims of channels, at the boundaries between the green and the grey. They are large. They are slow. They are built low to the ground, with thick hides that the wind cannot penetrate and broad feet that distribute their weight on the smooth stone. They resemble no animal you know. They have the general shape of a sloth but the size of a bear, and they move with a deliberation that suggests either great age or great patience, and they feed on the vegetation of the thickets with a methodical thoroughness that leaves the shrubs cropped and neat. They are solitary. You never see two together. They regard you with an expression that is not curiosity and not fear but something older than both — an assessment, perhaps, a calculation of whether you are worth the energy of concern. You are not. They return to feeding.

There are birds. There must be birds — the plateau is too open, too exposed, too full of updrafts for there not to be birds — but you do not see them. You hear them. Their calls cut through the wind with a piercing clarity that the drops of water achieve and nothing else does, high thin notes that rise and fall in patterns too complex to be random and too irregular to be song. They come from above, always from above, and when you look up you see nothing — the sky is empty, or the birds are too high, or they are the color of the sky and invisible against it. But they are there. The plateau is full of presences you cannot see.

---

And now the strangeness.

It builds slowly. It is not a single thing. It is an accumulation of small wrongnesses that individually mean nothing and collectively mean something you cannot name.

The sound, first. The wind and the water and the birds — they are too patterned. Not rhythmic, not musical, but *structured*. There are moments when the wind drops and the drops fall and the birds call and the three sounds align in a way that produces a chord — not a musical chord, a *spatial* chord, a sound that seems to come from a specific point in space and nowhere else, a point that moves, that circles you, that retreats when you approach and advances when you retreat. You tell yourself it is coincidence. You tell yourself the acoustics of the plateau are complex and the wind is variable and the drops are random and the birds are animals and there is no intention here. You tell yourself this and you almost believe it.

The light, second. The soft-focus quality of the plateau's illumination is not constant. It comes and goes. There are moments — brief, unpredictable — when the light sharpens, when the shadows harden, when the world snaps into a clarity so intense it is almost painful, and in that clarity you see things you did not see before. The stone is not smooth. It is *textured*, covered in patterns too regular to be natural — spirals, lattices, branching forms that look like ferns or river deltas or the bronchi of lungs, carved into the surface at a scale visible only in the sharpened light. And then the light softens and the patterns are gone and the stone is smooth again and you are not sure you saw them at all.

The thickets, third. They are too green. Not the green of healthy vegetation — a green that is deeper, more saturated, more *present* than chlorophyll should produce. A green that seems to emit light rather than reflect it. At night — and the nights here are dark, the elevation stripping away the atmosphere's protection and exposing you to a sky so full of stars it seems to sag — the thickets glow. Faintly. A bioluminescence, perhaps, or a phosphorescence, or something else. The green light pulses, slowly, in a rhythm that matches nothing — not the wind, not the drops, not the birds, not your heartbeat, not your breathing. It pulses to its own clock, and the clock is old, and the pulse is the plateau's pulse, and standing in the dark at the edge of a glowing thicket with the wind screaming and the stars burning and the stone beneath you warm with a heat that has no source, you understand that the plateau is not a place.

It is a thing.

Not alive, not in the way you understand life. Not conscious, not in the way you understand consciousness. But *active*. *Present*. *Attentive*. The erosion that wore the mountains to their roots did not create the plateau — it *revealed* it. The plateau was always here, beneath the mountains, beneath the stone, beneath the surface of the world. It is the world's skeleton. It is the structure on which everything else was built, and it is old, and it is patient, and it is not finished.

The turbulence is not weather. The sound is not wind. The light is not sun. These are the plateau's processes, its metabolism, its way of being in time. It is doing something — has been doing something, will continue to do something — and the thing it is doing is so slow and so vast that the concepts of purpose and duration do not apply to it. It is not building toward anything. It is not decaying toward anything. It simply *is*, in the way that deep things are, in the way that old things are, in the way that the bones of the world are.

And you are standing on it.

And it knows.

Not in a way you can feel. Not in a way you can prove. But the drops fall where you are not. The wind pushes where you are going. The thickets glow when you look at them and dim when you look away. The structures are empty but the tools are sharp. The animals watch but do not flee. The birds call but do not show themselves.

The plateau is not hostile. The plateau is not friendly. The plateau is *interested*.

And the wind screams, and the drops fall, and the light sharpens and softens, and the green glows in the dark, and the stone is warm beneath your feet, and the sky is too large, and the world is too flat, and the sound is too loud, and the silence inside the thickets is too deep, and you are very small, and very high, and very far from anything you understand.

And the plateau continues.

As it always has.

As it always will.

Until the erosion that made it is itself eroded, and the flatness is finally, truly, irrevocably flat, and the wind has nothing left to scream across, and the water has nothing left to weep from, and the stone is ground to its last atom, and the plateau — the old, patient, attentive plateau — finally, quietly, finishes whatever it is doing.

But not today.

Today the wind screams.

Today the drops fall.

Today the green glows.

Today you stand on the roof of the world and feel it breathe beneath you, and you do not understand, and you are not meant to.

You are only meant to be here.

To witness.

To be witnessed.

To be, for a moment, part of the plateau's long, slow, incomprehensible becoming.

---

*The wind does not stop. It never stops. You learn to sleep in it, to think in it, to exist inside its roar as though it were silence. You learn to read the weather in the color of the sky and the temperature of the stone. You learn to find the thickets by the change in the wind's pitch, a subtle flattening that your ears learn before your mind does. You learn to drink from the channels, to eat the thicket's fruit — small, dark, sweet, warm from the sun — to shelter in the structures when the rain comes and the wind is at its worst. You learn the plateau's rhythms, which are not rhythms but something older, something that predates rhythm, something that is to rhythm as stone is to sand.*

*And one day you realize you have stopped trying to leave.*

*Not because you cannot.*

*Because the plateau has not finished with you.*

*And you have not finished with it.*
