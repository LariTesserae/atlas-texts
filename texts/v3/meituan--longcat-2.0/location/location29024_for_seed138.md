# The Boiling Quarter

You come down into it through a cut in the rock — a passage that is still sharp at its edges, still showing the pale strata of the stone where the tools went through. The cut is young. The rock has not yet learned to round itself. Everything here is still in the shape it was left.

The air changes before you see anything. It thickens. It carries weight and warmth and a mineral smell like hot iron left in rain. Then the passage opens and the Quarter is below you, spread across the floor of a basin that might be a quarter mile across — small enough that you can see its edges, the carved walls rising on all sides, the sky (if there is sky) lost behind a ceiling of steam that glows a dim, bruised yellow-green, the color of a bruise that will never heal because nothing here ever heals or worsens. The light does not shift. It has not shifted.

The ground is dark stone, and the stone is wet. Not flooded — not drowned — but *wet* in the way of a body that sweats. Water finds every surface and films it. It weeps from the basin walls in slow, silver threads that do not hurry and do not stop. It collects in the low places into pools that are perfectly still except for the faintest tremor where new water enters, and the surface of each pool carries a skin of mineral deposit, white and pale green, like a scab that is always forming and never thickens. The water is hot. You can feel it through the air before you touch it — a warmth that is not quite steam, not quite liquid, something between, something that clings.

The machines stand in the basin like the bones of enormous animals. There are perhaps a dozen of them, and they are spaced far apart — you could walk between them for a hundred paces and hear your own footsteps on the wet stone, though you would not hear them for long because the sound of the machines fills everything. They are industrial things: tall, iron-dark, riveted, pipe-choked, their surfaces beaded with condensation. Some of them vent steam from valves that open and close in rhythms that never vary. Some of them turn — great wheels or drums or shafts that rotate at a pace you could set a clock by, if clocks here could mean anything. They are connected by channels cut into the stone, channels that carry water from pool to pool and machine to machine, and the water moves through them at a constant speed, a constant volume, the same gallons per second it has always moved and will always move.

The sound is the first thing that becomes normal and the last thing you stop hearing. It is loud — a deep, layered drone that is part engine, part water, part the groan of metal under heat. It does not peak. It does not fade. It is a single, sustained chord that the Quarter has been sounding since it began and will sound until it ends, and there is no indication that it will end. You feel it in your chest before you hear it with your ears. After a while you realize you have stopped noticing it, and then you realize that this is because it has not changed enough to notice. It is always the same. It is always exactly the same.

Between the machines, the open ground stretches. This is where the fauna move.

They are abundant. You see them everywhere once your eyes adjust to the dim, wet light — creatures that belong to this place the way fish belong to a reef. Some of them are large: slow, low-slung things with thick, hairless hides that glisten with condensation, that move from pool to pool with a deliberation that matches the turning of the machines. They drink. They stand. They move to the next pool. Their paths through the Quarter are worn into the stone — not deeply, because the stone is young and hard, but visibly, and the paths do not change. The creatures walk the same routes they have always walked. Some of them are small: quick, many-legged things that skitter across the wet stone near the base of the machines, that seem to feed on the mineral films or on something in the warm water. They move in clouds that part and reform. There are flying things too — not birds, something with translucent wings that catch the dim light — that navigate through the steam in patterns that loop and repeat, that never vary by an inch.

The creatures do not interact with each other beyond what they have always done. They do not startle. They do not deviate. A large one pauses at a pool, and a small one skitters across its foot, and neither reacts, because this has happened before and will happen again and is always, in some sense, happening.

Vegetation is scarce. The heat and the mineral load of the water are too much for most growing things. But in the corners where the stone is coolest — where the walls of the basin cut deepest and the machines' warmth reaches least — there are mats of something low and dark, something between moss and fungus, that spread across the wet rock in patches no larger than a hand. They do not appear to grow. They do not appear to recede. They are simply there, as they have always been.

The built structures are not the machines alone. There are also the channels, the pipes, the walkways — a network of iron and stone that connects everything, that gives the Quarter its sense of intention, of design. The walkways are narrow and they run along the walls of the basin and between the machines, and they are empty. No one walks them. They were built for walking and they have never been walked on, or they were walked on once and the walking has stopped, or the walking is happening somewhere you cannot see. The iron is wet and dark and the rivets stand out in rows, precise, unrusted — the air is too mineral, too strange for rust, or time does not work here the way it works elsewhere.

The water is the medium of this place. It is the blood in the channels, the sweat on the stone, the steam in the air, the content of the pools. It is always moving and always the same. It enters the basin from seeps in the walls and from a larger flow at the far end — a stream or a pipe, something that delivers a constant volume — and it leaves through drains in the floor that carry it away at the same rate it arrives. The water level in every pool is fixed. The flow in every channel is fixed. The steam in the air is fixed. The Quarter is in equilibrium — a perfect, closed balance of input and output, of heat and water and motion, and it will not change.

You stand at the edge of the basin and you watch a machine vent steam in a long, slow plume that rises and joins the ceiling of vapor above, and the plume is the same shape it was a moment ago and the same shape it will be in an hour. A creature walks past you on a path it has worn in the stone, and its gait is unhurried, and its eyes are half-closed, and it is going where it always goes. The sound of the engines fills the air like a held note. The water runs. The steam rises. The light does not change.

The Quarter is not dead. It is full of motion and warmth and life. But it is a place that has found its rhythm and will not leave it. It is a place that is always exactly itself.
