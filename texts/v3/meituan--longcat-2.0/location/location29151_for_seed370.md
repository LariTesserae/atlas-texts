# The Hall of Unceasing Drone

---

You are standing in a chamber perhaps forty feet across, though it is difficult to judge because sound has replaced distance. The walls, floor, ceiling — everything is salt. Not the coarse pink-grey of a kitchen grinder, but dense, ancient halite, pale as old bone, translucent at the edges where the light catches it, and everywhere carved. Carved with marks of stone tools, with dragged lines and cupped hollows and repeating geometries that no longer mean anything. The carvings are so dense they overlap, layer upon layer, centuries of hands working the same surfaces, and the salt has begun to flow and blur the oldest cuts, so that deep in the walls the marks have softened into something like memory.

The chamber is packed with forms. Pillars, if you can call them that — rough, salt-block columns rising from floor to ceiling, carved with the same obsessive patterning, placed so close together that in places you must turn sideways to pass between them. And other shapes too: low walls, partitions, seats, basins, stacks and rows of smaller blocks, all salt, all hand-cut, all covered in marks. The floor is uneven where blocks have been set directly onto the salt bed and have begun, over immense time, to fuse. There is no empty space here. Every surface is occupied. Every volume is filled. The chamber is saturated with itself.

And it is *loud*.

The sound is not a tone, not exactly. It is a drone — a vast, continuous, layered drone that seems to come from everywhere at once, from the walls, from the blocks, from the air itself. It is the sound of the salt singing. The mineral is under stress — the weight of the overburden above, the slow thermal warmth that pervades the chamber — and the crystalline lattice of the halite is continuously, minutely deforming. Individual crystals are slipping, cracking, relaxing, and each event sends a vibration into the surrounding mass. One crystal is inaudible. A chamber full of them, packed together, all vibrating in sympathetic chorus, is deafening. The sound is low, enormous, felt in the sternum and the teeth as much as heard. It has a beating quality — phases of reinforcement and cancellation as the waves move through the dense salt, so that the volume swells and recedes without ever reaching silence. There are higher overtones too, thin and piercing, the sound of smaller crystals, sharper fractures. And beneath it all, a subsonic pressure that you feel as much as hear, a vibration in the floor that never stops.

The light is dim and warm. It comes from no visible source. It may be the salt itself, some faint luminescence from trace minerals — lithium, perhaps, or something stranger — or it may be light filtering down through hundreds of feet of overlying rock, so attenuated by the time it reaches this chamber that it barely registers. Everything is the color of deep amber, of old honey, of candlelight through flesh. The shadows between the pillars are thick but not black. They hold a warm, brownish depth.

The warmth is constant. Not hot — not the killing heat of a forge — but warm as a body, warm as the inside of a closed hand. The air is dry beyond any earthly desert. It has never held moisture. The salt absorbs what little humidity might exist, and the air itself seems to have been dried over millennia until it is nothing but nitrogen and ancient noble gases, still and close.

There is no movement. No air current. No settling dust. The salt does not crumble; it flows, but so slowly that a human lifetime would not detect the change. The forms in this chamber have been here since before the carvings on the walls were made, and they will be here after the last of those carvings has flowed into unreadability. The sound does not change. The warmth does not change. The light does not change. The blocks do not shift. Nothing enters. Nothing leaves.

Nothing lives here. There is no fungus, no bacterium, no extremophile lurking in the salt. The dryness and the warmth and the saturation of the mineral environment have precluded even the simplest colonization. This is a world before biology, or after it, or beside it. The ecology of this place is crystal and stress and sound.

And yet — the marks. The carving was done by hands. Primitive hands, using stone tools, patiently working the salt over what must have been generations. The work is too dense, too extensive, too intentional to be natural. Someone made this. Someone sat in this deafening chamber, in this dim warmth, and cut patterns into the walls with a hand-held stone, over and over, filling every surface. And then they left, or died, or ceased to come, and the salt has been flowing ever since, slowly erasing what they did.

The chamber is near the summit of an ancient formation. Outside — though you cannot see outside, the walls are solid — the land has been worn down to near-flatness. Mountains have become hills have become plains have become this: a low, pale, salt-encrusted expanse, featureless, wind-scoured, ancient beyond the reach of any living memory. The elevation is high only in the way that a table is high — it is simply the highest remaining point of a landscape that has been almost entirely removed. The chamber sits near the top of this last prominence, buried under a thin cap of overburden, sealed and forgotten.

You are alone in a space that was made to be full. The drone is so loud that your own thoughts are difficult to hold. The warmth seeps into you. The light holds you. The salt holds you. And nothing — nothing — changes.
