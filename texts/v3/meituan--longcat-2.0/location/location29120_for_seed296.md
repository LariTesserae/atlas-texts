# The Plateau of Sunk Lēah

From a distance — if you could find a distance from which to see it — the plateau would look like a table left out in a field for ten thousand years and then forgotten. It is not a mountain. Mountains are young things, all ambition and fracture. This is what remains after ambition has been punished. The land has been planed down to something almost philosophical in its flatness, a vast disc of exposed stone sitting so high that the air up here has forgotten how to hold warmth. You feel the altitude in your chest first — a tightness, a thinning — and then in the quality of the light, which arrives from the sun but seems to have lost conviction somewhere on the way up, filtering through a sky that is almost always the color of wet slate.

The stone is old. Not old in the way that a cathedral is old, or a riverbed, or even a cathedral's foundation. Old in the way that a thought is old — something that has been turning over in the dark for so long it has become smooth and strange and slightly illegible. The rock is a deep grey-violet, shot through with veins of a mineral that might be feldspar or might be something that simply grew tired of being any one thing. In places the stone has been eroded into shallow basins, each one holding a film of water that reflects the low sky like a thousand dull mirrors. These basins are the plateau's punctuation marks, breaking the flatness into readable units. They fill during the intermittent rains — brief, half-hearted affairs that come and go like a guest who can't decide whether to stay — and then they evaporate slowly over weeks, leaving behind rings of mineral deposit and a fine sediment that is the color of bone.

## The Lēah

What grows here grows low and grows dense, and grows with the desperate patience of organisms that have accepted they will never be tall. The dominant plant is a moss-like thing — though it is not truly moss, or not only moss — that spreads across the stone in carpets of deep, saturated green so dark it reads as black in the perpetual twilight under the clouds. The Lēah, the locals call it, though "locals" is a generous word for the three thousand or so people who live up here and who may or may not have chosen to. The Lēah is warm to the touch. Not hot — warm, like skin. It metabolizes slowly, pulling nutrients from the ancient stone through a root system so fine and so extensive that a single colony can cover a hectare and be, in biological terms, a single individual. Some of the colonies on the plateau are old beyond easy reckoning. They have been growing, dying back, and growing again for millennia, and in their densest patches the accumulated organic matter forms a substrate nearly a meter deep — a false soil, a mattress of slow life built on stone that has never known true soil.

Between and through the Lēah grow ferns — small, tightly curled, their fronds beaded with moisture from the constant condensation. They unfurl to the size of a child's fist and no larger. Higher still, in the crevices where the stone has cracked along ancient fault lines, a kind of dwarf heather holds on, woody and gnarled, producing in its season a flower that is almost exactly the color of the sky, so that the plant seems to disappear into the air when it blooms. Lichens crust every exposed surface in patterns that, from a height, look like writing in a script no one has spoken for geological ages.

## The Moisture

It never really rains. That is the first thing visitors notice. Water arrives differently here. It arrives as mist that rolls across the plateau in slow, tidal waves, so thick sometimes that visibility drops to three meters and the world becomes a sphere of grey-green silence. It arrives as condensation that forms on every surface — on stone, on metal, on skin — and runs in thin, persistent streams toward the nearest basin, the nearest crevice, the lowest point. It arrives as dew so heavy in the small hours that the Lēah audibly drinks, a sound like a thousand tiny mouths opening. The air is never dry. Your clothes never fully dry. The pages of a book left out overnight will curl and soften as if remembering they were once trees. Everything on the plateau carries a thin film of moisture, a sheen, a slight tackiness, and this film is the medium through which the whole ecosystem conducts its quiet business.

There are streams, too — small, dark, moving water that appears suddenly where the stone dips and then vanishes again where the gradient flattens, as if the water itself is embarrassed by its own presence. The streams are cold, fed by condensation basins and by the slow weeping of the Lēah, and they support a sparse aquatic life: a translucent crustacean the size of a fingernail, a black algae that coats the undersides of stones in velvet patches, and — very rarely — a blind, pale thing that might be a cave fish that has somehow forgotten it lives on the surface.

## The Settlement

Sunk, the town, sits in a shallow depression near the plateau's center where the stone has sagged, over unthinkable time, into a bowl roughly two kilometers across. The buildings are built from the local stone — that same grey-violet — and from a dark, dense timber that comes from the dwarf heather when it is old enough to have a trunk. They are low buildings, built to withstand winds that come screaming across the flat stone when the weather turns, which it does unpredictably and with a kind of impersonal fury. The architecture is practical, almost brutal: thick walls, small windows, flat roofs where the Lēah has already begun to recolonize. But the buildings are connected by covered walkways and enclosed corridors, so that the town reads from above as a single sprawling organism, a stone-and-wood mycelium threading between the older, larger basins.

Industry hums. Not loudly — the sound of the plateau is mostly quiet, a hush broken by wind and water and the occasional crack of thermal stress in the stone — but it hums. There are manufactories here that process the Lēah itself, rendering it into a dense, fibrous material used in insulation and filtration across the lowlands far below. The process is chemical and requires heat, which is provided by geothermal taps driven deep into the plateau's fractured basement rock, where the residual warmth of a cooling planet still lingers. Steam vents from a hundred small pipes across the town, and the steam rises into the mist and joins it, indistinguishable. The effect is that the town is always slightly clouded in its own exhalation, a warm breath in cold air, and the Lēah loves it. Around every vent, every warm pipe, the moss grows thicker, greener, more exuberant, forming dense halos of life around the industrial infrastructure until it is impossible to tell where the machine ends and the organism begins.

Electricity is generated by the same geothermal system and distributed through cables that run along the covered walkways, humming at a frequency just below conscious hearing but felt in the teeth if you stand too long near a junction box. The light is dim — the bulbs are old, the voltage inconsistent, the windows few — and so the town exists in a permanent state of underexposure, its streets and corridors washed in a yellowish half-light that makes the stone look bruised and the Lēah look black. Shadows pool in corners and do not dissipate. Faces are hard to read at more than a few meters. The people of Sunk have learned to identify each other by silhouette, by gait, by the particular rhythm of footsteps on wet stone.

## The Silence and Its Contents

Fauna is rare, and this is perhaps the plateau's most unsettling quality. The Lēah is everywhere, dense and warm and ancient, and yet there is almost nothing that moves through it with intent. No birds, or almost none — occasionally a raptor will spiral up from the lowlands on a thermal, hunting the edge of the plateau, but the altitude punishes it and it does not stay. There are insects: a flightless beetle that lives in the deepest layers of the Lēah, a moth that is active only in the mist hours and that beats its wings so slowly it seems to swim through the air, a species of midge that emerges from the condensation basins in numbers just large enough to be annoying and not large enough to be a swarm. There are worms in the false soil, pale and thin, and there is a mouse-like thing — not a mouse, something older and stranger, with a tail like a fern frond and eyes that have not quite closed over but have stopped being useful — that lives in the walls of the oldest buildings and comes out at night to feed on the Lēah.

The silence is not empty. It is full of small, wet sounds. The drip of condensation. The slow crack and settle of stone. The almost subliminal rustle of the Lēah growing, which is not a sound so much as a texture of silence, a granular quality to the quiet, as if the air itself were being very slowly subdivided. The steam vents exhale. The distant turbines thrum. And sometimes, in the mist, there is a sound that no one has satisfactorily explained: a low, resonant tone that seems to come from the stone itself, as if the plateau were a bell that had been struck a very long time ago and was only now, after all these millennia, reaching the end of its ring.

## What Moves

The place is not still. It is active in ways that require patience to perceive. The weather cycles through its small repertoire — mist, drizzle, brief clearing, wind, mist again — on a rhythm that is not quite daily and not quite weekly but something in between, as if the plateau were breathing in shallow, irregular breaths. The Lēah grows, visibly if you watch it for an hour, sending out new growth in filaments so fine they are almost individual cells, advancing across bare stone at a rate you can see if you know how to look. The condensation basins fill and empty on cycles of weeks, and their edges advance and retreat like tide lines, leaving behind their rings of mineral deposit, building the stone's topography in increments too small for a human lifetime to notice but too consistent for the stone to resist.

The industrial processes turn. The Lēah is harvested in sections, cut and processed and shipped down the mountain on a cable system that groans and shudders in the wind. New Lēah is encouraged to grow over the harvested patches through a process involving steam and mineral slurry, and it grows, always it grows, warm and dark and patient. The geothermal taps are maintained by a crew who work in silence, communicating by hand signal, because the roar of the deep earth through the pipe system makes speech impossible in the wellheads. They are the only people on the plateau who regularly leave it, descending to the lowlands on leave and returning with a look on their faces that is not quite relief and not quite regret.

## The Strangeness

There is something wrong with the light. It is not just dim — it is *selective*. Colors at the red end of the spectrum survive the journey through the mist and cloud better than blues, and so the plateau is suffused with a warmth that has no thermal correlate, a visual warmth that exists in opposition to the actual cold of the air. Shadows are not black but a deep, saturated purple, the color of the stone itself, so that dark places seem to be made of the same substance as the light places, only more concentrated. When the mist is thickest and the visibility lowest, people report seeing structures that are not there — corridors that turn where no corridor turns, basins that hold water that is not rainwater but something thicker, darker, warmer. The old colonists — the ones who have lived in Sunk for decades and whose families have been here for generations — do not talk about this. They walk faster through the thickest mist, and they do not look at the basins.

The Lēah, for its part, grows over everything with a thoroughness that seems intentional. Abandoned buildings on the plateau's margins are consumed within a generation, pulled down by the weight of moss and root and the slow, hydraulic pressure of growth in every crack and joint. The oldest sections of Sunk, where maintenance has lapsed, show the same process in its earlier stages: a doorframe slowly widening as the Lēah pushes from within, a corridor narrowing as the walls advance toward each other, a ceiling sagging under the accumulated weight of decades of growth. The town is not being destroyed. It is being *continued* — the Lēah building on top of and through and around the stone and timber structures, using them as scaffolding for its own slow architecture, incorporating the industrial and the organic into a single, undifferentiated mass.

At the plateau's edge, where the ancient stone finally gives way to the first tentative slopes of descent, there is a place where the erosion has exposed a cross-section of the plateau's full depth: layer upon layer of compressed time, each one a different color, each one a different mineral composition, each one a chapter in a story so long that the beginning is no longer legible. The people of Sunk call this place the Face, and they avoid it. Not because it is dangerous — it is not, or not more dangerous than any other cliff edge in fog — but because something about looking at it produces a feeling that is difficult to name and impossible to hold. A sense of depth. A sense of duration. A sense that the plateau is not a place at all but a process, and that the process is not finished, and that when it is finished there will be nothing left but a flatness so complete it will be indistinguishable from the absence of the world.
