# The Drowned Plateau

---

The water comes first. It is everywhere — not falling, not flowing, but *sitting*, vast and still, a warm mineral sea spread across the highland like a held breath. You stand at its edge and cannot see the far shore. The surface is low and glassy, barely distinguishable from the air above it, and the air itself is so thick with moisture that the boundary between water and atmosphere has become a negotiation rather than a line. You are already damp before you touch it. You are already half-submerged just by being here.

The water is not clear. It carries a faint opalescence, a milky luminescence that seems to rise from within rather than reflect from above — dissolved silicates, perhaps, or calcium salts leaching from the young rock below, turning the shallows the color of weak tea and the deeper channels into something closer to mercury. In certain lights it glows faintly green. In others, a pale and unsettling gold. It is warm against the skin, not hot but *blood-warm*, as though the plateau itself is running a low fever.

And the plateau is high. You feel it in the thinness of the air despite the humidity, in the way the sun hits harder than it should, in the quality of the light — bright, unfiltered, almost surgical. This is a landscape that was recently, geologically speaking, *elsewhere*. The rock is young. It has not yet learned to be smooth. Every surface is sharp-edged, freshly fractured, still wearing the memory of its making. Basalt columns rise from the water at odd angles, their faces unweathered, their edges still capable of cutting. Veins of quartz and olivine run through the dark stone like frozen lightning, catching the high light and throwing it back in hard white points. There are places where the rock looks *new* — where a fracture has exposed a surface so fresh it seems to still be cooling, the mineral grains glittering with an almost metallic sheen. The land has not had time to soften. It is all angles and edges and sudden drops, a topography that has not yet been taught to compromise.

The water has found every crack, every fissure, every depression in this young stone. It pools in the hollows. It fills the valleys. It has risen — or the land has sunk, or both — until the plateau has become a vast, shallow, warm-water sea studded with rock formations that break the surface like the knuckles of a half-submerged hand. Some of these rise only a few inches. Others tower thirty, forty, fifty feet above the waterline, their flanks sheer and unweathered, their summits sharp enough to split the sky. Between them, the water stretches in every direction, and it is in these stretches that the vegetation begins.

---

The plants here do not grow *beside* the water. They grow *in* it, *through* it, *as* it. The shallows are thick with them — dense, layered, a living mat that covers the water's surface so completely that in places you cannot tell where the vegetation ends and the water begins. Broad-leaved things with waxy surfaces, their pads overlapping like scales. Filamentous algae that hang in curtains from submerged rock, swaying in currents so slow they might be imagined. Tall reeds and rushes that rise six, eight, ten feet from the water, their stalks so closely packed that you could not push a hand between them. And beneath the surface, a submerged forest of aquatic plants — feathery, branching, tangled — that fills the water column from bottom to top, turning the shallows into a thick green soup of life.

The density is extraordinary. Every cubic foot of water seems to contain more living matter than water itself. The plants layer upon each other — surface floaters, mid-water growers, bottom-rooted perennials, free-floating filaments — each occupying its own stratum, each pressing against its neighbors, each competing for the abundant light and the warm, mineral-rich water. The air above the mats is thick with spores and pollen and the sweet, heavy scent of growth and decay happening simultaneously. Everything is fertile. Everything is *growing*. And yet the growth has a quality that is not quite —

---

There is something about the patterns.

The vegetation arranges itself in ways that seem, at first glance, almost intentional. The reed beds form near-perfect circles. The floating mats spread in shapes that echo each other across hundreds of yards, as though the same template has been stamped again and again across the water. The submerged forests grow in rows that are too regular, too evenly spaced, to be entirely the product of chance. It is not *built* — there is nothing here that was made, nothing that was planned — but it *looks* built, or at least composed, and this is the first of the place's quiet strangenesses.

The second is the stillness. The water barely moves. There is no wind to speak of — the air is heavy, humid, almost *reluctant* to stir. The surface of the water holds its shape for hours at a time, unbroken, a perfect mirror that reflects the sky and the rock and the vegetation with a fidelity that is almost aggressive in its clarity. When a bubble rises from a gas vent in the deep rock — and they do rise, occasionally, single spheres of methane or carbon dioxide wobbling up from the fractured basalt — the ripples spread in perfect concentric circles and take a very long time to die. You can watch a single ripple travel fifty yards. You can watch it *think* about traveling fifty yards.

The third is the sound, or rather the absence of it. The place is quiet. Profoundly, comprehensively quiet. No birds — the fauna here is sparse, limited to a few species of water-skimming insects, some small crustaceans in the shallows, and occasionally a lone bird passing overhead that does not land. No frogs. No fish that break the surface. The vegetation itself is silent; there is no wind to make it rustle. The only sounds are the occasional *plink* of a water droplet falling from a reed, the low *bloop* of a gas bubble breaking the surface, and a sound that might be the rock itself — a faint, sub-audible hum, felt more than heard, that seems to come from the mineral veins in the basalt, as though the young stone is still vibrating with the energy of its formation.

---

The light is normal in the sense that it is sunlight, unfiltered by cloud or canopy, falling at the angle appropriate to the latitude and the hour. But the water vapor in the air catches it and scatters it, giving the light a quality that is both bright and diffuse — everything is illuminated, but nothing casts a hard shadow. The world is lit from within and without simultaneously. The opalescent water glows. The waxy leaves glow. The mineral veins in the rock glow. And in the late afternoon, when the sun drops toward the horizon and the light turns gold, the entire plateau seems to catch fire — not with heat but with *luminescence*, the water and the plants and the stone all throwing back the light in shades of amber and copper and deep, unsettling green.

At these times the patterns in the vegetation become more visible. The circular reed beds. The parallel rows of submerged forest. The way the floating mats seem to channel the water into slow, spiraling currents that have no business existing in a body of water this still. You begin to see that the plants are not just growing — they are *directing*. Channeling the water. Filtering the minerals. Building, slowly, over years and decades and centuries, structures that are not structures, patterns that are not designs, architectures that are entirely accidental and yet entirely precise.

---

This is a place that is still becoming. The rock is young. The water is still rising, or the land is still sinking, or both — the process is slow, imperceptible, a change that will not be visible in a human lifetime but is nonetheless happening, has been happening since the plateau first cracked and the water first found its way in. The vegetation is still spreading, still filling every available space, still layering itself thicker and denser with each passing season. The mineral deposits are still building — you can see them at the waterline, where the warm water leaves its dissolved load on the rock face, building up terraces and shelves of white and gold and pale green, creating formations that look almost *sculpted*, almost *intentional*, but are nothing more than the patient accumulation of mineral on mineral, year after year after year.

Nothing here is finished. Nothing here is old. The sharp edges have not been rounded. The deep channels have not been filled. The patterns in the vegetation have not yet reached whatever final form they are slowly, slowly approaching. The place is *early*. It is *fresh*. It is a landscape that has just emerged from whatever process made it — volcanic, tectonic, hydrological, some combination of all three — and has not yet had time to become anything other than what it is: warm, wet, dense, strange, and profoundly, almost aggressively *still*.

You stand at the water's edge. The water is blood-warm. The air is thick. The light is gold. The reed beds form their perfect circles. The gas bubbles rise, one by one, from the deep rock. The ripples spread. The ripples die. The water is still again.

The plateau waits.
