# The Cirque of Weeping Stone

---

You stand in a bowl.

Not a valley — a *bowl*. Carved from the summit of something so high that the air has thinned to a permanent whisper, this is a closed amphitheater of dark rock, perhaps three hundred meters across, its walls rising in sheer tiers like the seats of a theater built for gods who never arrived. The stone is old. Not old the way a ruin is old, but old the way a bone is old — settled into itself, weathered to a matte charcoal-black that drinks light rather than reflecting it. Water streaks the walls in long vertical calligraphy, not flowing but *seeping*, a slow exudation from fissures so fine they look like cracks in glaze. The rock is weeping. It has been weeping for longer than memory.

The floor of the cirque is open. That is the first thing you notice after the walls — the *openness*. Despite the lushness, despite the life, the space feels hollow, almost void-like, as though the bowl were a held breath. The ground is a mosaic of low mosses and cushion plants, tight to the stone, in colors that seem wrong for this altitude: deep viridian, copper-rust, a blue-green like oxidized bronze. They grow in patches, not carpets — islands of vegetation separated by stretches of bare, water-polished rock. Between the patches, nothing. Vast space between presences. The emptiness is not absence but *interval*, as if each living thing in this place has been given exactly the room it needs and no more.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. There is no stream, no pool — not exactly. But the air is wet. It condenses on your skin, on the rock, on the fine hairs of the moss. The weeping walls feed a perpetual dampness that never quite becomes a flow. In the lowest corner of the bowl, where the walls converge, the moss grows thickest and the stone disappears entirely under a pad of saturated green that quivers when you step near it, as though something beneath were breathing. The water table is at the surface here. The ground is a membrane.

The sound is extraordinary.

Wind moves through the cirque in a way that seems to have been *designed* — funneled by the bowl's geometry into a low, continuous tone, almost a chord, that rises and falls but never stops. It is not howling. It is *singing*. The weeping walls add their own voice: a constant, irregular patter of droplets striking stone, striking moss, striking the thin film of water that covers everything. And beneath these, the fauna. The cirque is alive with sound-makers. Insects — small, dark, unnamed — hum in the moss patches at a frequency that seems to resonate with the wind-tone, as if they are part of the same instrument. Birds move along the walls, quick shapes against the dark rock, calling in sharp, metallic notes that echo and multiply in the bowl until a single call becomes a dozen. Something larger moves in the high crevices — you hear it shift, hear the scrape of claw on stone, but you do not see it. The abundance is audible before it is visible. The place is *loud* with living.

The light is normal — that is, it is the light of a high, clear day, unfiltered, slightly harsh, casting shadows that are sharp-edged and black. But the bowl's walls create a strange temporality of illumination. The sun enters from the east, rakes across the floor, and is gone by early afternoon, leaving the cirque in a cool, shadowed dimness that feels like evening but is only the geometry of stone. In the shadows, the weeping walls glisten. The moss seems to generate its own faint luminosity — not bioluminescence exactly, but a quality of *holding* light, of releasing it slowly, so that the green patches glow softly in the shade like embers.

There are structures here. Scattered. Small.

A cairn near the eastern rim — not a marker but a *stack*, stones balanced with a precision that suggests patience rather than engineering. It is waist-high, conical, and it has been there long enough that moss has begun to colonize its lower stones, integrating it into the floor. Further along, a partial shelter: three walls of dry-stacked stone, open to the south, roofless, the interior filled with a drift of dead moss and the small bones of birds. The stones are hand-sized, chosen for flatness, fitted without mortar. Primitive. The work of hands that understood weight and balance but nothing more. There are marks on the walls of the cirque itself — faint, almost erased by time and water. Not writing. *Scratches*. Parallel lines, grouped in fives, pecked into the dark stone with a harder stone. Counting, perhaps. Or prayer. They are high on the wall, in a place that would have required climbing to reach, and they face the center of the bowl, as though whatever was being counted or invoked was *down here*, in the open space, in the emptiness.

The coolness is pervasive but not bitter. It is the coolness of deep shade, of stone that has never known full sun, of air that has been in contact with water for a very long time. It smells of wet rock and green growth and something else — a faint mineral tang, like the air around a thunderstorm, though the sky above is clear. Ozone, perhaps. Or something the stone releases when it weeps.

The dynamic quality of the place is the thing that stays with you. Nothing here is still. The wind shifts and the tone of the bowl changes. The water seeps and the moss swells visibly, almost imperceptibly, but *visibly* if you watch long enough. The insects pulse in their patches — the hum rising and falling in waves that move across the floor like weather. The birds trace the walls in patterns that seem random but repeat, repeat, repeat. A small mammal — something between a pika and a vole, with rounded ears and dark, wet eyes — emerges from a crevice, crosses the open stone at a desperate sprint, and vanishes into another patch of moss. The interval between emergence and disappearance is perhaps four seconds. The space between presences is that kind of space: open enough to be dangerous, empty enough to be seen across.

And the weirdness — the slight, persistent *wrongness* — is in the way the bowl seems to *listen*. Not metaphorically. The acoustics are such that any sound you make — a footstep, a breath, a word — is caught by the walls and returned to you changed, layered, as though the cirque were repeating it back in a voice that is almost but not quite your own. The weeping walls. The singing wind. The humming insects. The calling birds. The scraping of something unseen in the high dark. All of it held in this small, high, wet, empty, living bowl of stone, at the top of the world, where the air is thin and the water comes through the rock like memory through bone.

You are not the first to stand here. The cairn knows. The scratched walls know. The shelter with its roof of open sky knows.

But the bowl does not care. The bowl is busy with its own processes — the slow seep, the patient growth, the wind's long note, the counting of small lives in the moss. It has been doing this for longer than the structures, longer than the scratches, longer than whatever hands stacked the stones. It will continue. The water will weep. The walls will sing. The emptiness will hold exactly as much life as it needs, and not one thing more.
