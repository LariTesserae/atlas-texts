# The Span

From a distance — if there were a distance from which to see it whole, which there isn't, because it extends past the curve of the horizon in three directions — the Span looks like a mountain range that someone has been carving into a city for ten thousand years and then forgot about halfway through.

But you can't see it from a distance. You can only be in it.

---

The first thing you notice is the floor. There is no ground. There is only the built surface beneath you — a vast, weathered expanse of ferroconcrete and riveted plate steel and old brick and poured aggregate and something that might be petrified wood but isn't, it's a composite, it was always a composite — and it goes on. It goes on in every direction, rising and falling in long, gentle undulations, like the back of something enormous and sleeping. The surface is not smooth. It is not meant to be walked on, most of it — it is meant to be the floor of a factory, the deck of a platform, the roof of a structure beneath, the wall of a structure beside, and over the centuries (longer than centuries) it has been all of these things at once and the distinctions have softened. Drainage channels cut across it at irregular intervals, carrying thin sheets of water that catch the light. The water is always moving somewhere. You can hear it.

The light comes from above, but "above" is a complicated concept here. The Span is open to the sky in places — great gashes where something collapsed long ago and was never repaired, or where the structure simply ends and there is air and brightness pouring in. In other places, the sky is ocstructed by the Span itself, by upper decks and catwalks and the undersides of platforms that rise in layers so dense and so interwoven that the light filters through in shafts and patches, bright and warm and alive with motes. The motes are mostly spores. The air is thick with them. Everything is thick with everything.

---

The vegetation does not grow *on* the Span. It grows *as* the Span. This is the thing that takes time to understand.

The structures — the buildings, the platforms, the bridges, the tanks, the housings, the thousand thousand built things that make up the fabric of this place — are not separate from the life that covers them. The ferns do not sit on the concrete; their roots have entered it, found the old rebar, followed it, become entangled with it, and in places the concrete has become a kind of soil, has been broken down by acids and freeze-thaw and the patient chemistry of moss until it is a substrate that is neither built nor grown but both. The trees — and there are trees, real ones, tall ones, their canopies thirty and forty feet up — do not grow from planters. They grow from the junction points where three or four structural members meet and have, over decades, accumulated a mulch of leaf litter and rust and bird droppings and the slow, fine dust of disintegrating aggregate, and in that accumulation they have found purchase, and they have reached upward toward the light, and they have made their own light by breaking open the roofs above them.

The warmth helps. The Span runs warm — not hot, but warm, a steady, ambient warmth that comes from below, from the deep machinery that still turns in the lower levels, from the composting action of a million tons of organic matter slowly becoming soil, from the simple thermal mass of a structure the size of a geography holding the heat of the day and releasing it through the night. It never gets cold. It never gets truly hot. It stays in the range where things grow, and so things grow.

And the water. The water is everywhere, but it is never flooding. It condenses on the cooler upper surfaces — the steel beams that reach into the open air, the sheet-metal roofs of the old factories — and it runs down in rivulets, and it drips, and it pools in the hollows of corroded tanks, and it feeds the moss, and the moss feeds the ferns, and the ferns feed the soil, and the soil feeds the trees, and the trees break open new surfaces for new condensation, and the cycle is so old now that no one could say whether the water was designed to be here or whether the design simply learned to keep it.

---

The fauna is abundant in the way that a place is abundant when it has been left alone long enough to become itself.

Birds nest in the upper structures — not just a few birds, but populations, flocks, species that have adapted to the vertical complexity of the Span the way cliff-dwellers adapt to a canyon wall. Their calls echo between the platforms and are answered from below, from the mid-levels where the light is green and filtered and the air is heavy with moisture. Insects hum in clouds around the standing water. Lizards — or things very like lizards, scaled and quick and iridescent — sun themselves on warm steel plates. There are mammals in the lower levels, in the dark, in the warm: small ones, mostly, things that live in the walls and come out at the edges of day. You hear them moving in the infrastructure, a constant, soft, scurrying presence, like the Span itself is breathing.

And there are the bees. The bees are everywhere. They have found the flowers that grow in the condensation zones — small, pale, hardy things that bloom in the spray of leaky pipes and the mist of drainage channels — and they have built hives in the hollow cores of structural columns, in the abandoned control rooms of factories, in the spaces behind panels where the warmth is constant. Their sound is part of the sound of this place, a low, dense, vibrating hum that you feel in your chest before you hear it with your ears.

---

The sound. The sound is constant and layered and it does not resolve into individual sources easily.

There is the industrial sound — the deep, rhythmic thud of a compressor somewhere far below, the whine of a turbine spinning in a housing that has been spinning for longer than anyone can remember, the clank and hiss of steam from a process that still runs, that has always run, that produces something (no one is entirely sure what) in the deep levels where the light is artificial and orange and the air tastes of oil and ozone. The electricity is still on. Not everywhere, but in enough places that the hum of it is a baseline, a frequency that underlies everything, that makes the fillings in your teeth ache if you listen for it.

There is the water sound — dripping, flowing, pooling, the occasional rush when a drainage channel reaches capacity and spills over its lip in a thin waterfall that drops ten or twenty feet to the next level, where it is caught and channeled again.

There is the biological sound — the birds, the insects, the small mammals, the rustle of leaves in a breeze that moves through the open gashes in the structure, the creak of trees slowly, slowly levering apart the concrete that holds them.

And there is the structural sound. The Span creaks. It settles. It sighs. It is a vast, built thing, and it is old, and it moves — not much, not quickly, but it moves, the way a ship moves at sea, a constant, slow, living adjustment to load and temperature and the patient work of gravity. You feel it more than you hear it: a vibration in the floor, a slight sway in the upper levels, a sense that the thing you are standing on is not fixed but is, in some deep and fundamental way, *floating*.

---

The scale is the thing that undoes you, eventually.

You walk for an hour and you have not left the district you started in. You walk for a day and you have passed through three or four distinct neighborhoods of the Span — areas where the character of the structure changes, where the old factories give way to residential blocks (abandoned? repurposed? still occupied? — it is hard to tell, and the answer may be different in each block), where the density of the vegetation shifts from fern-and-moss to something more arboreal, more canopy-like, where the light changes from the bright, open glare of a collapsed roof to the green, aqueous dimness of a fully enclosed level where the only illumination comes from bioluminescent fungi that have colonized the walls and from the occasional, still-functioning industrial lamp.

And it keeps going. You can see it going. From a high point — a crane that still stands, its arm reaching up into the open air, its cab rusted but intact — you can look out over the Span and see it extending to the horizon, a vast, uneven, layered landscape of built things and grown things so thoroughly intertwined that you cannot tell where one ends and the other begins. The elevation changes. It rises in places into towers and spires — old communication masts, the cores of elevators that no longer elevate anything, the chimneys of processes still running — and it falls into depressions where water collects and the air is thick and warm and the vegetation is so dense it is almost a jungle, a built jungle, a jungle of steel and concrete and living green.

---

The people. There are people.

Not many, in any one place. But they are here. They live in the Span the way animals live in a forest — in the interstices, in the spaces that the structure provides, in the old rooms and corridors and platforms that have been claimed and enclosed and made habitable. They farm in the light zones, where the collapsed roofs let in the sun and the condensation provides water and the soil — the made soil, the built soil, the soil that is rust and concrete dust and leaf litter and time — is deep enough to grow food. They keep bees. They maintain some of the old machines, the ones that still do something useful — the pumps, the generators, the water treatment systems that keep the dampness from becoming a flood. They leave other machines alone, the ones that run on their own, the ones that have been running so long that no one knows what would happen if they stopped.

They are not primitive. They are not advanced. They are *industrial* — they work with what is here, with the materials the Span provides, with the tools that can be made from the tools that can be made from the steel and copper and old, old plastic that is everywhere. They have electricity, sometimes. They have running water, usually — the Span provides. They have light, from the sun and from the lamps and from the bioluminescence and from the fires they burn in the lower levels where the air is warm and still and the smoke rises through a thousand cracks and channels to the open air above.

They have been here a long time. They do not question the Span. The Span is the world.

---

And the Span endures. It endures the way a mountain endures — not by resisting change but by absorbing it, by being so large and so complex and so deeply, deeply established that the changes happen within it rather than to it. A column fails and the load shifts to the columns beside them, which were designed for this, or which have grown into this, or which have been reinforced by the roots of trees that have wrapped around them like hands. A platform sags and the water that was draining east now drains west, and a new marsh forms in the west, and new reeds grow, and new birds come to nest in the reeds, and the ecology adjusts, and the Span continues.

It is not a ruin. A ruin is something that has been abandoned by its purpose. The Span has not been abandoned. It has *become* its purpose. It is a place where things live. It is a place where water moves and light falls and warmth rises and things grow and things decay and the machinery turns and the bees hum and the birds call and the structure creaks and settles and the whole vast, impossible, total-built thing breathes its slow, warm, damp breath into the bright air, and goes on.
