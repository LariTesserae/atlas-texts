# The Plateau of Weeping Stone

---

You come up through the last of the cloud layer and the world opens into something that should not exist at this height.

The air is thin but heavy — saturated, almost chewable. Every breath tastes of mineral and cold moisture. You are above the tree line, above the soil line, above the line where most things decide to live. The elevation is extreme: a vast plateau, flat enough to suggest intention, broken enough to suggest age. It stretches in every direction farther than the brightness allows you to see, which is saying something, because the light here is *aggressive*.

The sun — or whatever serves as sun in this place — sits high and white and unfiltered. There is no haze to soften it, no canopy to dapple it. It strikes the stone and throws it back at you doubled. Shadows are knife-edged and black. The rock itself is pale — a bleached, almost bone-white limestone or something like limestone, something that was once alive in a deep geological sense and has since been exfoliated, scoured, and left open to the sky. It is riddled with holes. Not pockmarks — *holes*. Windows, chimneys, tubes, passages. The plateau is a sponge of calcium and time, and from every opening, water weeps.

Not flows. Not drips. *Weeps*. A slow, constant, seeping exudation that keeps every surface slick and shining. The water emerges from the rock itself as though the stone is perspiring, as though the plateau is a single vast organism with a fever. It has nowhere to go. It pools in the shallow depressions, fills the carved channels, stands in sheets no deeper than a finger's width across the flat expanses. In the low spots it gathers into clear, still pools that reflect the white sky so perfectly you cannot tell where the water ends and the air begins. The sound of it is everywhere — not the crash of waterfalls or the rush of rivers, but a constant, layered, *loud* susurrus. A whispering. A million tiny voices saying the same thing over and over in a language just below comprehension.

The erosion here is old. Patient. The kind of erosion that has stopped fighting and started collaborating. The stone has been carved into forms that look almost deliberate — arches, flutes, columns, hollowed chambers open to the sky like mouths mid-sentence. Some of these formations are massive, the size of cathedrals. Others are small enough to cup in your hand. They repeat. They *pattern*. Walk a hundred paces and you will see the same shapes again, as though the plateau is iterating on a theme, trying variations, working something out. The density of these forms is high — they crowd against each other, lean together, share walls and bases. In places the stone lace is so intricate you could not fit a blade between the pillars. In others it opens into clearings, flat and bare and flooded with that merciless light.

And in these clearings, and along the ridges between the carved channels, and tucked into the lee of the larger formations — structures. Small ones. Low walls of stacked stone, dry-fitted, no mortar. Enclosures that might be pens or rooms or something without a name. A leaning slab propped against another to make a shelter. A circle of standing stones no taller than your knee, arranged with a precision that feels either devotional or functional. They are scattered, these marks. Not a settlement. More like a place that has been visited many times by many hands, each leaving a small thing behind, none staying long enough to build anything that would last. The tech is simple — stone on stone, bone tools perhaps, cordage of sinew or plant fiber from somewhere far below. Some of the stones in the walls are carved with lines, grooves, simple geometries. Some of the standing stones have been rubbed smooth on one side, as though generations of hands have touched the same spot.

No one is here now.

No one living, anyway.

The fauna register reads zero and holds. No birds ride the thermals above the plateau. No insects skitter across the wet stone. No lichen — and this is the thing that strikes you after the light and the water and the silence-under-the-sound, the thing that takes a moment to land: there is no lichen. No moss. No algae film. No biofilm. The stone is bare. The water runs clear over sterile calcium. The pools have no edges of green, no floating motes of life. The vegetation parameter is near-zero and it *shows* in a way that makes the place feel not dead but *prior* — as though life has not yet arrived, or has been and gone, or has simply never been invited. The ecology, such as it is, runs on mineral and water and light alone. Dissolution and precipitation. The slow breathing of calcium carbonate in and out of solution. The plateau is eating itself and rebuilding itself in the same motion, and it has been doing this for longer than anything has been alive to watch.

But the density reading is high. The place is *full*.

Not of life. Of *presences*.

You feel it first as a pressure behind the eyes, a sense of being observed by something that does not have eyes. The carved stone forms — they are not random. They are not even just erosional. Some of them are too regular. Too repeated. The same flute shape, the same hollow chamber, the same spiral groove, appearing again and again across the plateau in a distribution that feels less like geology and more like *writing*. As though the water, in its patient work of dissolution, is transcribing something. As though the plateau is a record, and the record is being read by the light, and the reading produces sound — that constant, loud, whispering susurrus that you cannot quite resolve into words no matter how long you listen.

And then there is the other thing. The thing the instruments flag as *weirdness*.

The light does not always come from the same direction.

You will be walking across a flat expanse, your shadow stretching long and black to your left, and then — without transition, without cloud or movement — your shadow will be to your right. The sun has not moved. The sky has not changed. But the light has *shifted*, as though the source has been quietly replaced by another source in a slightly different position, and the old light has been turned off without ceremony. It does not happen often. Once an hour, perhaps. But it happens. And each time it happens, the carved shadows in the stone change, and the patterns of light and dark on the plateau rearrange themselves into a configuration that looks, for just a moment, like a face. Or a map. Or a sentence in a script you almost recognize.

The water does other things.

In the still pools, the reflection is not always of the sky above you. Sometimes it shows the plateau from an angle you are not occupying — from above, or from the side, or from a vantage point that does not exist on the surface. Sometimes it shows the plateau at a different time of day, the light raking from an impossible angle, the shadows long and wrong. Once, you look into a pool and see the plateau at night — a night that has not happened yet, or that happened long ago — and in the reflection, the structures are not scattered and small. They are connected. They are *many*. There are walls and roads and enclosures covering every flat surface, and the carved stone forms have been incorporated, built around, built into, as though someone learned to live *inside* the weeping rather than beside it. The reflection lasts three seconds. Then a drop of water falls from a ledge above and breaks the surface, and when the ripples clear, it is only sky again.

The sound changes too. The whispering rises and falls in waves, and in the rises you can hear — or think you can hear — rhythms. Patterns. Something like language, or like music, or like the sound of stone being worked by tools. Tap. Tap. Tap-tap. Pause. Tap. The same sequence, repeated. Coming from inside the rock. Coming from the holes and chimneys and passages that riddle the plateau. As though something is still building. Still carving. Still transcribing the record into the stone with patient, primitive tools, and has been doing so for so long that the sound has become indistinguishable from the sound of water.

You find a tool. It is lying in a shallow pool in one of the clearings, half-submerged in water so clear it is almost invisible. It is a hand-axe. Simple. Elegant. Made of a dark stone that does not match the pale limestone of the platform — chert, perhaps, or obsidian, carried from somewhere far below or far away. The edge is still sharp. The grip is worn smooth. It fits your hand as though it was made for a hand exactly like yours, which is impossible, because the hand that made this was not your hand, and the hand that held it last was not your hand either, and yet it fits, and when you pick it up the whispering stops.

All of it. Everywhere. Across the whole plateau. The water still moves, the light still strikes, but the *sound* — the constant, loud, layered sound of a million tiny voices — ceases. The silence is so sudden and so total that your ears ring with the memory of it. You stand in the clearing with the axe in your hand and the white light pressing down and the wet stone shining and the absolute silence ringing, and you understand that the plateau has been waiting for someone to pick this up.

Then the sound returns. Louder than before. And it is not whispering anymore.

It is *singing*.

The carved stone forms are resonating. Each one, each flute and chimney and hollow chamber, is producing a tone — a single, clear, sustained note — and the notes are different for each form, and together they are making a chord. A vast, complex, shifting chord that moves across the plateau like weather. The water in the pools begins to vibrate. The standing stones in the little circles begin to hum. The light shifts again — not just direction now but *color*, taking on a faint, impossible blue, as though the sun has been replaced by a sun made of sky.

And the reflections in the pools — they all show the same thing now. Not the plateau. Not the sky. A door. A simple, low, stone-framed door set into a wall of weeping limestone, standing open, leading into darkness that the light does not penetrate.

The singing rises. The water weeps faster. The stone beneath your feet is warm — not hot, but warm, as though something vast and slow has begun to breathe beneath you, and the plateau is not a plateau at all but a surface, a skin, a lid, and what is underneath has just noticed that you are standing on it.

The axe in your hand is vibrating at the same frequency as the singing.

You have a choice. You know you have a choice, because the place is showing you one — the door, the open door, the darkness beyond — and the choice is as old as the stone and as simple as the tool in your hand.

But that is a story for another time.

For now, the plateau sings. The water weeps. The light shifts and shifts again. The carved forms resonate with a chord that has been building for millennia, and the scattered structures — the small walls, the leaning slabs, the circles of standing stones — are not ruins. They are *offerings*. Left by everyone who came before. Everyone who heard the singing. Everyone who picked up the tool.

Everyone who chose.
