# The High Drown

The first thing is the wet. Not rain — there is no rain here, no falling. The wet is simply the condition of being. The air is so saturated that it has weight, a warm pressure against the skin like a held breath. You do not so much breathe it as receive it. At the surface — if surface is even the right word — the distinction between air and water has become a negotiation rather than a boundary. There are places where you walk on stone and the warm liquid comes to your knees, places where it comes to your chest, places where you tilt your head back and your mouth breaks through into something you can breathe, barely, a thin stratum of air that tastes of minerals and green.

This is the High Drown. A plateau that should not be underwater, that was not underwater, that is becoming underwater by degrees so slow they can only be measured in the growth rings of the great bladder-kelp that colonizes every standing stone. The elevation is real — the sky above is thin and far, and on the rare occasions when the cloud-ceiling breaks you can see that you are impossibly high, that the true sea-level is a vertical distance below that would kill a falling body. But the water has come anyway. Not from below. From the air itself, condensing, accumulating, a warm rain that fell for generations and never stopped, that filled the calderas and the rift valleys and the collapsed lava tubes until the whole plateau became a shallow sea of extraordinary warmth.

The rock is new. This is the thing that strikes you after the wet and the warmth — the rawness of the stone. There has not been time for weathering. The erosion here is embryonic, a process just beginning to remember itself. The basalt and obsidian and pumice that form the bones of this place are sharp-edged, glassy, unrounded. Where water has touched stone longest, there are beginnings — a slight softening of an edge, a faint etching, the first tentative grooves that in ten thousand years might become channels. But for now the geology is a fresh wound. Columnar basalt rises from the shallows in hexagonal pillars so precise they look cut, and they are cut — by cooling, by fracture, by the clean mathematics of contraction. Obsidian blades jut at angles that catch the dim light and throw it back in colors that obsidian should not produce: a deep amber, a bruised violet, a green that has no name in any language spoken here.

The light is dim. The cloud layer above is permanent, a ceiling of warm vapor that diffuses whatever sun exists into a directionless amber glow. There is no shadow here, or rather, shadow is a gradient so gentle it can only be perceived by closing your eyes and opening them again, by the slight adjustment of the pupil. At midday the world is a warm twilight. At what passes for night, the darkness is absolute but brief, and the water retains the day's heat so faithfully that you cannot tell the difference by touch.

And the water is thick. Not viscous — that is too strong a word. But it has a body to it, a presence. It is mineral-rich, saturated with dissolved silica and iron and sulfur from the young rock, and this gives it a weight, a silkiness, a reluctance to let things pass through it. Your hand moved through warm water leaves a trail of tiny bubbles that rise so slowly they seem to be considering whether to rise at all. The surface tension is high. Insects — the few that exist here — can stand on it, and do, their legs making dimples rather than breaks.

The vegetation is lush in the way that a wound is lush with healing tissue — urgent, excessive, almost aggressive in its vitality. The bladder-kelp is the dominant form, great bulbous stalks that rise from the deeper channels and spread across the surface in mats so dense you can walk on them if you know the paths, if you distribute your weight, if you read the subtle language of which bulbs are firm and which are ready to burst. Between the kelp mats, in the shallow water over the rock platforms, there grows a moss that is not quite moss — a colonial organism that secretes a thin film of silica from the water, building tiny glassy structures, spicules and lattices, so that the rock surfaces glitter with what looks like frost but is alive. The moss is warm to the touch. It metabolizes geothermal heat from below and the dissolved minerals from the water and it grows, slowly but without ceasing, building up layers that will in time become something like soil, though soil has no precedent here.

There are taller things. In the deeper channels where the water is too deep to stand, there are structures that might be trees or might be coral or might be something that has no analog — vertical growths of mineral-encrusted organic matter that rise ten, fifteen, twenty feet from the bottom and branch into fractal canopies just below the surface. They are not wood. They are not stone. They are a composite, built by centuries of slow accretion, layer upon layer of silica-organic matrix, and they are hard as rock and light as pumice and they ring with a clear, high note when struck. The people here use them for everything.

The people. Yes. There are settlements — dense, layered, built with a patience that matches the landscape's own slow tempo. The largest is called, in the local language, something like "the place where you can stand" — a name that carries the weight of necessity, because standing, here, is not guaranteed. The settlement is built on and around a cluster of basalt pillars that rise above the waterline, connected by bridges of woven kelp-fiber and lashed mineral-wood, by floating platforms buoyed by sealed bladder-kelp bulbs, by causeways of piled stone so precisely fitted that they have survived the slow rise of the water for generations.

The construction is primitive in its tools but sophisticated in its engineering. There is no metal here — the water corrodes it, or perhaps the ore does not exist, or perhaps the people have not found it. They work with obsidian, with bone, with the mineral-wood that they harvest from the deep structures and shape with patient abrasion using sand from the volcanic beaches. Their blades are sharp enough to split a hair. Their cordage is kelp-fiber twisted on the thigh, strong enough to hold a platform against the slow current. Their architecture is post-and-beam, lashed not nailed, flexible not rigid, designed to move with the water rather than resist it.

The density of the settlement is remarkable. Structures are built on top of structures, platforms stacked three and four levels high, connected by ladders and ramps and rope bridges that sway with a motion that becomes, after a time, as natural as breathing. The population is not large in absolute terms — perhaps three thousand in the largest settlement, a few hundred in the smaller outposts — but the built environment is so layered, so interwoven, that the sense of human presence is constant. You are never alone in the settlement. You can always hear, if not voices, then the creak of fiber, the splash of water displaced by movement, the tap-tap-tap of someone working stone two platforms above.

The water is rising. This is the central fact of life here, the fact around which all culture organizes itself. It rises slowly — an inch in a generation, perhaps two — but it rises. The oldest platforms are submerged now, their posts standing in deep water like the ruins of a drowned civilization, which in a sense they are. The people build upward. They have always built upward. Their origin stories speak of a time when the water was lower, when you could walk on dry stone from one pillar to another, but these are myths, unverifiable, and the practical reality is that the water is here and the water is rising and you build up or you swim.

The fauna is rare. The warm, mineral-heavy water supports life, but not abundantly. There are small crustaceans in the kelp mats, translucent things that filter the water for algae. There are eel-like creatures in the deep channels, pale and blind, that hunt by sensing the vibrations of the water. There is a kind of jellyfish — if jellyfish is the word — that drifts in the upper layers, its bell pulsing with a faint bioluminescence that is the only light in the absolute darkness of the brief nights. The people eat these creatures, and the kelp, and the moss (which is nutritious if you can get past the grittiness, the tiny silica spicules that crunch between the teeth). They do not farm. They gather. They are, in this sense, a culture at the very edge of the neolithic, suspended between foraging and something more organized by the simple fact that the water will not let them plant.

The strangeness. I have been circling it, but now I will name it. The water here is not entirely water. The dissolved silica, the iron, the sulfur — these are present in concentrations that should not support life, yet life thrives. The mineral-wood structures in the deep channels are not entirely organic. The moss that builds glass is not entirely plant. There is a blurring here, a category collapse, as if the boundary between living and mineral is more permeable than it should be. The people know this. They have a word for it — a word that means something like "the slow becoming" — and they use it to describe the way the kelp mats gradually incorporate mineral particles from the water and become, over decades, something like stone. They use it to describe the way their oldest buildings, the ones that have been submerged, are now encrusted with living moss that is building new structures on top of the old, so that the settlement is growing downward even as it grows upward, a double city, one above the water and one below, connected by the same posts, the same pillars, the same slow patient accretion.

The sound. It is mostly quiet. The thick air muffles. The water absorbs. The kelp mats dampen. Sound here is intimate — you hear what is close to you, and distant sounds arrive softened, rounded, stripped of their harsh edges. The dominant sound is the lapping of water against stone and wood, a sound so constant it becomes silence, becomes the baseline against which all other sounds are measured. There is the creak of fiber, the tap of stone on stone, the occasional call of a worker to a neighbor. At night, if you submerge yourself to the ears, you can hear the deep structures singing — a low, resonant hum produced by water moving through the fractal canopies, a sound that is felt as much as heard, a vibration in the chest, in the bones.

The warmth is constant. The geothermal heat from below, the insulating cloud layer above, the specific heat of all that mineral-rich water — these combine to produce a temperature that varies by no more than a few degrees across the entire plateau, across the entire year. It is the temperature of blood, of a bath, of the inside of a body. You are never cold here. You are never hot. You are held.

And the water rises. And the people build upward. And the moss builds its glass. And the deep structures sing. And the obsidian catches light it should not be able to catch and throws back colors that have no name. And the warm wet air presses against your skin like a held breath. And the place becomes more itself, slowly, patiently, in the way of all true things — not by arriving, but by continuing.
