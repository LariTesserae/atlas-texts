# The Treadway

The first thing you notice is that the ground breathes.

Not metaphorically. The surface — a dark, compressed mat of peat and fibrous silt that stretches perhaps three hundred paces across — rises and falls in slow, almost imperceptible exhalations, as though something enormous beneath it were sleeping lightly and dreaming of water. You can feel it through your soles if you stand still: a gentle swell, a slow subsidence, like the chest of a hound at rest. The people here have learned to read this motion the way farmers read cloud cover. When the ground exhales sharply, they say the deep water is restless. When it goes flat and still for too long, they grow quiet and move their children to higher structures.

The settlement occupies the entire dry-ish surface, because dry-ish surface is all there is. Structures crowd together with the closeness of a reef — wattle-and-daub cells stacked two and three high, their walls bowed outward with the pressure of neighbors, their roofs thatched with reed bundles lashed to frames of green wood that have not yet stopped growing. In some places, the wood has rooted where it touches the ground and become architectural, so that a wall is also a root system, a roof beam also a living branch, and the whole settlement has the quality of a garden that has been trained into habitation. The gaps between buildings are narrow enough that you must turn sideways to pass through them, and these passages — these accidental streets — are roofed with whatever came to hand: hide, bark, woven matting, sheets of pressed peat. The result is that much of the settlement exists in a kind of twilight, even at midday, the light filtering through a patchwork of organic filters, green-brown and dim.

Water is the constant. It seeps up through every floor, pools in the low corners of every room, condenses on the walls in cool beads that slide down and rejoin the dark film covering the packed-earth surfaces. The people don't fight this. They floor their homes with slatted wood raised a hand's breadth above the wet ground, and the space beneath — the underfloor — is where the water lives, a shallow, black mirror that reflects the slats above like a worried eye. It is here that they keep certain things: preserved fish in reed baskets, clay pots of sour mash, bundles of something that might be herbs or might be unidentifiable. The air smells of wet earth, of smoke from peat fires that burn low and stubborn, of the faintly tannic tang of water that has passed through a hundred different kinds of root.

The settlement is new. You can see this in the sharpness of everything. The peat walls of the oldest structures haven't yet rounded with erosion — their corners are knife-straight, their edges crisp as a fresh cut. The channels dug between buildings to drain excess water are still raw, their banks vertical and unstable, little clods of dark earth sloughing off into the brown water at intervals. There are places where the ground has cracked in the sun — a rare occurrence, and one that alarms the inhabitants — and the cracks are as sharp as if cut with a spade, the edges not yet softened by water or footfall. Even the people seem young in their settlement: they haven't yet worn paths between their buildings so much as they have worn a chaos of trails that braid and unbraid across the surface, constantly revised, constantly negotiated. The infrastructure is primitive — there are no metal hinges, no nails, no fired brick — but it is intricate. Lashings of sinew and gut, pegs of hardwood, joints cut with stone adzes and fitted by hand. The whole thing has the density of a problem solved many times over, each iteration visible in the next, a living archive of adjustment and compromise.

There is a strangeness here that settles into your bones gradually, like the cool.

It is partly the silence. Not true silence — there is the ever-present drip of water from roof to floor, the creak of wood adjusting to the breathing ground, the occasional muttered exchange between neighbors — but a kind of listening quality, as though the place itself were attending to something just below the threshold of sound. The few animals that live here move quietly: a species of small, dark-furred rodent that has adapted to the wet by developing webbed feet and a waterproof pelt, rarely seen but sometimes heard scratching in the underfloor spaces; a kind of blind, pale insect that eats peat and leaves behind intricate, branching tunnels on the surface of the walls, so that the oldest structures are covered in fine, dendritic patterns that the inhabitants call "the writing" and will not discuss with outsiders. Once or twice, you might see something larger moving at the edges of the settlement — a shape that resolves into a heron, or into nothing at all, depending on how directly you look at it.

But the deeper strangeness is in the water itself. It is not still. It moves, yes — seeping, pooling, draining — but it moves with a kind of intentionality that water should not have. The channels between buildings do not always flow in the same direction. Sometimes they reverse, slowly, as though the settlement were a basin being tipped first one way and then the other by an unseen hand. The underfloor pools rise and fall not with the rain but with some other rhythm, slower and deeper, that the inhabitants track with marked sticks and that they say corresponds to "the turning of the deep water." And in the lowest room of the oldest structure — a building so old its walls have begun to fuse with the peat beneath them, so that it is no longer clear where architecture ends and ground begins — there is a pool that does not seep from below. It weeps from the wall, a constant slow condensation that has no source, and the water from this pool is not brown and tannic like the rest. It is clear. Impossibly, perfectly clear. And it is cold — colder than the air, colder than the ground, cold enough to ache your teeth if you hold it in your mouth. The inhabitants do not drink it. They do not touch it. They have built a small, roofed enclosure around it, and they leave offerings there: woven reeds, carved wood, small things of value. When asked what it is, they say only, "It is the water that remembers."

The settlement changes. This is the last thing you notice, and the most unsettling: it is not fixed. Buildings are unmade and remade overnight. A passage that was navigable yesterday is sealed today; a new opening appears in a wall that was solid the evening before. The population shifts — not in number, but in arrangement, as though the inhabitants were a single organism redistributing itself across a surface that cannot hold them all at once. At any given time, perhaps a third of the structures are empty, their slatted floors slowly sinking into the dark water beneath, their reed roofs collapsing into themselves with a soft, fibrous sound. And at any given time, a third of the structures are new — green wood and fresh lashing, the walls still weeping sap, the floors still dry enough to raise smoke from a fire. The settlement is a breath: inhalation and exhalation, construction and decay, and the people move through it like cells through a living body, maintaining the whole by constantly revising the parts.

You leave by the same path you came in, and you find that it is not the same path at all.
