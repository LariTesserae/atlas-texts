# The Deepgut

You are underground. You have always been underground. The air tells you this before anything else — it is cool and still and tastes of wet limestone, of mineral long-breathing, of a dampness that does not come from rain but from the slow exhalation of the earth itself.

The sound hits you like a physical mass. It is not one sound. It is the white roar of a river that fills the cavern to your left — no, not a river, a *flow-system*, a watercourse that has been carving this place for so long that the water and the stone have reached an understanding — layered over the clicking, the skittering, the high thin piping of ten million small throats and legs and wings, layered over the drip and ping of water falling from a ceiling so high it exists only as absence, layered over a low vibration that you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears, something massive and rhythmic, like the place itself is breathing.

You cannot see. The darkness is total. Not the darkness of a room with the lights off, where your eyes adjust and shapes emerge. This is geological darkness, the kind that has never known light, that has no memory of light, that would not recognize light if it arrived. Your pupils are wide open and they are drinking nothing.

But the dampness. You can feel it on your skin — not rain, not spray, but a fine condensation that beads on every surface, that makes the air itself feel *wet*, that runs in slow rivulets down the walls you cannot see. The walls are close. You reach out and your fingers find stone — slick, carved, rippled into shapes that speak of water's patient argument with calcium, a negotiation that has lasted longer than your species has had language. The erosion here is *old*. Not crumbling, not chaotic — *settled*. Every surface has found its final curve. The place has become what it intends to be.

The floor beneath you is uneven but not jagged. Worn. Rounded. You realize you are walking on what was once rubble that the river has since polished into cobbles, and that the cobbles are *moving*, very slightly, underfoot — not sliding, but *settling*, as if the ground itself is a slow liquid. You step more carefully.

Something brushes your face.

You flinch, but there is no time to flinch before something else brushes your arm, your neck, the back of your hand. The air is full of them. You cannot see them but you can feel them — small bodies, warm-blooded or something adjacent to warm-blooded, moving in a current of their own making, a three-dimensional river flowing counter to the water below. The sound of their wings is like static, like the hiss between radio stations, like the noise a thousand thousand tiny hairs would make if they all brushed against each other at once.

They are not attacking you. They are *going around* you. You are an obstacle in their corridor. A brief interruption in a flow that has been continuous since before your grandparents were born.

The river — you can feel it now, a few meters to your left, a great wet presence pulling air toward it in a slow convection. The air near it is warmer, or maybe that is just the contrast with the ambient cool. The sound of it is not a crash but a *roar*, deep and sustained, the kind of sound that stops being a sound and becomes a medium, like water itself. You could wade into that sound and it would hold you up.

The density of life here is almost unbearable once you tune into it. Every surface is *coated*. The walls are not stone — stone is merely the substrate. The true surface is a living rind, a composite skin of organisms you have no names for, each performing its own small chemistry in the dark. You run your fingers along it and it is soft and slightly yielding, like leather left in water, and it *moves* — a faint retraction, a ripple of response to your touch, as if the whole wall flinched. Fungal, maybe. Or something that isn't fungal but fills the same niche, something that eats the mineral sweat of the stone and the organic rain that falls from the creatures above and the river's mist and its own dead, cycling everything in a tight closed loop that needs no sun, has never needed sun, would die in sun.

Above you, the ceiling — if it is a ceiling — is alive with a different order of thing. You can hear them up there, clinging, dropping, catching themselves, the clicking of hard bodies on hard bodies, the soft patter of things falling and *catching*. Bats, perhaps. Or something bat-shaped that is not a bat. The sound of their movement is like dry rain. Every few seconds one falls past you, close enough that you feel the wind of its passage, close enough that its wing-tip might graze your upraised hand.

The strangeness — you are beginning to feel it now, the parameter you could not name at first because it is everywhere, because it is the *baseline*. It is not the darkness, which is merely an absence. It is not the dampness, which is merely water in the wrong phase. It is the *wrongness of the proportions*. The life here is too dense. The sound is too loud. The darkness is too complete. The river is too close and too big and too loud and too *present*. Everything is scaled wrong — or you are scaled wrong. You feel like a single note dropped into a symphony, like a single cell in a body, like a visitor to a place that is operating at a frequency you were not built to perceive.

And yet. And yet there is a *rightness* too, a sense of deep equilibrium. This place has been here for — you do not have a number. The erosion tells you. The smoothness of the stone, the completeness of the cavern forms, the *settledness* of everything. This is not a place in the process of becoming. This is a place that has *become*. The river found its path and stayed. The creatures found their niches and filled them. The dampness cycles. The sound cycles. The darkness cycles — no, the darkness does not cycle, the darkness is the *constant*, the one unchanging thing, and everything else moves within it like blood within a body.

Something larger moves in the river. You hear it — a displacement, a surge, a shape rolling in the current that is not water. Something pale and massive and boneless, something that has never needed eyes, turning slowly in the deep channel, feeding on whatever the river brings it. It surfaces for a moment and the sound of its breathing — a great wet bellows, a sound like a forge — cuts through everything else, and then it is gone, and the smaller sounds rush back in to fill the space it left.

The condensation on your skin is running now. You are wet. You have been wet since you arrived and you will be wet when you leave, if you leave. The dampness is not weather. It is *architecture*. It is the way this place breathes — inhaling moisture from the river, exhaling it onto the walls and the ceiling and the living rind and you, always you, the moisture passing through you like a message you cannot read.

You take a step. The ground shifts. The creatures adjust their flow around you. The water speaks its one long word. The darkness holds everything.

This is not a hostile place. It is not a welcoming place. It is a place that does not know you exist, that would not care if it did, that has been here since before caring was invented, and will be here after, when the river finally finds a lower path and moves on and leaves these chambers to a new kind of silence — not the silence of emptiness, but the silence of a place that has finished its conversation with water and is waiting, patiently, in the dark, for whatever comes next.
