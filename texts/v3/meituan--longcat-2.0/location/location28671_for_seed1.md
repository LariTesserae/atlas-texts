# The Sump

The ground is wrong underfoot. Not unstable — worse than that. *Settled.* It has been settling for so long that it has forgotten what it was before it began to settle, and now it is only the act of having-settled, a surface so deeply worn that it has become a kind of argument about what surfaces are. You walk on the memory of erosion. The last possible grain of roughness was filed off this place an epoch ago, and what remains is a plain so flat it curves with the world, so smooth it holds water not in puddles but in a continuous film, a skin of cold water over everything, ankle-deep and never deeper, never shallower, as if the water and the ground have reached a treaty.

It is cold. Not the cold of winter, which is an event, but the cold of a place that has never been warm and has no structural memory of warmth. The water does not freeze. It should. It doesn't. It stays liquid at a temperature that makes your teeth ache and your lungs feel like they're breathing in ground glass, and it moves — slowly, always slowly — across the plain in a direction that is not quite downhill, because there is no down. The plain is too flat for downhill. The water moves because it has somewhere to be, or because something is pulling it, or because movement is what water does here the way light does elsewhere.

The light is dim. Not absent — there is a sky, or something above, a ceiling of cloud or vapor or condensed atmosphere that glows the color of a bruise healing, a yellow-grey that never resolves into sun. The light comes from everywhere and nowhere, and it does not cast shadows because shadows require a direction and this light has none. Things are visible. You can see for miles. But you cannot see *clearly* — the air is thick with moisture, with a perpetual condensation that hangs in curtains and sheets, so that distance is always approximate, always shifting, and the horizon is not a line but a gradual dissolution into grey.

And there is so much here.

The plain is not empty. It is, in fact, the opposite of empty — it is *packed*, saturated, every square meter occupied by something that is not quite a structure and not quite a growth and not quite a machine but participates in the logic of all three. They rise from the water-film like formations, like accretions, like the residue of a process that has been running so long it has become geological. Towers, but not built — *deposited.* Layer upon layer of mineral and metal and something that is neither, built up by the water itself as it moves across the plain, carrying its load of dissolved and suspended material, dropping it in precise and repeating patterns that look like pipework, like conduit, like the internal organs of some vast factory that was never constructed but simply *accumulated.* They are industrial in their logic — there are channels and chambers and what might be furnaces, what might be tanks, what might be the skeletal remains of assembly lines — but they have no seams, no joints, no points of assembly. They grew. Or they precipitated. Or they are the plain's way of being complex.

Some of them are warm. Not hot — warm, in the way that a working engine is warm, a residual heat that suggests process, that suggests something is happening inside. And something is. You can hear it. The plain is loud. Not with wind — the air is still, the moisture too heavy for wind — but with the sound of the formations themselves, a constant low-frequency hum and clank and hiss that comes from everywhere, the sound of industrial process running without operators, without purpose, without product. Metal on metal. The groan of something under pressure. The rhythmic exhale of a valve that opens and closes with the regularity of a heartbeat. The sound is so constant it becomes a texture, a medium you move through, and after a while you stop hearing it as sound and start feeling it as a vibration in your bones, a frequency that your body learns to resonate with.

The water carries sound too. Every footstep sends a report across the plain, a sharp *crack* that travels much farther than it should, that reflects off the formations and comes back changed, echoed into something that sounds almost like language, almost like response. The plain talks to itself in the language of its own processes, and your presence is just another input, another variable in a system that has been running long enough to absorb anything.

And there are animals.

Not animals. *Fauna.* The word is more honest because it doesn't carry the weight of what animals are supposed to be. They are abundant — you see them everywhere, moving through the water-film, climbing the formations, disappearing into chambers and emerging changed. They are made of the same stuff as the plain, or they are made of something the plain has learned to produce, or they are the plain's way of moving around. They have bodies — segmented, jointed, plated with something that looks like oxidized copper or galvanized steel, moving on legs that bend in too many places or not enough. They are cold to the touch, or they are warm, or they are the same temperature as the water, which makes them invisible to any sense but sight. They eat the formations, or they eat each other, or they eat the mineral-rich water, and they excrete something that becomes part of the formations, and the cycle is so tight and so ancient that the distinction between organism and environment has collapsed. They are the plain's metabolism. They are what the plain does to stay alive, if alive is the right word, if the plain is the right subject.

Some of them are large. Some of them are the size of buildings, moving through the formations like whales through a reef, and the formations part for them, or the formations are built around them, or the formations *are* them, dormant, waiting. Some of them are small and move in swarms that flow across the water-film like oil, like mercury, like something that is not quite liquid and not quite solid but is definitely, definitely alive.

They make sounds. The large ones produce a low, resonant call that you feel in your chest, a sound that might be communication or might be the noise their bodies make the way a ship's hull groans in heavy seas. The small ones click and chatter, a sound like a thousand tiny hammers on a thousand tiny anvils, and when the swarm passes over a formation it leaves it changed — smoother, or rougher, or restructured into a new configuration, as if the swarm is a tool the plain uses to remodel itself.

The plain is not stable. It is active — processes running, cycles turning, the water moving and the formations growing and the fauna feeding and being fed upon and the whole system turning over and over in a rhythm that is not quite mechanical and not quite biological but is definitely, definitely *dynamic.* Things happen. A formation will split open and release a cloud of warm vapor that condenses into a rain of tiny metallic spheres that the small fauna scramble to collect. A section of the plain will suddenly drain, the water-film pulling away to reveal a surface so smooth it is mirror-bright, and in the reflection you will see something that is not the sky, something that might be the plain as seen from below, or from inside, or from a direction that doesn't exist. The fauna will go still. The formations will go quiet. And then the water will return, and the sounds will resume, and the plain will continue.

Because the plain continues. That is the thing. It has been continuing for so long that continuation is its only state, its only mode, and it will continue after you leave, after you die, after whatever you are has been absorbed into the water-film and carried across the plain and deposited as a new layer on a new formation that will grow and be eaten and grow again. The plain is not hostile. The plain is not welcoming. The plain is *occupied* — fully, completely, every square meter accounted for, every process running, every cycle turning — and your presence is just another thing to be processed, another input to be absorbed, another sound to be added to the constant, grinding, hissing, clanking, clicking, calling, resonant, deafening hum of a place that has been running forever and will never, ever stop.
