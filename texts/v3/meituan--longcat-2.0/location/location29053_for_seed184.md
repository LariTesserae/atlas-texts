# The Drowned Refinery

---

You descend through a column of warm, green-black water, and the light fails in stages — first the blues leach out, then the greens, then everything settles into a deep amber murk that seems to come from nowhere and everywhere at once. The pressure is gentle but insistent, a hand on every surface of your body. You are below the surface. You have been below the surface for some time.

And then the refinery opens around you.

---

It is a single chamber. That is the first thing to understand — not a complex, not a network, not a city. One room. But the room is the size of a cathedral nave, perhaps two hundred meters long and half that across, and every cubic meter of it is *occupied*. The ceiling — if it can be called that, a vaulted lattice of iron ribs and dripping concrete — arches overhead in a long curve, and from it hang curtains of something between kelp and cable, thick ropes of vegetation that have swallowed old conduit and been swallowed by it in turn, so that you cannot tell where the machine ends and the plant begins. They sway. Not with current — there is almost no current — but with their own slow metabolism, a vegetative breathing, an inch per hour.

The walls are machinery. Or they were. Great cylindrical tanks, each one the size of a house, stand in rows along both flanks of the chamber, and they are *packed* — not with what they once held, but with life. Each tank has rusted through in places, and from every breach, vegetation has erupted: dense, warm-water growths in colors that don't quite belong — a deep arterial red, a pale gold, a violet so dark it reads as black until a passing light catches it. The plants have filled the tanks entirely, packed tight as flesh in a wound, and they have continued outward, spilling over lips and flanges, colonizing pipes, threading through valve wheels, climbing the iron ribs of the ceiling in thick festoons. The density is total. There is no empty space. Every surface is covered, every gap is filled, every volume is claimed by something growing or something built or something that is both.

The floor is a lattice of grating, and below it, more water — deeper water, darker — and through the grating you can see that the machinery continues downward, tank below tank, growth below growth, a vertical city of rust and root that extends further than the amber light reaches.

---

The warmth is notable. This deep, this dim, the water should be cold, but it isn't. It is blood-warm, almost body-temperature, and it comes from the machinery — because the machinery is still running. Not quickly. Not loudly. But running. Deep in the walls, behind the vegetation, inside the tanks, something is still processing. You can feel it as a vibration more than a sound: a subsonic hum that you perceive in your sternum, in your teeth, in the fluid of your inner ear. It cycles — a long, slow pulse, perhaps one beat every forty seconds, a rhythm so gradual that you don't recognize it as rhythm at first. It is the heartbeat of the refinery, and it has been beating for a very long time.

The light comes from the machines, too. Not from any source you can point to — no bulbs, no panels — but from the vegetation itself, or from the interface between vegetation and machine. Where root meets wire, where leaf wraps around conduit, there is a faint luminescence: amber, mostly, with occasional pulses of a cooler blue-green that travel along the growths like signals along a nerve. The light is dim. It does not illuminate so much as *suggest*. You see shapes. You see textures. You see the way a surface curves or recedes. But you do not see clearly, and the refinery keeps its details in reserve, revealing them only as you move through it, a slow striptease of rust and leaf and iron.

---

The strangeness announces itself gradually.

It is in the geometry. The refinery was built on a grid — you can see that in the regularity of the tank placement, the right angles of the walkways, the parallel runs of pipe. But the vegetation has not respected the grid. It has grown in curves, in spirals, in branching patterns that follow some logic of their own, and where it has encountered the grid, it has *bent* it. Pipes curve where they should be straight. Tanks lean where they should stand vertical. The iron ribs of the ceiling have been pulled — slowly, over what must have been centuries — into new configurations by the steady tensile force of climbing roots. The result is a space that feels *organic* in its proportions, almost intestinal, as though you are inside the body of something that was once a building.

It is in the materials. The vegetation has a quality that is not quite plant-like. It is too warm to the touch. It has a faint pulse of its own, independent of the machine's heartbeat. When you brush against a hanging curtain of it, it *recoils* — not quickly, not like an animal, but with a slow, deliberate withdrawal, a contraction that takes several seconds and is followed by an equally slow re-extension. It is aware of you. Not intelligent, not in any way you can communicate with, but *aware*. The refinery knows you are here.

And it is in the water itself. It is fresh — or was, once. Now it is something else. It carries a faint mineral sweetness, a taste of iron and copper and something organic, something almost nutritious. It is thick with microscopic life, with spores and larvae and dissolved compounds that the refinery's ancient processes are still, slowly, producing. The water is not just water. It is a medium. It is a broth. It is the blood of this place, and it is warm, and it is full of things growing.

---

The sound — or the silence — is profound. The subsonic hum is below hearing. The vegetation moves without sound. The water is still. Occasionally, somewhere in the deep structure of the refinery, a bubble of gas is released — a slow, fat sphere that rises through the murk and breaks against the ceiling with a sound like a kiss, like a whisper, like nothing at all. That is the loudest thing here. The rest is silence so complete that you become aware of your own body: the rush of blood in your ears, the creak of your joints, the soft percussion of your heart. You are the noisiest thing in the refinery, and the refinery does not care.

---

There are animals. Rare. You see one, perhaps, in the entire time you spend here: a pale, eyeless thing the size of your hand, with too many legs and a shell that looks like it was grown rather than secreted. It moves across the grating of the floor with a deliberation that matches the refinery's own pace, and it is grazing — scraping something from the surface of the metal with a mouth you cannot quite see. It does not react to your light. It does not react to your presence. It has been here longer than you, and it will be here after you leave, and it is part of the refinery's ecology in the way that a single cell is part of a body: necessary, but not sufficient to explain the whole.

---

The erosion is complete. This is a place that has finished changing shape and has settled into what it is. The rust has reached its final color — a deep, matte umber that is almost black. The concrete has been worn to a smooth, rounded softness, all its angles dissolved, all its edges gentle. The iron has thinned in places to the consistency of paper, and through those thin places, you can see the vegetation inside the walls, the roots that have threaded through the structure like veins through flesh. Everything that can corrode has corroded. Everything that can grow has grown. The refinery has reached an equilibrium — not stasis, because the machines still pulse and the plants still grow and the water still circulates, but a *balance*, a state in which every force is matched by an equal and opposite force, and the whole system turns slowly, like a wheel in a stream, powered by its own decay.

---

You move through it. There are walkways — iron grating, mostly intact, though in places the vegetation has pulled them from the walls and they hang at angles, suspended by root and rust, still bearing weight but no longer level. You follow them because they are the only clear path, the only space not fully occupied by growth or machine. The walkways lead you the length of the chamber, from the great valve-assembly at the near end — a wall of wheels and levers now frozen in a thick sleeve of golden vegetation — to the far end, where the ceiling lowers and the tanks crowd closer together and the amber light deepens to something almost red, and the hum of the machinery is stronger, and the warmth increases by a degree or two, and you understand that you are approaching the heart of the refinery, the place where whatever is still being processed is being processed, the warm core of this drowned, living, patient thing.

---

It is not hostile. It is not welcoming. It is *occupied* — fully, densely, completely occupied — and you are a visitor in a place that has been busy with its own affairs for longer than you can imagine. The refinery does not need you. The refinery does not need anything. It is warm and dim and quiet and strange, and it is growing and rusting and pulsing in its slow, slow way, and it will continue to do so long after you have left, long after you have forgotten it, long after the surface world above has changed beyond recognition.

The water holds you. The vegetation sways. The machines beat their deep, slow rhythm.

The refinery endures.
