# The Rooftop Garden

---

You arrive by a long climb. Not the dramatic ascent of a peak — there is no summit here, no jagged crown — but a grinding, lateral crawl up a face so ancient it has forgotten it was ever a cliff. The rock under your hands is warm, almost feverish, and when you crest the last real edge, what you find is not a summit at all but a table. A vast, wounded table, stretching in every direction until the air itself curdles and the land simply stops being visible.

The sky is enormous. That is the first thing. Not because the sky is any larger than elsewhere, but because the land has been so thoroughly unmade that it offers no competition for your attention. You stand on a surface that was, some incomprehensible time ago, a mountain range. Now it is a series of broad, shallow bowls and low, rolling swells — the stumps of mountains, planed down by wind and rain and the patient abrasion of centuries beyond counting. The rock is old bone. Old dead bone. It is the color of dark copper and dried blood, and in places it is polished to a dull sheen, smooth as a river stone, as though the last erosive force simply gave up and went home.

The air is warm. Not hot, not tropical-humid, but the particular warmth of a place that sits in sun for eleven hours a day at this altitude, where the thinner atmosphere lets through more light but holds less of it. The warmth sits on your skin like a cloth. It does not move. And the air is damp — not raining, not dripping, but saturated at the edge of itself. You can feel the moisture on your lips, in your nostrils. Every breath tastes of water that hasn't decided whether to become cloud yet.

---

The sound hits you after the sky.

It is not one sound. It is a continuous, layered, living wall of sound, and it is coming from everywhere and nowhere, and it is so loud that after a few minutes you realize your ears have simply stopped trying to parse it. You are inside it now. The sound is the medium, like water to a fish.

Chittering. That is the base layer — a constant, granular, high-frequency chittering that comes from every direction at once, millions of tiny voices overlapping into a static hiss that rises and falls with the slow rhythm of breathing. Then the deeper calls — honks and rattles and long, descending moans that come from the vegetation, from somewhere in the green dark beneath the canopy. And above all of it, the sky-voices: sharp, clean cries that slice through the murk, the calls of things that fly in enormous flocks, wheeling and banking in formations so dense they blot out the sun for seconds at a time, a passing eclipse of wings.

You cannot think over this sound. You can only exist inside it.

---

And then you see the plants.

They are the architecture. They are the city. They are the landscape itself.

The vegetation does not grow on the ground. The ground is incidental. The vegetation has *replaced* the ground, has built itself up over the eroded rock until the rock is just a foundation, a basement, a memory. What you are standing on — what you have been climbing toward — is not soil but a living platform, a mesh of roots and stems and fungal mats so thick and so ancient that it has become structural. You could build a house on it. Someone has.

The plants themselves — you do not have the vocabulary for them. They are not trees, though they have trunks. The trunks are wide and pale and ridged like muscle, and they rise thirty, forty, fifty meters before they begin to branch, and where they branch they do not form canopies so much as *platforms* — broad, flat, interlocking horizontal surfaces that spread outward and meet the platforms of neighboring trunks, weaving together into a continuous ceiling of green that admits light only in coins and fragments, little bright holes that move across the ground as the sun passes. The leaves are enormous — some of them three meters across — and they are not the green of lower altitudes but a deep, almost black green, the color of a bruise, because at this elevation the light is fierce and the leaves must protect themselves. They are waxy, almost rubbery, and when the damp air condenses on them, the water beads and runs to the edges and drips, and the sound of the dripping is a constant under-note to the chittering, a metronome of *plink plink plink* that never stops.

But here is the thing about the density, the strange and hollow truth of this place: between the trunks, there is nothing. The trunks are spaced so far apart — twenty, thirty meters — that the space beneath the canopy is a vast, dim cathedral. The ground between them is bare rock or thin root-moss, and the air moves through freely, carrying the damp, carrying the sound, carrying the smell of green and rot and the sharp, almost metallic tang of the epiphytes that coat every trunk and branch. The vegetation is total in the sense that it covers everything you can see, but it is also *absent* — the space between the living structures is so immense, so open, that you can stand in the center of a hundred-meter clearing and feel alone. The plants are the walls and the ceiling, but the room is empty.

And in that emptiness, the animals live.

---

The fauna is not something you observe. It is something you move through, like water, like weather.

The floor between the trunks is crisscrossed with movement. Small things — rat-sized, cat-sized — dart through the root networks with the speed and purpose of commuters. They are dark-furred, bright-eyed, and they travel in loose groups of six or eight, pausing to snuffle at the moss, to dig at the root-mat, to flick their ears at the sound of a call from above. They do not flee from you. They have learned, over generations, that the large, slow, two-legged things that occasionally arrive are not predators. They are simply odd. They give you the same wide berth they give a boulder.

Above them, in the trunks, there is a different world. The pale bark is pocked with holes — some natural, some chewed — and from each hole comes a different occupant. Things with too many legs. Things with shells like polished wood. Things that are not insects and not reptiles and not anything with a clean name, things that evolved in this strange, high, warm, damp place and took forms that seem borrowed from dreams. A creature like a millipede but the size of your forearm, its segments a deep, glossy blue-black, moves up a trunk with the unhurried confidence of something that has nowhere to be. Something else — a flash of red and gold, quick as a fish — darts out from a hole, snatches something from the air, and disappears. The trunks are apartment buildings. They are ecosystems unto themselves.

And above the canopy — the canopy where the leaves interlock and the light comes through in coins — there are the flyers. They are not birds, or if they are, they are birds that have forgotten the rules of birds. They are small, mostly, fist-sized, and they travel in flocks that number in the thousands, and when the flock passes overhead, the sound is a roar, a physical pressure of wingbeats and calls that you feel in your chest. They are the source of the sky-voices, the sharp clean cries. They eat the epiphytes. They eat the small things on the ground. They are the circulation system of this place, the blood moving through the body.

There are larger things too. You have seen signs — a depression in the moss where something heavy rested, a trunk scarred with long, parallel gouges at shoulder height. You have not seen the large things. They move through the far clearings, the ones beyond the nearest trunks, and you only know they are there by the way the smaller animals go quiet, by the way the chittering drops an octave, by the subtle, subsonic vibration in the root-mat that you feel in your soles more than hear. They are rare. They are old. They are the reason the small things live in holes.

---

The water is everywhere and nowhere.

It does not fall as rain — not often. The damp comes from the clouds that roll across the tableland, low and slow, and when they touch the canopy, they leave their moisture behind. Every leaf is a catchment. Every stem is a pipe. The water runs downward, always downward, through the layers of vegetation, through the root-mat, and finally onto the bare rock below, where it follows the ancient, eroded channels — the ghost-rivers, the memory of drainage — toward the edges of the plateau, where it will eventually fall. The plateau is a sponge. It drinks the clouds and holds the water in its tissues for days, weeks, releasing it slowly, constantly, so that the air is never dry and the moss is never crisp and the animals never thirst. But you cannot find a river. You cannot find a lake. The water is distributed, dispersed, held in solution in the living body of the landscape. To drink, you must squeeze a handful of moss. You must lick a leaf. You must become part of the cycle.

---

And then, in a clearing — a wider one, where the canopy has fallen in and the light comes down in a single, solid column — you find the building.

It is small. It is made of stone — the same dark, ancient stone as the plateau — and it is roofed with the great black-green leaves, layered and woven in a pattern that is clearly intentional, clearly the product of hands. There is a low wall, waist-high, forming a partial enclosure. There is a fire pit, cold, with ash that is old but not ancient. There are tools — stone tools, chipped and shaped, laid out on a flat rock with the care of someone who values them. A hammer. A scraper. A blade the length of your hand, its edge still sharp enough to draw blood. There is cordage made from plant fiber, coiled and stored in a niche in the wall. There is a bed of dried moss.

No one is here. But someone was here recently — within days. The cordage is still pliable. The moss bed still holds a body's impression. The fire could be rekindled with a single coal.

You are not the first to climb to this place. You are not even the thousandth. The plateau has been visited for longer than memory, by people who came to harvest the plants, to hunt the small things, to gather the epiphyte-gum that is worth more than metal in the lowlands below. They come and go. They leave their small stone shelters and their tools and their fire pits, and the plateau absorbs them. The moss grows over the walls. The roots crack the stone. In a hundred years, the shelter will be a mound. In a thousand, it will be a faint irregularity in the root-mat, indistinguishable from the natural swell of the land.

The people who come here do not build to last. They build to visit. They are guests on the rooftop, and they know it.

---

The light is normal. That is the strange thing. After everything else — the sound, the scale, the ancient, eroded, impossible-to-comprehend age of the place — the light is simply *light*. It comes from the sun. It is warm and bright and it makes the leaves shine and the shadows sharp and it moves across the sky at the normal speed and sets in the normal way and the plateau goes dark and the chittering changes pitch and the temperature drops and the damp rises and the night animals come out, the ones with the huge eyes and the silent wings, and the soundscape shifts but does not quiet, never quiets, and you realize that the sound is the permanent thing, the sound is the *voice* of this place, and everything else — the light, the water, the visitors, the buildings, the living and the dead — is just the body through which the sound moves.

You stay for three days. You sleep in the stone shelter. You eat the small, sweet fruits that grow on the epiphytes. You drink the squeezed water. You listen.

On the third day, you see one of the large things.

It comes out of the far tree-line, where the trunks are thickest and the canopy lowest, and it moves into the clearing with the slow, considered gait of something that has never been prey. It is the size of a cow but shaped wrong — too long, too low, with a back that ripples like water as it walks. Its skin is the color of the rock, the same dark copper, and it is covered in a short, dense fuzz that holds the damp and glistens. It has a face. Not a kind face, not a cruel face — a face that has passed beyond such judgments, a face that is simply *old*, that has the same quality as the eroded stone, as the ancient root-mat, as the plateau itself. It looks at you. Its eyes are large and dark and they hold a light that is not reflection but something internal, something that burns low and steady at the bottom of all that time.

It does not threaten. It does not flee. It regards you the way a mountain regards a cloud — with a patience that is not patience but simply *duration*.

Then it turns and moves back into the trunks, and the moss closes over its footprints, and the chittering rises again to its full, deafening pitch, and the water drips, and the leaves turn their enormous faces toward the light, and the plateau continues its long, slow, ancient business of being exactly what it is.

You leave the next morning, climbing down the long, smooth face, back toward the lowlands, where the air is thick and the sky is small and the sounds are the sounds of human things. But the chittering stays with you. It will always stay with you. You have been inside the voice of the world, and now you carry it, a permanent, high-frequency hum beneath everything else, the sound of ten million small lives doing what they have always done, in a place that has always been, on the rooftop of everything, in the warm, damp, ancient, empty-full light.
