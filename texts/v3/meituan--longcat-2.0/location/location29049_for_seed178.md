# The Sill Fields

The ground is wrong underfoot — not unstable, but *unfinished*. You can feel it through your boots: a vibration that isn't quite seismic, a hum that rises through the sole and into the bone, as though the earth here is still deciding what it wants to be. The terrain is low, barely above the water table, and the water table is barely below the surface. Everything weeps. The rock faces — and there are rock faces, sharp-edged and freshly exposed, their fractures still bright with mineral sheen — sweat moisture from a thousand capillary seams. The air tastes of iron and cold stone and something else, something that prickles the sinuses, a faint metallic sweetness like the air before a transformer blows.

It is dark. Not night-dark — the sky is a low ceiling of cloud, pewter-grey, luminous enough to see by but offering no sun, no direction, no shadow. The light is ambient and sourceless, the way light behaves in a room with many walls. Vegetation crowds every surface: mosses so thick they've turned the boulders into green pillows, ferns unrolling from crevices with a vigor that seems almost aggressive, liverworts and hornworts forming dark, wet carpets that squelch underfoot and release a smell like bruised celery and wet cardboard. The plant life is lush beyond what the latitude should allow — the cold should stunt it, should slow it, but instead it grows as though time-lapsed, fronds still wet with the effort of uncurling, new growth visibly lighter than old, the whole landscape a gradient of greens so dense it becomes almost black in the underlayers.

And the sound. The sound is *enormous*. It is the first thing you notice and the last thing you stop noticing. A roar, but not a single roar — a composite, a layering, a wall of noise built from a thousand constituent voices. Water is the bass note: not a river, not a waterfall, but the sound of water *everywhere*, moving through the substrate, percolating up through the moss, sheeting down the rock faces, pooling and draining and pooling again in a constant hydraulic cycle that never resolves into a single channel. Over that, the mid-range: the creak and groan of things growing, of fibers tightening, of ice forming and cracking in the deeper rock where the cold has penetrated. And above everything, a high, thin, almost electronic whine — the sound of the structures.

Because there are structures. Scattered, half-consumed by the vegetation, half-dissolved by the water. They are not buildings in any conventional sense. They are *sills* — horizontal intrusions of something that is not quite metal and not quite stone, driven into the bedrock at intervals that seem random until you've walked long enough to sense the pattern. They protrude from the ground at angles, some only a hand's breadth above the moss, others rising to knee-height or waist-height, their surfaces smooth and dark and faintly warm to the touch. They hum. Each one hums at a slightly different frequency, and the interference pattern between them creates a standing wave in the air that you feel in your teeth, in your sternum, in the fluid of your inner ear. The whine is not coming from any single sill. It is coming from the *space between them*.

The sills are young. You can tell by their edges — still sharp, still bright where the moss hasn't claimed them, still weeping a clear, viscous fluid that might be coolant or might be something else entirely. They were emplaced recently. Geologically recently. The rock around them is still adjusting, still cracking and settling, still finding its new equilibrium. The erosion here is in its earliest stages: the water has only just begun to find its paths, the freeze-thaw cycles have only just begun to open the first real joints, and the vegetation has only just begun to send its roots into the fractures. Everything is fresh. Everything is sharp. The landscape has the quality of a wound that has only just stopped bleeding.

And it is moving. Not slowly — not in the way that geology usually moves, with the patience of deep time. This landscape is *turbulent*. The water table rises and falls on a cycle of minutes, not seasons. Pools appear and drain as though the ground is breathing. The moss visibly swells with moisture, then contracts, then swells again. Small slides occur constantly — not landslides, but *adjustments*, the ground settling into new configurations as the substrate beneath it is reworked by whatever process the sills are driving. You can see the water change color as it moves: clear where it emerges from the rock, then cloudy with suspended minerals, then clear again as it passes through a stand of vegetation that filters it with impossible efficiency. The whole system is cycling, processing, turning over at a rate that feels almost biological.

The fauna is abundant and strange only in its *ordinariness*. Insects — midges, mostly, and caddisflies, and a species of stonefly you don't recognize — rise in clouds from the moss when you disturb it. Spiders have strung webs between the sills, and the webs are heavy with condensation, each droplet catching the grey light like a lens. Something small and quick moves in the undergrowth — a vole, perhaps, or a shrew, its body heat briefly visible as a distortion in the cold air before it vanishes into the ferns. Birds are present but silent, which is the most unsettling thing: they perch on the sills, on the rock faces, on the taller ferns, and they watch, and they do not sing. The sound is too loud for singing. The sound is too loud for anything but endurance.

The sills are doing something to the water. You realize this gradually, as you move through the landscape and begin to notice patterns. The water that emerges from the rock is cold — barely above freezing, cold enough to numb your fingers in seconds. But the water that has passed through the sill-field, that has moved through the network of pools and channels and moss-beds that the sills seem to organize, is *warm*. Not hot — warm. Blood-warm. And the vegetation nearest the sills is the lushest, the most aggressive, the most *alive*, as though the sills are not just organizing the water but *feeding* it, adding something to it, some energy or nutrient or information that the plants recognize and respond to.

You find a place where two sills cross, their surfaces meeting at an angle that shouldn't be possible given their apparent rigidity. The fluid weeping from both has pooled in the junction, and the pool is *steaming* in the cold air, and the moss growing around it is so thick and so green it looks artificial, looks *painted*, and the sound here is the loudest, the interference pattern at its most intense, and you feel it not just in your teeth but in your *thoughts*, a pressure behind the eyes, a sense of something vast and patient and utterly indifferent to your presence, working through the rock and the water and the cold and the dark, building something that is not yet finished, that may never be finished, that is — for now, for this moment — still becoming.
