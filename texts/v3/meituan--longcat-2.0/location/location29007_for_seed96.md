# The cistern-works of Amhara

---

You stand at the threshold of a passage that was cut — not carved, *cut*, with straight edges and a flat floor — into stone so pale it seems to hold light like a membrane holds water. The passage extends ahead of you, and behind you, and when you turn you find that it extends in every direction: a geometry of corridors radiating outward from this point and from every other point, a lattice so thorough that the mountain has become nothing but passage, nothing but the space between walls.

The walls are not dressed smooth. They bear the marks of their making — fine, regular striations running horizontally, the trace of a stone blade drawn again and again across the face of the rock, each pass removing a hair's depth. You can feel them under your fingertips. The craft is meticulous but unapologetic about its own process. Nothing has been polished to hide the work. The work *is* the surface.

And the surface is everywhere.

There is no natural stone left. No rough-hewn cavity, no untouched outcrop, no place where the mountain shows its original face. Every plane has been struck. Every joint between blocks — for much of this is not carved from living rock but assembled from pieces, fitted without mortar, seated with a precision that makes the seam nearly invisible — every joint is tight, deliberate, final. The builders consumed the substrate. What they left behind is only structure, only the made thing, repeating.

---

The air is cool. Not cold — you do not see your breath — but cool in the way of deep places, of altitudes where the sun's warmth arrives thin and leaves quickly. The elevation is considerable. You know this because of the quality of the light, which has a clean, unfiltered sharpness to it, and because when you find an opening — a shaft cut vertically through sixty feet of stone — the sky at the top of it is a blue so deep it borders on violet, the blue of distance, of air with nothing between it and the upper atmosphere but more air.

But the light reaches you here, in the deep passages. It reaches you because the builders cut for it. They cut channels — not windows, *channels* — narrow slots in walls and ceilings that catch light at the surface and redirect it downward, bouncing it from pale stone to pale stone, losing a little at each reflection but never losing all of it, so that even in the innermost chambers there is a luminous grey glow, sourceless, even, like light filtered through water. The stone itself assists. It is a quartzite, perhaps, or a very pale granite, with a faint internal sparkle — mica, or feldspar, or something else — that catches and holds whatever light falls on it and re-emits it slowly, so the walls seem to glow from within, so that even in passages far from any shaft, you can see.

The brightness is unusual. Not harsh, not glaring, but *present*, a steady luminous pressure, as though the air itself has been made to carry light. You begin to suspect the water has something to do with this.

---

The water is everywhere.

It runs in channels cut into the floors — shallow troughs, no more than a finger's depth, with straight sides and flat bottoms, following the corridors with the same geometric regularity as the corridors themselves. The water is clear and very slow. It does not rush. It moves with the patience of something that has nowhere else to go, that has always been moving and will always move, that has found its level and rests there, creeping forward by fractions. You cannot hear it from a distance. Only when you kneel beside a channel and lower your ear do you catch the faintest suggestion of movement, a sound more felt than heard, a wet whisper against stone.

It weeps from the walls. In places where two blocks meet, where the seam is tight enough to resist a knife blade but not tight enough to resist moisture, water seeps through and runs down the face of the stone in thin, bright films. The films catch the light. They make the walls shimmer. They leave no stain — the water is pure, mineral-pure, carrying nothing that would deposit or discolor — but they leave the stone dark where they touch it, a wet patina that dries slowly in the cool air and then darkens again when the seep resumes.

It condenses on the ceilings. The temperature differential between the stone and the air is enough to pull moisture from the atmosphere, and the ceilings beaded with droplets — not dripping, not yet, but gathering, each droplet holding its shape, growing slowly until it reaches the threshold of its own weight and falls. The sound of a single drop hitting the floor of a corridor is the clearest sound in this place. It rings. The stone carries the vibration, and you hear it from far away, a bright, brief chime that fades into stillness, and then another, somewhere else, not regular, not patterned, just the patient arithmetic of accumulation and release.

The air is damp. Not oppressively so — you do not feel wet — but the moisture is present, a coolness on your lips, a faint weight to each breath. Your skin adjusts. After a while you stop noticing, and then you notice that you have stopped noticing, and the dampness returns to your awareness as something almost intimate, as though the place is breathing on you, slowly.

---

There is no green.

You search for it. In any other place with this much water, this much seepage, you would find something — moss in the cracks, algae on the wet walls, lichen on the exposed surfaces at the surface, ferns in the shafts where light and moisture converge. But there is nothing. The channels run bare. The weeping walls are bare. The condensation beads on naked stone and falls to naked stone and runs into channels that are naked stone all the way down.

You look closer. You examine the water itself, holding your hand in it, bringing your fingers to your lips. It tastes of nothing. Not flat, not stale — *nothing*. Not even mineral. Water that has been so thoroughly filtered through so much stone that it has been stripped of everything dissolved, everything suspended, everything that would feed a cell or seed a crystal. It is water as an idea of water, as close to H₂O as the natural world allows, and it is sterile in a way that feels not accidental but designed.

Or perhaps it is the stone itself. Perhaps the stone is wrong for life — too hard, too dense, too lacking in the trace elements that living things mine from their substrates. The walls offer nothing. No purchase for a rootlet, no crevice deep enough for a spore to lodge and wait. The builders chose this stone, or found it, or were given it, and it is stone that wants to be only stone.

---

The complex is vast. You walk for what feels like hours and the corridors do not repeat, or if they repeat they repeat with variations — a channel running along the left wall instead of the right, a shaft cut at a slightly different angle, a chamber that opens to your left where before a chamber opened to your right. The scale is not monumental in the way of cathedrals or tombs. The corridors are low — you must duck in places — and narrow enough that you can touch both walls if you extend your arms. The chambers are small, intimate, purposeful. But there are so *many* of them. They connect. They branch. They descend by shallow ramps and ascend by worn steps and level out again into long, luminous hallways where the channels run in parallel lines like the lines of a musical staff, carrying their thin, bright notes of water in silent concert.

Every surface is worked. Every surface is built. There is no room between the presences — the stone fills every gap, the water fills every channel, the light fills every shaft, the air fills every chamber, and in the air the moisture fills every space between the molecules of nitrogen and oxygen, saturating, saturating. The place is full. It is complete. It does not have the feeling of a ruin, of something abandoned and returning to nature. It has the feeling of a machine that is running, that has always been running, that will always be running, attended or unattended, watched or unwatched.

---

And yet it is primitive. You see this in the tool marks, in the slight irregularities that no amount of care could fully eliminate, in the wooden levers racked against a wall in one chamber — wood, the only non-stone material you have found, dark with age and damp, but still solid, still shaped to a purpose you can read in their curvature. Wood and stone. Abrasive sand, perhaps, for the finer work. Water itself, used as a cutting medium, channeled and directed against stone to wear it away. No metal. No fire, or no evidence of fire. No kiln, no forge, no slag. The builders worked with what was softer than stone against stone itself, with patience and repetition and time, and they made this.

The freshness of the cuts suggests they were made recently, or that whatever process is wearing them is so slow as to be negligible over any timescale you can imagine. The edges of the channels are still sharp. The striations on the walls are still crisp, still catching light in their grooves, still throwing tiny shadows that give the surfaces their texture. Water has been flowing here — is flowing here — and yet it has not erased the marks of the tools. Either the water is too pure to abrade, or the stone is too hard to yield, or the time has not yet been long enough. The place feels young. Not new — there is nothing hasty about it, nothing provisional — but *early*. As though it has only just emerged from the process of its own making. As though the last block was fitted a season ago, or a year, or a generation, and the water has only just begun its patient work of settling, of finding every channel, of learning the shape of what it has been given to fill.

---

There is no animal. You have seen no insect in the channels, no spider in the ceiling joints, no bat in the shafts. You have heard nothing that was not water or stone or your own body moving through space. The silence is not empty — it is *occupied*, occupied by the faint, pervasive presence of water moving, of light falling, of the slow thermal breathing of stone warming and cooling by fractions of degrees — but it is silence nonetheless, the silence of a place where nothing with a voice has ever lived, or where nothing with a voice has been allowed to remain.

You begin to wonder if this is intentional. The tightness of the joints. The purity of the water. The absence of any organic trace — no oil from a hand, no fragment of fiber, no bone, no seed carried in on a foot. The place has been kept clean, or was built clean, or was built to *stay* clean, to resist colonization, to remain what it is: a system of stone and water and light, functioning without biology, without the messy, branching, opportunistic presence of living things.

---

The strangeness — and it is strange, though the strangeness grows on you slowly, like the damp, like the cool — is not in any single element but in the combination. A place entirely made, but made primitively. A place full of water, but water that sustains nothing. A place bright with light, but light that falls on nothing that grows. A place where every surface has been touched by intention, but where no intention is legible beyond the functional — no decoration, no inscription, no symbol, no mark that says *I was here* or *this means* or *we believed*. Only the marks of tools. Only the evidence of process. Only the fact of making, repeated so thoroughly that it has become the nature of the place, so that you cannot tell where the mountain ends and the work begins, because there is no mountain left, there is only the work, and the work has become the world.

You stand in a chamber where six corridors meet, where the channels from each corridor converge into a single deeper channel that descends by a ramp into the dark — not the dark of absence of light, but the dark of light not yet arrived, light that is making its way down through the channels and shafts and reflections, that will reach this depth eventually, that is always reaching, always arriving, always just behind you and just ahead of you.

The water moves. The stone holds. The light falls.

The place breathes its cool, damp, luminous breath against your face, and you breathe it in, and it tastes of nothing, and it is enough.
