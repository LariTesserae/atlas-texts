# The Weeping Terrace

---

You stand at the edge of something that has forgotten it was ever built.

The ground beneath you is not stone, not soil, but a dense, warm mat of compressed root-fiber and mineral deposit — the accumulated skin of ten thousand years of slow, patient growth. It yields slightly underfoot, like the hide of something sleeping. The air is thick with moisture, not falling but *present*, a warm fog that clings to every surface and makes the light behave strangely — bending, pooling, gathering in the hollows of things like liquid held in cupped hands.

This is a low place. You can feel it in the way the air sits heavy, the way sound dies quickly, the way the horizon — if there is one — stays close and intimate. The terrace is perhaps two hundred meters across, a shallow bowl pressed into the earth, and at its center, the ground dips just enough to hold a still, dark pool that reflects nothing accurately. The water there is not deep, but it is old. It has been condensing out of the air and seeping back into the ground for longer than the concept of counting would find useful.

The vegetation is lush but *selective*. Broad-leafed things with waxy surfaces catch the light and hold it, their edges beaded with condensation that drips in slow, arrhythmic patterns. There are no trees — nothing that reaches for height. Instead, everything spreads: low fern-analogues with fronds like crumpled silk, ground-cover that shifts color as you move around it, mosses so thick they have their own topography, tiny hills and valleys of green that breathe. The plants here do not compete for light. They have made some other arrangement.

And the light — it is bright, but not from any single source. The fog itself seems to glow, a diffuse, warm luminescence that comes from everywhere and nowhere, as if the air has been taught to carry photons the way it carries water vapor. Shadows are soft-edged and short. At what passes for midday, the entire terrace is lit like the inside of a pearl.

The structures are few and strange. A low wall of something like concrete but warmer, its surface pocked and smoothed by ages of moisture, runs along the northern curve of the terrace for perhaps thirty meters before dissolving into the ground — not crumbled, but *absorbed*, the boundary between built and grown becoming uncertain, then meaningless. Near the pool, a series of thin, dark poles rise to about chest height, arranged in no pattern you can discern. They are not metal. They are not wood. They hum at a frequency just below hearing, and if you touch one, your fingers come away tingling, and the moisture on the surface of the pole rearranges itself into patterns that might be writing or might be the residue of some process you don't have a name for.

This is the beyond-modern made quiet. The technology here is not loud. It does not announce itself. It has been running so long that it has become geology. The poles, the wall, the pool — they are not artifacts. They are *organs*. The terrace is a body, and these are its bones, its glands, its slow-beating heart. Whatever built this place built it to outlast its builders, and it has. The engineering is not visible because it has become the world. The water cycles, the light cycles, the slow respiration of the root-mat — these are not natural processes. They are *maintained* natural processes, tended by systems so deeply embedded in the substrate that to remove them would be to remove the ground itself.

The fauna is overwhelming, but not in the way you expect.

There are no large animals. Nothing that charges or stalks or fills the air with calls. Instead, the terrace is *thick* with small lives. Beetle-things with carapaces like polished stone click and skitter through the moss. Winged things — not insects, not birds, something between — drift through the fog in loose clouds, their bodies translucent, their flight paths describing slow, mathematical curves that suggest they are not navigating but *computing*. The pool's surface is dimpled with the feeding of thousands of tiny, silver-scaled things that move in synchronized waves, creating patterns that spread and interfere and dissolve. On the far side of the terrace, a colony of soft-bodied, many-legged creatures the color of rust moves across the root-mat in a slow, deliberate migration, their bodies leaving trails that glow faintly for a few seconds before fading.

The sound is mostly quiet. The drip of water. The click of carapaces. The almost-subsonic hum of the poles. The soft, wet sound of things growing. But beneath all of this, there is a silence that feels *intentional* — as if the terrace has learned to be quiet, to keep its own counsel, to let its processes run without announcement.

The strangeness is in the details. The way the fog moves against the wind. The way the pool's reflection shows a sky that isn't there — a sky with two moons, or no moons, or a sun that is the wrong color. The way the root-mat occasionally *shifts*, just slightly, as if something large is turning over in its sleep far below. The way the winged things sometimes gather in a dense cloud above the pool and hold perfectly still, their bodies aligned, and the light bends around them in a way that makes your eyes water and your thoughts go slippery.

This is a place that has settled into its final form. The erosion is ancient not because things have been worn away, but because the wearing has *finished*. The terrace has found its shape and will hold it now, indefinitely. The water will continue to weep from the rocks. The light will continue to glow. The small lives will continue their slow, bright, overwhelming dance. The poles will continue their subsonic hum. The pool will continue to reflect whatever it reflects.

And you will stand at the edge of it, warm and damp and small, and you will understand that you are not the first to stand here, and you will not be the last, and the terrace will not notice the difference.
