# The Womb-Hollow

High in the belly of a mountain that no longer has a name, there is a room the size of a cathedral — and the city of Ren-Kesh fills every inch of it.

You enter through a throat of worked stone so narrow that two people cannot walk abreast, and the passage spirals upward for what feels like hours before it opens without warning into the Hollow. Your first sensation is the sound: a living, breathing roar. Not the roar of machinery or of catastrophe, but the accumulated voice of ten thousand small lives moving at once — wings, mandibles, claws on stone, the dry rustle of scaled bodies sliding through crevices, the percussive clicking of something large and many-legged shifting its weight in the dark. The acoustics of the dome throw sound in layers. Nothing is ever fully silent here. Even in the deepest hours, when the insect choirs thin and the flying things roost, you can hear the slow, patient groan of the mountain settling, and beneath that, a sound like breathing — warm air exhaling from vents in the floor, inhaling through fissures near the ceiling.

The air is thick. Not wet, exactly — there is no water in Ren-Kesh, no standing pools, no streams, no rain. But the warmth (it is always warm, the deep warmth of geological time) carries moisture in a form so fine it does not fall. It clings. Every surface is filmed with it. Run your fingers along the curved wall of the dome and your hand comes away slick, not with water but with a mineral secretion that the stone sweats perpetually — faintly alkaline, faintly sweet, smelling of copper and something older. The people who live here call it *vein-milk*. They collect it in small clay cups sealed with resin, and they drink it, and it is enough. It has always been enough.

---

The Hollow itself is ancient beyond reckoning. The walls have been worn to a smoothness that is almost organic, like the inside of a skull or the curve of a bone. There are no sharp edges anywhere. Eons of that warm, mineral-laden air have dissolved every ridge, every spur, every crack into flowing undulations — a surface like frozen honey, pale grey-veined-with-amber, faintly luminous in the dimness. The light comes from nowhere you can name. It seeps. It gathers in the alcoves and pools in the low places like a slow liquid, never bright, never absent. Some say it comes from the stone itself. Some say it comes from the things that grow.

And the things that grow are *everywhere*.

---

There is no soil in Ren-Kesh, and no sun, and yet the vegetation is a riot. It clings to the walls in cascading curtains of deep viridian and blue-black — not moss, not fern, but something between, with fronds as fine as eyelashes that wave constantly in the warm updrafts from below. It grows in the cracks between buildings, up the sides of towers, across rooftops, threading through windows and doorways until the architecture and the green become indistinguishable. The people prune it constantly and it returns overnight. They eat it. They weave it into cord and cloth and rope. They feed it to the animals, or the animals feed on it, or both — the relationships are tangled beyond anyone's ability to fully map, a thousand strands of dependence braided so tight that pulling one would unravel a dozen others.

In the low places, where the vein-milk collects in the stone's natural declivities, the vegetation grows dense as a fist — tangled mats of something like liverwort but rubbery, resilient, the color of dark jade. It squelches underfoot. Things live in it. Things live under it. The city is built on top of it, around it, through it. The green is the foundation. The green is the infrastructure. Cut a cross-section through any street and you would find, beneath the flagstones, beneath the packed earth, beneath the old foundations of older buildings — the compressed, half-decomposed mat of a thousand years of growth and regrowth, a substrate so dense and so saturated with life that it is almost indistinguishable from living tissue.

---

The city climbs the walls. This is the first thing you understand about Ren-Kesh as a *built* place — it is not flat. The floor of the Hollow, such as it is, lies perhaps a third of the way down from the apex, a shallow bowl of dark stone where the vein-milk seeps up through a thousand hairline vents, warming the air, feeding the roots of the hanging gardens that curtain the upper reaches. Below the floor, the dome continues narrowing to a point lost in shadow and steam. Above, the walls rise in that long smooth curve to the ceiling's highest point, maybe sixty meters up, where the light is strongest and the air is thinnest and the vegetation grows pale and etiolated, reaching.

Every surface is built upon. The lower terraces — broad, flat platforms of stone and compacted earth, ringed with low walls — are where the markets operate, where the animals are slaughtered (a constant, necessary sound, the wet thud of the mallet, the brief high squeal, the immediate eager scramble of the cleaners who swarm the moment the body drops). Above the terraces, the buildings begin: single-story structures of unmortared stone, their walls thick, their roofs flat, each one pressing against its neighbor, sharing walls, sharing heat. They are built in rings around the curve of the Hollow, and the streets between them are not streets so much as crevices — narrow, dark, crowded with bodies and noise and the smell of cooking fat and animal musk and the sweet alkaline tang of the vein-milk.

Higher still, where the walls steepen, the construction becomes more daring. Towers of wood and stone cling to the curved surface like barnacles, anchored with pegs driven directly into the rock, connected to each other by bridges and walkways so narrow that you must turn sideways to pass. These are the living quarters of the older families, the ones whose claim to the Hollow goes back generations beyond counting. Their walls are black with age and with the soot of the oil lamps that burn perpetually in the dimness, and the vegetation has claimed them so thoroughly that from below they look less like buildings and more like growths — strange vegetative extrusions from the living stone.

---

The tools are simple. Stone hammers, bone awls, knives of obsidian and of a dark, heavy metal that the smiths extract from the vents in a laborious process involving charcoal and bellows made from whole animal hides. The metal is poor — brittle, prone to cracking, never fully purified — and so the people of Ren-Kesh have never moved past it. Stone is more reliable. Bone is more reliable. The green things that grow in the walls, dried and twisted into cord, are stronger than anything the forges produce. The technology of Ren-Kesh is a technology of the hand, the eye, the patient accumulation of small expertise. A stoneworker can feel the grain of the rock with her thumb and know exactly where to strike. A weaver can produce cord so fine and so strong that it will hold the weight of a person suspended over the abyss below the floor. A hunter can read the patterns of claw-marks on the tunnel walls and tell you, within an hour, when the thing that made them last passed and in which direction it traveled.

---

The animals.

There are so *many* of them.

They are everywhere, in every size, filling every niche of the Hollow's ecology with a density that borders on the obscene. The smallest are mites — clouds of them, visible only as a shimmer in the air near the vegetation, a constant slow drift of microscopic bodies that coats every surface, that gets into your clothes, your hair, your lungs. They are the base of everything. They eat the vegetation and the mineral films and each other, and they are eaten in turn by the small hunters — the spiders (every size from pinhead to palm, some solitary, some colonial, their webs strung across every alley and walkway so that moving through the city at certain hours means pushing through curtain after curtain of silk), the scorpions (small, fast, their venom a sharp bright pain that fades to numbness), the beetle-things with carapaces like polished stone that click and rattle as they move in their hundreds through the lower streets.

Above the insect-layer, the vertebrates. Lizards everywhere — on the walls, on the ceilings, darting across the terraces with a speed that makes them look like drops of water rolling uphill. They are small, most of them, finger-length, and they eat the mites and the spiders and are eaten in turn by the snake-things — legless, smooth, the color of the stone, motionless for hours before they strike. The snakes are preyed upon by the burrowers, low-slung furred creatures the size of a fist with broad clawed feet and blunt faces that root through the vegetation mats for eggs and grubs. The burrowers are preyed upon by the flyers.

The flyers are the lords of the upper Hollow. Birds, mostly — or things close enough to birds that the distinction doesn't matter. Small, dark-feathered, sharp-beaked, they roost in the thousands in the high reaches of the dome, and at dawn they descend in a screaming, chattering river to hunt the lizards and the snakes and the burrowers in the streets and terraces below. The sound of their descent is the sound that wakes the city each morning — a rising wave of shrieks and the beat of ten thousand wings that drowns out everything else, that you feel in your chest, that sets the mite-clouds swirling and sends the spiders fleeing into their crevices. It lasts perhaps ten minutes. Then the birds disperse and the city returns to its own noise and the hunt continues at a smaller scale through the day, individual birds perched on rooftops and walls, watching, waiting, dropping like stones onto some small movement in the vegetation.

---

Below the floor of the Hollow, where the steam rises and the stone narrows, there are larger things. No one goes there willingly. The hunters who have descended into the dark have reported — before they stopped reporting, before they stopped coming back — a space even larger than the Hollow itself, where the vein-milk wells up in great warm pools and the air is so thick with moisture that it coats your skin instantly, where the vegetation grows to the height of trees and the sounds are not the sharp bright sounds of the city but deep, slow sounds, the movements of heavy bodies in darkness, the splash of something surfacing and submerging in the warm mineral pools.

The people of Ren-Kesh do not speak of what lives below. They leave offerings at the edge of the floor — bundles of vegetation, small animals, cups of the vein-milk itself — and the offerings are taken. The vents continue to breathe. The warmth continues to rise. The arrangement holds.

---

It is loud. It is always loud. The city never sleeps because the city is not a place of sleep — it is a place of continuous activity, of work and hunting and building and eating and breeding and dying, all of it happening simultaneously, all of it contributing to the roar. The noise is not chaotic. It has a rhythm, a pulse, like the breathing of the mountain itself. The morning descent of the birds. The midday hammering of the stoneworkers and the constant background clicking of the beetle-things. The evening lowing of the burrowers in their nests and the high insect hum that rises as the temperature peaks and the vents exhale their strongest. The deep night sounds, when the birds are roosted and the hunters move through the streets with their stone-tipped spears, listening for the claw-scratch of something that has climbed up from below, something too large for the normal predators, something that requires the cooperation of several hunters and a great deal of noise and the throwing of burning brands into the dark.

---

There is no history in Ren-Kesh that anyone can read. The oldest buildings have been rebuilt so many times that their original shape is gone, replaced by the slow accretion of repair and modification, each generation adding a wall here, closing an opening there, until the structures are as layered and as dense as the stone itself. The people know they have always been here. They know the Hollow has always been here. The mountain, the stone, the vein-milk, the warmth, the green things, the animals — these are not conditions of life. They *are* life. The distinction between the city and the ecosystem, between the built and the grown, between the people and the ten thousand other species that share their space, has never been sharp and has grown less so with every passing generation. The people of Ren-Kesh are not separate from the Hollow. They are the Hollow's most complex expression, its thinking part, the piece that builds and plans and remembers — but only just. Only barely. The green things are growing over the highest towers. The birds nest in the council chamber. The spiders have claimed the archive. The beetle-things rattle through the streets at night in their thousands, and the people step around them, and the beetle-things flow around the people's feet, and neither species gives the other more than a moment's attention.

---

It is warm. It is loud. It is dense with life and with the smell of life — the mineral sweetness of the vein-milk, the green smell of crushed vegetation, the musk of the burrowers, the sharp ammonia tang of the birds' roosting-places, the thick animal warmth of ten thousand bodies pressed close together in the narrow streets. The light is dim and comes from nowhere and is always just enough to see by. The stone beneath your feet is smooth and warm and faintly damp and humming with the deep vibration of the vents below.

This is Ren-Kesh. This is the Hollow. It has been here since before memory and it will be here after, the warm wet beating heart of a mountain that no longer has a name, hidden in the high places of a world that may or may not exist beyond the narrow throat of the entrance passage, which spirals up and up and up and emerges — if it emerges at all — onto a plain of dry stone under a sky so vast and so empty that the people of Ren-Kesh, if they could see it, would not recognize it as real.

They do not go up. There is no water up there. There is nothing up there.

Everything is here.
