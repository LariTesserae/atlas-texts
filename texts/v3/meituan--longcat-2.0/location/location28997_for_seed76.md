# The Lithic Plateaus

---

The air is thin here — not dangerously so, but enough that every breath feels deliberate, as though the body must *choose* to take it in rather than being given it. The altitude is immense. You are above most of the atmosphere, or what passes for atmosphere on this world — a pale, dry veil of gas so attenuated it barely distorts the light. And the light itself: harsh, white, almost surgical in its clarity. There is no haze, no moisture to scatter it, no water vapor to soften its edges. Shadows are absolute. Where the sun — or suns — strikes, the world is a brightness so total it seems to erase texture, flattening every surface into pure luminosity. In shadow, the details rush back in.

The ground stretches in every direction. Not flat — never truly flat — but broadly level, like the surface of a sea that forgot it was supposed to have waves. It is a plateau, or rather a system of plateaus, stepped and terraced across a span so large that curvature becomes relevant. From certain high points you can see the land bowing gently away, the horizon closer than it should be, and beyond it more land, and beyond that more still, a continental body of terrain that has no coast because it has never needed one. There is no ocean below. There is no ocean anywhere. Water, in any meaningful quantity, does not exist here — not on the surface, not in the atmosphere, not locked in ice beneath the regolith. The chemistry of this place is anhydrous. It has been so for longer than the word *dry* can convey.

And yet.

And yet there is life.

---

Not life as you might expect. Not green. Not soft. Not fed by rain or root or the quiet commerce of cells in aqueous solution. What grows here grows from light and mineral, from the slow accretion of atmospheric chemistry drawn down into crystalline lattices by processes that are, depending on your framework, either a form of photosynthesis so alien it barely deserves the name, or a kind of mineral metabolism — a geology that has learned to *want.*

They rise from the plateau floor in clusters and groves and sometimes vast continuous carpets of form. Call them what you like. The closest word might be *forest*, but only if you have never seen a forest and have only been told that forests are vertical, are dense, are living. They are vertical. They are dense, in places — the lushness parameter is real, and it manifests here as abundance, as riotous structural complexity, as a profusion of form that crowds the eye even as the spaces between the clusters remain enormous.

They are not tall, most of them. Some reach the height of a person. Others are low, spreading, crust-like — shelf growths and nodules that coat the rock in dense, overlapping patterns like mineral lichen magnified a thousandfold. But some rise higher. Spires, columns, branching lattices of crystalline and pseudocrystalline material that extend upward toward the light, some as tall as trees, a few as tall as towers. They are not uniform. They are riotous. Their shapes speak of branching algorithms, of growth patterns that fork and bifurcate and recombine in ways that echo — but do not replicate — the dendrites of earthly plants. Some are simple: straight columns with occasional lateral offshoots, like organ pipes or candle drippings. Others are extravagantly complex, their surfaces covered in smaller growths, their upper structures fanning into broad, flat arrays of thin, translucent panels — light collectors, you realize. Solar panels. Grown, not made.

Their colors are not the colors of leaves. There is no chlorophyll here, no green. Instead: grey, yes, and white, and pale mineral hues — the blues and lavenders of certain silicates, the yellows and ochres of iron-bearing compounds. But also, in places, startling saturation. Deep ruby reds in the mineral-dense clusters. Vivid amethyst in the spires of certain regions. Emerald and teal where copper compounds have been incorporated into the lattice structures. And everywhere, in the translucent panels and the thin-surfaced leaves and the filigree of the smallest growths, a quality of *internal glow* — not bioluminescence, exactly, but the slow release of captured light, a faint luminescence that persists in the material even in shadow, so that a grove of these beings, seen from a distance at the dimmer hours of the day, appears to pulse with a soft, multi-hued radiance. Like stained glass lit from within. Like a city seen at night, except there are no cities here. There is no built thing. There is only this — only growth.

---

The spacing is the thing that surprises you, once you've accepted the growth itself. It is not continuous. The plateaus are vast, and the living clusters occupy only a fraction of the surface. Between them: open ground. Stretches of bare mineral substrate, pale and cracked in geometric patterns, or covered in a fine regolith that shifts and resettles in the wind. These empty spaces are not small. They extend for kilometers — sometimes tens of kilometers — flat and featureless and profoundly silent, so that when you emerge from a dense grove of crystal-structures with their faint hum and their subtle light, the transition is jarring. You step from life into absence. The emptiness has a weight.

And yet it is not truly empty. The ground itself is structured. Patterned. Cracked into polygons by thermal cycling — the extreme temperature swings between the bright hours and the dark hours pulling the mineral surface apart and pushing it back together over millennia, creating tessellations of such regularity they look almost intentional. In some areas the cracks have deepened into canyons — not water-carved, never water-carved, but opened by thermal stress and widened by wind erosion into channels meters deep, their floors littered with angular debris. These crack-canyons form networks across the open ground, dividing the bare areas into irregular cells, and in the sheltered depths of the deeper ones you sometimes find small, hardy growths — the dwarf forms, the crusts and nodules — clinging to shaded walls where the thermal stress is slightly less and trace atmospheric moisture — not water, never water, but some other volatile — can condense.

Because there is weather here. Not water-weather. Not rain. But the atmosphere moves. Winds rise off the open plateaus with surprising force, carrying fine mineral dust that abrades the growths and sculpts their surfaces. The taller spires are all windward-leaning, their light-collecting panels angled to face the sun on one side and sheared clean on the other by decades, centuries, millennia of persistent aeolian erosion. You can read the prevailing wind in their shapes the way you'd read current in a kelp forest. And the erosion is not merely destructive — it is *formative.* The growths and the wind exist in equilibrium. They grow, and the wind wears them, and the wearing shapes them, and the shaping directs their growth, and the growth redirects the wind. It is a dance that has been going on for so long — the parameter says *established*, says *settled-form*, says *in-balance* — that the forms you see are not accidental. They are the product of deep co-evolution between life and environment, a steady state that has persisted across spans of time you cannot easily hold in your mind.

---

The sounds. You expected silence, and silence is mostly what there is. The thin atmosphere carries sound poorly; the air itself seems to absorb rather than transmit. But there are sounds, if you are patient.

The growths themselves make noise. Not loudly. A faint, high-frequency singing — the mineral lattices vibrating in the wind, each spire and panel and filament tuned to its own resonant frequency by its size and shape and internal structure. In a dense grove, the collective effect is a shimmering, ethereal chorus — not musical in any conventional sense, but undeniably *tonal.* Like wind chimes made of crystal. Like the hum of a vast, frozen instrument. It is the most prominent sound in the landscape, and even it is barely above the threshold of hearing. You feel it in your teeth before you hear it with your ears.

Thermal stress produces sharper sounds. Clicks and ticks and occasional sharp cracks as mineral surfaces expand or contract past their tolerance. In the bright hours, as the sun's warmth penetrates the outer layers of the growths, there is a slow, rhythmic *tinking*, almost like distant ice breaking. In the dark hours, as the temperature plummets and the lattices contract, the sounds are deeper — low groans and moans from the larger structures, the sound of rock under strain. These thermal sounds are the voice of the deep geology, the planet itself breathing in and out with the day-night cycle.

And beneath it all — below the hearing threshold, below even the feeling threshold, perhaps — a sound that may be imagination. A deep, subsonic pulse. The planet's own frequency. The hum of a world that is not dead but merely operating on a timescale that makes your own existence a flicker.

---

The fauna. Rare, yes. But present. There are things that move across the plateaus — slowly, so slowly that you might watch one for an hour and barely perceive its progress. They are not animals in any familiar sense. They are — what? Mineral grazers. Lithovores. Organisms that consume the growths, that incorporate the crystalline material into their own structures, that move across the landscape in search of fresh growth like cattle moving between pastures. They are large — the size of a small vehicle, or a large one — and they move on multiple limbs, low-slung bodies with broad, flat feet designed for traversing the cracked regolith without falling into the canyons. Their surfaces are armored with the same mineral compounds as the growths they eat; they are, in a sense, mobile extensions of the same metabolic system. They eat, and they grow, and they excrete, and their waste becomes the substrate for new growth. The cycle is closed.

You will not see many of them. They are sparse — density is low across the board, and these beings are the largest and rarest fauna the world supports. You might find their tracks: wide, shallow depressions in the regolith, trailing across the open ground toward a distant grove. You might find their leavings: piles of processed mineral, slightly altered in composition, already being colonized by pioneer growths. You might, if you are fortunate or unlucky, encounter one itself — a slow, deliberate shape on the horizon, making its unhurried way across the brightness, casting a long shadow behind it. It will not acknowledge you. It is not hostile or friendly; it is *indifferent.* You are not part of its chemistry.

There are smaller things. Microfauna, mesofauna — organisms that live within the growth structures themselves, burrowing through the mineral lattices, feeding on the living crystal from the inside. You would need magnification to see them, and instruments you do not have. But their effects are visible: the pitting and channeling of spire surfaces, the honeycombing of older structures, the slow internal dissolution that contributes to the erosion cycle. The growths are ecosystems unto themselves, and the things that eat them are part of the balance. Birth, growth, consumption, decay, regrowth. The wheel turns, just slowly. Just so slowly.

---

The sky. The sky is the dominant feature, and it is a deep, deep blue — darker than any earthly sky, because the atmosphere is so thin, because there is so little to scatter the light. At the zenith it approaches indigo, almost black. The sun — there is one sun, or what functions as a sun; a star of such brightness and proximity that the plateaus are bathed in more light than any terrestrial desert — burns with a white intensity that is uncomfortable even through closed eyelids. And yet the temperature remains cool. The thin atmosphere holds little heat. The ground radiates freely into space. The result is a landscape of extremes: blistering radiative heating in the direct sun, and biting cold in the shade, and a mean temperature that hovers in the cool-to-cold range, day and night. You could sunburn and freeze at the same time. You could stand with one foot in light and one in shadow and feel the temperature differential like a physical barrier.

At night — and there is night, a true darkness, because there is no atmospheric glow to soften it — the stars are not twinkled. They are steady, hard points of light, thousands of them, in a sky so clear it seems to have depth without limit. The Milky Way — or whatever galaxy this world belongs to — is a band of luminous dust so bright it casts shadows. The growths, faintly luminescent, dim further but do not go dark; they hold their captured light for hours after sunset, fading slowly, so that a grove at night is a landscape of soft, ghostly glows — red and amber and pale green — scattered across the dark mineral ground like embers in ash.

And the weather — not rain, never rain, but *movement.* Thermal winds that rise from the heated ground in the bright hours and sink back in the dark hours, creating a daily breathing of the atmosphere. Dust devils that spin across the open ground, lifting fine mineral particles into twisting columns that wander for kilometers before dissipating. On rare occasions, when atmospheric conditions align in ways that depend on pressure and temperature and the trace chemistry of the thin air, there are *electrical events* — silent discharges, sheet lightning in the upper atmosphere, or coronal arcs that dance between the tallest spires of a grove, feeding energy directly into the growths' light-harvesting systems. The groves *lean into* these events. You can see it. The taller structures orient toward the electrical activity, their panels tilting, their lattices humming at a higher pitch. They are feeding. The storm is not destructive here — or rather, it is destructive in the way that eating is destructive. It is consumption. It is energy transfer. It is life.

---

The age of this place is its most unsettling quality. Not the individual growths — though some of those are ancient, their cores dating back to periods so remote that the geological substrate around them has changed, the plateaus themselves having risen or sunk or shifted in ways that leave the oldest structures stranded at odd angles, half-buried in newer regolith. No, the unsettling thing is the *system.* The equilibrium. This is not a young landscape finding its shape. This is a landscape that has *found* its shape and held it. The growth-and-erosion cycle has been running for — you cannot say. Long enough that the tallest spires have been worn down and regrown a thousand times. Long enough that the crack-canyon networks have deepened and shallowed and deepened again in response to tectonic pulses that no longer occur. Long enough that the fauna have co-evolved with the growths to the point where neither could exist without the other, and the whole system has settled into a state of dynamic homeostasis that will persist, with minor variations, for as long as the star burns and the atmosphere holds and the planet's internal heat drives the tectonic processes that occasionally — very occasionally — renew the mineral substrate.

It is not a fragile equilibrium. It has survived disruptions you can read in the geological record: impact events (the craters are old, softened by erosion, overgrown — but visible from elevation), volcanic episodes (layers of dark mineral in the canyon walls, overlain by regrowth), shifts in stellar output (isotopic signatures in the oldest crystal cores that speak of a dimmer sun in the distant past). Each time, the system has absorbed the disturbance and returned to its steady state. It is resilient in the way that only very old, very well-adapted systems can be.

---

You stand on the plateau edge and look out. The land descends in a series of broad steps — old erosional surfaces, each one a former plateau level, now exposed as a terrace in the descending terrain. Far below, the lower plateaus catch the light differently; they are dustier, their growths sparser, their colors shifted toward the reds and browns of iron oxidation. The elevation change is significant — kilometers of vertical relief — and with it comes a change in atmospheric density, in temperature, in the character of the growths. But the system is the same. It scales. It persists. From the highest terraces to the lowest basins, the same slow metabolism operates, the same light-harvesting, the same mineral cycling, the same patient co-evolution of structure and process.

You are very small. You are very loud. Your breath, your heartbeat, your movements — these are the most violent sounds in this landscape. You walk across the cracked ground between two groves, and your footsteps ring out like hammer strikes in the silence, each one startling, each one too much. The ground is hard. The regolith is thin. Beneath it, the mineral substrate is dense and ancient, and it carries your weight with a faint, resonant *knock* that travels farther than it should in the thin air.

A growth cluster approaches. You slow. The nearest structures are low — knee-height, waist-height — spreading forms that coat a low rise of bedrock in overlapping plates and nodules. As you draw closer, the taller forms appear behind them: columns, spires, branching lattices that rise to shoulder height, head height, above your head. The light is different inside the cluster. Filtered. Diffuse. The translucent panels overhead break the harsh sunlight into a dappled mosaic of softened brightness, and the internal glow of the structures adds its own illumination — warm, multi-hued, gentle. It is like entering a cathedral. It is like entering a lung. The air inside is fractionally warmer, fractionally denser, enriched by the slow metabolic processes of the growths themselves. It smells — if smell is the right word for the faint, sharp, mineral tang that reaches your olfactory receptors — of something alive. Of chemistry in motion. Of a world that is not dead, not static, not finished, but actively, slowly, *patiently* being itself.

You reach out and touch a spire. It is warm on the sun-facing side, cool on the shadow side. Its surface is textured — fine crystalline ridges, almost like bark, almost like skin. It hums faintly against your palm. You can feel the vibration of its metabolism, the slow pulse of energy flowing through its lattice, the patient accretion of mineral from atmosphere and light. It is growing, right now, as you touch it. Imperceptibly. But growing.

This is the world. Bright and cold and dry and old. Vast beyond comprehension, sparse beyond loneliness, lush beyond expectation. A world without water that has found another way. A world that has settled into its own form and held it across spans of time that make mountains look like sandcastles. A world that hums, that glows, that breathes in thermal tides and electrical storms and the slow, slow metabolism of light-eating stone.

It is not yours. You are merely visiting. And it will be here, humming its quiet hum, long after you are gone.
