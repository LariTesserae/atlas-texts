# The Hanging Lung

It is not a cave. It is a *breath held too long*.

You are inside the chest cavity of something that is still alive.

The space is small — perhaps thirty paces across at its widest, though distance is hard to judge here because the walls refuse to stay still. They are not stone. They are not wood. They are the layered, fibrous interior of a root-chamber so old that its own structure has become geology, though geology that sweats, and pulses, and exhales. The surface is a deep maroon-purple, glossy with moisture, ridged in whorls like fingerprints or the whorls of an ear canal. Water beads on every surface and falls in a constant, patient rain — not from any ceiling crack, but from the walls themselves, which *condense*. The air is warm and thick enough to wear, somewhere around the temperature of blood, and it carries a smell like turned earth and hot copper and the particular sweetness of compost that has passed through some enormous digestive process and come out the other side refined.

Everything drips. Everything weeps. The floor — if it can be called a floor — is a shallow soup of clear fluid no deeper than your ankle, heated from below by the slow burn of metabolic processes you cannot see. It is not stagnant. It moves in lazy, directional currents, pulled toward drains that are not drains but *sphincters* — circular openings in the tissue that open and close at intervals of about four seconds, each opening accompanied by a low, wet, involuntary sound, like a mouth tasting air. The sound is everywhere. It is the base note of this place. Hundreds of sphincters, opening and opening and opening, and the gurgle of fluid passing through them, and the deeper, slower thunder of something larger pumping somewhere below — something the size of a building, something with a rhythm of about one beat every nine seconds.

The walls are not bare. They are colonized.

Not by moss, not by fungus, not in the way that caves are colonized. They are colonized by *organism-as-infrastructure*. Ribbed fans of tissue, each one the size of a dinner table, are set into the walls at irregular intervals — and they *beat*. Slowly, mechanically, they flex and relax, flex and relax, moving the heavy air in a circulation pattern that never varies. They are lungs. They are fans. They are the ventilation system of a factory that has no blueprint, only biology. They make a sound like canvas sails in a wind that never shifts — a heavy, rhythmic *fwuh, fwuh, fwuh* that overlays the sphincter-sounds and the deep bass pump-beat and creates a soundscape so layered and so constant that after a few minutes you stop hearing it as noise and start hearing it as silence. It is the loudest quiet you have ever experienced.

Between the fans, the walls are threaded with vessels. Some are as wide as your arm, some as wide as your body, and they carry fluid — different fluids, different colors, some dark red, some pale amber, some a startling, chemical green that luminesces faintly even in the normal light that filters in from somewhere above. The vessels do not simply sit against the wall. They are *the* wall. They are embedded, subcutaneous, visible through a translucent outer membrane like veins on the back of an old hand. And they *thrum*. Something is being moved through them under pressure. The pulse is not a heartbeat exactly — it is too regular for that, too metronomic. It is a *pump*. Something in the deep substrate is doing the work of an engine, and the engines are alive, and they have been alive for so long that they have become reliable. Industrial. You could set your watch by them if you wore a watch here, which you do not, because there is nothing to build a watch from, because there are no structures, because nothing here is built — everything here simply *grew into its function* and then stopped changing.

That is the most unsettling thing. Not the wetness, not the warmth, not the living walls. The *stability*. This place is in equilibrium the way a river is in equilibrium — every drop replaced, every force balanced, nothing accumulating, nothing diminishing. The fans beat at the same rate they beat a thousand years ago. The sphincters open on the same interval. The fluid in the floor is the same depth. The temperature is the same temperature. Nothing is dying and nothing is being born at a rate that would change the totals. It is an ecosystem that has solved itself, a machine that has reached steady state, and it will not change because change would require a perturbation, and there are no perturbations, because the system is sealed, because it is hidden, because it is *inside*.

You are sparse here.

The space between presences is large. You stand ankle-warm in the slow-current soup, and the nearest wall-fan is ten paces away, and the nearest vessel-cluster is five, and the nearest of the *animals* — the fauna, the abundant, scattered fauna — is somewhere. You hear it before you see it: a click, a scrape, a small wet sound. Then it emerges from behind a ridge of wall-tissue: a creature about the size of a cat, six-legged, low-slung, with a carapace that looks exactly like the wall material — maroon, ridged, moist. It regards you with eyes that are not eyes but heat-pits, and it does not flee, because it has never needed to flee, because nothing here preys on it in a way that would require the behavior to evolve, because the system is too old and too balanced for predation-as-desperation. It clicks — a sound like a wrench on concrete — and moves past you, along a route it has traveled ten thousand times, to a spot on the wall where a valve opens and releases a brief jet of warm vapor. It positions itself in the vapor. It is *using* the machinery. It has always used the machinery.

There are more of them. They are always here, always abundant, but always *spaced*. The system supports them but does not crowd them. Each one has its route, its valve, its fan-warmth spot, its feeding cleft in the wall where metabolic byproducts seep out in a thin nutritious film. They are the fauna of an industrial biome, and they have the same relationship to the machinery that pigeons have to buildings — they live in its interstices, they depend on its outputs, they are not separate from it.

A valve hisses open behind you. A puff of gas — oxygen-rich, startlingly cool — billows past, and for a moment the condensate on your skin evaporates, and you feel briefly, oddly dry, and then the warm wet closes back in. The valve closes. The rhythm continues. Nine seconds, pump. Four seconds, sphincter. Two seconds, fan. Nine. Four. Two. Nine. Four. Two.

The light is normal. That is, it comes from no single source you can identify — it filters from above, through translucent layers of tissue, diffused and directionless, the color of late afternoon in a greenhouse. It does not flicker. It does not vary. It has not varied in a time you cannot conceive of.

And nothing changes. Nothing changes. Nothing changes.

The lung breathes, and you are inside it, and it will breathe forever, or until the organism that is not quite an organism and not quite a machine dies, which it will not do, because it has not done it yet, because it has found the equilibrium that is the closest thing biology comes to immortality.

You stand in the warm wet, and the creatures click and scrape along their routes, and the fans beat, and the pumps pump, and the water falls from the walls like rain in a place that has never seen sky, and the sound is enormous, and the space is small, and everything is alive, and nothing is new.
