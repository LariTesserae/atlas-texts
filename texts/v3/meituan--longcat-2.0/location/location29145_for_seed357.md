This is a high-altitude plateau of violent, sweating birth. The elevation is sharp and breathless, but the air is thick, sweltering, and saturated—a bizarre, oppressive marriage of altitude and tropical heat. The sky is a pale, bleached white, casting a normal, unfiltered light that makes the wetness everywhere gleam with a blinding, clinical intensity. 

Beneath your feet, there is no soil. The erosion here is young, the geology freshly sheared and sharp, but the natural substrate is entirely entombed. This is a fully constructed environment, a made-landscape built entirely without tools or technology. The ground is a dense, interwoven mat of roots, vines, and hardened biological resins—a living, breathing floor that has been woven and secreted into existence. It is plant-as-architecture, a total botanical construction. Yet, the density is sparse. The woven root-floor is broken frequently by wide, steaming fissures, revealing the jagged, newly formed obsidian-like rock beneath, hissing with geothermal heat. 

Across this vast, open expanse, the vegetation does not grow—it builds. Scattered widely across the plateau, balanced with immense stretches of empty, dripping space, are colossal botanical monoliths. These are not trees; they are grown structures. Towers of braided hardwood rise hundreds of feet, arching together to form cathedral-like vaults. Giant, hollow reeds as wide as houses stand in loose, town-sized clusters, their tops blooming into vast, funnel-shaped canopies that catch the heavy, condensing atmosphere. Water weeps continuously from every surface. It beads on the waxy leaves, runs in thick rivulets down the braided trunks, and drips from the high, woven ceilings in a constant, deafening percussion. 

The sound is overwhelming. There are no animals here—no birds, no insects, no rustling of small creatures in the undergrowth. The silence of life is replaced by the roar of biology in overdrive. The landscape is hyperactive, turbulent, caught in a state of Solaris-like creation and destruction. The monoliths are growing visibly, groaning and cracking as they expand. Vines lash out like whips, thickening and hardening into new buttresses in a matter of hours. A tower of woven cellulose suddenly bulges at its base, splitting with a deafening *crack* that echoes across the plateau, before toppling sideways and shattering into a spray of fibrous debris and hot, viscous sap. Before the dust even settles, new shoots are already erupting from the wreckage, reaching hungrily for the pale sky. 

The water is the blood of this frantic system. It boils up from the young, fractured rock below, filling the hollow reeds and the woven floor with rushing, heated channels. The air is so saturated that visibility shifts in waves; the structures in the distance seem to breathe, their outlines blurring and sharpening as the condensing atmosphere thickens and thins. 

The ecology is a closed, furious loop. There is no decay in the traditional sense, because the environment consumes itself so quickly. Dead plant matter is immediately broken down by the intense heat and the rushing, enzyme-rich water, absorbed back into the root-network to fuel the next violent surge of growth. It is a pure-plant ecosystem operating at a fever pitch, a bazaar of biological construction where the currency is water and the architecture is constantly tearing itself down to build itself back up. 

To stand here is to stand on a construction site the size of a valley, built by no hands, inhabited by nothing but the wet, sweltering, deafeningly loud ambition of the green world.
