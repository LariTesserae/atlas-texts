# The Stillworks

---

From above — if there were an above, if there were a vantage from which to stand apart and look down — the ocean would appear to be a single held breath. Not frozen. Not calm. *Holding.* Every wave in the same place it has always been, crest raised to the same impossible height, spray suspended in curtains that do not fall. The water is green-dark, the color of deep bottle glass held up to a cloudy sky, and it stretches to every horizon with a uniformity that makes the eye ache for a landmark, a break, a single point of difference.

There is no difference.

The sea is cool. Not cold — not the bite of Arctic water, not the numbness of the deep trenches. It is the temperature of a root cellar, of stone that has never known sun, of a room in an empty house where someone once kept vegetables in crates. The cool has texture. It is the first thing your skin notices and the last thing it forgets. It does not fluctuate. It has never fluctuated.

---

And the plants are everything.

Not *in* the water. Not *on* the water. The water is the medium and the plants are the architecture and there is no meaningful distinction between the two. From the surface to the lightless bottom — fathoms down, impossibly down — vegetable matter fills the world like a lung fills with air. Kelp-like fronds the width of cathedral doors rise from the depths, not drifting but *standing*, held rigid by their own architecture, their own fiber, their own design. They do not sway. There is no current to sway them. They simply are, exactly as they are, have always been, will always be.

Between the great fronds, smaller growths fill the water column like a three-dimensional lacework. Filamentous structures, hair-thin and miles long, connect larger bodies in a web so dense it seems solid until you move through it and your body passes between the strands with a faint resistance, like pushing through a beaded curtain. Spherical growths the size of houses — buoyant, gas-filled, trembling with the pressure of their own internal atmosphere — hang at various depths, tethered by cables of braided vine. Some are pale green, translucent, and inside them you can see the slow pulse of fluids moving through channels that look, unmistakably, like plumbing.

That word — plumbing — will come to you again.

The plants manufacture. They do not build, not in the way a creature builds, not with intent and plan and the movement of hands. But they *produce.* The fronds exude resins that harden into structures. The filaments conduct something — not electricity, not exactly, but a slow galvanic hum that you can feel in your teeth if you hold very still. The spherical bodies filter the water passing through them and extrude, from their undersides, ropes of processed mineral that fall away into the depths like slow rain. Everything is being made. Everything has always been being made. Nothing is ever finished because nothing ever stops.

It is, in every functional sense, an industry. There are assembly lines of sap and cellulose. There are refineries of brine and light. There are factories the size of cities, built of living tissue, operating at a pace so slow that a single cycle takes longer than a human life, but operating nonetheless — operating eternally, without pause, without product, without waste, because the waste becomes the input for the next cycle and the next and the next and there is no end and no beginning and no change.

The vegetation has been here so long that it has forgotten what it was before it became this. The roots — if they are roots — have fused into the ocean floor, and the ocean floor has fused into the roots, and below that is rock that has been worn to a perfect smoothness by millennia of vegetable patience. The erosion here is not dramatic. There are no cliffs crumbling, no canyons being carved. Everything is *settled.* The shapes the plants take are the shapes they took a thousand years ago and a thousand years before that. A great frond arches overhead in a curve that is mathematically perfect, and it will arch in that curve forever, because the forces that shape it are in balance, because the growth rate equals the decay rate, because the tension in its fibers matches the tension in the water that holds it, and the whole system has found its equilibrium and will not leave it.

---

The spaces between the great structures are vast.

This is the sparseness — the emptiness that makes the fullness possible. Between one forest of fronds and the next, there may be a mile of open water, or five miles, or twenty. The distances are not random but they are not regular either; they have the quality of something that grew into its own spacing, each organism claiming the room it needed and no more, until the whole ocean achieved a distribution that was, in some sense, optimal. You can swim — if you are a creature that swims — for hours through clear, green, empty water, seeing nothing but the faint filament-web in the distance, hearing the low hum of the deep machinery, feeling the cool press of the water against your skin, and then the next forest will appear ahead of you like a city skyline, vertical and intricate and impossibly tall, and you will swim toward it and it will take you hours more to reach, and it will not have changed at all while you traveled.

The elevation is high. The whole world sits at an altitude that would, on a more ordinary planet, support glaciers and thin air and the kind of wind that strips flesh from bone. But the water is dense — dense with dissolved minerals, dense with the metabolic products of the vegetable world — and the atmosphere above the water is thick and warm and wet, and the sky is a low ceiling of cloud that has never broken, never rain, never thinned, never moved. The light that comes through is diffuse and even and directionless, the color of old pewter, and it does not shift with time because time does not shift. It is always the same hour. It is always the same quality of light. The sun — if there is a sun — is somewhere behind the clouds, and its position is fixed, and the shadows it casts through the water are fixed, and the patterns of light and dark on the ocean floor are fixed, and they have been fixed since before anything alive can remember.

---

The animals move through this world like blood cells through a body.

They are abundant — not swarming, not teeming, but *present,* in numbers that remind you this is a living system and not merely a mechanical one. Fish, or things like fish: long, silver, many-finned, moving in schools that hold their formation with a precision that suggests not intelligence but *design,* as though the school itself is a single organism and the fish are its cells. They follow the same paths they have always followed, turning at the same fronds, pausing at the same junctions in the filament-web, rising and falling through the water column in patterns that repeat with the regularity of breathing. But they are not breathing. They are not doing anything that implies a before or an after. They are simply *in motion,* perpetually, the way a planet orbits — not because it has been launched and is coasting, but because orbit is its state, because it has no other state to be in.

Larger creatures move through the open spaces. Things with shells, things with tentacles, things with bodies that are mostly mouth and mostly patience. They feed on the plants, or the plants feed on them, or they feed on each other in a cycle so perfectly balanced that no population ever grows and no population ever shrinks. A great shelled creature — the size of a small boat, plated in calcium carbonate that has the look of old concrete — drifts through the water column with its tentacles extended, filtering the filament-web for nutrients, and it has been drifting in this exact posture for as long as anyone can determine, and it will continue to drift in this exact posture for as long as anything continues to be.

The sounds are loud.

Not the sounds of animals — though there are clicks and groans and the deep thrumming of creatures too large to see — but the sounds of the system itself. The plants make noise as they work. The great fronds creak and groan as their internal fluids shift. The spherical bodies emit a low, rhythmic pulse — *whump, whump, whump* — as they contract and expand, processing water, and the sound carries for miles through the dense medium, overlapping with the pulses of other bodies until the whole ocean is filled with a layered, polyrhythmic drone that is almost music, almost language, almost the sound of a vast engine running at idle. The filament-web vibrates with the movement of water through it, producing a high, constant hiss, like steam escaping, like static, like the sound of a thousand tiny voices speaking words you almost understand. And beneath it all, from the depths, comes a sound that is felt more than heard — a subsonic rumble, the voice of the planet itself, or of the machinery that has replaced the planet, or of the planet *as* machinery, grinding through its eternal cycle without friction, without wear, without end.

---

There is nothing built here.

No walls. No roads. No tools. No artifacts of any kind. The plants have made structures that look like buildings — arches, vaults, corridors, chambers — but they grew that way, or they grew into that shape over centuries of slow accretion, and they are as natural as a coral reef, as a termite mound, as a bird's nest the size of a cathedral. The animals do not build. They do not modify their environment. They move through it, feed on it, contribute their waste to it, and the system absorbs everything and returns everything and nothing is lost and nothing is gained.

And yet the industrial quality is unmistakable. The scale of production. The efficiency of the cycles. The sense that this is not a wilderness but a *works* — a vast, living factory that has been running since before memory and will run until after. The plants are the machines. The water is the coolant. The animals are the product, or the workers, or the waste, or all three at once. The whole system hums with purpose, but the purpose is not directed toward any goal. It simply *is.* It runs because running is what it does. It produces because production is its nature. It maintains itself because maintenance is the only state it has ever known.

---

The strangeness is in the stillness.

Not the stillness of death — this is not a dead world, not a frozen world, not a world that has stopped. It is a world that has *arrived.* Every process is active. Every organism is alive. Every wave is in motion, every frond is growing, every animal is swimming, every sound is sounding. But nothing is *changing.* The wave will be in this position tomorrow. The frond will have grown by exactly the amount it grew yesterday. The animal will be in the same place, following the same path, making the same sound. The system has achieved a state of perfect dynamic equilibrium — not stasis, but *stability,* a condition in which all the forces are balanced and all the cycles are closed and the whole thing can run forever without deviation, without evolution, without surprise.

It is the most productive system imaginable, and it produces nothing new.

It is the most alive world you will ever encounter, and nothing on it will ever do anything it has not already done a thousand times.

It is loud, and cool, and green, and vast, and it is exactly, precisely, eternally itself.

---

You swim through it. The water is thick against your skin. The filament-web parts around you and closes behind you. Ahead, the next forest of fronds rises from the depths like a skyline, and the pulse of the spherical bodies beats in your chest, and the hiss of the web fills your ears, and the great subsonic rumble vibrates in your bones, and you move forward through the cool, green, living dark, and nothing changes, and nothing changes, and nothing changes.
