**The Hollows**

You go down through a dry throat of salt, hand over hand on a rope polished by a thousand palms, and the sound reaches you before the light does. It comes up the shaft like weather: a pressure, a thickening, a roar with grain to it. Then the shaft opens and you step out under the ceiling of the world, and the roar becomes everything.

This is a country beneath rock. Not a cave — a country. A chain of halls and valleys-linked-underground, the kind of span where "a day's walk" is the honest unit of measure: a day north to south, three days east to west through the linked galleries, each hall a valley, each valley floored edge to edge in green. There is no sky. There is something better, or at least louder: the Roof.

The Roof is a continent hung upside down. Orchards of it. A woven matwoood of glowing vines and lamp-fruit, kilometers of interlaced luminous growth generating a steady milky light that has no source-point and therefore casts almost no shadows — everything lit evenly, softly, from a whole glowing Firmament at once. It is daylight here, a light like noon under high overcast, and it does not waver for days at a time. Then, irregularly, the lamp-organisms of the Roof darken — a dimming the floor-folk call *dimsleep* — and for ten or twenty hours the country goes to blue dusk while the fauna rearranges its entire soundscape around the new register. The energy of all this light is old and strange: the vast root-systems of the Roof plunge down through the living floor into the warm young rock below, where the heat of the forming world itself is drawn up and spent, overhead, as light. An inverted sun. The star of this place burns downward through its own body.

There is no water. Say it plainly, because the greenness will lie to you: there is no water. The sap of this world is not water. It runs thin and warm and amber, tasting of pine and hot iron — the people drink it pressed from the oil-gourds, and the plants conduct it everywhere through wax-lined veins, an entire biosphere running on solvent that is not wet. Nothing here drinks. Everything here *drinks*, but not water — and true moisture is the one pollutant. The rare seep that works down from the drowned world above kills every green thing it touches, and the old buttonholed tunnels where it gathers are the haunted places, marked with woven warning-signs the size of doors. The floor-folk test any liquid the way we might test for poison: if it beads on wax, fear it.

And the floor. You walk on the country's own autobiography. Layer on sealed layer of it — living mat on root-loam on generations of dead growth gone down and hardened into resin, century under century, depth unknown. Some halls have stairways cut into this fossilized stratum and give up after thirty meters of former forests. The rock below is geologically newborn, still hot in vents and seams, which is why the whole country is blood-warm, air and stone both — warmth like a drying loft, like the inside of an animal — and why heat rather than cold is the traveler's enemy. You sleep without blankets in the middle of anywhere, and you keep your hands off the breathing-vents, where the deep roots trade warmth for minerals and steam thin scents of hot turpentine into the dark.

Rivers run here, slow ones. Not water — rivers of raw resin, molasses-slow, cool amber veins that wander the floors for generations and then set, and become ground, and grow over. The landmarks give even the Resinfall: a falls of warm gum pouring off a Roof-vent into a wide slow channel, the people's favorite spectacle, where the water-that-isn't-water goes over the cliff in a glossy rope and the light of the Roof shines into it all the way down.

Because of the light, leaves here are mirrors. The floor-plants mine halite from the young rock and grow it into faceted salt-leaves angled to catch the Roof's dim directional drift — whole hillsides flash when you cross them, gardens of aimed brightness, a plant economy where shade is poverty and reflected light is currency. Trees lean their mirrors toward their neighbors like generosity or theft.

Now: the sound. This is what the visitors never believe beforehand and never forget after. The country is deafening, permanently, structurally deafening, and the reason is an evolutionary arms race that never ended: in a world where every pollinator and mate and alarm is a voice in a permanent hurricane, everything that needed to be heard got louder than everything else, forever. The dominant instrument is the click-swarms — thumb-sized beetles in numbers that make the air itself grainy, billions clacking their wing-cases in synchrony — and their drone has *fronts* and *systems* like weather; it changes key with the cycle, deepens during dimsleep, spikes when the swarms migrate in tides. Over it: the seed-cannons, one-seed pods that detonate with a rifle-crack to throw their young, a constant desultory gunfire from the shrub-line. Under it: the grinding of the mill-beasts, house-sized grazers with stone gizzards milling the living floor day and night, a tectonic gravel that your teeth feel before your ears find it. Between it: the warbling of lampmoths, the clatter of grooming-finches, the elastic squeal of resin rivers cooling, and — threaded through everything like a telegraph — the drum-language of the people. There is no quiet. The one place in the whole country where silence exists is the Burnt Hall, where generation ago someone struck a spark in an oil-warm world. The flash took a valley in minutes. Nothing grows there yet, and it is silent, truly silent, and the people make a pilgrimage to it to remember what quiet costs. Silence, in the Hollows, is either god or wound.

The fauna is not a presence in this landscape; it is half the landscape's mass. Surfaces crawl. The click-swarms are weather, the mill-beasts are geology, and the Roof casts its own predators: shadow-mantas, wide flat gliders that hang from the glowing canopy and drop at the first *interruption* in the roar — they hunt the holes in the sound, which is why every animal here screams continuously and why every local silence is a held breath. When the swarm-drone suddenly ceases, the entire floor drops flat for a three-count, a whole ecosystem prostrate, before the roar resumes and life stands up again and re-reads itself into the noise.

The people live inside all this the way you live inside a heartbeat — because it never stops. You will not speak to them with your mouth. Over the roar they use hand-tongue, a full signing grammar, quick and raucous with humor; children are fluent in it before they can walk far, and the elders, mostly deaf-handed-down from birth in this country, are the *back-listeners*, who lie flat on the floor and read the valley through the spine — beast-passes, drum-messages, the tremor of new halls opening. For talk across distances they beat coded phrases on the exposed root-butts of the great architecture-plants, and the root-net carries it hall to hall like signal-wire, faster than walking.

Their craft fits the world: stone, bone, chitin, fiber, and resin — resin above all, which cures harder than wood and takes an edge out of all proportion to their "primitive" reputation. No metal, no fired clay (clay needs the wet), and no fire, ever, at all: the flame prohibition is the first law, taught as a sign before children learn their own names. Their homes are grown, not built — cultivated gourd-pods, coaxed to house-size, hollowed and dried from a doorway cut in the green; a house lasts about a decade, so a village is a migration timed to the maturing of the next generation of homes, and the "old town" behind any village is a rank of sunken, half-floor-swallowed husks like the shed skins of the settlement itself. Scattered farther out, in the deep green, are the remains of braver ones: solitary hollow-husks gone utterly to jungle, notched trail-trees marking paths that were honest a season ago, shrines where some great mill-beast molt-casing stands dressed in garlands and offered lamp-fruit.

Because this whole country is *young* — that is the fact beneath every other fact. The Hollows are not old caves weathered and worn; they are a world mid-birth, still being made, opening. At the margins — always farther out, always by tomorrow — is the Soft Edge: raw new galleries of naked dark rock, walls still warm, spaces that did not exist last year, into which the green is pouring with a frontier mania. New ground is born lacquered in hardened resin flows, and the pioneer mats stitch it in days; a gardener-storm of defoliating swarm-bloom can strip a hall to its skeleton overnight, and the regrowth comes back *rearranged* — paths gone, hills different, a couple of new landforms nobody voted for. Nothing here is older than ten years, except the floor and its dead centuries, and the mapmakers gave up generations ago. The people instead memorize living landmarks — this dressed tree, that tamed grazer, the mill-beast highway whose great mild architects plow their stone gizzards from hall to hall and are followed, like slow ships, by whole dependent villages. The mill-beasts are the roads. You do not follow a trail here; you follow an animal.

And what is hollowing all this? What keeps making rooms? The rootsayers — who graft whatever cutting onto whatever stock, always successfully, always, and who hold therefore that every green thing in the country is one single plant wearing many masks, one organism the size of a valley, dreaming in forms — say the world is opening because the plant is still growing and its growth *includes its rooms*: space as an organ. The heretics say there are two plants, and that the turbulence, the bulldozing gardener-storms, the overnight rearranging, is their argument. Nobody knows. But everyone who has stood at the Soft Edge, hands flat on rock still warm from being-born, listening to the roar pour on ahead of the light into darkness that was solid stone within living memory, carries away the same guess: that somewhere ahead, softly, the loudest thing in the Hollows is working — some digger that dreams galleries, some patient excavation the whole bright roaring green world is merely the scar-tissue of.

You will leave eventually, up the salt throat, and the roar will ride with you for days, ringing in the bones of your jaw, so that silent rooms above ground feel amputated. The people have a sign for this too: both hands cupped, as if holding a bowl of air up to the ear — the hollow — then opened, empty. It means *listen to what is missing.* Then they laugh, slap the root they're sitting on, and turn back down into the noise, into the warm, into the half-made world that is building itself as fast as it can.
