# The Resonant Expanse

---

From a distance, it looks like a sea that forgot how to be water.

The horizon is a rumpled green-gold line, broken at intervals by shapes that might be mountains except that they breathe. They do breathe — you can see it if you watch long enough, a slow expansion and contraction, a rhythm that has nothing to do with wind. These are the Pillars. Each one is a single organism, or perhaps a colony so ancient that the distinction has ceased to matter. They rise from the plateau floor like the trunks of trees, except that the smallest is wider than a city, and the largest — the one called the Throat, or the First Nostril, depending on which people you ask — is so vast that it generates its own weather systems along its upper flanks, clouds spiraling around its crown like thought around a mind.

Between the Pillars: space. Immense, wind-scoured, sun-hammered space. The plateau stretches in every direction, and the distance between one Pillar and the next is measured in hours of walking, sometimes days. This is the sparseness — the emptiness that makes each Pillar an event, a destination, a world unto itself. You can stand in the open steppe between them and see nothing but the ochre ground, the heat shimmer, the sky pressing down like a hand, and then the next Pillar rising from the flatness as if the earth simply decided, at that particular point, to become something else.

The ground itself is alive, though not in the way you might expect. It is a mat — a crust of interwoven root-hairs and fungal threads and something that might be lichen or might be the shed skin of the Pillars themselves, a dry, papery substance that crunches underfoot and smells faintly of cinnamon and ozone. This crust is the true substrate of the Expanse. Everything grows from it, or on it, or through it. It holds moisture like a sponge holds water — not much, but enough. In the mornings, when the temperature drops just slightly, the crust sweats. Beads of condensation form on its surface, and the ground glistens, and for a few hours the Expanse is damp, almost tender, before the heat returns and drinks it all back up.

The heat. It comes from below as much as above. The plateau is high — the air is thin enough that your lungs work harder, your thoughts move differently, the light has a quality of aggressive clarity, every edge too sharp, every shadow too dark. But the heat is not the thin heat of altitude. It is a thick heat, a wet heat, rising from vents in the crust, from the Pillars themselves, from the metabolic processes of a biomass so enormous that it functions as a geological force. The Pillars are warm to the touch. Their surfaces radiate heat like bread fresh from an oven, and the air around them shimmers, and the creatures that live on and in them have adapted to a world that is, at its core, a warm-blooded thing.

---

The Pillars are not smooth. They are not simple. Their surfaces are a topography unto themselves — ridged, fluted, pocked with cavities large enough to shelter a village, draped with curtains of aerial root-matter that hang in sheets and sway in the constant wind. The bark — if it is bark — is deep furrowed, the furrows running vertically like the channels of a Corinthian column, and in these furrows water collects after the morning condensation, and mosses grow, and tiny bright lizards dart, and the whole surface crawls and hums and seethes with life.

Because the fauna is the other thing. The other overwhelming thing.

You notice the sound before you see the source. The Expanse is never quiet. Even in the open spaces between Pillars, there is a low hum — the sound of the crust itself, vibrating at some frequency below hearing, felt in the sternum more than the ears. But near a Pillar, the sound becomes a roar. It is the sound of millions of mouths. Millions of wings. Millions of legs on bark. The Pillars are infested — no, that's the wrong word. The Pillars are *inhabited*. They are ecosystems entire, vertical worlds, and every surface, every cavity, every furrow and fold is occupied.

The most visible are the Swarm-Wings. They are not insects, though they look like insects — iridescent, many-winged, the size of your hand or your head or, in the deep furrows where the oldest ones live, the size of a dog. They rise from the Pillar surfaces in columns that spiral upward like living smoke, and the sound of their wings is the base note of the Expanse's deafening chorus, a drone that never stops, that you feel in your teeth, that becomes so constant you cease to hear it until it stops and the silence is more terrifying than the noise.

Below the Swarm-Wings, in the furrows, the Crawlers move. They are segmented, many-legged, some armored in chitin that has the same papery quality as the crust, some soft and pale and eyeless, living in the deep places where the Pillar's own heat is strongest. They eat the bark, or they eat the moss, or they eat each other. The food web of a single Pillar is complex enough to occupy a lifetime of study, and no one has ever fully mapped one. The creatures have their own migrations, their own seasons, their own wars.

And then there are the Ones Who Walk Between.

They are not the largest creatures on the Expanse, but they are the most unsettling. They move on six legs, low to the ground, with bodies that are flat and wide and covered in the same papery substance as the crust, so that when they stop moving they disappear — they become ground, they become texture, they become invisible. They are the size of large dogs, or small cattle, and they travel in groups of twenty or thirty, and they are the primary prey of the Flyers — the great raptor-things that nest in the Pillar crowns and descend on thermals with wingspans that blot out the sun. The Ones Who Walk Between are fast and they are numerous and they are the reason the Flyers do not starve, and the Flyers are the reason the Ones Who Walk Between do not overrun the Expanse, and this balance has held for longer than anyone can say.

---

The people — because there are people, or something like people — live in the spaces between.

They are small. Their structures are small. A settlement is a cluster of low shelters built into the lee of a Pillar's base, half-buried in the crust, roofed with woven root-fiber and sealed with a resin they harvest from the Pillar's own wounds. The resin is amber-colored and smells of honey and something sharper, something almost medicinal, and it is the basis of their technology. They use it to glue, to waterproof, to preserve. They have tools of bone and stone and, in the settlements near the thermal vents, simple copper — native copper, found in the vents' mineral deposits, worked cold or with gentle heat, hammered into blades and hooks and needles.

They do not build high. They do not build large. Their marks on the Expanse are faint — a few shelters, a few paths worn into the crust, a few stone cairns to mark the walking routes between Pillars. They are a scattered people, not because they are few but because the Expanse is so vast that even a large population thins to nothing across its distances. A settlement might see a visitor from the next Pillar once a season, or once a year, or once a generation. They have stories about the other settlements, but the stories have the quality of myths — possibly true, possibly not, impossible to verify.

They hunt the Ones Who Walk Between with nets and spears. They gather the Swarm-Wings in the evening, when the creatures are torpid with the cooling air, and eat them roasted, and store them dried. They collect the condensation water in the mornings, channeling it from the crust into clay vessels. They burn the papery bark-substance for fuel, and the smoke smells like the ground smells — cinnamon and ozone — and the smell is the smell of home.

They have a relationship with the Pillars that is not quite worship and not quite husbandry. They know that the Pillars are alive. They know that the Pillars are aware — not in the way a person is aware, but in the way a forest is aware, slowly, chemically, through networks of signal and response that operate on timescales of days and weeks rather than moments. They know that if you cut too deeply into a Pillar's bark, the Pillar will react — not with pain, exactly, but with a change in the local ecosystem, a shift in the creature populations, a closing-off of the wounded area that can take years to heal. They harvest carefully. They ask permission, or they used to, or they still do in some settlements — a ritual touching of the bark, a spoken word, an offering of water poured into a furrow.

---

The strangeness of the Expanse is not in any single thing. It is in the accumulation. It is in the way the light behaves near the Pillars — refracted through the columns of Swarm-Wings, diffused by the aerial root-curtains, reflected off the condensation-beaded crust — so that the air itself seems to have texture, to be thick with visible currents and eddies, like looking through water. It is in the way the sound changes as you move around a Pillar, the drone of the Swarm-Wings modulated by the Pillar's own shape, so that standing in one spot you hear a chord, and walking fifty paces you hear a different chord, and the Pillar is singing, has always been singing, a song made of millions of small voices and the wind in its furrows and the creak of its own slow growth.

It is in the way the crust sometimes moves. Not much. Not quickly. But if you lie on the ground and press your ear to it, you can feel a pulse — slow, deep, arrhythmic — that might be the Pillars' roots shifting in the rock below, or might be something else, something that lives in the deep substrate, something that the Pillars themselves grow from, or feed, or are part of. The people have a word for this pulse. The word translates roughly as "the dreaming." They say the Expanse is asleep, and that the Pillars are its dreams made solid, and that the creatures are its thoughts, and that the people are — what? They disagree. Some say the people are the Expanse's awareness of itself. Some say they are a fever. Some say they are nothing, and the Expanse does not know they exist, and this is the most frightening possibility of all.

---

The weather of the Expanse is its own. The plateau is so vast that it generates its own pressure systems, its own winds, its own rain. The rain comes rarely — the water parameter is low, the dampness is mostly condensation — but when it comes, it comes as a storm that builds over hours, the clouds massing along the Pillar-crowns, the air pressure dropping until your ears pop and the Swarm-Wings fall silent for the first time in your memory, and then the rain hits, and it is not gentle. It is a deluge, a sudden drowning, water sheeting off the Pillar-bark in rivers, the crust drinking and drinking and then saturating, and for a few hours the Expanse is a wet world, a dripping world, and the creatures shelter in the furrows and the people huddle in their root-fiber shelters and listen to the roar of water on resin roofs, and then it stops, and the heat returns, and within an hour the ground is dry again, and within two the condensation cycle begins anew, and the Expanse has returned to what it always was.

---

The erosion is old. The Pillars are old. The furrows in their bark are not just texture — they are history, channels carved by millennia of water and wind and the slow grinding of Swarm-Wing wings against the surface. Some of the Pillars have cavities so large that they are caves, and in these caves the air is cooler and damper, and different creatures live — pale things, slow things, things that have never seen the sun. The walls of these caves are smooth, polished by time and moisture, and in some of them there are marks that might be natural and might not — patterns that repeat, spirals and lattices, that could be mineral deposits or could be something else. The people do not go into the deep caves. They say the dreaming is stronger there. They say the pulse is louder. They say that if you go too deep, you can hear the Expanse thinking, and that its thoughts are not for minds as small as theirs.

---

This is the Resonant Expanse. A high, hot, vast place where the plants are the world and the world is alive and the life is loud and the loudness is a kind of silence if you stay long enough. A place where the distance between things is as important as the things themselves. A place where the people are small and the structures are small and the marks are faint, and the Pillars are enormous and the creatures are numberless and the sound never stops, and the ground breathes, and the air shimmers, and the light is too clear, and the whole place hums with a frequency that you feel in your bones, that you feel in your teeth, that you feel in the space behind your eyes where thoughts begin.

A place that has been here a long time.

A place that will be here longer.
