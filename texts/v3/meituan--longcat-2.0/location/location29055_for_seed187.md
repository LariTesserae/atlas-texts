# The Stillworks

---

You arrive at the edge of a low, wide basin — though *basin* implies a bowl, and this is more like a shallow depression worn into the world's oldest surface. The rim is low, barely a suggestion of rising ground, and the interior spreads out perhaps three hundred meters across, flat as something planed. Flat as something *given up* trying to be anything else.

The ground beneath your feet is not ground. It is a surface — a skin — a continuous living membrane of compressed plant matter so ancient it has darkened to the color of wet slate, smooth as worn bone, cool to the touch. You can feel that it has *give*, that it is not mineral, that beneath it there is hollow space. You press your heel and the surface dimples, holds, slowly rises back. It has been here so long that it has forgotten it is alive, and you have forgotten that alive can mean this.

---

The vegetation here is total. This is the first thing you understand and the last thing you can fully explain. Every surface, every structure, every visible plane is plant matter — but it does not grow *upward*. It has had, it seems, all of time to decide what shape to take, and it has chosen to spread, to flatten, to become architecture without architects, to become substrate that is also the thing itself. Low buttressing walls of compressed fiber rise in places to knee height, to waist height, curving into archways and channels that lead nowhere and everywhere. The material is like wood, like cartilage, like dried moss — layered, laminated, showing centuries of growth rings so fine they read as texture rather than pattern. In some places the walls are thick as your arm; in others they are gossamer, translucent membranes through which light passes greenish and diffuse. The air inside them is cool, still, faintly damp.

---

And this is the strangeness: the basin is almost entirely *empty*. The plant structures exist as a delicate lattice — walls, struts, arches, honeycombs, vaulting sheets — but the space *between* them is vast. You stand in a cathedral of living material that is mostly void. The density is near-nothing. You can see clear across the basin through gaps in the latticework, sightlines opening and closing as you shift your head, and what you see is more lattice, more emptiness, more of the same low, flattened architecture of compressed vegetable matter stretching to the far rim. The place is a single organism — you are increasingly certain of this — but an organism that has decided its body will be almost entirely *space*. That the living walls will enclose volumes they do not fill. That it will be a machine made of rooms.

---

And it *is* a machine. This is the other thing that takes time to hear.

The sound is enormous. It fills the basin completely, a layered drone and clatter and hiss and rhythmic thud that seems to come from the walls themselves, from the ground, from the air. You follow it and find its source: the organism is *processing*. In the deeper sections of the lattice, where the walls thicken into chambers, you see (and hear) the mechanisms. There are vessels — great bulbous cisterns of thin membrane, filled with dark fluid, connected by valved channels to thinner tubes that branch and branch again into the finest capillary networks. The fluid moves. It is pumped by rhythmic contractions of the vessel walls — slow, powerful, industrial in their regularity. Each contraction sends a pulse you can hear as a deep, resonant *thoom* that travels through the structure and re-emerges as a metallic ringing somewhere across the basin. The whole place is plumbing. The whole place is a refinery.

There are bellows too — expandable chambers high in the lattice that inflate and deflate with a sound like forge-work, pushing air through channels, creating drafts that move through the empty spaces at ground level. You feel them pass: cool, damp gusts that smell of mineral water and old chlorophyll. Condensation forms on the interior walls of these air channels, beading and running in thin streams down to collection points — small basins worn into the membrane surface, where the water pools and is drawn back into the vascular system. The organism recycles its moisture with the efficiency of something that has been doing it for geological ages. The dampness you feel — 0.9 on whatever scale matters — is not weather. It is *exhalation*. The place breathes out moisture as a byproduct of its internal industry, and the air is never quite dry, never quite wet, just perpetually on the edge of condensation. Your skin prickles with it. Your clothes grow faintly tacky.

---

The light is ordinary. It comes from above — sky, you assume, though you cannot see it directly through the lattice overhead, only catch it in shafts and diffused pools. It is the light of a mild overcast day, or perhaps the light of a place that has been under cloud for so long that the cloud has become normal. It is enough to see by. It is enough that the thin membranes glow faintly green where it passes through them, and the thicker structures cast soft shadows that never quite resolve into darkness. The basin exists in a permanent gentle noon.

---

The fauna is abundant but you have to *look* for it, or rather you have to understand what you are seeing. The creatures are the inhabitants of the Stillworks, and they are many, and they are *small*. Beetles — but beetles like polished brass gears, their carapaces reflecting the green light — crawl the interior walls in slow, deliberate processions, following channels worn into the membrane. Worm-things, pale and fibrous as the plant material itself, burrow just beneath the surface, visible as slow ripples in the skin of the floor. Flying things — something between moths and thistledown — drift through the empty spaces on the currents from the bellows, hundreds of them, thousands, filling the air with a constant soft particulate motion like dust in a sunbeam, except the beams are green and the dust is alive.

And there are larger presences. In the deepest part of the basin, where the lattice is thickest and the walls rise to nearly head height, you find *them* — the tenders, or the parasites, or the organs; you cannot tell. They are the size of dogs, low to the ground, covered in a pelt of fine root-hairs that match the color and texture of the substrate so precisely they are nearly invisible until they move. They have too many legs, or perhaps they have limbs that split and rejoin; they move with the same mechanical regularity as the pumps, and you realize after watching them for some time that they are *part of the system*. They interface with the walls at specific points — pressing their bodies against membrane panels that recede at their touch, reaching into openings with specialized limbs, performing maintenance or feeding or something between the two. They are fauna. They are also function. The distinction has been eroded away along with everything else.

---

The temperature is cool. Not cold — cool the way a root cellar is cool, the way deep shade is cool. The organism generates no heat of its own that you can feel; its processes are slow, enzymatic, efficient. The dampness amplifies the coolness. You are aware of your own warmth in this place, of the small disturbance your body makes in the equilibrium. Your breath does not visibly condense, but it wants to.

---

The erosion is 2.9 and you understand this now. This place has been wearing down for longer than it makes sense to say. Every edge has been rounded. Every surface has been smoothed. The original vigor of growth — whatever it was, however tall and wild and vertical the organism once reached — has been flattened by time into this low, stable, final form. There are no peaks. There are no dramatic structures. Everything curves gently. Everything meets the ground at a shallow angle. The basin itself may have once been a mountain; the organism may have once been a forest. Now it is this: a worn, low, cool, damp, loud, living machine in a state so close to equilibrium that it might as well have stopped.

And yet it has not stopped. It will never stop. The pumps pump. The bellows breathe. The tenders tend. The moths drift. The beetles walk their channels. The water cycles. The sound continues — that vast, layered, industrial-organic drone that is the voice of a process so old it has become geology, so stable it has become silence's opposite, so eternal that it cannot imagine its own ending.

---

You stand in the center of the basin and turn slowly. The lattice surrounds you at every distance — near walls you could touch, far walls that fade into a greenish haze of depth and membrane and empty air. The sound comes from everywhere. The ground is alive and gives slightly underfoot. The air moves in slow, damp pulses. The creatures go about their ancient business. The light does not change. Nothing changes. The Stillworks works, and has always worked, and will work until the world beneath it erodes past even this, the flattest thing left, the last living surface, still pumping, still breathing, still loud and cool and damp and empty and full.
