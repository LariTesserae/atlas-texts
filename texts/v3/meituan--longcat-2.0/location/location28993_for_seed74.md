# The Sunken Quarter of Mirehollow

---

## Arrival

You descend through a break in the canopy — not a dramatic cliff, but a gradual folding of the land, as if the earth here simply exhaled and didn't inhale again. The path narrows between two enormous living buttresses, their bark silver-grey and slick with the perpetual mist that clings to this low basin. The sound changes as you drop. Above, wind and the distant complaint of engines. Below, dripping. Always dripping.

Mirehollow sits in a shallow depression, perhaps a kilometer across, ringed by the roots of trees so large they constitute a wall. Not a forest wall — a *settlement* wall. The roots have been shaped, or have shaped themselves, into terraces and channels and the suggestion of streets. This is the first thing you understand: the plants here are not growing *around* the town. They *are* the town.

---

## The Living Architecture

The structures of Mirehollow are grown, not built — though "grown" implies a passivity that misrepresents the relationship. The people here (and there are people, though you may not see them immediately) have been cultivating these forms for generations. The primary building material is a species of woody vine with a dense, pale heartwood that hardens to something like cork but with the tensile strength of seasoned oak. It grows in spirals. Left to itself, it produces hollow columns. Guided — trained along frames of older, dead wood, pinned with copper staples gone green with patina — it produces walls, arches, floors.

The largest structures are the **Stilts**: living trees, or perhaps a single organism with many trunks, that rise fifteen meters from the basin floor. Their canopies merge overhead into a continuous roof of broad, waxy leaves that shed water outward, away from the settlement. Beneath this roof, the Stilts' trunks have been hollowed — carefully, over decades — into workshops, storage rooms, living quarters. The wood still lives. You can see the slow pulse of sap if you press your palm to the inner walls. The rooms are warm. Not hot — this is a cool place — but warmer than the air outside, as if the building is breathing.

Between the Stilts, the spaces are open. This is the sparseness. Mirehollow is not dense. There are gaps — courtyards, really, some no larger than a room, some the size of a small plaza — where the ground is visible and the mist collects in shallow pools. The pools are not stagnant. They connect, through channels cut into the root-mass, to a slow circulation system that feeds the lower terraces and eventually drains out through the eastern wall of roots into whatever lies beyond the basin.

---

## The Damp

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It does not rain often here — the canopy sees to that — but the air is heavy with moisture, and condensation is constant. The leaves above weep. The roots weep. The stone (there is some stone, old and dark, used for foundations and the bases of the industrial structures) is always slick. You will not find a dry surface in Mirehollow. Even the interiors of the Stilts carry a film of moisture on their walls, and the people who live there have developed a culture of textiles — woven from a fibrous inner bark — that wicks and breathes and never quite dries but never quite rots.

The water has a taste. Mineral. Slightly sweet. It carries the faintest trace of something organic — not decay, but *life*. The taste of sap, perhaps, or of the mycelial networks that thread through every surface in this place. The mycelium is visible in places: pale, hair-thin filaments running along the undersides of roots, across the floors of the lower terraces, threading through the walls of the older structures like veins. It is not parasitic. It is structural. The people of Mirehollow call it **the Lace**, and they tend it as carefully as they tend the vines.

---

## The Industrial Quarter

On the western side of the basin, the character changes. Here the living architecture gives way — not entirely, but partially — to something harder. The **Foundry Terrace** is built on a platform of packed earth and stone, raised half a meter above the waterline, and it houses the settlement's industrial works.

The machinery is old. Not ancient — you can see the influence of modern engineering, the clean lines of cast iron and steel — but *settled*. These machines have been here long enough that the plants have begun to reclaim them. Vines thread through gear housings. Moss fills the gaps between bolted plates. A large steam-driven pump — the heart of the water circulation system — sits in a housing that is half stone, half living wood, its chimney rising through a gap in the canopy where the leaves have been trained into a funnel to direct smoke upward.

The Foundry produces two things: **processed fiber** (the inner bark of the Stilts, stripped and beaten and woven into the textiles that are Mirehollow's primary trade good) and **sap extract** (a thick, amber liquid tapped from the deeper roots, used as a sealant, a medicine, and — in small quantities — a stimulant). The work is mechanized but not automated. People operate the machines. The rhythm is steady, not frantic. The sound is low: the chuff of the pump, the rhythmic thump of the beating hammers, the hiss of steam. It carries across the basin but does not dominate. The mist absorbs sound. The leaves absorb sound. Mirehollow is, despite its industry, a quiet place.

---

## The Ecology

The plants here are not diverse. This is a monoculture, or near to it — a single dominant species (the Stilt-trees and their associated vines) with a handful of understory species that have adapted to the low light and constant moisture. **Lungmoss** covers every horizontal surface in a thick, spongy carpet that ranges from deep green to almost black. **Drip-ferns** hang from the lower branches of the canopy, their fronds long and narrow, each tip forming a point where condensation gathers and falls. **Ghost-caps** — small, pale mushrooms with translucent flesh — cluster at the bases of the Stilts, feeding on the Lace.

The fauna is sparse. You will not see birds — the canopy is too thick, the space too enclosed. Insects exist but are not numerous; the damp keeps their populations in check, and the Ghost-caps produce a mild insecticide that permeates the air. The largest animal you are likely to encounter is the **root-runner**, a lizard-like creature the length of a forearm that moves through the channels and pools with an unsettling, fluid grace. It is blind. It navigates by vibration. It feeds on the Lace and is, in turn, fed upon by the people — its flesh is said to taste of the sap extract, sweet and faintly numbing.

There are fish in the deeper pools. Small, pale, slow. They are not fished. They are considered part of the water system, not separate from it.

---

## The People

You will see them, eventually. They move through the settlement with the unhurried pace of people who have lived in the same place for a long time and see no reason to rush. Their clothing is the woven bark-fiber — loose, layered, in shades of cream and grey and the pale green of new growth. Their hands are stained amber from the sap extract. Their skin carries the cool, slightly damp quality of the place itself.

They are not unfriendly. They are *attentive*. They notice you the way they notice a change in the water level or a new shoot on a Stilt — with interest, with assessment, but without alarm. Mirehollow receives visitors. Not many. But some.

They will offer you tea. It is made from dried Ghost-cap and hot water drawn from the pump's outflow. It tastes of earth and something faintly electric. It will make your fingers tingle.

---

## The Rules

There are rules here, though they are not written. They are grown into the place.

**Do not cut the Lace.** The mycelial network is load-bearing. Sever it, and walls weaken. Floors sag. The Stilts sicken. This is not metaphor. The people of Mirehollow have seen it happen.

**Do not take sap from the upper roots.** The deep roots produce the extract. The upper roots produce something else — something that smells like honey and causes, in sufficient concentration, a paralysis that is not fatal but is deeply unpleasant. The distinction is taught to children before they are taught to walk.

**Do not light fires above ground.** The canopy is dry enough, in its upper reaches, to burn. All combustion happens on the Foundry Terrace, in the stone-lined furnaces, under watch.

**Do not speak loudly near the pools.** The root-runners are sensitive to vibration, and when agitated, they release a pheromone that attracts — something. The people do not discuss what. They simply keep their voices low near the water.

---

## The Atmosphere

The light is diffuse. The canopy filters it into a green-gold wash that does not change much through the day — there is no direct sun here, only the general brightening and dimming that marks the passage of hours. Shadows are soft. Edges are soft. The mist softens everything further, so that the settlement appears to you as a series of impressions rather than sharp forms: the dark columns of the Stilts, the pale haze of the Lace, the glint of water in the channels, the slow movement of figures between the terraces.

The air smells of wet wood and sap and the faint, clean bitterness of the Ghost-caps. Underneath that, something older — the smell of deep root systems, of earth that has not seen sunlight in centuries, of a place that has been exactly what it is for a very long time and intends to continue.

The sound is water. Dripping, flowing, the slow gurgle of the circulation system, the occasional deeper note of the pump. And beneath that, so low you feel it more than hear it, the hum of the Stilts themselves — the vibration of living wood, of sap moving, of a root system that extends far beyond the basin in every direction, connecting this small, damp, improbable settlement to something much larger and much older that you cannot see and cannot name.

---

## The Dynamic

Mirehollow is not static. The Stilts grow — slowly, but measurably. The settlement must be pruned, guided, reshaped. The Lace spreads and must be trimmed back from areas where it is not wanted. The water system requires constant adjustment as the root channels shift and the pools deepen or silt. The Foundry's machinery wears and is repaired with parts that are themselves grown — replacement gears carved from dense heartwood, bearings packed with sap extract, drive belts of woven fiber that stretch and are tightened and stretch again.

There is a rhythm to it. Not a daily rhythm — the light does not change enough for that — but a seasonal one. In the wet season (which is most of the year, here in the basin), the water rises and the lower terraces flood and the people move upward, into the higher rooms of the Stilts. In the brief dry season, the water recedes and the lower terraces are cleaned and repaired and the Ghost-caps fruit in enormous quantities, and there is a harvest, and there is a quiet celebration that involves the root-runner meat and the sap extract and songs that are sung in voices kept low out of respect for the pools.

The place is alive. Not metaphorically. The settlement is a living system — plant and fungus and water and machine and people, all intertwined, all dependent, all in motion. It has been this way for longer than anyone remembers. It will be this way, with patience and care, for longer still.

You are standing in it now. The mist is on your skin. The Lace is threading, slowly, across the sole of your boot. Somewhere below you, water is moving through channels older than your language, and the Stilts are growing, and the pump is chuffing, and the people are watching, and the place is exactly what it is.

Welcome to Mirehollow.
