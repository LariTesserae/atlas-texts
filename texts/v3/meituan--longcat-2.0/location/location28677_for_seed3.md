# The Highworks

---

You arrive by the long switchback road that climbs through cloud, and for the last hour of ascent there has been no other traffic. The air thins and cools. Your ears pop. The road surface changes from asphalt to something like sintered ceramic, pale grey, warm to the touch even in the chill — it hums faintly, a low frequency you feel in your sternum before you hear it. The cloud closes around you. Visibility drops to thirty metres, then twenty, then ten. The headlights catch moisture on every surface: the road, the rock walls to either side, the guardrail that isn't metal but something denser, something that rings like a bell when you strike it and the note sustains for far too long.

Then you break through.

---

The sky above the cloud layer is not what you expected. At this elevation — and you are high, absurdly high, higher than any city has a right to be — the sky should be a hard dark blue, almost violet, the sun a fierce and lonely point. Instead the sky is the colour of old pewter, diffuse and depthless, as though someone has stretched a membrane across the top of the world. The light that comes through is dim, directionless, shadowless. It does not illuminate so much as *admit*. You can see, but you cannot say where the light is coming from. There are no shadows beneath the structures. There are no shadows beneath *you*.

The cloud layer below is not white. It is a deep, bruised ochre, and it moves — slowly, so slowly — in a vast clockwise spiral that you can only perceive by fixing your gaze on a single point and waiting. Minutes pass. The cloud turns. It is the largest slow thing you have ever seen, and it is turning like soup in a bowl, and at the centre of the spiral, directly below the city, there is a darkness that might be a hole.

---

The Highworks sits on a plateau that should not exist.

The geology here is *young*. That is the first thing the surveyors noted, and it is the first thing you feel underfoot: the rock is unweathered, uneroded, sharp-edged in a way that mountain stone never is. The plateau is not a worn-down remnant of some older, taller range. It is not the product of uplift and exposure and the patient work of wind and water. It is *emerging*. The strata are visible in the cliff faces to the north — not the compressed, metamorphosed layers of ancient seabeds, but something that looks almost sedimentary in the act of becoming. You can see bands of material that haven't decided what they are yet: not quite stone, not quite metal, not quite glass. They catch the dim light and hold it, faintly luminous, as though the rock itself is generating a small amount of warmth and radiance from some slow internal process that has nothing to do with the sun.

The erosion here is embryonic. There are no valleys carved by rivers, no scree slopes, no glacial polish. The water that exists — and there is water, though not much — does not run in channels. It *appears*. Condensation forms on every surface in the early hours, beading on the ceramic roadways, on the walls of buildings, on your skin, and it is slightly viscous, slightly warm, and it tastes of copper and ozone. It does not flow downhill so much as it *gathers*, pooling in shallow depressions that have no business being as regular as they are — almost circular, almost deliberate, as though the rock is slowly learning how to hold water and hasn't quite mastered the geometry of it. After an hour in the sun — the dim, sourceless sun — the pools evaporate, and the surfaces are dry again, and the air is damp, and the cycle begins again.

It rains perhaps once a week. The rain is warm. It falls from a sky that, moments before, held no clouds.

---

The city itself is built into and onto the plateau in a way that suggests the builders understood the rock was not finished. The foundations do not sit *on* the stone; they are *fused* with it. The oldest structures — the smelting works, the compression towers, the long low buildings that might be factories or might be something else — have bases that blur into the plateau floor, the transition from worked material to raw geology so gradual that you cannot say where construction ends and mountain begins. The rock around these buildings has a different texture: denser, darker, with a faint geometric grain, as though the presence of human industry has taught the stone something about right angles.

The newer construction is more conventional — steel frame, concrete panel, glass — but even here the buildings have a quality of *leaning in*, of pressing close to the rock face, as though they are listening to something. The streets are narrow and steep, following the natural contours of the plateau, and they are loud. The sound is industrial and constant: the deep thrum of engines somewhere below, the clatter of machinery in the factories that line the eastern ridge, the hiss of steam from vents in the roadway, the rhythmic bang-bang-bang of a press or hammer that never stops, that has perhaps never stopped, that you feel in your teeth and your fingertips and the soles of your feet. The sound does not echo. The plateau absorbs it, or the strange sky does, or the rock does — the noise is loud but contained, pressing against you from all sides without bouncing back, a wall of sound that is also somehow intimate.

The power plant is at the city's centre, and it is the loudest thing here. It is a cluster of tall cylindrical structures — cooling towers, perhaps, though they do not steam — connected by a web of pipes and conduits that pulse with a deep orange light. The light is visible through the pipes, through the walls of the buildings, through the ground itself if you look down at the right angle: a network of faint orange veins running through the ceramic roadways, through the rock, converging on the plant like a circulatory system. The plant burns something. No one will tell you what. The fuel arrives in sealed containers on the switchback road, and the containers are always warm, and they hum at the same frequency as the road itself.

---

The vegetation is lush where it exists, and it exists in the gaps.

Between the buildings, in the narrow strips of ground where the rock has cracked or been broken, plant life grows with a vigour that seems impossible at this elevation, in this dim light, in soil that is barely soil. Ferns — enormous ferns, the size of small trees, their fronds a deep, almost black green — crowd the spaces between walls. Moss covers every north-facing surface in a thick, velvety carpet that is startlingly soft and slightly warm to the touch, as though it is composting something beneath it. There are flowers: small, white, five-petalled, growing in clusters from cracks in the rock, and they open and close on a cycle that does not correspond to the light, because the light does not change. They open for approximately four hours, then close for approximately four hours, and the interval between is not day or night but something else, some rhythm internal to the plateau itself.

But the vegetation is sparse in its distribution. Between the green pockets there are vast stretches of bare rock, bare road, bare wall. The city is mostly mineral. The plant life appears in scattered, concentrated bursts — a courtyard full of ferns, a rooftop garden gone feral, a crack in the cliff face from which a whole ecosystem of moss and flower and tiny, tiny insects erupts — and then nothing for a block, two blocks, half a kilometre. The effect is of a city that is being slowly, selectively colonised, as though the vegetation is testing the ground, finding the places where it can take hold, and leaving the rest bare.

The trees — and there are trees, though not many — grow at angles. Not bent by wind; there is no wind here, or almost none. They grow *toward* something. Their trunks curve, their branches reach, and if you map the direction of their growth, they all point toward the centre of the plateau, toward the power plant, toward the place where the orange light pulses strongest.

---

The animals are everywhere, and they are wrong in ways that are easy to overlook until you've been here a while.

The most common are the skitterers — small, six-legged, furred, about the size of a rat, with large dark eyes and a chirping call that is almost but not quite like a bird. They move in loose groups along the walls of buildings, in the spaces beneath the roadways, in the ventilation shafts of the factories. They are bold. They will approach you. They will sit on the ground a metre away and watch you with an attention that feels evaluative, as though they are deciding something about you. They eat the moss, the flowers, the small white insects that cluster around the condensation pools. They also eat things that are not organic. You will see a skitterer sitting on a pipe, gnawing at the metal, and the metal will show tooth marks, and the skitterer will pause and look at you, and its eyes will reflect the dim light with a faint orange tint that matches the light in the pipes.

There are larger animals. The cliff goats — and they are called goats, though they are not goats — move along the plateau's edges in herds of twenty or thirty. They are the size of large dogs, with thick grey fur, short legs, and heads that are too flat, too wide, with eyes set too far apart and mouths that open sideways rather than vertically. They graze on the moss and the rock itself, and their teeth make a sound like a file on stone. They are not afraid of the drop. They walk along ledges that are centimetres wide at elevations that would kill a human in minutes, and they do it slowly, deliberately, with a patience that seems geological.

There are birds, or things that fly. They are most active at what the locals call the dimming — the period, roughly every eight hours, when the already-faint light fades further, and the sky takes on a quality of deep grey velvet, and the temperature drops by two or three degrees, and the condensation forms. The flyers emerge from crevices in the cliff face: winged, feathered, but with too many wings — four, six, eight — and bodies that are too long, too flexible, that undulate in flight like ribbons. They do not sing. They produce a low, resonant hum that harmonises with the sound of the power plant, and when they fly in groups — and they do, in groups of hundreds, spiralling upward into the strange sky — the combined hum is so loud it drowns out the factories, and the city goes quiet in comparison, and the people stop and look up, and the flyers spiral higher and higher until they are specks, until they are nothing, until they have gone somewhere that is not the sky.

---

The people of the Highworks do not talk about the sky.

They talk about the work. The factories process something that comes up from below — raw material, ore, something that arrives in the sealed containers and is fed into the machines and is transformed into something that goes back down the switchback road in different containers. The work is shift-based, three shifts, eight hours each, and the shifts do not correspond to day or night because there is no day or night, only the dim and the dimmer and the dimmest, and the people work when the schedule says they work, and they sleep when the schedule says they sleep, and the skitterers watch them from the walls, and the flyers hum, and the power plant pulses, and the cloud below turns, and the rock beneath the city continues its slow process of becoming.

The people are not unhappy. They are not happy. They are *occupied*. They have the look of people who have decided that the best response to a strange place is to work in it, to build in it, to maintain the machines and the roads and the buildings and to not ask certain questions. The questions they do not ask are: Why does the rock glow? Why do the trees grow toward the plant? Why do the flyers leave and where do they go? Why does the cloud turn? What is at the centre of the spiral? What is the city built on top of?

These questions are not forbidden. They are simply *uninteresting*. The people of the Highworks have a relationship with their environment that is pragmatic to the point of being philosophical: the world is what it is, and it is doing what it is doing, and the work must continue, and the damp will come and go, and the skitterers will watch, and the flyers will hum, and the light will be dim, and the rock will be warm, and the road will hum, and the containers will arrive, and the machines will run, and the city will stand on its plateau at the top of the world, and the cloud below will turn, and turn, and turn.

---

But there are things that even the pragmatists notice.

The way a building that was three storeys tall last month is four storeys tall today, and no construction was done, and the fourth storey is made of the same material as the rock beneath the city, and it has windows that look out onto streets that don't exist yet.

The way the condensation pools, if you watch them long enough, begin to show reflections of things that are not above them — structures, landscapes, skies that are not the pewter sky of the Highworks, and the reflections are clear and detailed and they move, and they are showing you somewhere else, or somewhen else, or something else entirely.

The way the skitterers, when they gather in large numbers — hundreds of them, thousands, covering a wall or a roadway in a living carpet of fur and eyes — begin to move in patterns. Not random. Not foraging. *Patterns*. Geometric, precise, repeating, and the patterns are not decorative. They are *functional*. They are doing something to the surface beneath them. They are etching something into the rock, or the metal, or the road, and the etching glows faintly orange for a moment and then fades, and the skitterers disperse, and the surface is unchanged, or almost unchanged, or changed in a way that you can only see from a certain angle, in a certain light, and the light is always dim.

The way the cliff goats, when they stand at the very edge of the plateau and look down into the turning cloud, sometimes step forward, and do not fall, and walk on the air, or on something that is not air, and their hooves make a sound — a faint, bell-like ringing — on a surface that isn't there, and they walk in a slow spiral downward, following the spiral of the cloud, and they disappear into the ochre murk, and they do not come back, and the herd does not seem to notice their absence.

The way the flyers, when they return from wherever they go — and they do return, eventually, folding themselves back into the crevices in the cliff face — sometimes carry things in their mouths. Small things. Bright things. Things that glitter and hum and pulse with the same orange light as the power plant, and they carry them into the crevices, and the crevices glow for a moment, and then the glow fades, and the flyers are still.

---

The Highworks is not a finished place.

This is the thing that becomes clear the longer you stay, the thing that the pragmatists know but do not say, the thing that the rock and the water and the light and the animals are all quietly, slowly, patiently demonstrating: the city is not built on a foundation. The city is built on a *process*. The plateau is still rising. The rock is still forming. The geology is still deciding what it wants to be, and the city is part of that decision, and the factories and the machines and the people and the skitterers and the flyers and the cliff goats are all part of it, and the process is slow, so slow, geological in its patience, and it has been going on for longer than the city has existed, and it will continue long after the city is gone, and the cloud below will turn, and the spiral will tighten, and the centre of the spiral — the darkness at the centre of the spiral — will do whatever it is going to do when the process is complete.

The damp will come and go. The light will be dim. The sound will be loud. The rock will be warm.

The Highworks will continue becoming whatever it is becoming.

And the road will hum, and the containers will arrive, and the machines will run, and the people will work, and the skitterers will watch, and the flyers will hum, and the cliff goats will walk on nothing into the cloud, and the trees will grow toward the light, and the flowers will open and close on their own slow rhythm, and the pools will show you reflections of places that are not here, and the buildings will grow new storeys in the night, and the rock will glow, and the sky will hold, and the world at the top of the world will be patient, and strange, and loud, and dim, and damp, and warm, and unfinished.

And it will be, for now, enough.
