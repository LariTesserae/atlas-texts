The wind is the first thing. Not the first thing you notice — the only thing. It has been blowing since before the stone beneath your feet had a shape, and it will be blowing after that stone has been scoured to nothing. It moves at the speed of a sustained shout, sometimes faster, and it never stops. It has never stopped. The sound is not a howl or a moan or a whistle, because those words imply variation. This is a single, unbroken, full-spectrum roar — the noise of air moving over rock at the limits of what rock can endure. It is so loud that after a few hours you stop hearing it as sound and start feeling it as pressure, as a physical thing pressing against your eardrums and your sinuses and the backs of your teeth.

You are standing on a high alpine plateau, but "plateau" is too gentle a word. This is a place that the rest of the world forgot to continue building upward, so it simply stopped — a shelf of dark, fine-grained stone suspended between the last ragged teeth of a mountain range and the hard white glare of a sky that seems too close. The elevation is extreme in the way that makes your lungs work twice as hard for half the air, where each breath feels thin and insufficient, where your lips crack and your eyes dry out and the moisture on the surface of your eyeballs threatens to evaporate before tears can form. The light is extraordinary — a flat, shadowless, overwhelming brightness that comes from everywhere at once, from the sun and the sky and the pale stone and the high thin clouds that race past without ever seeming to break. There is no haze. There is no softness. Every surface is edged in razor clarity. You can see a hundred kilometers in any direction and everything you see is wind and stone.

The stone is dark — a deep charcoal grey, almost black in the recesses, shot through with veins of something lighter, milky or rust-colored, that catch the light and gleam. It has been shaped by the wind into forms that are smooth and curved and strange: long sinuous ridges, deep bowl-shaped scoops, pillars and fins and delicate arches that look impossible but stand anyway, the hard grain of the rock holding against the soft persistence of the air. The erosion here is not dramatic. There are no fresh fractures, no loose scree piles, no evidence of recent collapse. Everything has been worn to its final form and then held there, suspended in a state of perfect equilibrium between the force of the wind and the strength of the stone. The surfaces are polished. Some of them are polished to a mirror finish, and when the angle is right you can see the sky reflected in a slab of dark rock and it looks like a window into a second, inverted world where the wind blows upward.

Water exists here in the way that ghosts exist — not present, but detectable. The air is damp in a way that has nothing to do with humidity and everything to do with speed; the wind carries moisture ripped from distant valleys and lakes and seas, and when that fast-moving air hits the still air in the lee of a rock formation, the moisture condenses instantly, coating every sheltered surface in a film of cold water. Walk into the wind shadow of a large boulder and you will feel the air temperature drop ten degrees in a step. The water beads on the stone, runs in thin threads down the polished surfaces, collects in the bowl-shaped depressions that dot the plateau like craters on a pale moon. Some of these pools are no bigger than your hand. Others are ten meters across and deep enough to swallow your arm to the shoulder. The water in them is black and still and so cold it hurts, and they have no inlet and no outlet — they are fed entirely by condensation, and they evaporate at exactly the rate they fill, and they have been doing this for so long that the stone beneath them has been worn into perfect smooth concavities, as if the water itself is a cutting tool.

The vegetation is present but barely — a thin, tough, desperate life that exists in the margins. In the deepest wind shadows, where the air moves slow enough to hold a shape, there are patches of something like moss but harder, darker, more mineral than plant, that grows flat against the stone in patterns that look almost like writing. There are lichens in pale orange and pale green that spread across the rock surfaces in slow, centuries-long fronts, consuming the mineral grain by grain and converting it into color. There is a kind of grass — if it can be called grass — that grows in tight, low, dark-green hummocks no taller than your thumb, each hummock rooted in a crack so small you can barely see it, each one a tiny island of life in a sea of wind and stone. The hummocks are spaced far apart — twenty, thirty, fifty meters between each one — and from any vantage point the landscape looks empty, barren, a desert of dark rock and bright light. But walk the distances between them and you begin to see that the emptiness is an illusion. The plateau is dotted with life, each instance of it isolated, self-sufficient, enduring.

And the animals. The animals are everywhere, once you learn to see them.

They are birds, mostly — or bird-like things, because at this elevation the normal rules of taxonomy start to blur. There are large, dark-plumed riders that use the wind the way fish use currents, soaring in wide, effortless circles without ever once flapping their wings, their eyes fixed on the stone below, watching for the small movements that mean food. There are tiny, hyperactive flitters that live in the wind shadows and feed on the condensation insects — pale, soft-bodied things that emerge from cracks in the stone when the moisture comes and vanish back into the cracks when it dries. There are burrowers, too — small, round, fur-heavy creatures that live in tunnels they dig into the moss patches and emerge to feed on the lichens and the hummock-grass, their bodies so well-insulated against the cold that a thin rime of frost forms on their fur in the open air and doesn't melt even when they've retreated into the relative warmth of their dens.

The abundance of fauna is deceptive, because the density of the landscape is so low. The animals are spread thin across the plateau, each one occupying a vast personal territory, each one adapted to a life of extreme dispersal. You might walk for an hour and see nothing but wind and stone and the distant glint of a soaring bird. Then you round a rock formation and find yourself in a hollow where the air is still and the condensation pools are full and the hummock-grass grows thick, and suddenly there are birds everywhere — dozens of them, hundreds, bursting from the moss patches and the pool edges and the air itself, a brief, chaotic eruption of life in a place that had seemed utterly barren seconds before. Then you move on and the silence returns, and the wind, and the stone, and the empty distances.

The plateau extends in every direction to the limits of visibility, and beyond. It is not uniform — there are features, landmarks, places that differ from each other in ways that matter if you are trying to navigate or survive. To the north, the land rises slightly into a series of wind-carved fins that jut from the surface like the vertebrae of some enormous buried creature, each fin twenty meters high and a hundred meters long, the gaps between them channeling the wind into focused streams of even greater velocity. To the south, the plateau breaks into a series of stepped descents, each step a few meters high, each step worn smooth and curved by the wind, the whole formation looking like a giant's staircase laid on its side. To the east and west, the land simply continues, flat and open and endless, the dark stone stretching to the horizon under the white sky, broken only by the occasional cluster of boulders or the distant flash of a condensation pool catching the light.

There is nothing built here. No structures, no markers, no signs of construction or fabrication. The only works are the works of wind and water and time — the polished stone, the carved ridges, the deep bowls, the smooth concavities. If there are people who come here — and there must be, because this is a place that a person with simple tools and strong motivation could reach and survive — they leave no trace. They camp in the wind shadows and drink from the condensation pools and hunt the small, fur-heavy burrowers, and when they leave, the wind takes everything — their footprints, their fire scars, their waste — and the stone returns to what it was, which is nothing but itself, which is stone and wind and light and the slow, patient, ancient process of becoming less.

The stillness is the strangest thing. Not the lack of wind — there is no lack of wind, there will never be a lack of wind — but the lack of change. The wind blows at the same speed it blew yesterday and will blow tomorrow. The condensation pools fill and empty in the same rhythm they have followed for centuries. The birds ride the same currents, the burrowers dig in the same moss patches, the lichens spread at the same imperceptible rate. There are no seasons here, or rather, the seasons exist but they do not affect this place; the winter wind and the summer wind are the same wind, the winter light and the summer light are the same light. The temperature is always cool — never cold enough to freeze the condensation pools solid, never warm enough to make the wind feel like anything other than a cold, constant pressure against your skin. The only variable is the angle of the sun, and even that changes so slowly, so predictably, that it feels less like change and more like the turning of a very large, very slow, very indifferent clock.

Time moves differently here. You feel it after a few hours — the sense that the hours are not passing so much as accumulating, that each one adds a thin layer to the present moment without displacing what came before. The light at noon and the light at evening are almost identical. The wind at dawn and the wind at dusk are the same. The stone has been here since before there were eyes to see it and it will be here after the last eye closes, and in the meantime it simply endures, shaped and reshaped by the same forces, wearing away at a rate so slow it might as well be zero, a monument to persistence in a world that has forgotten what persistence means.

The weirdness is subtle. It is not the weirdness of the alien or the supernatural but the weirdness of a place that has been pushed so far past the normal parameters of landscape that it has started to feel like a place with its own logic, its own rules. The condensation pools that never overflow and never empty. The wind that never varies. The life that persists in conditions that should not support it. The stone that has been worn into shapes so smooth and so strange that they look intentional, as if the plateau is a sculpture that someone — or something — has been working on for a very long time and is not yet finished with. There is a feeling, standing here, that the place is watching you. Not with eyes, not with intention, but with the simple, implacable attention of something that has been paying attention for longer than your species has existed and will continue to pay attention long after your species is gone.

The light is leaving now. The sun is sinking toward the horizon and the shadows are deepening — actual shadows, for the first time all day, pooling in the wind-sculpted depressions and the gaps between the fins and the hollows behind the boulders. The temperature is dropping, though not by much; a degree, maybe two, a slight intensification of the chill that has been with you since you arrived. The wind is unchanged. The sound is unchanged. The stone is unchanged.

In the last light, the condensation pools turn from black to silver, and for a few minutes they catch the sun and throw it back in long, thin, horizontal lines that stretch across the plateau like cracks in a dark mirror. The birds settle onto the stone, folding their wings, becoming dark shapes against the dark stone. The burrowers retreat into their tunnels. The lichens and the moss and the hummock-grass close up, tighten, prepare for the night that is coming — not a true night, because at this elevation and this latitude the sun never fully abandons the sky, but a dimming, a softening, a brief reprieve from the full force of the light.

The wind blows. The stone endures. The place remains.
