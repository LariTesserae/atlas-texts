# The Loom-Caverns of Suth-Vael

---

It begins as a fissure — barely wide enough for a person to turn around in — opened by some recent geological shrug, no more than a few decades ago. The rock is still sharp here, not yet rounded by wind or water, and the edges catch on clothing and skin with the eager teeth of new things. The fissure descends at a slant, narrowing and widening like a throat, and the air changes as you move through it: cooler, wetter, carrying a mineral sweetness like wet limestone and the faint, sharp green of growing things in the dark.

You emerge into the pocket.

It is small — that is the first thing. The scale is intimate in a way that makes your skin tighten, as though the space itself is aware of you. Perhaps fifty paces across, perhaps less, a roughly oval cavern let into the earth like a secret folded into cloth. The ceiling is low enough that you could touch it if you reached up, and it glistens with condensation — a slow, constant weeping of water from the rock, each droplet forming with a patience that feels almost deliberate, swelling at the lip of a stalactite-that-isn't-quite-a-stalactite, falling, striking a surface below with a sound so small it exists only at the threshold of hearing.

The water is everywhere but nowhere you can name. It does not pool in rivers or lakes. It lives in the air, a perpetual haze of near-fog that hangs at chest height and makes every surface slick. It sheets down the walls in slow-moving curtains, gathering in the porous rock, feeding the roots that have found their way down from the world above through cracks too narrow for anything larger than a beetle. It drips from the ceiling in a rhythm that is almost, but not quite, regular — a syncopated percussion that the mind tries to resolve into pattern and cannot.

The cold is not the cold of ice. It is the cold of deep stone, of water that has never seen sun, of air that sits still and learns stillness. It is the kind of cold that seeps into fabric, into hair, into the spaces between your fingers, and stays. Not brutal. Patient.

And the dark. The dark is nearly absolute. Above, the fissure opening lets in a thin blade of gray light at midday — enough to tell you that somewhere up there, a world with sky still exists — but here, at the floor of the pocket, the light is merely a memory. A faint luminous suggestion that allows you to see the shapes of things without seeing the things themselves. Edges. Volumes. The pale gleam of water on surfaces.

But the dark is not empty.

---

The plants came after the opening. They always do. Some carried by water, some by the small mammals that found the fissure before anyone else, some by mechanisms that no one in Suth-Vael can explain and have stopped trying to. They came, and they grew, and they grew strangely.

They are the architecture here.

Not metaphorically. Not as decoration. The structures of the settlement — and there is a settlement, small and sparse but unmistakably organized — are living plants. Or rather, they are plants that have been persuaded, over generations, into the shapes of structures. Massive cordyceps-like fungal bodies, pale and woody, form the pillars and beams of the largest enclosures, their caps spreading overhead like roofs, their surfaces thick with a moss that seems to generate its own faint, greenish luminescence — not enough to read by, but enough to navigate, to see the outline of a hand, to know that you are inside rather than outside.

Vines of something like wisteria but thinner, more tensile, have been woven and trained across the frameworks to form walls — not solid walls, but membranes, breathing, swaying slightly in air currents you can barely feel. They filter the constant moisture from the air, and on their surfaces, communities of tiny bromeliads have taken root, each one cupping a small reservoir of water like a chalice, and in each reservoir, a microcosmos: larvae, rotifers, tiny crustaceans going about their lives in a thimble of cold water.

Between the structures, the ground is a mat of deep moss — spongy, ankle-deep, saturated — and through it, roots run in visible networks just beneath the surface, some as thick as a wrist, some fine as hair, all of them glistening with moisture. Walking on the moss is like walking on a living thing's breath. It compresses, sighs, and slowly rises back.

The settlement is sparse. You can cross it in a hundred paces. There are perhaps a dozen structures — enclosures, platforms, a few things that might be called rooms if rooms could be made of breathing green walls and floors that yield underfoot. They are scattered through the pocket with the irregular spacing of a forest that has not yet decided where to grow, and between them, open space — the emptiness is deliberate, or at least inhabited, because in the open spaces, the water collects more freely, and where the water collects, the moss thickens, and in the moss, things move.

---

The people of Suth-Vael — and there are people here, though you may not see them at first, and you may not see them at all — live in the way that people live when the world has told them very clearly that they are the least important thing in it.

They are small. Not in stature, necessarily, but in number, in footprint, in the gentle violence they do to the place. A few dozen, perhaps. They have built their settlement into the plant-architecture with a respect that looks, from the outside, like fear, but is something more practiced than that. They know which roots to cut and which to leave. They know which moss to harvest and which to step around. They know the seasons of the pocket — not the seasons of the world above, which are irrelevant here, but the internal seasons: the wet season, when the weeping from the ceiling intensifies and the air becomes so thick with moisture that you can taste it, and the dry season, when the condensation slows and the moss crisps at the edges and everything holds its breath.

Their tools are stone and bone and wood. They carve the soft fungal wood with implements made from the bones of the creatures that live here — and there are creatures here, creatures in such numbers and variety that the word *overwhelming* begins to feel like an understatement.

---

You hear them before you understand what you are hearing.

The pocket is mostly quiet — that is true. The dominant sound is the drip and weep of water, and beneath that, the sub-audible hum of growing things, a sound that is less heard than felt, a vibration in the floor, in the walls, in the bones of your inner ear. But within the quiet, there is movement, and the movement makes sound, and the sound is — at first — incomprehensible.

It is a clicking. A rustling. A dry, papery whisper, as though thousands of tiny pages are being turned simultaneously. It comes from the walls, from the ceiling, from the moss underfoot. It is the sound of fauna.

They are arthropods, mostly. Beetles of a kind that has no name above — small, dark-shelled, with legs that move in patterns too fast to track. They live in the moss, in the walls, in the water-cups of the bromeliads, in the spaces between the fungal pillars. They are the first fauna you notice, and they are everywhere, so numerous that the moss seems to be made of them rather than growing beneath them, and when you step, they scatter in a wave, a ripple of small dark bodies flowing around your feet like water around a stone, and then, just as quickly, they flow back, filling the space you have disturbed with their patient, clicking industry.

But they are not the most numerous.

That distinction belongs to the pale, many-legged things that live in the walls — centipede-like, but softer, paler, their bodies almost translucent in the faint bioluminescence. They move in columns along the root networks, following paths that have been worn into the moss by generations of passage, and they are the sanitation system of Suth-Vael, consuming dead plant matter, fungal spores, the shed exoskeletons of their beetle cousins, and — when the opportunity presents itself — the dead of any species, including their own, including, it is understood, the people.

The people do not bury their dead.

And there are others. Small, sightless fish in the deepest water-pools, their scales the color of nothing. Salamanders that climb the walls and hang from the ceiling by toes that secrete a sticky silk. Moths — or moth-like things — that do not eat, that live only a few days, that exist solely to reproduce, and that fill certain sections of the pocket in fluttering, silent clouds when the conditions are right, their wings so pale they are almost white, their bodies dusted with a powder that glows faintly when disturbed, so that a swarm of them looks like a cloud of sparks, like a thought trying to become light.

The people have a name for the moths. It translates, roughly, to *the forgetting*, because when the moths swarm, the darkness seems to lift — not into light, but into a kind of luminous confusion in which the edges of things blur and you lose track of where you are, and there are stories of people who followed the moths deeper into the pocket and did not come back, and of people who came back but were different, who spoke of a place deeper down where the roots converged and something lived that was not plant and not animal and not fungus but something older, something that the pocket grew around the way a tree grows around a nail.

---

The strangeness of the place is not any one thing. It is the accumulation of small wrongnesses.

The water, for instance. It should not be this clean. The pocket is a closed system, fed only by seepage and condensation, and yet the water is clear, almost sterile, as though something is filtering it with a precision that geology cannot explain. The people drink it without illness. They say the roots do it — the root network, which extends deeper than anyone has followed, into warmer rock, into places where the air changes and the sound of the world above is replaced by something else, a low, rhythmic pulse that might be water moving through stone or might be something else entirely.

The temperature is strange too. It is cold, yes — consistently, deeply cold — but it is cold in a way that does not vary. There is no warmer spot near the ceiling, no cooler hollow near the floor. The cold is uniform, distributed with an evenness that feels less like physics and more like intention, as though the pocket has decided on this temperature and enforces it.

And the light — the bioluminescence — is wrong in a way that is hard to articulate. It is not the blue-green glow of familiar fungi. It is warmer than that, almost amber, and it pulses. Slowly, almost imperceptibly, it brightens and dims in a cycle that does not match any known rhythm — not the day, not the month, not the tides. The people of Suth-Vael have tried to measure it and have given up. They say it has its own time.

The moss grows in directions that do not correspond to water flow or light source. It grows upward sometimes, forming columns and ridges that look almost deliberate, almost like writing, and the old people — the ones who have lived in the pocket longest — say that if you watch the moss long enough, you can see the shapes change, that the moss is trying to say something, that it has been trying to say something since before the fissure opened, since before the pocket existed, since before the mountain was a mountain.

---

The settlement has no center. This is unusual for a human place, and it is the thing that makes Suth-Vael feel, even to its inhabitants, like a place that is not entirely under human control. The structures are arranged around the open spaces rather than filling them, as though the empty areas are the important parts and the buildings are merely what grows at their edges. The largest open space — a clearing perhaps ten paces across, carpeted in moss so thick it rises to the knee — is where the people gather, and it is the quietest place in the pocket, because the moss there is so deep that it absorbs sound, and the creatures that live in it are so numerous that their individual noises blend into a silence that is louder than silence.

In this clearing, there is a structure that is not a structure. It is a mass of roots — thick, ancient-looking roots that have grown together into a shape that is almost, but not quite, a chair, or a throne, or a bed, or a table. It is large enough for a person to sit in, and its surface is worn smooth, not by tools but by use, by the pressure of a body over time. The people do not sit in it. They go near it. They leave things there — small offerings of water, of moss, of beetle-shells arranged in patterns — and they say that on certain nights, when the moss-glow is at its brightest and the moths are swarming and the dripping from the ceiling reaches a crescendo that is almost musical, they can hear a sound from the direction of the roots, a sound that is not a voice but carries the shape of a voice, speaking a language that is not language, saying something that they cannot remember when they try to repeat it.

---

The people themselves are adapted, in the small ways that people adapt. They see well in the dark — not perfectly, but better than they should. Their skin is pale, almost translucent in the bioluminescence, and it is always damp, and they do not seem to mind. They wear clothing made from processed moss-fiber and beetle-shell — layered, flexible, quiet — and they move through the pocket with a gait that is half-walk, half-glide, their feet barely lifting from the moss, their hands trailing along the walls for balance, for information, for comfort.

They are not primitive in the way that the word is usually meant. They are not simple. They know things about this place that would take a lifetime to learn and a longer lifetime to explain. They know which roots carry water and which carry — something else. They know the moods of the beetle-swarms, can read in their movements the approach of the wet season or the deepening of the cold. They know that the moths come when the root-pulse changes frequency, and that the frequency changes when something happens deep below, in the warm rock, in the place where the roots go and do not come back from.

They have stories about the place before the fissure opened. They say the pocket was here first — that it was a seed, a cyst, a dream the mountain was having, and that the fissure was the mountain waking up. They say the plants are not plants but memories of something that lived here before plants, and that the animals are not animals but the immune system of a body that is still growing, and that the people are not people but — 

They stop there. The story has no ending, or the ending is something that cannot be spoken in the presence of the root-throne, or the ending is the sound that comes from the roots on certain nights, which is not an ending but a continuation of something that has not yet begun.

---

It is cold. It is dark. The water drips. The moss glows its amber pulse. The beetles click their endless, tireless industry. The moths hang in the still air like thoughts that have not yet found a mind. Somewhere below, in the warm dark where the roots converge, something pulses — slowly, slowly — in a rhythm that is not a heartbeat but could be mistaken for one.

And in the pocket, in the settlement that is not quite a settlement, the people of Suth-Vael go about their lives — harvesting moss, weaving walls, reading the movements of small bodies in the dark — and they are quiet, and they are cold, and they are surrounded by more life than any one place should be able to hold, and they do not ask why.

The pocket does not answer questions. It only grows.
