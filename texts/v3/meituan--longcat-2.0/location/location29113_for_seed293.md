# The Weep

---

The ground breathes. Not metaphorically — the substrate beneath your feet rises and falls in a slow, tidal rhythm, a diaphragm of interlocked root-mass and something that is not quite root-mass, something older, something that has forgotten whether it was ever built or grown. The surface is warm. It has been warm for longer than the concept of warmth has had a name.

You are standing in a clearing roughly the size of a cathedral nave, though the walls are not walls. They are trunks — or what have become trunks over epochs of erosion so thorough that all distinction between column and organism has been sanded away. Each one is as wide as a house, rising maybe thirty feet before the canopy closes, and each one weeps. Water sheets down their surfaces in continuous veils, not from any visible source, but from the bark itself, which exudes moisture the way old stone sweats in summer. The air is thick with it. Your skin is wet before you have taken three steps. The light — normal light, the ordinary light of a sun that may or may not be the sun you know — filters through the canopy in shafts that turn to silver columns in the mist, and the mist never clears.

The water does not pool. It finds the channels.

The channels are everywhere. They groove the surface of the living substrate in patterns that might be street-planning or might be the capillary structure of a leaf magnified to the scale of a town. They are lined with a material that is smooth and dark and faintly iridescent — something between ceramic and chitin — and the water moves through them with purpose, fast and loud, a constant churning conversation of flow. The sound is the first thing that strikes you as wrong. It is too loud for water. It has harmonics. It resonates in frequencies that you feel in your sternum before you hear them with your ears, and if you stand still long enough you begin to suspect the channels are not merely carrying water but are doing something to it, or with it, or that the water itself is a component in some process that has been running so long it has become geology.

The settlement — because it is a settlement, unmistakably, though the word feels like a glove on the wrong hand — occupies perhaps two dozen of these clearings, connected by the channel-network and by walkways that are themselves alive. The walkways are bridges of woven tissue, pale and fibrous, and they flex under your weight with a give that is not quite plant and not quite muscle. Some of them are old. Some of them are new. The distinction is difficult to make because the old ones do not decay — they are continuously resurfaced, continuously repaired, by the substrate itself, which does not differentiate between the walkway and the trunk and the channel-lining and the ground. It is all one system. It has been one system for so long that the concept of separate objects has become a local anomaly, a brief perturbation in a field that has settled into its final form.

And yet it is not still. That is the paradox that takes time to resolve. The erosion here is ancient — everything has been worn to its essential geometry, every edge softened, every structure reduced to its most load-bearing, most necessary form. There is nothing rough. Nothing new. Nothing that has not been touched and retouched by millennia of the same slow forces. The trunks are smooth as river stones. The channels have been carved to hydraulically perfect curves. The walkways have the patina of objects that have been used by millions of feet over millions of years, and yet —

And yet the place is in constant upheaval.

Things grow. Things collapse. Things dissolve and are rebuilt in the same hour. A trunk at the far end of the clearing is splitting — slowly, with a sound like a ship's hull failing — and from the split a new structure is emerging, something architectural, something with right angles and deliberate proportions, and it is growing at a rate visible to the naked eye, pushing outward in smooth increments, and the water-channels around it are rerouting in real-time, the dark lining-flow diverting, reconnecting, finding new paths with an intelligence that is not intelligence but is also not not-intelligence. The fauna — and there is so much of it, a constant rustle and click and chirp and something deeper, something that sounds like a cello being played in a cave — moves through this turbulence with the ease of creatures for whom destruction and construction are the same event. Things the size of your hand with too many legs. Things the size of your head with no legs at all, pulsing through the water-channels like living clots. Things in the canopy that you can hear but not see, and things on the ground that you can see but which are so still you mistake them for architecture until they breathe.

The technology is not visible. That is the wrong way to say it. The technology is the only thing visible. It is the substrate. It is the channels. It is the way the water moves against the gradient in certain places, uphill, silently, as if gravity has been locally renegotiated. It is the way certain trunks hum at frequencies that make your teeth ache. It is the way the light sometimes — not often, but sometimes — bends around a clearing in a way that suggests the air itself has been structured, layered, given a refractive index that air should not have. The engineering here is so old and so thorough that it has become the environment. There is no device. There is no machine. There is only the place, functioning, and the function is so far beyond current physics that the only honest description is: it works, and it has always worked, and the working is indistinguishable from the living.

The warmth is close and even. It does not vary. The air tastes of minerals and green and something faintly metallic, like the taste of a coin held under the tongue. The humidity is total. Your clothes are soaked. Your hair is soaked. The water finds its way into everything — your pack, your pockets, the pages of any book you might have brought — and it is warm water, body-temperature, so that after a while you stop noticing the wetness and begin to feel that dryness would be the aberration, that you have been dry all your life until now and this is the correction.

The settlement has inhabitants. You see them — or you see shapes that might be inhabitants — moving between the trunks with a gait that suggests they are not walking on the surface but through it, as if the substrate yields to them in a way it does not yield to you. They are not human. They are not not-human. They are something that has been here long enough to have been shaped by the same forces that shaped the place, and they move through the turbulence the way the fauna moves through it, with an ease that suggests they are not enduring the chaos but participating in it, that the constant growth and collapse and dissolution and rebuilding is not something happening to them but something they are doing, or something they are.

The sound builds. The water in the channels is rising — not flooding, but intensifying, the flow doubling and doubling again, and the harmonics are climbing into registers that you feel as pressure behind your eyes, and the trunks are weeping harder, and the canopy is rustling with a sound like applause, and somewhere a structure is completing itself with a sound like a bell being struck once, cleanly, and the note hangs in the humid air and does not decay but instead is absorbed, taken up, incorporated into the constant churning chord of the place.

You are standing in a clearing the size of a cathedral. The ground breathes. The water climbs. The light is normal. The warmth is total. The place has been here forever and it is being born right now and these are the same statement.

You take another step. The substrate yields. The channels reroute around your foot. Something in the canopy calls out — a sound like a question in a language that predates language — and something on the ground answers, and the answer is not a sound but a vibration, a pulse that travels up through the sole of your boot and into your bones, and for a moment you understand something about the place that you will not be able to articulate later, something about the relationship between erosion and growth, between the ancient and the instantaneous, between the built and the grown and the wept.

Then the moment passes. The water keeps moving. The place keeps working. You are wet and warm and standing in the middle of something that has no name, and the name is not important, because the place does not need one.

It has been here long enough to have forgotten it ever needed to be called anything at all.
