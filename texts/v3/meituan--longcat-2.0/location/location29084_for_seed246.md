# The Settling

It is a basin — a depression worn into the high country so thoroughly that the word *basin* feels too active for what remains. Bowl is worse. Think rather of a palm that has been held open so long the hand forgot it was ever closed, the tendons set, the cup of it permanent. The rim is low, almost nothing. The floor is wide.

Water arrives here the way weather does — sideways, in pieces, in no particular hurry. Mist fingers in from the east and thins against the far wall. Condensation gathers on every surface the way grief does, without decision, a slow beading that reaches some critical weight and falls. The falls are small. They have been small for longer than the concept of large. There are rivulets that do not connect to any system you would call a stream. They appear, persist for some distance, and sink. The floor drinks.

Cold, yes. Not the cold of drama. The cold of *near*. One degree above, two below, oscillating with the hour, never committing. Frost at the lip by morning. Thaw by the middle of the day, if day is the right word for what passes overhead — a white sky, even, thin, letting through light that does not seem to originate from a source so much as *be present*. Normal light. Correct light. Light without warmth.

And the columns.

They rise from the floor of the basin in a pattern that is not random. Thirty or forty of them, some standing, some leaning, some broken and fallen and half-swallowed by the moss that covers everything. They are not stone. They are not bone. They are *built* — that is the only honest word — constructed of something that resembles paleite, that resinous mineral secretion left behind by an organism or a colony or a process that no longer exists here. The columns taper. They have surfaces that catch and release moisture in turn. They are spaced with a regularity that would be called urban planning if there had been planners. As it is: spacing. Infrastructure. The ghost of density without the density itself, because the spaces between them are enormous, hollow, the basin mostly empty air and the echo of footsteps you have not taken.

The moss is the true citizen here. It is lush in the way that patience is lush — thick, deep green to black-green, covering the fallen columns, the floor, the lower reaches of the standing ones, building up in centuries-thick cushions that hold water like memory. Liverworts. A few ferns, low, curled against the cold. No flowers. The vegetation does not announce itself. It *accumulates*.

You would call this place strange. Not because it is alien but because it is *settled*. Everything has reached its final state and stayed there long enough that the state itself becomes uncanny. The erosion is nearly complete — the basin will not get deeper, the rim will not get lower, the columns will not get taller. What you see is what it decided to be, long ago, and it has been that for so long that the decision has fossilized. The weirdness is in the stillness of *completion*. Most landscapes are in process. This one is not.

And yet it is active. The water cycles — arrive, gather, fall, sink. The moss grows, infinitesimally. The frost cracks micro-fractures in the column surfaces and the moss colonizes them. A wind enters from the east pass and moves through the standing columns and produces a sound. Not a tone — a sound. Each column has a pitch, or a texture of silence, and the wind activates them in sequence as it moves across the basin, and the result is *loud*. Not a howl. A chord. Multiple tones. A chord that shifts as the wind moves, as the moisture in each column's surface changes its acoustic property, as the temperature adjusts the air's willingness to carry it. The place is always making this sound. You walk into it the way you walk into someone else's conversation and do not know if it includes you.

There are almost no animals. A beetle, black, slow, living its entire life in a single cushion of moss. A bird that passes over occasionally and does not land. A spider between two columns, in a web that catches mist more than insects. The rarity of fauna is not a symptom of the cold or the emptiness — it is a symptom of the completeness. The niche is filled. The basin has settled into a configuration that does not require consumers.

You are the first animal to walk here in some time, perhaps in a long time, and the sound of your movement through the moss — that particular soft compression and release — joins the chord the columns are making, and for a moment the basin is a little more populated than it has been, and a little stranger, and then you pass, and it settles back, and the water arrives, and the moss grows, and the sound continues, and nothing else happens, and nothing else needs to.
