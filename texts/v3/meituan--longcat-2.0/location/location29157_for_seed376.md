# The Throat of the Green Engines

There is no soil. There has not been soil for a long time.

What passes for ground is a mat — a deep, ancient, breathing mat of compressed plant matter so thoroughly interwoven that it has achieved a kind of geological status. Not rock, but no longer dirt. A substrate of roots and lignin and slow-decayed cellulose, layered and re-layered over centuries until it is twelve, fifteen, twenty feet thick in places, and it holds warmth the way stone does, which is to say poorly, and it holds water the way a sponge does, which is to say always.

The elevation is high enough that the air has a thinness to it, a clarity, but the cold is the first thing you feel — not the dramatic cold of exposed peaks but a damp, seeping cold that rises from the mat itself. The moisture is everywhere and nowhere: not rain, not quite, but a perpetual condensation that beads on every surface and runs in slow, vertical streams down the stems of the taller growths. The humidity is a presence. Your clothes will be wet within minutes. Your breath is a white flag.

---

**The plant life is the landscape.**

There is no separation between them. You are standing *on* plants, *in* plants, *between* plants. The mat beneath your feet — if you could peel it back, if you could cut a cross-section — would reveal strata of growth: dead stems packed so tight they've petrified in all but name, living roots threading through the dead like veins through tissue, fungal networks visible as pale threads stitching everything together, and at the surface, the current generation of growth pressing upward in dense, competitive columns.

The species are numerous but visually they read as a single textured entity — a green so deep it verges on black in the shaded understory, silver-green where the condensation catches the light, pale yellow-green at the growing tips. There are no flowers. Or rather, there are structures that might be flowers, but they are small and closed and their purpose seems to be something other than display. Some of them hum. Not audibly — you hear nothing — but if you press your palm against the base of a thick stalk, you can feel it: a vibration, a low frequency thrumming, like standing next to a transformer station.

---

**The industrial quality announces itself slowly.**

At first you think the sound you cannot hear is absence. Then you begin to notice the *shapes*. Rising from the mat at irregular intervals — some close enough to touch, others separated by hundreds of yards — are structures that are clearly plants but are clearly doing something *else*. Thick, columnar trunks, eight to fifteen feet tall, with smooth bark that has a waxy, almost rubbery sheen. They taper to a blunt point. Around their bases, the mat is compressed into hard, flat platforms, as if heavy machinery has stood there for decades. And perhaps it has, in a sense: these columns exude a resinous substance that accumulates in pans and channels cut — or *grown* — into the surrounding mat. The resin pools. It is collected by no one. It sits in its shallow basins, slowly thickening, slowly darkening, and the smell of it is sharp and mechanical — turpentine, hot metal, something almost like ozone.

The channels. This is where the *tech* lives. The channels are not built. They are grown — or rather, they are the result of the resin being secreted along such consistent paths that the plant matter beneath has been suppressed, killed, compressed, and eventually formed a smooth, trough-like surface. They form networks. They branch and converge. They carry the resin — or other fluids, darker, more viscous — downhill, always downhill, toward the low points of this undulating terrain where the water collects.

---

**The low points.**

In the slight valleys between rises of the mat, the water has nowhere to go. It pools. Not as ponds — the mat is too absorbent, too porous for open water to persist — but as *saturated zones* where the plant matter is swollen and dark and the cold becomes almost painful. Walking here, your foot sinks six inches and water rises around your boot, black water, tannin-stained, smelling of peat and iron. The vegetation changes in these zones: the tall columns give way to a lower, denser growth, and the trunks that do emerge are wider, more swollen, almost bladder-like. Some of them are clearly hollow. You can knock on them and they resonate — a deep, muffled tone, like knocking on a water tank.

And these are where the *processing* happens. You don't see it. You don't hear it. But you can feel it through your feet: a rhythmic, subtle pulsing in the mat, a slow peristaltic squeeze, as if the entire saturated zone is a kind of gut, digesting. The fluids in the channels move faster here. The resin-thickened basins fill and overflow and refill. There is a mechanical logic to it — input, throughput, output — but the mechanism is entirely biological, or entirely botanical, or whatever word applies when a plant has become a factory without anyone building it.

---

**The silence is the thing that will unsettle you, if anything does.**

No birds. The fauna rating is low and you understand why — there is nothing here for a bird, no open sky to speak of, the canopy is too dense and too low, and besides, the air itself feels wrong, too humid, too heavy with whatever volatile compounds the resin columns are emitting. But it's not just the absence of animals. It's the absence of *everything*. No wind in the upper branches — the canopy is too dense for wind to penetrate. No creak of wood. No rustle of leaves. The plant matter is too interlocked, too compressed, too mutually supported to move independently. It is a single structure, and single structures do not creak.

When you walk, your foot sinks into the mat and the mat closes around your boot with a faint, wet compression — and then silence. Complete silence. The damp air absorbs sound. The dense growth absorbs sound. You could scream and the sound would travel perhaps thirty feet before the vegetation swallowed it. You do not scream. The silence seems to ask for something else.

---

**The scale.**

You can see, in places where the columnar trunks thin out or where a section of the mat has subsided, that this extends. Not to the horizon — the terrain rolls and undulates, and beyond a few hundred meters the shapes dissolve into a green-gray haze of condensation and distance — but it extends enough that you understand you are in the middle of something regional. A valley, perhaps, or a high plateau, ten or fifteen miles across. The slight downhill trend of the channels suggests a drainage, a slow movement of fluids toward some central low point that might be miles away. The whole system slopes, very gently, like a shallow basin.

And in the distance — or what you take to be distance; the haze makes judgment difficult — there are vertical shapes that are too tall, too regular, too *grouped* to be the columnar trunks. They could be something else. They could be more of the same, but larger. They could be the places where the system concentrates, where the resin-production reaches some industrial scale, where the gut-valleys widen into something more like organs. You cannot tell. You will not go closer. Not yet.

---

**The weirdness is subtle.**

It is not the plants-as-architecture that is weird, though that would be strange in another context. It is the *intentionality* that creeps in at the edges of your perception. The channels do not just drain — they route. The resin does not just accumulate — it is sorted, by viscosity, by color, into different basins, as if different grades are being produced for different purposes. The columnar trunks are not randomly distributed — they are spaced with a regularity that suggests planning, or at least a kind of distributed decision-making, as if the mat itself is managing its own industrial process.

And then there is the warmth. Not everywhere — most of the landscape is cold — but in specific spots, directly above certain of the hollow trunks, the air shimmers faintly. Heat. Not the heat of decomposition, which would be diffuse and steady, but the heat of something *active*, something with a rhythm, something that cycles. If you hold your hand above the vent of one of these trunks, you feel it: a pulse of warm, moist air, rising in a slow, regular beat. Like breathing. Like a bellows. Like the exhaust of an engine running at idle.

---

**The turbulence is everywhere and invisible.**

This is the final thing to understand, and it takes time: this landscape is not stable in the way a forest is stable. It is stable the way a factory floor is stable — everything is in motion, everything is processing, everything is changing, but the system holds. The mat grows and is compressed and grows again. The channels are carved and re-carved by the flow of fluids. The resin is produced and collected and transformed. Old trunks collapse and are absorbed and new ones erupt from the mat with a speed that, if you could watch time-lapse, would look like a city being built. The whole thing is in a state of perpetual renovation, perpetual production, perpetual *work* — but it is silent, and it is cold, and the mist hangs motionless in the still air, and from a distance it looks like nothing more than a hillside covered in low, dark vegetation.

It is not a hillside. It is not a forest. It is a plant that has become a place, and the place has become a machine, and the machine has been running for so long that no one remembers — if anyone ever knew — whether it was designed or whether it simply grew this way, the way a river grows a canyon, the way a glacier grows a valley, the way a root grows through concrete.

It is still growing.
