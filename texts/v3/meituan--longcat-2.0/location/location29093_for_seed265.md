# The Drench

---

It begins with the sound of water that isn't rain.

A low, continuous seepage — not dripping, not flowing, but *exhaling*. The ground breathes moisture. Every surface is filmed, glazed, weeping. You touch a rock and your fingers come away dark, not with wetness exactly, but with a kind of dense, warm damp that clings like breath on glass. The air is thick enough to wear.

This is low country. Not below sea level, but *pressed* — a broad, shallow basin that holds water the way a palm holds it, cupped and unwilling to release. The terrain is young. That's the first thing that unsettles. The rock faces are sharp, unrounded, their edges still carrying the memory of whatever force recently exposed them. Fracture lines run clean. Sediment hasn't yet learned the shape of the land. There are places where the earth looks *cut* — not eroded, not weathered, but opened, as if something vast peeled back a layer and then stopped, leaving the wound fresh.

And it is dark. Not night-dark. Not overcast-dark. A darkness that seems to rise from the ground itself, as though the water in the soil has leached the light out of everything. You can see, after a fashion — shapes resolve at the edge of perception, close things take on dim definition — but distance collapses. Twenty feet out, the world softens into a grainy, shifting murk. Beyond that, nothing. The darkness has texture. It has weight. It presses against your eyes like warm fingers.

---

The structures appear gradually.

Not buildings, exactly. *Remnants.* A concrete stanchion rising from the mire at a slight angle, its rebar teeth exposed and rusting in neat, bright-orange rows. A corrugated wall, half-submerged, its lower edge furred with something that might be moss or might be mineral deposit — pale, fibrous, faintly luminous in a way that seems wrong for biology. A tower of some kind, skeletal, its framework still standing but its purpose unreadable. No roof. No floors. Just the geometry of industry, stripped of its function, left to stand in the wet dark like a ribcage in a swamp.

They are scattered. Not a ruin — ruins imply a whole that has broken. These are *individual* things, separated by hundreds of yards of open ground, each one alone with itself. A generator block, its housing cracked, its innards long since taken by water. A length of pipe, waist-thick, running along the ground for a distance and then simply stopping, its open end facing the dark like a speaking trumpet aimed at nothing. A control panel, its face still intact, its dials and switches arranged in a language of function that no one is left to read. Some of these things are still faintly warm. Not hot. Just — *maintaining*. As though the processes they once served have not so much stopped as *slowed*, continuing at a pace too gradual to perceive, turning over in their sleep.

The tech here is industrial but not modern. It belongs to an era of heavy things — cast iron, rivets, analog gauges, the aesthetics of torque and load. And it is still, in some minimal sense, *operating*. You can feel it in the ground: a sub-audible vibration, more pressure than sound, a hum that lives in the water table and conducts up through the soles of your feet. Something is still running down here. Something has been running for a very long time, at a pace that makes glaciers look hasty.

---

The fauna is abundant. You understand this before you see it.

The darkness is full of small movements. Not skittering — nothing here moves with the frantic energy of vermin. These are slow, deliberate motions. A shape the size of your hand, low to the ground, pulling itself across the wet stone with a motion like a slug but with too many joints. Something hanging from the underside of the broken pipe — a cluster, a colony, a single organism with many bodies — that shifts and resettles as you pass beneath it, not startled, just *adjusting*. In the water that stands in every depression, concentric rings appear and disappear, not from rain but from below. Something rising. Something sinking. Something turning over in the warm, still water.

They are not afraid. That is the notable thing. The creatures of the Drench do not flee from your presence because your presence is not, to them, remarkable. You are warm. You are wet. You are slow. You belong to the same gradient they do. A thing with a beetle's carapace and a mammal's eyes regards you from the lip of the generator block with what you can only describe as *professional interest* — the look of a colleague assessing a new variable in a shared system. Then it returns to whatever it was doing, which appears to be licking rust.

The ecology is sparse in the botanical sense — there is almost no green here, no leaf, no stem. What vegetation exists is fungal, algal, something in between. Mats of it coat the lower surfaces of everything, pale and slick, and in the absolute darkness it has learned to make its own faint light — not bioluminescence exactly, but a kind of *leakage*, as though the metabolic processes are so inefficient that energy escapes as a dim, blue-white glow. It is not enough to see by. It is enough to know that you are surrounded by living surfaces, that the walls and the ground and the standing water are all *coated* in a thin, warm, slowly metabolizing skin.

---

And then there is the other thing. The thing the parameters called *weirdness*.

It builds slowly. You don't notice it at first because the darkness and the wet and the strangeness of the place have already stretched your sense of what is normal. But it accumulates.

A sound that comes from the wrong direction. You hear something behind you — a wet, heavy displacement, like a body pulling itself out of water — and you turn, and there is nothing, but the sound continues, now to your left, now above you, as though the source is not moving but your *hearing* is.

A structure that is two things at once. The tower — the skeletal one — is, from one angle, a simple frame of rusted steel. From another angle, it is a tower of rusted steel with a platform halfway up and a ladder still attached. From a third angle, it is a tower of rusted steel with no platform, no ladder, and a different number of legs. You walk around it. The number of legs does not resolve. Your eyes insist on a count. Your memory of the count changes while you are still looking.

The water behaves incorrectly. In places, it flows uphill. Not dramatically — not a stream climbing a slope — but a thin film of moisture on a rock surface that moves, with slow, inevitable patience, from a lower point to a higher one, as though gravity is a suggestion that the Drench has learned to decline. In other places, water hangs in the air — not mist, not spray, but discrete droplets, suspended at chest height, motionless, warm to the touch, refusing to fall. They do not evaporate. They do not coalesce. They simply *are*, a permanent feature of the air, and the fauna moves around them with the casual precision of things that have always lived in a world where water has no obligation to the ground.

Time does something here. Not dramatically — the clocks still work, the heart still beats — but the *sequence* of events becomes unreliable. You hear a sound, and then you hear it again, and it is not an echo because the second instance contains a detail the first did not. You see a creature cross your path, and then you see it cross your path again, and it is the same creature but it is moving in the same direction both times, as though the stretch of ground it crossed has been *reset*. The industrial hum in the ground shifts in frequency, and for a moment you are certain that the structures around you are in a different state of decay — more rusted, less rusted, differently arranged — and then the hum shifts back and everything is as it was, or as it was a moment ago, or as it will be.

The Drench is not a place where reality breaks. It is a place where reality is *thin*. Where the rules are not violated but *negotiated*. Where the water and the dark and the slow, patient industrial processes have been grinding at the fabric of the ordinary for so long that the fabric has become permeable, and things seep through — not dramatically, not catastrophically, but in the way that water seeps through stone. Slowly. Warmly. Inevitably.

---

You stand in the center of it. The darkness presses close. The water seeps. The creatures move around you in their slow, deliberate orbits. The structures stand at their scattered posts, still humming, still processing, still performing whatever function they were built for at a pace that will not complete for centuries. The air is warm and thick and tastes of iron and electricity and something organic that has no name.

Somewhere, a gauge turns. Somewhere, a droplet rises. Somewhere, a thing with too many joints and not enough eyes is doing something that looks, from a distance, like maintenance.

The Drench continues. It has always continued. It will continue long after you have left, or long after you have stopped being able to tell the difference between leaving and staying.

The water does not care either way.
