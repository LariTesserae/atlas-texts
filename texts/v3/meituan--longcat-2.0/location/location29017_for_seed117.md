# The Dripworks

The ceiling is low enough to touch in most places, and you do touch it — everyone does, eventually, the way you'd trail your fingers along a wall in a dark hallway. It comes away wet. Everything here comes away wet.

The chamber is roughly the size of a modest cathedral, though it doesn't feel sacred so much as *occupied* — densely, thoroughly occupied, the way a reef is occupied, or a termite mound, or a drawer that someone has filled with small objects over decades without ever throwing anything away. The space is not large, but it is *full*. Stalactites descend in clusters so tight they nearly touch the formations that have risen to meet them, and in several places they have met — columns of calcium carbonate, some as thin as your wrist, some as wide as a barrel, running floor to ceiling like the pillars of a church that forgot to stop building. Between them: water. Water in sheets, water in threads, water beading on every surface and falling in a constant, irregular percussion that is the closest thing this place has to a voice.

The water is not rain. It is older than rain. It rises from below — from a geothermal seam that runs beneath the chamber floor, warming the rock to the temperature of blood, pushing mineral-laden water up through fissures and pores and the gaps between stones. It condenses on the cooler ceiling and drips back down. It has been doing this for a long time. The formations here are not young; they have the settled, self-satisfied look of things that have found their shape and see no reason to change. Some are translucent, amber-colored, lit from within by a faint bioluminescence that lives in the mineral matrix itself — or seems to. Others are opaque white, or rust-red where iron has seeped in, or a deep, bruised purple that you can't quite explain and that doesn't appear in any geology textbook you've read.

The light is dim. It comes from the formations, mostly — that faint, organic glow — and from a few old fixtures that are still, against all reason, connected to something. They hang from the ceiling on corroded brackets, glass housings fogged and mineral-crusted, and they flicker at irregular intervals, as if the electricity itself is uncertain whether it still belongs here. When they come on, they cast a yellowish, industrial light that makes the water look like honey and the stone look like skin. When they go off, the bioluminescence takes over, and the whole chamber shifts to a blue-green twilight that feels less like darkness and more like a different kind of seeing.

The built things are scattered, and they are old. A pipe runs along the eastern wall — iron, six inches in diameter, flaking with rust but still intact, still carrying water from somewhere to somewhere else. You can hear it: a low, continuous murmur, like a conversation in the next room. Near the center of the chamber, there is a structure — a platform, perhaps, or the base of a machine that has been mostly removed or has mostly collapsed. It is made of wood and iron, and it is covered in the same mineral deposits as everything else, so that it is difficult to tell where the construction ends and the cave begins. A rail runs from it into a side passage, and on the rail sits a cart, or the remains of a cart, its wheels locked in place by calcium buildup, its bed filled with still water in which something small and pale is moving.

There are other artifacts. A cable, snaking along the floor, its rubber housing cracked and leaking a faint, acrid smell. A tool — a wrench, or something like a wrench — lying in a pool, half-encrusted, as if it were a stone that had always been shaped that way. A boot. A gauge, its glass face opaque, its needle frozen at a reading that might mean something if you knew what it was measuring. These things are not arranged. They are not displayed. They are simply *here*, in the way that everything is here — deposited, abandoned, absorbed into the mineral logic of the place.

And the fauna. The fauna is abundant, and the fauna is strange.

They are small, mostly. The size of your hand, or your finger, or your thumbnail. They move through the water and across the wet stone with a fluid, purposeful grace that suggests they have been doing this for a very long time and have gotten it right. They are pale — white, translucent, the pinkish-gray of things that have never seen the sun. Some are segmented, like shrimp. Some are soft and undulating, like slugs. Some are hard-shelled, like beetles, but with too many legs, or legs that bend in the wrong places, or no legs at all, just a muscular foot that grips the rock with a slow, deliberate suction.

They eat the minerals. Or they eat the things that eat the minerals. Or they eat each other. The food chain here is short and strange, and it doesn't rely on anything green, anything photosynthetic, anything that remembers the sun. The base of it is chemosynthetic — bacteria that feed on sulfur, on iron, on the heat that rises from below — and everything else is built on top of that, layer by layer, a whole ecosystem that runs on geology instead of light.

Some of them glow. Not all of them, and not all the time, but enough that the chamber has a living light of its own — points of blue and green and, occasionally, a deep, unsettling red that pulses slowly, like a heartbeat, from the shell of something that looks like a clam but isn't. When the old electric lights flicker on, the bioluminescence dims, as if the creatures are shy, or as if the two kinds of light are competing, or as if the electricity is a kind of predator that they have learned to hide from.

The sound is mostly quiet, but it is not silent. It is the sound of water, first and always — dripping, flowing, pooling, the occasional *plink* of a drop hitting a surface that is just different enough to make a different note. It is the sound of the pipe, that low murmur. It is the sound of the creatures moving — a faint, wet scraping, like a fingertip on a window. It is the sound of the old lights, a buzz and a flicker and, sometimes, a sharp *tick* as something inside them shifts. And occasionally, from deeper in the chamber or from one of the side passages, there is a sound that is none of these things — a low, resonant groan, like a ship in heavy seas, or like the chamber itself is settling, or like something large is breathing very slowly in its sleep.

The air is cool and thick with moisture. It tastes of stone and iron and something faintly sulfurous, like a match that has just been struck. It is not unpleasant, but it is not neutral either — it has a weight to it, a presence, as if the air itself is a substance you are moving through rather than simply breathing. Your skin is damp within minutes. Your clothes are damp within an hour. Everything you bring in will be damp before you leave, and it will carry the smell of this place with it for days — that mineral, geothermal, alive-but-not-in-any-way-you-recognize smell that is the signature of the Dripworks.

The rules of this place are simple, but they are not the rules of the surface. Water flows, and you follow it, or you don't, but it will be there regardless. Stone grows, but so slowly that you will never see it happen — and yet it is growing, always, the formations extending by fractions of millimeters per year, the columns thickening, the ceiling lowering, the chamber slowly, slowly closing in. The creatures move through their cycles — feeding, breeding, dying, their shells and bodies adding to the mineral deposit, becoming part of the stone they once crawled across. The old machines flicker and murmur and occasionally do something — a valve opens, a pump engages, a light comes on — for reasons that no one living understands, powered by a grid that may or may not still exist above.

And the boundary between the living and the not-living is thin here, thinner than it should be. The formations grow in shapes that look almost intentional. The creatures build shells that look almost architectural. The mineral deposits form patterns that look almost like writing, or like circuitry, or like the branching of rivers seen from very high up. You will find yourself staring at a cluster of stalactites and thinking, for just a moment, that you recognize a face, or a hand, or a word in a language you almost know. You will see a creature moving across the stone and think, for just a moment, that it is the stone itself, flowing like water, reshaping itself, becoming something new.

This is the Dripworks. It has been here a long time. It will be here a long time more. It is not hostile, but it is not welcoming either. It is simply *itself* — wet, dim, dense, alive in its own way, strange in its own way, and utterly, completely indifferent to whether you understand it or not.
