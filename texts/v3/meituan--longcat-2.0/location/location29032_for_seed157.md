# The Gathering at Stillhollow

There is a basin in the high country where the mountains cup their hands and hold water like an offering. It is not large — a thousand paces across at its widest, rimmed by shoulders of dark stone that lean inward like the walls of a half-collapsed cathedral. The sky above is a permanent gray, not storm-cloud exactly, but a ceiling of dense, unmoving fog that presses down on the basin's lip and spills over in slow, cold condensation. This is where the water comes from — not rain, not stream, but the fog itself, weeping continuously from every surface, beading on stone and leaf and fur until the entire basin drips and gleams with a fine, perpetual wetness. The rock walls are slick with it. The moss drinks it and is never full. The air tastes of minerals and the faint sweet rot of old growth.

The elevation is high enough that the cold has settled in and decided to stay. Not bitter cold — not killing cold — but a coolness that never leaves, that has never left, that has saturated the stone and soil so thoroughly that everything in the basin exists at the same steady, cool temperature. Your hand on the rock feels the same as your hand in the air feels the same as your hand in the shallow pool at the basin's floor. There is no gradient. No warm patch. No cool draft. The temperature is one temperature, everywhere, always.

The vegetation is dense beyond the possibility of passage. Every surface is layered — first the rock, then a deep cushion of moss (black-green, sodden, springy as flesh), then ferns growing from the moss, then liverworts and filmy ferns and things with no common name that grow in the spaces between fern fronds, then shrubs with small waxy leaves that arch over the moss and trap the fog-drip into rivulets that run down their stems and into the root mat. The shrubs are old. Their trunks are thick with moss of their own, and from their branches hang curtains of pale lichen that sway without wind — not swaying, actually, because there is no wind. The lichen hangs still. Everything hangs still. The whole basin is a layered, intergrown, saturated mat of life that has been packing itself tighter and tighter for centuries until there is no gap, no bare patch, no empty space between one organism and the next.

And in this saturated, dripping, cool-dark world, the animals have arrived and stayed and multiplied until they are everywhere. The basin is so full of them that the biomass of creatures probably exceeds the biomass of plants. Beetles in the moss. Spiders between the ferns. Millipedes under every stone. Slugs on every leaf. Frogs — small, pale, almost translucent — sitting motionless on moss cushions, their throat-sacs pulsing. Birds nest in the shrub-layer, small gray-green birds that move through the vegetation like fish through coral. Bats roost in the crevices of the stone walls during the dim day and emerge at what passes for night. Crustaceans — tiny, pale, ancient — live in the thin sheets of water that flow over the rock faces. Insects fill the air in a constant low cloud, a living haze that drifts at knee-height and parts around your leg as you move through it.

Among them, the crows. Or something like crows — dark-feathered, heavy-billed, watchful. They move through the basin in a loose society, and they use tools. Not complicated tools. A twig stripped of bark, used to probe beetle-holes in rotting wood. A small stone, carried in the beak, dropped onto a snail shell to crack it. A strip of moss, held in the foot, used to wipe moisture from the eyes before flight. The tools are simple. The tool-use is consistent, passed from bird to bird, stable across generations that have never known anything different.

This is the thing about the basin that goes beyond ecology and enters something else: nothing here changes. The fog weeps at the same rate it wept a thousand years ago. The moss grows at the exact pace it is grazed by millipedes. The beetles reproduce at the exact pace they are eaten by the birds. The water flows over the rock faces at the same depth, the same speed, the same branching pattern of rivulets. The temperature does not vary. The light does not vary. The population of every species is the same as it was — as it will be. This is not a stable ecosystem in the way ecologists use the phrase. This is a place where change has been *removed*. Where time has become irrelevant. Where every process is in such perfect equilibrium with every counter-process that the whole system has locked itself into a single, permanent state and cannot — will not — move.

The consequence is a place that feels, when you stand in it, not peaceful but *suspended*. The animals move and feed and preen, but there is no urgency to any of it. The birds do not sing — or rather, they produce calls, but the calls are always the same calls, at the same intervals, serving functions so rigidly fixed that communication has become indistinguishable from machinery. The frogs pulse their throat-sacs. The insects drift at knee-height. The water drips from the fog, and the fog presses down on the basin's rim, and the moss is black-green and saturated, and the temperature is one temperature, and the light is one light — dim, gray-green, filtering through fog and canopy and lichen-curtain until it reaches the moss floor as a luminous dusk that never brightens and never fades.

You can walk through the basin. There is no path, but the vegetation parts around your feet and closes behind you, and the insects part around your legs, and the pale frogs watch you with their dark eyes, and the tool-using crows watch you from the shrub-layer with their dark eyes, and the fog weeps on your face, and the water beads on your hands, and the whole place — every organism, every surface, every drop — is exactly as it was before you arrived and will be exactly as it is after you leave. You are the only variable. You are the only thing that moves with intention. Everything else is locked in its perfect, wet, cool, dark, crowded, dripping stillness, and it will not change. It will never change.
