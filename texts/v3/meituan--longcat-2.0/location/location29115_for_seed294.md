# The Aeolian Straits

You are standing on something. Or rather, you are standing *in* something — in the understanding that the distinction between standing and being submerged has long since collapsed here.

---

Water is the medium. Not water as you know it from coastlines, not a surface with a top and a bottom, but water as *atmosphere* — total, enveloping, so thoroughly present that the air has forgotten how to be air. Imagine an ocean that never found a sea floor, or rather, found one so long ago that it wore the floor into lace. The water here is cold — not the killing cold of glaciers, but the cold of deep time, of pressure without heat, of something that has been still for so long it forgot warmth was ever an option. It presses against you from every direction with the polite, absolute insistence of something that has nowhere else to be.

And you can breathe it. Or something in you has adapted to breathe it. Or perhaps the water here is thin enough — some alchemy of altitude and mineral — that it parts around your lungs and offers up what you need. The details are unclear. The Straits do not explain themselves.

What is clear is the *structure*.

---

Rising from the deep — from wherever down becomes — are the Columns. They are not natural, except that they are. They are built, except that no one built them. They are stone, or something like stone: calciumite, aragonite, the mineral precipitation of ten million years of water moving through water, depositing infinitesimal layers in the slowest construction project ever conceived. Each Column is the width of a cathedral and the height of ambition. They rise from the black depths and vanish into the dim heights, and they are shaped — *shaped* — into forms that your eye reads as deliberate. Fluted shafts. Buttressed walls. Arches that spring from capital to capital with the logic of load-bearing, except they bear nothing but themselves. Window openings, perfectly round, punched through walls of mineral thirty feet thick. Stairways that spiral upward along the exterior of each Column, wide enough for two to walk abreast, leading to landings and platforms that serve no evident purpose except the purpose of having been made.

There are hundreds of them. Perhaps thousands. They are scattered across a region so vast that the farthest ones fade into the blue-dark distance like a memory of architecture. The space between them is enormous — you could swim for an hour between one Column and the next, through open water so clear and so empty that your mind begins to invent things in the middle distance. The Columns do not cluster. They do not form cities or villages. Each one stands alone, separated from its neighbors by stretches of open, cold, mineral-rich nothing, as though the builder — the process — the whatever-it-was — believed in personal space on a geological scale.

And the Columns are old. Old the way mountains are old, old the way the concept of "old" starts to lose meaning. They have been here since before the water was this cold, since before the altitude was this high, since before — and this is the part that resists — whatever made them finished making them and left. Their surfaces are not fresh-carved. They are *worn*. Every edge has been softened by current into a curve. Every sharp corner has been negotiated away by the patient diplomacy of flow. The fluting is still visible but smoothed, like a fingerprint pressed into clay that's been left in the rain. The window openings are larger than they were originally — water has eaten them wider, and in doing so has given them a kind of beauty, a softness, that the original geometry lacked. The stairways are passable but treacherous, each step worn into a shallow bowl by the passage of — what? Not feet. Something. Or nothing. Perhaps just the current itself, flowing upward and downward in patterns that, over epochs, have the same effect as traffic.

The Columns are the bones of the world. Everything else is commentary.

---

The water moves. This is the central fact that the stillness of the Columns tries to hide. The Straits are not calm. They are *turbulent* — not in the way of surface storms, not in the way of crashing waves, but in the way of deep currents that have somewhere to be and disagree about the route. Water moves between the Columns in great slow rivers, currents so large they have their own currents, eddies within eddies, spirals within spirals. The Columns disrupt these flows and are disrupted by them in turn, and the result is a constant, low, everywhere vibration — a hum that you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears. It is the sound of a world in motion, of water that has never once in its existence been still.

And the water is not uniform. It comes in layers — warmer strata and colder strata, mineral-heavy water and mineral-light water, water that has been to the surface and water that has never seen light. These layers slide past each other like the pages of a book being slowly shuffled, and where they meet, there are *fronts* — visible boundaries in the water, shimmering curtains of refraction that hang in the air-water like veils. Crossing a front is a sensory event: the temperature shifts, the pressure shifts, the light bends differently, and for a moment you are in a different world, one that is similar to but not the same as the one you just left. The fronts move. They drift between the Columns like slow, purposeful animals, and the Columns stand among them like trees in a forest where the wind never stops but never quite arrives.

---

There is light, but it is not light you can source. It comes from above — from very far above — filtered through so much water that by the time it reaches you it has been reduced to a dim, directionless glow, the color of a winter sky seen through closed eyelids. It is enough to see by, barely, in the way that a memory is enough to remember by. The Columns catch this light and hold it, their mineral surfaces faintly luminous, so that each one glows with a pale, internal, blue-grey radiance, like a lamp turned down to its lowest setting. In the distance, the farthest Columns are just suggestions of light, vertical smudges in the dark, and beyond them nothing. The light does not come from below. Below is black. Below is the kind of black that has texture, that has weight, that you could reach into and pull up handfuls of.

---

And there is life. Abundant life. Life that has never seen a plant, never touched soil, never known anything but water and stone and the cold.

The Columns are *covered* in it — not in the way that a reef is covered, not in the bright, colorful, tropical way, but in the way that a ruin is covered by the slow accretion of habitation. The surfaces are thick with growths that are not quite coral, not quite fungus, not quite anything with a name in a language you'd recognize. Pale, branching structures that reach into the current like hands. Encrusting mats that spread across the stone in patterns that look almost deliberate — spirals, whorls, the ghost of intention. Filamentous curtains that hang from the window openings and sway in the current like the tassels of an old and forgotten tapestry. They are white, mostly. White and grey and the palest blue. The colors of things that have never needed pigment, that have never had a reason to be bright.

And moving among these growths: animals. Not fish, exactly, though fish is the closest word. They are the size of your hand, most of them, and they move in schools that number in the thousands — vast, coordinated, shimmering clouds that flow between the Columns like thoughts through a mind. They are translucent. You can see their hearts beating, can see the blood — or whatever serves as blood — moving through their bodies in slow, cold pulses. They eat the growths on the Columns, or they eat each other, or they eat the mineral particles that drift in the current like snow. The food web here is simple and ancient and has been running, uninterrupted, for longer than most species have existed anywhere else.

Larger things move in the deep. You see them as shapes in the black below — slow, massive, patient. They do not come up into the light. They do not need to. They have the depths, and the depths have them, and the arrangement seems to suit both parties. Occasionally one will rise far enough to be glimpsed — a curve of something pale, a suggestion of scale, an eye the size of a dinner plate that catches the dim light and reflects it back with an intelligence that is not quite intelligence, that is something older and less negotiable. Then it descends again, and the water closes over the space where it was, and you are alone with the Columns and the current and the hum.

---

The sound of the Straits is mostly the hum. Mostly the vibration of water moving through structure, of current negotiating obstacle, of pressure finding equilibrium and failing to find it. But there are other sounds, quieter sounds, sounds that you hear not with your ears but with the bones of your skull. The creak of mineral under stress — a Column adjusting, settling, responding to a current that has shifted by a fraction of a degree. The click and scrape of the small animals feeding, a sound like rain on a tin roof but slower, more deliberate, more *hungry*. The occasional deep, resonant tone that comes from somewhere below, from one of the large things moving in the dark, a sound that is less a sound than an event, a vibration that passes through you and leaves you changed in some way you can't articulate.

And silence. Long stretches of it. The silence of a place that is mostly empty space, mostly water, mostly nothing. The silence between the Columns, in the open stretches where there is no structure and no life and no current strong enough to carry sound. You can float in these spaces and hear your own heartbeat and think that you are the only thing in the world, and the thought is not frightening but peaceful, the peace of a place that has been empty for so long that it has made a virtue of emptiness, that has polished its solitude the way the current has polished the Columns, into something smooth and rounded and complete.

---

The Straits are high. You understand this not because you can see the surface — the surface is impossibly far above, a memory of brightness that might be sky or might be something else — but because of the pressure, which is wrong. It is too low for the depth you sense below you. The water is thin here, stretched by altitude, and it has a quality of *reach* to it, as though it is trying to be in two places at once — the deep ocean it remembers and the high, cold place it has become. The Columns know this. They were built — grown — precipitated — in a time when the water was different, when the altitude was different, when the world was different, and they have been adjusting ever since, adding mineral to their tops, dissolving from their bases, a slow migration upward that will take another million years to complete. They are not what they were. They are not yet what they will be. They are in the middle of a process that has no end, only continuation.

---

This is the Aeolian Straits: a place where water has replaced air, where stone has replaced earth, where the only construction is the construction of time and chemistry and flow, where life thrives in the cold and the dim and the current, where the silence is as present as the sound, where the ancient and the turbulent coexist without contradiction, because here, in the deep and the high and the old, they were never two things to begin with.

The Columns stand. The water moves. The small animals pulse through the dark like living light. And somewhere below, something large turns over in its sleep, and the whole world hums.
