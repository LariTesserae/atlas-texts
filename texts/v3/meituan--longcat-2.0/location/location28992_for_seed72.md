# The Cavern Works

The rock is young. You can feel it — not in any single feature, but in the way the walls still weep, the way the surfaces have not yet decided what they are going to be. This is a lava tube in its adolescence, a geothermal throat still cooling from whatever pushed it up, and the stone has the dull, damp warmth of something that hasn't finished becoming itself. The ceiling arches maybe forty meters above the floor — not a vast hall, but a generous one, a single lung of space. The walls are ribbed with frozen flow-lines, smooth in places and jagged in others, and everywhere the rock is dark: basalt, scoria, the occasional glassy streak of obsidian that catches the light and throws it back in a thin, cold line.

The heat comes from below. Not violently — this is not an eruption, not a vent — but steadily, the way a body is warm. The floor is uneven, a terrain of collapsed blocks and mineral-encrusted pools, and in the lowest places water has gathered: shallow, still, opaque with dissolved minerals. The surface of each pool has that particular oily stillness that means it's hot to the touch. Steam rises from some of them in thin, lazy threads. The air is thick with moisture — not raining, not dripping in any single dramatic way, but *present*. Condensation beads on every surface. The machinery is slick with it. Your skin is slick with it. The air tastes of iron and sulfur and wet stone.

The light is dim and comes from two sources. First: the works themselves. Electric lamps on tall stands, their bulbs fogged with moisture, casting yellow pools that don't reach far. They are spaced widely — every twenty meters, every thirty — and between them the darkness is nearly total. Second: the fauna. But we'll come to that.

The works are industrial in the way that a body is industrial — not a factory floor, but a system. Pipes run along the walls and across the floor, some as thick as your torso, some thin as your wrist, all of them sweating condensation and carrying water, steam, or something in between. Pumps squat on concrete pads at irregular intervals, their engines turning slowly, their exhaust a low, continuous thrum that you feel in your chest before you hear it. There are settling tanks, filtration racks, a small electrical substation in a wire-mesh cage. The construction is organized but not dense — the machines are scattered across the cavern floor like pieces on a board, connected by the pipes and by catwalks of steel grating that arch over the pools. The catwalks are narrow. The railings are rusted at the joints. Everything is built for function, and everything has been here long enough to have acquired a skin of mineral deposits, a patina of working life.

The scale is bounded. You can see the far wall of the cavern — maybe two hundred meters away, maybe less, the darkness makes it hard to judge. The space has edges. It is a room, not a world. And within it, the structures are sparse enough that you can see the gaps between them, the stretches of bare rock and standing water where nothing has been built. The density is low. The emptiness is part of the design.

And then there are the crabs.

They are everywhere. The floor is thick with them — not carpeted, not swarming, but *present* in numbers that make the ground seem to move when you're not looking directly at them. They are small, each one the size of a coin, with pale, soft shells that have never seen sunlight. They are a species that belongs here, that has always belonged here, or that arrived so long ago that the distinction no longer matters. They have adapted to the heat, to the mineral water, to the industrial runoff. They feed on the algae that grows on the warm pipes, on the bacterial mats that form around the settling tanks, on each other. They are the dominant life form in this place, and they know it.

They move slowly. That is the thing that strikes you first — the slowness. The heat has made them patient, or the darkness has, or the sheer abundance of everything has removed the need to hurry. They walk. They stop. They wave their claws at nothing. They cluster around the warm bases of the pumps in groups of dozens, absorbing the heat through their legs. They line the edges of the catwalks in sparse, orderly rows, like commuters waiting for a train that never comes. When you step near them, they do not scatter — they shift. A small, collective rearrangement. A tide going out by a centimeter.

The sound is loud, but it is a loudness made of small things. The pumps thrum. The pipes tick and groan as they expand and contract. Water drips — not dramatically, but constantly, a percussion of random intervals that never stops. And underneath it all, the crabs. The sound of thousands of small legs on stone, on steel, on other crabs. A dry, continuous rustling, like leaves, like paper, like static. It is the sound of the place breathing.

The vegetation is sparse but not absent. In the wettest corners, where condensation runs down the walls in thin streams, there are mats of algae — dark green, almost black, slick to the touch. On the concrete pads, where the mineral-rich water pools and evaporates, there are crusts of lichen, pale orange and white, the first colonists of the artificial surfaces. Nothing grows tall. Nothing flowers. The plant life here is microbial, marginal, a green whisper at the edges of a mineral world.

The erosion is just beginning. The water is doing its work — slowly, so slowly — dissolving the softest minerals, depositing the hardest, building stalactites that are not yet stalactites, just thickened drips on the ceiling, bumps on the floor that will be pillars in ten thousand years. The rock is still deciding its shape. The cavern is still becoming. The works have been built on a surface that is not yet finished, and in places you can see where the floor has shifted — a pipe bent at an angle it wasn't installed at, a catwalk that now slopes toward a pool that didn't exist when it was built. The place is patient. The place is slow. The place is still under construction, and always will be.

The air is hard to breathe. Not because it is toxic — though it might be, in the lowest pockets where the sulfur gathers — but because it is *thick*. Hot and wet and heavy, it resists your lungs. You sweat immediately. The sweat does not evaporate. It sits on your skin and mixes with the condensation and the fine mineral dust that hangs in the air, and you become, within minutes, a part of the place — damp, warm, slowly dissolving.

The crabs watch you. They don't have eyes that you can see, but they know you are there. They always know. They shift around you in their slow, patient way, and the rustling of their legs rises and falls like a conversation you can't quite hear, and the pumps thump, and the water drips, and the rock weeps, and the heat rises from below, and the cavern breathes, and the works endure, and the crabs endure, and the place goes on being what it is: a hot, wet, dim, loud, slow, living machine, half-built and half-grown, a settlement at the boundary between geology and industry, where the rock is still soft and the crabs are still patient and the water is still deciding where to go.
