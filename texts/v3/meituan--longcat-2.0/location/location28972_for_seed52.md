# The Vales of Shedding

---

The ground is wrong underfoot. Not dangerously so — it holds weight, it bears the press of a boot — but it gives slightly more than stone should, and when you kneel and press your palm flat, you feel a warmth that has nothing to do with the air. The substrate is young. Geologically, absurdly young. It has the color of wet slate but the texture of something still deciding what it wants to be — smooth in one step, faintly ridged in the next, as though the surface were a slow liquid that forgot to finish settling. In places it sheens with a film of moisture that never quite becomes a puddle, that beads and retracts in slow rhythm, as if the ground itself were breathing through pores too small to see.

This is the vale — one of several, though "several" may not be the right word, because the vales don't sit adjacent to each other so much as *interleave*, separated by distances that feel inconsistent depending on which direction you're facing. The elevation is low. You are in a basin, or a series of basins, or a single basin that has been folded in on itself in ways that make a mockery of cartography. The air is warm and close, carrying a humidity that never resolves into rain but never quite releases into dryness either. It exists in a state of perpetual almost. Almost raining. Almost foggy. Almost comfortable. Your skin is always faintly damp. Your clothes never fully dry. Condensation beads on every surface that holds still long enough — which, here, is most surfaces, because stillness is the dominant condition of this place.

And yet things move.

---

The vegetation is lush but it is not abundant. That is the first paradox you encounter, and it will not be the last. Great swathes of the vale floor are open — bare, damp, young stone stretching in gentle undulations toward a horizon that seems farther away than it should be given the curvature of the land. But in scattered pockets, in the low places where moisture collects, in the lee of structures that may or may not be buildings, the growth is extravagant. Ferns the size of small trees, their fronds a deep and almost black green, uncurl from crowns of fibrous tissue that emerge directly from the substrate. Mosses in colors that don't have names — not quite blue, not quite grey, something in the space between — drape over surfaces in thick, velvety cascades. Flowering things appear without stems, without leaves, just the bloom itself sitting on the ground like a dropped piece of jewelry: waxy, luminous, faintly warm to the touch. They open and close on no schedule you can discern. Some of them hum, just at the threshold of hearing, a single sustained note that changes depending on how many are open in a given area.

The spacing is the thing. You will walk for ten minutes through open, empty, glistening ground, the air thick and still, and then you will come upon a cluster of growth so dense and so tall it constitutes a grove, a room, a world unto itself. And then it ends. Not gradually — it *ends*. The last fern gives way to bare stone with a clarity that feels deliberate, as though someone had drawn a line. The lushness is real. The sparseness is also real. They coexist without contradiction, though they should.

---

The structures.

They are the reason you know this is a city, though "city" is a word that strains at its seams here. They rise from the vale floor at irregular intervals — great, slow shapes, more grown than built, though "grown" implies a biology that may not apply. They are tall but not towering, broad but not blocky, and they share a quality that is difficult to name: they look *incomplete*. Not ruined. Not under construction. Simply as though the process that made them stopped at a point that was, for their purposes, finished, but which reads to human eyes as interruption. Surfaces that seem to continue in directions that aren't quite spatial. Edges that don't resolve when you look at them directly. Openings that may be doorways or may be windows or may be something else entirely — they change their aspect depending on the angle of approach, and if you circle one, you will find that the number of openings you counted on the first side does not match the number on the second.

The material is something like ceramic, something like metal, something like the substrate of the ground itself, as though the structures were extrusions of the vale floor that had been persuaded upward. They are warm to the touch. They are faintly damp. In some of them, you can hear a low, slow pulse — not mechanical, not biological, but rhythmic in a way that suggests *process*. Something is happening inside. Something is always happening inside.

Between the structures, the infrastructure. Not roads, exactly — paths of a sort, defined by a change in the substrate's texture, slightly raised, slightly drier, winding in curves that feel organic but lead, eventually, somewhere. Conduits run along and under these paths, visible as raised ridges in the ground, and if you put your ear to them you can hear a liquid moving, or a gas, or something that shifts between states as it travels. The conduits connect the structures to each other and to the ground itself, and they are the reason the moisture behaves as it does — appearing and disappearing in patterns that, if you mapped them, would probably resolve into a logic you don't have the framework to understand.

The density of construction is sparse. You can stand in most places in the vale and see two, perhaps three structures from any vantage. The rest is open ground, scattered growth, the low warm sky. And yet the *scale* of each structure is such that the total built environment feels vast — not because it fills the space, but because each piece of it implies more. More depth. More interior. More continuation in directions you can't access.

---

The light is dim. This is the quality that defines the atmosphere more than any other — a pervasive, directionless twilight that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere. There is a sky, or something above you that functions as a sky, but it is low and close and the color of old pewter, and it does not appear to contain a sun. The illumination is ambient, diffuse, slightly warmer in some areas and slightly cooler in others, as though the light itself were being emitted by the ground, the structures, the vegetation, all contributing their small part to a general glow that never quite reaches brightness. Shadows are soft and short. Depth perception is subtly unreliable. Things at a distance appear closer than they are, or farther, depending on — something. The moisture in the air, perhaps. The warmth. The particular quality of the light, which seems to have a thickness to it, a viscosity, as though photons were moving through a medium denser than atmosphere.

At what passes for night, the dimness deepens but does not darken entirely. The structures emit their own faint luminescence — a deep amber, a pale green, colors that seem to come from within the material itself rather than from any fixture or source. The flowering things glow. The mosses glow, faintly, in their blue-grey way. The vale at night is a place of soft, sourceless color, like being inside a closed eye that is remembering light.

---

The fauna are rare, and this is perhaps the strangest thing about a place that is strange in many ways. The vegetation is lush. The water, though intermittent, is present. The warmth is adequate. By every measure, this should teem with life. And yet you can walk for an hour and see nothing move that is not plant, nothing breathe that is not the slow respiration of the ground. Occasionally — rarely — you will catch a glimpse of something at the edge of vision. A shape that might be a bird, if birds were the size of your thumb and moved in straight lines. A flicker of motion in the ferns that resolves, when you look directly, into stillness. Once, perhaps, you will see something larger: a long, low shape moving through the open ground at a distance, leaving no track, making no sound, present for a moment and then simply not present, without transition, as though it had been edited out of the scene.

The structures, too, seem largely empty. You can enter some of them — the openings will permit you, or they won't, and there is no predicting which — and inside you will find spaces that are warm, damp, dimly lit, and largely featureless. Curved walls. Floors that give slightly underfoot. Conduits running along the walls, pulsing faintly. And a quality of *occupation* that is difficult to explain: the sense that someone or something was here recently, is here now in a way you cannot perceive, will be here again. Not a haunting. A *residence*. The structures are inhabited. You are simply not equipped to detect most of the inhabitants.

---

The soundscape is quiet. This is not silence — there is too much moisture in the air, too much slow process in the ground and the structures and the vegetation, for true silence. But the sounds are small and close and they do not carry. The faint drip of condensation from a fern frond. The almost-subsonic hum of the conduits. The soft, wet sound of your own footsteps on the yielding ground. Occasionally, from inside a structure, a tone — low, sustained, resonant, felt more than heard, as though the building were a bell that had been struck long ago and was still, very slowly, returning to rest. The wind, when it comes, is warm and damp and moves through the ferns with a sound like breathing, like the vale itself were inhaling and exhaling in a cycle too slow to perceive in real time.

---

The dynamic quality of the place is the thing that takes longest to perceive, because it operates on timescales that are not your own. The vale is active. Processes are underway. The ground is still forming — not quickly, not visibly, but if you were to mark a spot and return in a week, you would find the texture changed, the contours shifted by a millimeter, a centimeter, the substrate still settling into whatever final state it is approaching. The water moves through the conduits in cycles that correspond to no tide you know. The vegetation grows, flowers, retracts, grows again on rhythms that seem to respond to something other than light or temperature — perhaps to the pulse of the structures, perhaps to the slow respiration of the ground, perhaps to a logic that has no analogue in the biology you understand.

The weather, such as it is, cycles through variations of dampness and warmth that are subtle but real. There are times when the air is heavier, when the condensation increases, when the light dims further and the vale enters a state that might be called *deep* — a thickening of atmosphere, a slowing of all processes, a sense of the place drawing inward. And there are times when the air lightens, when the moisture retreats slightly, when the structures seem to brighten and the vegetation opens more fully and the vale feels, for a few hours, almost exuberant. These cycles do not correspond to day and night. They have their own rhythm. You will learn them, or you won't.

---

The weirdness is not in any single feature. It is in the *fit* — the way the place holds together despite being composed of elements that should not coexist. Young ground and ancient-seeming structures. Lush growth and sparse distribution. Urban scale and empty quiet. Beyond-modern technology expressed in forms that look organic, geological, *natural*, as though the engineering had been so complete that it had disappeared into the landscape itself, become indistinguishable from the substrate, the moisture, the warm dim air. The sense that you are inside something — not a building, not a city, but a *process*, a vast and slow and ongoing act of creation that has not yet finished and may never finish, that is comfortable with its own incompleteness, that regards your presence with the same patient, incurious warmth as the ground itself.

You are in the vales. The vales are young. The vales are warm and damp and dim and quiet and strange, and they are, in their slow and patient way, alive.
