# The Sallow Plain

---

The ground is old. Not old the way a mountain is old — proud, defiant, still jutting — but old the way a river stone is old, the way a jawbone in the dust is old. Worn. Given up. This is a landscape that has been eroding for so long it has forgotten what it once was, and the forgetting has become its identity.

You stand on a plain of pale, compacted earth — not quite clay, not quite stone — that stretches in every direction with the gentle, almost imperceptible undulations of a sheet settled on a bed. The elevation is low, barely above the water table, and the water table is barely below you. Every few steps the ground darkens underfoot, not quite mud, not quite firm, a damp give that speaks of saturation just beneath the surface. In the shallowest depressions — and there are many, because this land has been worn so flat that even the smallest gradient becomes a basin — water pools in sheets no deeper than your ankle. They are not ponds, not really. They are the land's own moisture, pressed up through pores too compacted to drain, held in dimples too shallow to be called lakes. The water is warm to the touch. The air is warm. Everything is warm, and wet, and still.

The sky is open. That is the first thing you notice after the ground — how much of it there is. The plain extends past the reach of comfortable vision, and because the land is so low and so flat, the horizon sits at a height that makes the sky feel like a dome rather than a vault. Clouds move through it with the slow, heavy drift of things that have nowhere to be. The light is normal — not the thin light of high places, not the amber light of dust-choked air, just light, full and even, the kind that makes everything look like itself.

But the plain is not featureless. It is *sparse*, which is different. Across the open distance, things rise from the flatness at wide intervals — not regularly, not in any pattern, but with the scattered logic of whatever was hardest to erode. Knobs of darker stone, iron-rich and rust-streaked, jut a few feet above the plain like the knuckles of a buried hand. Some are no bigger than a table; others are the size of a house, their bases softened into smooth, water-rounded curves where the damp earth has crept up and receded over millennia. Between them, the plain stretches bare and pale, and the distance between each one is enough that you feel alone even when you can see three or four at once.

The vegetation is spare. It does not carpet. It *punctuates*. Clumps of a low, sedge-like growth — pale green, almost grey-green, with blades that are stiff and slightly waxy — emerge from the damp ground in tight rosettes, each clump a foot or two across, separated by yards of bare earth. Here and there, a slightly taller plant rises: a stalk, really, thick and jointed like bamboo but topped with a spray of fine, hair-thin leaves that droop under their own weight. These stalks are the tallest living things on the plain, and they reach perhaps four feet. They grow singly or in pairs, never in groves. Their roots must go deep, or wide, or both — there is not enough surface water to support anything lush. The ground between the plants is bare, and the bareness is not the bareness of death but of *patience*. The earth is mineral-crusted, faintly white in places where dissolved salts have wicked to the surface and dried, and when you kneel and press your palm flat against it, it is cool and damp and gives slightly, like flesh over bone.

Water moves here, but slowly. That is the rule of this place: everything moves slowly. The pools have no visible inlet or outlet, but if you crouch beside one and wait — and waiting is the only pace this landscape recognizes — you can see the faintest current, a barely perceptible drift of the surface film toward some imperceptible low point. In the lowest areas, where several shallow basins have merged into something larger, the water is deeper — waist-deep in places — and the surface is covered with a thin, iridescent sheen that catches the light in oily rainbows. This is not pollution. This is biology. A film of microorganisms, ancient and simple, coats the still water, and it is the base of everything that lives here.

Because things do live here. Abundantly, in fact, though you might not think so at first glance. The plain looks empty. It is not.

The sound hits you before the life does. It is *loud* — a constant, layered, droning chorus that rises from every pool and every clump of sedge and every damp depression in the earth. Insects. Not the chirping, rhythmic kind, but a sustained, buzzing, trilling wall of sound produced by creatures that have filled every available niche in this spare landscape. Water boatmen skate across the pools in numbers that dimple the surface into a living texture. Dragonflies — large ones, with bodies the color of weathered copper and wings that catch the light like mica — hover and dart above the water in numbers that seem impossible for so sparse a place. They eat well here. The water film breeds midges, and the midges breed in clouds so thick they are visible as shimmering columns above the pools, and the dragonflies eat the midges, and something else eats the dragonflies, and the chain goes on.

Birds. There are birds here, though you may not see them at first because they are the color of the plain — pale, mottled, still. They stand in the shallow water on long, thin legs, motionless, waiting. They are heron-like but smaller, with downturned bills and a habit of standing with their necks coiled tight, like springs. When they strike, it is fast — the only fast thing in this landscape — and they come up with something silver and wriggling. They swallow, coil back into stillness, and the plain returns to its slow rhythm.

In the damp earth between the pools, things burrow. You can see the evidence: small holes, neatly bored, ringed with the faintest lip of excavated soil. What lives in them is mostly unseen — a kind of large, pale cricket, perhaps, or a burrowing frog that spends the driest times underground and emerges when the water table rises. But occasionally one surfaces: a smooth, moist, grey-brown creature the size of your thumb, with enormous hind legs and eyes that sit on top of its head like periscopes. It freezes when it sees you, then bolts — a single, explosive hop — and vanishes into another hole. There are enough of them that the plain is faintly pocked with their entrances, and if you walk quietly, you can hear the soft, wet sound of them moving just below the surface.

The air smells of water and warm earth and something faintly mineral, like wet stone. There is no rot, or very little — the warmth and the constant, slow movement of water prevent stagnation. What dies here is consumed quickly, broken down by the same microorganisms that film the pools, and the nutrients are taken up by the sedges and the jointed stalks, and the cycle continues at its own pace, which is slow, slow, slow.

There is no wind to speak of. The air is heavy and still, and sounds carry strangely — the drone of insects seems to come from everywhere at once, and a bird's call, when one makes it, rings out with a clarity that seems too sharp for the muffled, damp air. The light shifts as the clouds move, and the plain changes with it: in full sun it is pale and almost blinding, the mineral crusts glinting, the water sheets throwing back mirror-bright reflections; under cloud it is softer, greyer, the colors deepening to something more saturated, the rust on the stone knobs glowing like embers.

There are no paths. No structures. No sign that anyone has ever been here, or ever will be. The plain does not invite passage — the ground is too uncertain, the water too frequent, the distances too open and too featureless to navigate by. You could walk for hours and the view would not change: pale earth, dark water, scattered stone, sparse green, and the sky above it all, enormous and indifferent.

This is a landscape at the end of something. Not dying — it is too alive for that, too full of buzzing, breeding, burrowing life — but *finished*. The erosion that shaped it has done all it can do. The mountains that once stood here are gone, ground down to this flatness, their substance dissolved and carried away by water that has itself slowed to a near-stop. The plain is what remains when everything dramatic has been taken away: the last, most patient form of the land, warm and wet and quiet except for the sound of living things going about their business in the shallows.

It will not change. Not in any way you could notice. Not in a hundred years, or a thousand. The water will rise and fall by inches. The sedges will spread and retreat by feet. The stone knobs will soften by fractions. And the plain will remain, as it has remained, as it will remain — open, sparse, warm, wet, and loud with the sound of small lives in shallow water, under a sky that goes on forever.
