They are not the builders of the city. They are its *readers*. 

To understand what lives on the Weeping Plateau, you must first abandon the idea that life here is separate from the stone. There are no inhabitants in the way an ecosystem has inhabitants—no species that merely occupies the rock. The beings of the plateau are an *emergence*, just like the water that seeps from its pores. They are what happens when the plateau becomes aware of itself.

You do not see them at first. You feel them as a shift in the quality of the dampness, a subtle increase in the weight of the air. When you finally spot one, standing motionless in the middle of the weeping stone, your mind initially rejects it as a geological feature—a jagged outcropping, a shard of basalt caught mid-fracture. It is only when it moves that it resolves into a body.

They are tall, but not in a way that suggests height; rather, they are *deep*, as if they extend far down into the stone beneath their feet. Their bodies are composed of the same dark, fine-grained substance as the plateau, but where the stone of the ground is passive, their stone is tense, mineralized sinew pulled tight over calcite bone. Their joints are not mechanical hinges but negotiated compromises—cartilage-like seams of that same third-matter, the not-quite-chitin, not-quite-mineral substance that armors the tiny crustaceans in the water-film. When they move, it is with the fluid, startling efficiency of the cat-sized predators: no wasted motion, no dramatic gestures. A turn of the head, a reaching of a limb, and then absolute stillness again.

Their skin—if it can be called skin—is the color of wet slate, and it weeps. A thin film of the plateau’s emergent moisture coats them perpetually, making them glisten faintly in the high-altitude light. In the evening, when the stone glows with its own phosphorescence, their bodies catch that dark light and hold it, outlining them in a faint, pulsing luminescence. They do not generate heat; the cold here is too absolute for wasteful mammalian warmth. They are thermal silhouettes, matching the temperature of the air and the stone so perfectly that you can stand within arm’s reach of one and feel no radiating presence. They are as cold as the plateau, as damp as the wind.

They have no faces. Where features might be, there is only a smooth, slightly concave plane of dark stone, interrupted by a single, vertical fissure that runs from the crown of the head to the center of the chest. When they breathe—and they do breathe, slowly, drawing the thin, almost-oxygen air into some internal furnace of mineral chemistry—the fissure dilates, revealing a faint, amber glow from within. It is the only warmth they possess: a deep, subterranean heat, the memory of magma, kept banked and slow in the core of their bodies. When they exhale, the air around them shimmers, and the water on their skin ripples, and for a moment they are indistinguishable from the weeping rock.

They do not speak. Sound is too inefficient for the thin air, too easily absorbed by the dampness. Instead, they communicate through the stone itself. When one of them wishes to speak, it presses a long, multi-jointed finger to the ground, and the vibration travels through the weeping film, through the pores of the rock, across the plateau at the speed of geology. Another, standing a kilometer away, receives the message not as sound, but as a sympathetic tremor in their own chest-fissure. It is a language of resonance and pressure, a deep, subsonic grammar that translates the stone’s weeping into syntax. The plateau is not merely their home; it is their nervous system, and they are its thoughts.

They tend to the city. Not by building—the city builds itself—but by *reading* it. They move through the low, long structures that emerge from the ground, trailing their fingers along the seamless walls. When a wall needs to become a doorway, it is not because they command it, but because they have read the stone’s current state and understood what it wants to become. They are midwives to the plateau’s decision-making. They walk the vast, spare distances between the structures, stepping lightly on the damp moss, pausing to inspect the translucent crustaceans in the water-film, checking the health of the stone’s slow, cold blood.

They are rare. The plateau is vast, and they are few. You might walk for days and see only the evidence of their passing: a patch of moss pressed flat by a resting body, a sudden, geometric right-angle worn into a natural stone seam by a thousand years of passing hands. When you do encounter one, it will stop. The fissure along its chest will dilate slightly, the amber glow within flickering as it processes your presence. You are warm, loud, and wet—an anomaly of heat and noise on its cold, quiet rock. It does not fear you; the plateau has no predators large enough to threaten it. It merely observes. 

And then, after a pause that seems to last as long as a weather system, it will reach out and touch you. Its hand will be cold and damp, the grip surprisingly gentle. It will lay a single, sharp fingertip against your sternum, and you will feel a vibration—not in your ears, but in your bones, in the water that makes up your blood. It is reading you. It is tasting the mineral content of your sweat, the iron in your blood, the calcium in your bones. It is deciding what you will become: a part of the city, a part of the moss, or just a brief, fading warmth on the stone before you move on.

They are the plateau’s immune system, its sensory organs, its memory. They are the slow, cold consciousness of a young rock that is still deciding what it will become. And they are weeping, always weeping, a fine mist of mineral water that catches the light as it falls, returning to the stone from which they emerged, and to which, one day, they will return.
