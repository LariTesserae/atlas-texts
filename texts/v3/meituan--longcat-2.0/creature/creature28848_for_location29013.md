The kethra do not live in the Gullet. The kethra *are* the Gullet, in the way that a river is its current, or a forest is its mycelial net. To describe them as mere inhabitants is to mistake the wave for the water. They are a singular, distributed soma—a pale, breathing membrane stretched over the skeleton of the mountain, thinking slow thoughts in light and damp.

To look at them is to look at something that has slipped through the cracks of terrestrial biology. They are the color of things that have divorced themselves from the sun: a translucent, nephrite grey, the hue of deep glacial ice or the flesh of cave fish. Their bodies are soft, yielding, amphibian—devoid of the chitinous armor of the insect world. They are vulnerable things, these creatures, their skin a thin barrier between the world and the faintly glowing coals of their internal chemistry. You can see the machinery of them through that pale flesh: the slow, rhythmic squeezing of a tubular heart, the darker knot of a digestive tract, and the branching, luminous filaments of their nervous systems, firing in slow cascades of cold light.

They are not segmented like worms, nor articulated like arthropods. They are continuous, tapering things, the length of a human hand, resembling nothing so much as a pale, fleshy root or a severed nerve ending. The "legs" the observer counts are not true legs at all. They are pseudopodia, hydraulic filaments that extend and retract with a quiet, sucking urgency. The reason the number seems to shift, the reason the eye refuses to settle, is that these appendages are transient—extruded for traction against the wet stone, or to grasp a neighbor, and then reabsorbed into the body mass. They move not by walking, but by flowing, a slow, peristaltic crawl that leaves a faint, slick trail of mineral-rich mucus on the rock.

Their awareness is the most unsettling thing about them, because it is so profoundly *alien*. A single kethra is nothing—a scrap of animated flesh, reacting to touch and moisture with the blind tropism of a plant. But the Gullet holds millions of them, pressed together in a density that dissolves the boundary between individual bodies. They are not a colony; they are a confluence. Where one kethra ends and another begins is a question without an answer; they share their fluids, their chemistry, their light. 

When you step into the Gullet and the sound changes—the clicking shifting from a random patter to a synchronized, searching rhythm—it is not that millions of tiny minds have noticed you. It is that the *system* has registered an anomaly. A stone falls, and the ripples of bioluminescence that propagate outward are not a signal passed from creature to creature; they are the physical manifestation of a thought. The kethra think in light. They compute in damp. The recursive, geometric patterns that sweep across the walls are the Gullet processing the data of your arrival: the heat of your body, the displacement of the air, the strange, dry resonance of your footsteps. 

And there is the water. The kethra and the water are locked in a symbiosis so ancient and so total that they have become a single organism. The kethra do not drink the water; they *are* the water, in a sense. The seepage from the mountain is thin, mineral-poor, nearly sterile. But the kethra take that sterile thread and weave it into broth. They absorb the cold, raw water, strip it of certain minerals, and exude it back into the pools warm, faintly sweet, rich with their own metabolic waste. The opalescent sheen on the densest pools is a culture of their own microscopic shed cells and alchemical exhalations. 

They farm the moss, yes, but the relationship is more intimate than agriculture. The moss and the kethra share a respiratory loop. The moss feeds on the kethra’s exhalations, and the kethra feed on the moss's rootlets, or perhaps on the microscopic detritus the moss traps. The tiny channels and reservoirs they "build" are not constructions. They are mineral deposits—the calcified, compressed remains of ancient kethra, layered over decades into walls and basins. The living kethra secrete a cement of calcium and iron from the weeping rock, directing the water not with intention, but with the slow, patient physics of growth. They grow toward the water, and their dead bodies shape its flow, and the water feeds the living, and the cycle closes.

Time is different for them because they are time, made flesh. A human life is a flare of heat and motion; a kethra life is a slow exhalation of the earth. They live for decades, their metabolisms ticking over at a pace that makes a hibernating bear look frantic. They do not sleep, but they enter states of near-stasis, their glow dimming to a barely perceptible ember, their bodies drying to a hard, almost crystalline scale, waiting for the water to return and reanimate them. When they move, it is with the inevitable, creeping patience of a glacier calving, or frost heaving stone.

To be in the Gullet is to stand inside the mind of a creature that does not recognize you as alive. You are too fast, too hot, too dry, too loud. You are a sudden spike of data in a system that measures its existence in centuries of seepage. The kethra do not fear you, for fear requires an imagination of the future. They do not hate you, for hate requires an ego. They simply adjust to you. The light ripples, the water warms a fraction of a degree, the clicking recalibrates. 

They are the mountain’s dream of softness. They are the dark’s answer to the light. They pulse, and seep, and glow, and they will be here, tending their slow, damp garden, long after the glacier has returned to seal the seam, and the memory of the sun has faded from the stone.
