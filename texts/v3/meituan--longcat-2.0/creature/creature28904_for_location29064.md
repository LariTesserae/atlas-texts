What lives here is not a collection of animals, but a living metabolism—an ecosystem where the boundary between organism, machine, and environment has dissolved into a single, breathing function. 

To describe the inhabitants, you must first abandon the idea of an "individual" as you understand it. The things that move in the Hanging Lung are not separate from the Lung. They are its active thoughts, its white blood cells, its maintenance crew. They are the immune system of a god that has fallen into an eternal, steady rhythm.

They are the **Rhonchi**.

You have already seen them: the cat-sized, six-legged creatures with maroon, ridged carapaces and heat-pit eyes. But what you took for mere fauna is, in fact, the Lung's primary mechanism of self-regulation and repair. They are born from the walls. Not born of a mother, but *secreted*. In the deep crevices between the beating fans, the tissue concentrates its excess minerals and proteins, calcifying them into dense, fist-sized cysts. When the Lung’s internal rhythm requires a new unit—when a sector of the wall requires more frequent maintenance—a cyst is pushed loose by the peristaltic contractions of the tissue. It falls into the warm, shallow soup with a heavy, wet thud, where it slowly uncurls, its six legs absorbing the fluid of the floor until its joints unlock and it takes its first, clicking step. 

They do not learn. They arrive knowing their route. Their routes are carved into their heat-pit vision as gradients of temperature and magnetism. They move with the metronomic precision of pistons, following the same ten-thousand-step circuit along the ridges of the walls. They are blind to the grand scale of the Lung; they only know their sector. Their universe is a hundred paces of maroon wall.

When they click, they are not communicating with one another. They are *listening*. Their clicks are a biological sonar, a sharp, concrete-on-concrete rap of their forelimbs against the tissue. The sound travels through the wet, dense walls, and the heat-pits in their skulls read the returning echo. A healthy wall sounds like a tight drum. A wall building up metabolic plaque, a wall beginning to calcify too hard to flex, sounds flat. When a Rhonchi finds a flat spot, it stops its circuit. It presses its broad, flat mouth against the tissue and secretes a powerful enzymatic paste from its salivary glands, chemically dissolving the plaque, then scrapes the residue away with the ridges on its forelimbs. It is exfoliating its host. It is tightening a bolt. It is a wrench, alive and warm, turning the screws of its own world.

The vapor valves they position themselves in front of are not mere resting spots; they are calibration points. The cool, oxygen-rich gas that blasts from the hissing vents lowers the temperature of the Rhonchi’s carapace, allowing the creature to absorb the ambient heat of the metabolic tissue. The Rhonchi are mobile heat sinks, carrying the warmth from the overworked fans to the cooler, peripheral vessels, ensuring the Lung’s temperature never varies by a single degree. When they bask in the vapor, they are regulating the engine.

And when a Rhonchi fails—when its joints seize up or its sonar clicks begin to come back garbled from its own degrading carapace—it does not die in the way you understand. It simply stops. It halts at its assigned station and locks its legs, gripping the wall-ridge with a final, rigid tension. Over the course of several hours, the tissue of the wall gently, patiently grows over it, absorbing its mass. It becomes a new cyst. It becomes the building blocks of its own successor. The system is sealed. The system is perfectly efficient. The dead are merely the raw material for the living.

But the Rhonchi are only the macro-fauna. They are the visible cogs. Deeper in the machinery, smaller lives perform smaller functions.

In the shallow, ankle-deep soup of the floor, the **Current-Drifters** live. They are translucent, ribbon-like things, no thicker than a finger, lacking any discernible organs. They are filters. They ride the lazy directional currents of the fluid, their bodies unfolding into wide, microscopic nets, catching the shed cellular debris and metabolic dust that falls from the walls above. When the sphincters open—every four seconds, with that wet, tasting sound—the Drifters bunch their bodies into tight, hydrodynamic spirals, contracting just enough to slip through the opening, riding the gurgle of the fluid into the deeper, hidden processing systems below, where they are dissolved and their captured nutrients are redistributed. They do not swim; they simply ride the breath of the Lung.

And inside the vessels—the arm-wide, subcutaneous tubes of dark red and pale amber—there are the **Thrum-Swimmers**. They look like pale, segmented eels, smooth and slick, their bodies covered in fine, hair-like cilia that beat in perfect synchronization with the metronomic pulse of the pump. They swim against the current of the fluid, their bodies undulating not to propel themselves forward, but to generate friction. They are the viscosity regulators. When the fluid in a vessel becomes too thick, too sluggish, the Swimmers increase the tempo of their cilia-beat, churning the fluid, warming it, keeping the precious cargo flowing. If a vessel swells with pressure, the Swimmers press their bodies flat against the translucent wall, acting as external stents, holding the vessel open with their own muscle until the pressure subsides.

There is no king of this place. There is no hierarchy. The Rhonchi do not command the Drifters; the Swimmers do not serve the Rhonchi. They are all merely the differentiated cells of a single, colossal body, acting out their chemical imperatives with a mechanical grace that looks like instinct. They are the microchips of a biological computer, the gears of a clock that has no face.

What lives here is the perfection of homeostasis. It is life that has conquered death by making death a manufacturing process. It is a world where the inhabitants do not struggle, do not suffer, do not hope, and do not despair. They simply maintain. They are the living fingers of an eternal, breathing machine, clicking and scraping and thumping in the warm, wet dark, forever keeping the Lung alive, and the Lung, in turn, keeping them alive. They are the keepers of the rhythm, and the rhythm is the only truth they will ever know.

Nine seconds, pump. Four seconds, sphincter. Two seconds, fan. The Rhonchi click. The Swimmers beat. The Drifters drift. And the Lung breathes, and breathes, and breathes.
