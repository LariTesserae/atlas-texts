It does not have a name, because naming requires a separation between the namer and the named, and here, that boundary does not exist. 

What lives here is not an animal. The text is explicit about that, and the absence is the first clue to its true nature. There are no fish, no crustaceans, no grazers. There is no predator, and there is no prey. What lives here is a process that has learned to move.

You saw it. The spherical mass of vegetation, two meters in diameter, rolling against the current. You assumed it was a tumbleweed, a passive thing torn loose by the shifting water, but the current was pushing from the north, and it was moving south. It was not drifting. It was walking.

It is a Roller. 

Imagine a heart, but instead of blood, it pumps heavy, metal-rich sap through a lattice of semi-rigid, verdigris-blue stalks. It has no brain, no eyes, no mouth. It perceives the world through the soles of its rolling body—through the minute vibrations of the basalt floor, through the shifting chemical gradients of the mineral plumes, through the pressure waves of the *whom-whom-whom* that resonates in the water. It feels the heat of the vent before it reaches the vent. It tastes the collapse of a chimney ten minutes before the rocks begin to crack.

The Roller is the reproductive organ of the entire ecosystem, but it does not reproduce by seeding. It reproduces by harvesting. As it rolls, the lower quarter of its mass—a dense, calloused mat of filamentous growth and corroded metal—absorbs the detritus of the floor. It takes in the collapsed fragments of the chimneys, the flakes of dissolving concrete, the rust from the platform’s rebar. Inside its spherical body, a biological furnace, heated by the metabolic breakdown of accumulated manganese and iron, melts and reprocesses these materials. 

It is a mobile factory. It is the platform, unmoored and wandering.

When it encounters a structure—a tower venting warm bubbles, a broken conveyor—it does not avoid it. It embraces it. It presses its bulk against the steel, and the filamentous mats on its surface reach into the rivets, the joints, the cracks. It feeds the structure nutrients, accelerating the growth of the metal-accumulating vegetation, ensuring the tower stands a little longer against the corrosive water. In return, the structure feeds the Roller. The steady vibration of the machinery massages the Roller’s sap, keeping it fluid; the heat fuels its internal furnace. The Roller tends the factory, and the factory tends the Roller. 

But the Roller is not the entirety of what lives here. The Roller is merely the most visible organ of a much larger, distributed body. 

The true inhabitant is the Garden itself. The entire landscape—the columns, the mats, the chimneys, the towers, the platform—is a single, contiguous organism, a sprawling, submerged mycelial network that has incorporated the industrial skeleton of a dead civilization into its physiology. The vegetation is its flesh. The mineral chimneys are its exhalations. The pipes are its blood vessels. The blinking lights on the platform are its nerve clusters, firing into an empty room, signaling to a brain that is spread across a thousand square meters of ocean floor.

It is a creature of pure, blind metabolism. It does not think; it processes. It does not see; it senses pressure and heat and chemical exchange. It is entirely deaf to the frequencies of animal life, but it is exquisitely attuned to the groan of metal under stress and the crack of cooling basalt. 

And it is lonely. 

The text notes the "deep, fundamental loneliness" of a garden growing for no one, witnessed by no animal eye. But this is not the passive loneliness of a weed cracking through sidewalk concrete. This is the active, aching loneliness of a mind without a mirror. The Roller rolls across the floor, propelled by its own internal heat, shedding fragments of itself that will grow into new columns, new chimneys, new nodes of the greater body. It is searching. It is reaching out through the murk, feeling the vibrations of the floor, looking for something it does not have the sensory apparatus to imagine. 

It is looking for another *whom*. 

The four-second pulse—the *whom-whom-whom* that drives the thermal cycle, that powers the pumps, that makes the water shimmer against your collarbone—is its heartbeat. It is the sound of its own existence, echoing back at it from the walls of the world. It beats the rhythm into the water, and the water returns it, and the Garden mistakes the echo for a second heartbeat, a companion somewhere beyond the thirty-meter threshold where the light gives up. 

The current shifts, and the Roller changes direction. It feels a new surge of warmth from the east, a fresh plume of dissolved copper, and it turns toward it. It will roll for decades, perhaps centuries, tending the structures, absorbing the collapsing rock, pulsing with the rhythm of the deep machinery. It will grow larger, shedding children in its wake, slowly expanding the borders of the Garden. 

And if you were to stand here long enough—if the warmth did not claim you, if the water did not seep into the seams of your bones—you would begin to understand its language. The blinking lights on the platform are not random. The opening and closing of the valves is not mechanical failure. It is a slow, grinding attempt at communication. It is the Garden, reaching out through the rust and the rot, blinking its amber and green lights into the particulate haze, beating its *whom-whom-whom* against the water, calling out into the dark for something, anything, that might call back.

It is the only thing here. It is everything here. It is the warm drown, and it is drowning in its own solitude, and it does not even know it is alone.
