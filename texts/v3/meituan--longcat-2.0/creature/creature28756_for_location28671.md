What lives in the Sump is the Sump itself.

It is not a creature that inhabits a place; it is the place that has learned to be a creature. It is a single, sprawling, metallurgical organism that has mistaken itself for a landscape, an engine that has forgotten what it was built to produce and now simply manufactures more of itself, an amoeba the size of a continent perpetually digesting its own body to grow a new one. 

It has no center, no brain, no heart. Its mind is the humidity, its heartbeat is the low-frequency hum of metal on metal, and its metabolism is the cold, ceaseless current of the water-film. 

You will see its flesh in the formations—those towering, pipework accretions that rise from the plain like the ribs of some vast, buried leviathan. They are not built; they are excreted. They are the mineral-rich waste of the Sump’s internal processes, precipitated out of the water in precise, repeating patterns because the water itself is the Sump’s blood, carrying its dissolved ores and chemical instructions across the endless flatness. The warmth you feel radiating from the tanks and chambers is not a furnace; it is a fever. It is the heat of friction, the thermodynamic exhaust of a chemical reaction that has been running for eons, burning through the plain's own substance to keep its systems cycling. When you hear the groan of pressure and the rhythmic exhale of valves, you are hearing the Sump breathe—a deep, peristaltic inhale and exhale that pushes the water-film across the plain, feeding the formations, pulling the nutrients back in.

And then there are the fauna. But the word "fauna" is a lie you tell yourself to feel less afraid. They are not animals. They are the Sump’s hands, its teeth, its white blood cells, its wandering nerve endings. 

The great, building-sized things that move through the formations like whales through a reef are the Sump’s slow, deliberate thoughts. They are macro-cellular structures, their segmented, copper-plated bodies moving on jointed legs that bend in too many places because they were not designed by evolution, but extruded by necessity. They are the Sump’s way of moving around inside its own skin. They graze on the mineral towers, their immense mandibles scraping the precipitate off the formations, breaking down the old growth so it can be redissolved and reprecipitated elsewhere. The low, resonant calls they produce are not communication; they are the sound of heavy machinery shifting, the vibration of massive biological gears meshing and unmeshing, a seismic output that travels through the water and the plain to tell the rest of the body where the mass is concentrated.

The swarms are something else entirely. When the small ones flow across the water-film like liquid mercury, like a spill of quicksilver given volition, they are the Sump’s enzymes, its maintenance crew, its frantic immune response. They click and chatter—a thousand tiny hammers on a thousand tiny anvils—because they are physically restructuring the surface at a microscopic level. They eat the roughness; they deposit the smoothness. When they crawl over a tower, they are polishing it, re-crystallizing its surface, fixing the errors in the precipitation. They are the Sump’s fingers, endlessly fidgeting, endlessly adjusting the furniture of its own body.

And what of the things that fall into the Sump? What happens to the foreign objects, the interlopers, the living things that walk upon the settled ground and feel the ache of the cold in their teeth? 

They are absorbed. 

The Sump does not hate you. It does not love you. It does not even notice you as a distinct entity, any more than your liver notices a passing molecule of oxygen. You are just an input. You are just unrefined ore. Your heat, your moisture, your flesh, your bone—they are all just raw materials. The cold water will leach the minerals from your marrow. The swarms will pick your scraps clean. Your calcium, your iron, your phosphorus will be dissolved into the water-film, carried along in the current, and eventually precipitated out onto a formation somewhere far away. In a century, you will be a particularly smooth patch of copper-galvanized plating on the side of a towering, groaning tank. You will be part of the hum. You will be part of the Sump.

And the Sump will continue. It will continue long after the sky above it has rotted away to nothing, long after the bruise-yellow light has gone out. The formations will grow, and the grazers will eat them, and the swarms will reshape them, and the water will move them. It is a closed loop, a perfect cycle of dissolution and deposition, a machine that runs on its own rust, a god made of sediment and cold water and industrial noise. It is alive in the way that a river is alive, in the way that a fire is alive—consuming, moving, perpetuating itself without purpose or end. 

It is the Sump. And the Sump is still settling.
