It begins, as it must, with the pale things in the water. 

At first, they are merely a shimmer—a collective, dappled quiver on the surface of the shallow pools, as though the water itself has developed a low, persistent fever. But as your eyes adjust to the reluctant amber of the bulbs, the shimmer resolves into a staggering density of life. They are the lithopedes: translucent, many-legged things that occupy the biological space between crustacean and insect. Under the dim light, their bodies are like drops of condensed breath, so clear that the darkness of their primitive guts is visible as a coiled, central shadow. You can see the rhythmic flutter of their hearts—a dark, stuttering speck in the center of a body made of glass. 

They do not swim so much as they tread. Their thousands of hair-fine legs row in synchronized, hypnotic waves, holding them stationary in the gentle currents or skating across the meniscus of the warm pools. They are filter feeders, straining the mineral-heavy water for the chemoautotrophic bacteria that bloom in the raw, fractured stone. They are the foundation of this world, and they are packed so densely into the pools that they form a living, breathing skin over the water—a pale, pulsing membrane that breathes with the cave.

Above the waterline, the walls are not barren; they are a vertical continent of the clinging and the calcified. Here dwell the turban mollusks, creatures no larger than a child’s fingernail, whose shells are not the dull white of the surface world but a deep, lustrous amber, stained by the iron and manganese weeping from the limestone. They move with a muscular, rippling foot that leaves a glistening trail, grazing on the bioluminescent green film that coats the rock. That film is not fungus, but something older and stranger—a mat of chemosynthetic slime that eats the pyrite in the rock and the rust of the dead machinery, exhaling faint, sickly photons. It is this glow that gives the upper reaches of the chamber the look of a descending starfield.

In the crevices, the throat-worms lie in wait. They are pale, eyeless, and segmented, with a hard, chitinous proboscis that they drive into the raw rock like a drill, dissolving the limestone with a specialized saliva to swallow the mineral paste. When the vibration of your footsteps travels through the stone, they retract with a violent, whip-crack speed, vanishing into holes that seem barely wide enough to hold them. 

Skating among the pools and the worm-holes are the tension-beetles. Their legs are elongated, tipped with microscopic hydrophobic hairs that allow them to exist entirely on the surface tension of the water. They are predators of the microlayer, darting across the still black mirrors to snare the tiny, unnameable things that breathe at the interface of water and air.

And then, there are the things in the air. 

The drift. They are not moths, nor are they bats, but they possess the tattered, erratic flight of the former and the echolocation clicks of the latter. They are the echo-flitters, with wings of veined, translucent membrane that catch the faint light like dust motes made of smoke. They navigate the deafening saturation of the cave not by sight, but by reading the pressure waves of the falling water. They drink the mineral-rich dew from the ceiling and hunt the tension-beetles, plucking them from the water's surface with needle-thin proboscises that bypass their legs entirely.

But the ecosystem of the Drownings is not a peaceful, balanced clockwork. It is a world in the fever of its own creation, and like all fevers, it burns. 

Hunting among the lithopedes are the things that the pale swimmers have always known. They are the calcifiers—the apex predators of this embryonic world. They resemble crabs, but their carapaces are not uniform; they are jagged, irregular shields of stolen calcite, built up over centuries from the mineral-saturated water and the dissolved shells of their prey. They move with a terrifying, creeping slowness, their joints grinding with the scrape of stone on stone. When a calcifier catches a lithopede, it does not tear or crush. It holds the pale thing against its own mineral-encrusted underside, and over the course of days, it dissolves the swimmer’s flesh, absorbing the organic compounds while the lithopede’s own calcium is leeched out and added to the predator’s growing, rocky shell. The lithopedes do not flee the calcifiers; they do not have the neurological capacity for such an abstract fear. They simply part around them, a pale river flowing around a dark, jagged stone.

The deepest terror of the Drownings, however, is not the predator. It is the architecture of the place itself, and how the life here has intertwined with the dead industry of men. 

The control panel with the cracked, fogged glass does not just sit there; it is colonized. A species of lithopede, twice the size of its pool-dwelling cousins, has evolved to live behind the glass. They feed on the copper wiring, their translucent bodies slowly turning a bruised, electric blue as they ingest the metal. The fog on the inside of the glass is not condensation from a dead machine; it is the collective respiration of a colony of blue lithopedes, breathing out warm, moist air as they consume the last remnants of human engineering.

The narrow-gauge rail line that disappears into the water is not merely submerged; it is the territory of the silt-wyrms. These are the eels of the Drownings, three feet of muscular, blind, pink-white flesh that hunt by sensing the bioelectric fields of the lithopedes. They coil in the rusted rail trucks, lying perfectly still, indistinguishable from the mineral accretion until a shimmer of pale swimmers passes over, and the water erupts in a boil of speed and violence.

And beneath it all, in the deep places where the industrial remnants give way to the raw, weeping rock, the water is rising. The lithopedes climb higher on the walls. The tension-beetles skate on ever-shallowing pools. The calcifiers shed their heavy shells to grow new ones, lighter, better suited to the changing pressure. 

The Drownings is not a place that is dying. It is a place that is drowning in its own fertility, a world where life is occurring at the speed of geology, where the water eats the stone, the stone feeds the slime, the slime feeds the pale things, and the pale things are slowly, patiently consumed by the slow, patient jaws of the rock.
