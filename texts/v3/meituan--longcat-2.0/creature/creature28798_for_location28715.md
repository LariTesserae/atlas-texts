To ask who lives in the Sumpworks is to ask the wrong question. It implies a separation between the inhabitant and the inhabited, a tenant and a home. Here, that boundary has dissolved. The things that live in the Sumpworks do not occupy it; they *are* it, and it is them. 

But if you must point to something and say, "There, that is alive," you have to look at the tripartite symbiosis that holds the place together: the Keepers, the Weepers, and the Iron Heart.

***

**The Keepers**

The people of the Sumpworks are few, and they are quiet, but they are not passive. They are the conscious mind of the settlement, the ones who remember the original purpose of the rusted stenciling on the factory walls. 

They are small, these people. Generations of moving through narrow, root-woven corridors have favored a certain compactness, a economy of movement. Their skin is pale, perpetually damp, carrying the faint, clean smell of the cistern water and the mineral tang of verdigris. Their eyes are adapted to the green-tinged dimness; they do not squint in the dark, but rather open wider, catching what little light filters through the canopy. 

They move through the Sumpworks the way blood moves through capillaries—slowly, necessarily, without wasted motion. You will see them on the high walkways, barefoot, their toes gripping the living wood with the sureness of a spider on a web. They carry tools: not the heavy, violent tools of demolition, but the delicate instruments of maintenance. Pruning shears honed to a razor’s edge. Oil cans with long, curved spouts. Strips of oiled leather for wrapping leaking pipe joints.

Their work is the work of mediation. A root begins to pry a brick from the old factory wall; the Keeper does not kill the root, but redirects it, tying it back with hemp, guiding it toward a crack where its tension is needed to hold the structure together. A pump begins to stutter, its rhythm faltering; the Keeper is there within the hour, listening to the engine with a stethoscope made of carved wood and brass, replacing a gasket, adjusting a valve. They speak to the machinery in low, rhythmic murmurs, a constant, soothing litany of pressure and flow. They are the gardeners of rust, the shepherds of water, and they know that if they stop tending, the Sumpworks will not die—it will simply swallow them whole.

**The Weepers**

The flora of the Sumpworks is not a garden. It is an occupying force that has reached a détente with the architecture. The dominant species—the locals call them Weepers—are a type of arboreal vine with a root system that behaves less like a plant and more like a hydraulic jack. 

The Weepers are thirsty. They drink the water table, they drink the rain, they drink the condensation that beads on the rusted corrugated steel. Their leaves are broad, waxy, and dark green, angled to catch the diffuse gray light, shedding the constant moisture in a perpetual, gentle weeping. It is this weeping that gives the Sumpworks its ambient sound, the constant *plink-plink-plink* of water falling from leaf to root to pool.

But the Weepers are also the structural engineers of the new world. Their roots seek out the path of least resistance: the mortar joints between bricks, the gaps around window frames, the drainage holes in the steel. Over decades, these roots thicken, exerting tons of slow, patient pressure. They do not destroy the buildings; they *lace* them. A factory wall that should have crumbled fifty years ago stands upright because it is bound by a cage of living wood. The Weepers hold the Sumpworks together even as they consume it.

In the hollowed-out cisterns, a different kind of Weeper thrives—a pale, filamentous algae that forms a dense, spongy mat. This is the filter. The people feed the rainwater into the tops of the hollow trunks, and the algae drinks the impurities, the spores, the decay, and exudes water so clear it tastes of nothing but cold glass. The Weepers are the kidneys of the settlement, and the Keepers tend them with the same reverence they give the pumps.

**The Iron Heart**

And then there is the machine. 

At the center of the Sumpworks, by the tannin-dark pool, the great pump throbs. It is a beast of iron and brass, a relic of the industrial age, its housing green with verdigris, its pipes wrapped in a living sleeve of moss that has grown over the leaks like a scab over a wound. 

The pump is alive, in the way that a heart is alive. It has a rhythm—a slow, heavy *thump-whoosh, thump-whoosh*—that dictates the pace of life in the settlement. When the pump speeds up, the water level in the cisterns rises, and the Keepers know to open the sluices, sending water to the rooftop gardens. When the pump slows, the water table drops, and the Weepers retract their roots slightly, tightening their grip on the walls. 

The pump is the only thing in the Sumpworks that is entirely artificial, and yet it is the most dependent. It cannot eat the rust. It cannot drink the rain. It cannot repair its own gaskets. It requires the Keepers to feed it oil and replace its worn parts. It requires the Weepers to cool its housing and cushion its vibrations with their moss. It requires the pool to give it water. 

***

**The Symbiosis**

This is what lives in the Sumpworks: a closed loop of dependency. 

The Keepers maintain the Iron Heart. The Iron Heart feeds the Weepers. The Weepers filter the water and hold up the roofs over the Keepers' heads. It is a triangle of survival, a perpetual motion machine powered by decay and growth in equal measure.

If you stand at the edge of the central pool, watching the ripples spread from the silent springs, you can feel it. The boundary between the three blurs. The Keeper who places her hand on the pump’s vibrating housing to diagnose a fault is feeling the pulse of the machine, but she is also feeling the water moving through the roots of the Weepers. The moss that wraps the pipe is keeping the machine alive, but the machine is keeping the moss wet. 

There are no individuals in the Sumpworks. There is only the organism. It breathes in the warm, wet air. It drinks from the dark pool. It creaks and groans and drips, a vast, quiet, green and rust-colored thing that has settled into the shallow depression of the earth and decided, after all these years, to simply *be*.
