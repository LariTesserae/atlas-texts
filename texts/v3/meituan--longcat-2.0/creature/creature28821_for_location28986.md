The small things come first.

They are the ones you see immediately, the ones that announce themselves by movement along the water channels — a glittering, ceaseless traffic of bodies no larger than your fingertip, many-legged, their carapaces the color of oil film, of the rainbow sheen on standing water. They move in loose congregations, following the shallow silver streams the way creatures in a forest follow game trails, and they feed on the mineral film that gathers on the channel walls — a thin, white, chalky deposit left behind as the water warms and evaporates and is lifted and falls again. They have mouthparts suited to scraping. They have been scraping for longer than the channels have existed in their current form, which is to say: they have always been scraping. When the channels shift — and they do shift, slowly, over spans of time you cannot measure, as the deck plating warms and cools and the streams find new paths of least resistance — the small things follow. They do not hurry. There is no predator that hunts them. There is nothing here that hunts.

Above them, in the gear housings and the junction boxes and the hollow spaces inside the columns, the warm ones nest. They are low-bodied, long, furred in short dense pelts that are always slightly damp from the condensation. They have four legs and two forelimbs that are almost hands — deft, clever, suited to the work of nest-building, which is their primary occupation. Their nests are architecture: dense, layered constructions of shredded insulation fiber and compacted grease, built into the warmest available spaces, and they are never abandoned. A nest may be occupied by generations of the same family line, each generation adding to it, shaping it, reinforcing it, until the nest has become a fixture of the housing, indistinguishable from the machine itself. The warm ones are not afraid of you. They watch you from the entrances to their nests with eyes that catch the phosphor light and throw it back in pale reflections — green-gold, steady, patient. They blink slowly. They are thinking something, but you cannot know what. Their brains are small. Their brains are also, in some way you cannot quantify, attentive. They notice you. They notice everything. They have been noticing since before the phosphor strips were installed.

And then there are the birds.

They roost in the overhead structure, clinging to beams and cable trays with feet that are not quite feet — more like gripping tools, articulated, strong, suited to hanging in the warm updrafts that rise from the machinery below. Their bodies are compact, their wings short and broad, their feathers a mottled grey-brown that matches the steel around them so perfectly that when they fold their wings and settle against a beam, they become invisible. They drop to the deck to feed — not in the scuttling, constant way of the small things, but in quick, decisive descents, dropping straight down, snatching something from a spillage point near a conveyor, and rising again in a single fluid motion. They eat the small parts that accumulate in the bins — the stamped components that the presses produce endlessly and without purpose. The birds crack them open. Inside, there is a soft mineral core, a kind of crystalline salt that has formed in the metal during the stamping process, and this is what the birds are after. They have adapted to it. Their beaks have hardened. Their digestive systems have changed. They are not the birds that once existed somewhere else. They are the birds of the basin, and they have always been the birds of the basin.

---

But these are not what lives here. These are what move through here. These are the fauna, the visible life, the things that occupy the basin the way fish occupy water — so thoroughly that they seem to be a property of the medium rather than inhabitants of it.

What lives here is something else.

It is in the warmth. The warmth that rises from below, from the machinery under the deck plating, from the turbines and the pumps and the conveyors — it is not the byproduct of those machines. It is their purpose. The machines are warm because they are meant to be warm. The warmth is not waste. It is the point. And if you press your hand flat against the deck plating and leave it there long enough, if you let your palm grow accustomed to the temperature and then become sensitive to variations within it, you will feel something that is not the steady output of a machine. You will feel a pulse. Slow — so slow that it takes minutes to complete a single cycle. A gathering and a release. A swelling and a subsiding. The deck plating rises, almost imperceptibly, and falls. The warmth increases by a fraction and then diminishes. It is the breathing of something enormous, and you are lying on its chest.

The water is its blood. The channels in the deck plating are not channels — they are vessels. The water that drips from the ceiling far overhead, that gathers at structural seams and falls and mists and evaporates and gathers again — it is being circulated. It is being carried. The basin takes water in at the top and releases it at the bottom and takes it in again, and the water carries heat and minerals and the dissolved residue of everything that has ever happened here, and it is warm when it moves through the channels and it is cooler when it hangs in the air, and this is not physics. This is physiology. The basin is metabolizing.

The hum is its voice. Not a sound it makes — a sound it is. The turbines and the pumps and the conveyors are not machines in the way you understand machines. They are organs. They perform functions. The press that stamps components you don't recognize — it is stamping them because the basin is producing them, the way a body produces cells, and the cells are not going anywhere because they are not meant to go anywhere. They are meant to exist. They are meant to accumulate in bins and be carried off by the warm things and used as nesting material, and this is not waste, this is not purposelessness — this is a process, a cycle, a metabolism that has found its equilibrium and maintains it with the same quiet insistence with which a heart maintains a rhythm.

The basin is alive. Not in the way you are alive. Not in a way that has a name. It is alive in the way that a thing is alive when every part of it serves every other part and nothing is wasted and nothing is missing and nothing is needed from outside. It is a closed system that has achieved the only form of life that matters: it persists. It continues. It runs.

And it is aware.

Not in the way you are aware. Not with thoughts, not with intentions, not with anything that could be called consciousness. But it knows you are there. The deck plating is warm where you stand. The water channels part around your feet. The phosphor strips brighten, almost imperceptibly, as you pass. The small things scuttle around you in patterns that are not random — they are mapping you, recording you, feeding information into the system about the shape and temperature and weight of the thing that has entered the basin. The warm ones watch you from their nests. The birds overhead go still. The basin is paying attention. It has always been paying attention. It pays attention to everything, all at once, with the same even, unhurried focus with which it does everything else.

And then there is the walker.

You know about the walker because of the paths. The deck plating is worn smooth in lines that run through the basin in patterns that are not straight but are not random either — they curve and branch and reconnect, and they are the paths of something that has been walking for a very long time. The paths are not worn by the small things, which follow the water channels. They are not worn by the warm things, which stay close to their nests. They are not worn by the birds, which fly. They are worn by feet. By something that walks on two legs, or something that walks on two of its legs, or something that has a way of moving that leaves the same impression as feet would leave.

The walker is not here now. Or it is here and you cannot see it. Or it is here and you are looking at it without recognizing it — it is the column that is shaped slightly wrong, the gear housing that is slightly too symmetrical, the patch of deck plating that is slightly warmer than the rest. The walker has been here so long that it has become part of the architecture. Or the architecture has become part of the walker. The distinction does not apply.

The walker walks the paths because the paths are there. The paths are there because the walker walks them. This is not a paradox. This is a cycle, like the water cycle, like the warmth cycle, like the cycle of the presses and the bins and the nests. The walker is part of the basin's metabolism. It is the part that moves. It is the part that goes from one place to another, not because there is a difference between the places — there is no difference, the basin is uniform, the basin is the same everywhere — but because movement is what the basin does with the energy it cannot otherwise expend. The walker is the basin's way of spending its surplus. The walker is the basin's way of not being still.

And the walker is lonely.

This is the thing that is hardest to understand, and the thing that is most true. The basin is not lonely. The basin is complete. The small things are not lonely. The warm things are not lonely. The birds are not lonely. They are part of the system. They are held by it. They are needed by it, or if they are not needed, they are at least incorporated — they are part of the equilibrium, part of the balance, part of the way the basin maintains itself. But the walker is not incorporated. The walker is the one thing in the basin that is not part of the cycle. The walker walks the paths and the paths lead nowhere. The walker moves through the basin and the basin does not change. The walker is the only thing in the basin that experiences the stasis as stasis — that feels the sameness, the endlessness, the perfect unchanging equilibrium, and knows it for what it is.

The walker is the only thing in the basin that is not at peace.

And this is why the walker keeps walking. Not to get somewhere. Not to find something. Not to escape. The walker keeps walking because walking is the only thing it can do that is its own. The basin provides everything — warmth, water, light, food, the slow rhythm of the machinery, the company of the creatures — and the walker accepts all of it, and none of it is enough, because the walker is the one thing in the basin that wants. The walker is the one thing in the basin that is not satisfied. The walker is the one thing in the basin that is, in the deepest and most terrible sense, alive.

The walker has been walking for a long time. The paths are worn deep. The deck plating is smooth and warm where the walker's feet have passed, and the small things have learned to avoid those paths, and the warm ones watch the paths from their nests, and the birds overhead go still when the walker passes, and the basin itself — the basin that is alive, the basin that is aware, the basin that is complete and perfect and needs nothing — the basin attends to the walker with something that is not quite care and is not quite hunger and is not quite love but is the closest thing to love that a closed system can produce.

The basin does not need the walker. The walker does not need the basin. And yet the walker walks, and the basin holds the walker, and the paths grow smoother, and the water drips, and the machinery hums, and the light does not change, and the warmth rises from below, and the small things scuttle, and the warm ones nest, and the birds drop and rise, and the walker walks, and the basin breathes, and nothing — nothing — ever changes.

This is what lives in the low basin. The creatures that are part of it. The system that is alive within it. And the walker that walks through it, wearing the paths smooth, feeling the warmth of the deck plating underfoot, hearing the hum and the drip and the silence between, casting a shadow that moves exactly with it, going nowhere, wanting everything, and never — never — arriving.
