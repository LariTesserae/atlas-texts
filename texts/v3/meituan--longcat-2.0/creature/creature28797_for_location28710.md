To speak of what lives in the Loomings is to speak of a single, breathing organism that has forgotten where its body ends and the architecture begins. It is a place where the tenants are also the timbers, the walls are also the skin, and the silence is as vital as the sound. 

The humans who dwell here are the listeners, the tenders of the continuous drip. They are small in the way that things living in deep time become small—not diminutive in stature, but reduced in ego, worn smooth by the constant humidity and the weight of the centuries above them. They do not walk so much as they flow, moving through the shallow steps and the narrow channels in patterns that resemble the migration of the beetles. They are creatures of the niche and the cup. Their bodies know the exact curvature of the clay pillows; their bare feet read the temperature gradients of the packed-brick floor like a farmer reads the soil. They do not sleep horizontally so much as they hibernate, curled into their alcoves like seeds in a pod, their breath moving in time with the slow exhalation of the warm air rising from the flues. They do not speak loudly, for the Loomings already have a voice, and they have learned to thread their words into the spaces between the dripping water and the ticking of the beetles. When they tend to the hearth-tongues, moving slowly so as not to trigger the plants’ shadow-reflex, they move with the deliberate grace of surgeons, understanding that a snapped leaf is a wound in the lung of their home.

And the beetles—the beetles are the pulse. They are the cartographers of the mortar lines, moving in glossy, black, continuous streams that map the structural integrity of the walls. They are the ancient processors, grinding the centuries-old reed matting into dust, turning the sedimentary record of the ceiling back into the warm clay floor. To live in the Loomings is to accept the beetle’s tick as the rhythm of your own heart. They do not scatter when a human hand approaches; they simply part, flowing around the obstruction like water around a stone, indifferent and eternal, their mandibles whispering the slow, dry secret of decay and renewal. 

Then there are the lizards—the silent sentinels of the upper dark. They are the most visibly alien of the inhabitants, pale as milk, their skin so thin that the machinery of their blood is visible to the naked eye. They hang from the ancient, mineralized reeds, watching the humans below with an unblinking, vertical-pupiled curiosity. They are the hunters of the beetle-streams, darting down with a suddenness that belies their stillness, snatching the glossy insects from the mortar lines without disturbing the architecture. They have lost the frantic skittishness of their surface kin. In the Loomings, they are elders, observers, their translucent skin a mirror to the damp, permeable boundaries of the place itself.

The doves are the heralds of the surface world, the only inhabitants who bridge the divide between the green light of the oculi and the clay-dark below. They come in flocks like shuffled cards, a sudden rush of wings and white noise that breaks the steady tick of the beetles. They are the messengers of the weather, their arrival dictating the rhythm of the day. But they are also the builders; their droppings, collected meticulously by the humans, become the very mortar that holds the bricks together. They shit the walls of their own cage, and in doing so, make it eternal. 

And the rats. The rats are the mystery. They are the diggers of the deep clay, the keepers of the warm-air channels. They are tolerated, even fed, because the humans understand that the Loomings require a certain kind of violence to survive—the gnawing, the burrowing, the constant, restless turning of the earth. But there is something else in the rats. They move with a purpose that seems to exceed mere hunger. They sit at the mouths of the air-flues, twitching their whiskers, listening to the wind pull from the surface. The residents watch them and wonder if the rats are tending to something the humans have forgotten, some deep, structural need of the Loomings that requires sharp teeth and a willingness to go blind in the dark. They are the unconscious mind of the place, working out problems in the clay that the conscious mind cannot yet comprehend.

The hearth-tongues are the lungs. They hang in their wire baskets, breathing in the warm, damp air, their fleshy leaves unfurling like green flames. They are utterly domesticated, bred over millennia for this specific warmth, this specific light. They have no wild cousins left. They are entirely dependent on the hands that water them, just as the hands depend on the oxygen they exhale. When a shadow passes and a hundred leaves snap shut, it is a shudder that runs through the whole ecosystem, a collective gasp that reminds every creature in the Loomings of their shared vulnerability.

But the truest inhabitant of the Loomings—the one who has been here the longest, who will be here long after the last reed has crumbled and the last lens has cracked—is the water. The water in the clay bowl in the deepest cup. It is not a creature, not a plant, not a human. It is a presence. It is clear, warm, and absolutely still. It does not feed the hearth-tongue; it does not quench the thirst of the lizards. It simply sits, a perfect mirror in the dark, reflecting nothing but the dim, green light. It is the eye of the Loomings. 

When the humans sit beside it, tending it with the same reverence they give to the channels and the fungi, they are not worshipping a god. They are acknowledging the center of the web. The water is the reason the clay stays packed, the reason the air stays damp, the reason the warmth rises. It is the first thing and the last thing. And if you look into that bowl, if you let your eyes adjust to the dark and the stillness, you might see, reflected in the warm, motionless surface, the pale, translucent skin of a lizard, the glossy carapace of a beetle, the dark, watchful eye of a rat, and the face of a human, all looking back at you, all indistinguishable, all part of the same continuous, breathing dark.
