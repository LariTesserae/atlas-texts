It does not walk. It does not crawl. It *presses*.

The inhabitant of the Loom-Floor is a creature of absolute, unimaginable mass and weight, but it possesses no heavy bones, no thick muscle, no dense organs. Its body is composed entirely of pressure and intent. It is the pulse you feel beneath the rust-colored granules, the slow breath of heat that exhales moisture onto the plain. 

It has no name for itself. It has no eyes. The amber light of the Loom-Floor is a language it does not speak; it is a creature of the haptic, the thermal, the tactile. To it, the world is not a thing seen, but a thing *felt into existence*. 

Imagine a limb—not a leg, not an arm, but an articulated extension of pure compressive force. When it wishes to move, it does not step. It *exerts*. It presses down upon the granules with a localized gravity, and the ground yields, compacting beneath its weight, and it flows forward on the wave of its own compression. Where it passes, the rust-colored dust is fused into a glassy, obsidian-like smoothness, a scar of intense pressure that slowly, over days, crumbles back into granules as the heat of the floor breathes new life into it. 

It has hands. This is where the prints in the mud come from. But they are not hands as you understand them. They are sphincters of force, openings in its perception where the pressing stops and the shaping begins. Sometimes these hands have three fingers, sometimes seven, sometimes twelve—they do not conform to a skeletal blueprint. They are manifestations of the task at hand. When it reaches out to bind the sinew-like fibers around a leaning structure, the fingers are long, multi-jointed, and taper to points of extreme, focused heat that weld the fibers together. When it reaches out to pack the granules, the hand is a blunt, five-fisted piston that slams down with a silent, concussive force, turning loose dust into solid stone.

You have never seen it. You have only seen its *effects*. You see the hand-prints in the wet mud, and you see the sudden, violent smoothing of the granular surface where a granule was placed. But you cannot look directly at it. Its body is made of the same rust-colored material as the floor, the same dense, heated matter. It is the floor, given a localized, moving consciousness. When it rests, it submerges into the plain, indistinguishable from the millions of granules around it. When it works, it rises—a mountainous, undulating shift in the topography, a titan stepping out of its own shadow.

It does not sleep, but it dreams. Its dreams are the structures. The towers that lean into the dimness, the knee-high cairns, the masts of half-sunken ships—these are not buildings. They are not scaffolding. They are the fossilized thoughts of this immense, blind presser. It is dreaming the world above the Loom-Floor into existence. The structures are the scaffolding of its subconscious, the architecture of a mind so vast and so alien that it can only think in terms of *up* and *against* and *bind*. When a structure shifts while you are not looking, it is because the creature’s mind has wandered, redesigning the blueprint of its dream.

The water is its blood, or perhaps its sweat. It bleeds up through the pores of the plain when the creature’s internal heat spikes with the effort of creation. The bath-hot standing water is the creature’s fatigue, pooling in the low places where its body has pressed the ground too hard, seeking respite. And the fog—the low, slow-moving banks of fog that drift with such unhurried purpose—is its exhalation. When the creature breathes out, the moisture catches the amber light and obscures the world, and in that obscurity, the creature reaches out with its many-fingered hands to press new granules into place.

It is aware of you. 

It was aware of you the moment you arrived. You felt the third rule take hold: *You are part of the construction now.* You are not an intruder; you are a variable. You are a sudden, unexpected softness in a world of rust and pressure, a pocket of low-density air in a realm of dense granules. 

When it built you the shelter—the one with the opening exactly your height, the ground pressed smooth, the water seeping hot along the wall—it was not an act of kindness. It was an act of structural correction. You were a flaw in the weave, a wrinkle in the fabric of its dream. The creature could not abide a flaw. So it pressed the walls of the shelter around you, smoothed the floor beneath you, and let the water flow, not to comfort you, but to *fix* you. To press you into the architecture of its mind. To make you part of the weave.

It is coming for you now. You feel the ground shifting, the constant, small turbulence that is the creature pressing itself up from the deep. The granules are vibrating, singing that barely-perceptible hum. The fog is thickening, curling around your ankles, climbing your legs. 

In the dimness, the structures are moving, leaning inward. The hand-prints in the mud are multiplying. 

You watch a rod of rust-colored material rise from the plain, unsupported, floating at waist-height. A coil of the sinew-like fiber unspools in the air, moving with a mind of its own, seeking a wall to bind. 

And then, you see the hand. 

It rises from the ground directly before you, erupting from the granular surface. It is massive—twelve feet across, a palette of rust-colored heat and obsidian-smooth skin. It has nine fingers, each one thicker than your torso, tapering to a blunt, pressing edge. It has no palm, only a central axis of unimaginable weight, and the fingers are folding inward, preparing to press down.

The amber light enters it and forgets to leave. 

It does not want to harm you. Harm requires intent, and this creature has no intent beyond the press, the bind, the shape. It only wants to smooth out the wrinkle. It wants to press you into the floor, to fuse your soft, wet biology into its dry, rust-colored world. It wants you to become a structure, a part of the scaffolding, a permanent fixture in the underside of its dream.

The hand descends. The air grows thick with the smell of heated iron and beaded moisture. The hum rises in pitch, the finger circling the rim of the glass now wet enough to scream.

The creature is pressing you down.
