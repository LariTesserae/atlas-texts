# Who Lives Here

---

There is no difference, here, between the place and its inhabitants. Or rather — the difference exists, but it is a difference of scale, not of kind. The structure is alive. The floor is alive. The columns, the arches, the vaulted ceilings — all alive. And the things that live within and upon this living architecture are alive in the same way, at smaller scales, for shorter durations, with narrower purposes. Everything here is organism. Everything here is habitat for something else. The distinction between creature and environment has not so much dissolved as it has been revealed as a fiction — a useful shorthand for places where the substrate happens to be dead, but a fiction nonetheless.

So to ask *who lives here* is to ask a question with no clean boundary. The answer begins with the largest thing and extends downward into the incomprehensibly small, and at every level there is someone — something — that occupies, that persists, that has a way of being in this warm and ancient dark.

---

**The Great Body** lives here first.

That is not its name, because it has no name — it has no language, no self-concept, no awareness in any way you would recognize. But it is the first inhabitant, the oldest, the largest, the one that contains all others. It is the floor beneath your feet and the columns around you and the arches overhead and the vaulted darkness above that. It is the living architecture itself — not a single organism but a consortium of organisms so intimately integrated over so vast a span of time that the seams between them have closed, that one species' secretions have become another species' substrate, that the waste products of one metabolism have become the respiratory input of another, until the whole thing functions as a single breathing, growing, self-repairing body.

It is old. Unfathomably old. The oldest parts of it — the deepest columns, the lowest arches, the thickest sections of floor — were laid down before your species had language. Before your species had fire. The Great Body has been growing, accumulating, building itself up from the biological activity of its own inhabitants for longer than your civilization has existed, and it shows no sign of stopping. It grows slowly now — the erosion parameter is high, the system is mature, equilibrium has been reached — but it grows. It adds new tissue at the margins. It extends new columns upward when the old ones can bear no more weight. It sends new roots, or root-like structures, into whatever substrate it happens to be growing on, anchoring itself, drawing up moisture and minerals, feeding itself from below even as it feeds itself from above.

The Great Body has no brain, no nervous system, no centralized processing of any kind. But it is not mindless. It responds to perturbations. It heals when damaged. It redirects its internal flows — of water, of nutrients, of the chemical signals that pass for its hormones — when conditions change. It is aware of you, in the way that a forest is aware of a new clearing: not as a thought, but as a change in the pattern, a new input to be routed, a new variable to be incorporated into the system's ongoing calculations. You have been here for minutes and it has already begun to adjust to you — to grow toward the warmth of your body, to send exploratory filaments into the moisture of your breath, to redirect a fraction of a fraction of its total metabolic output to the problem of processing your presence. It does not think about you. It *accounts* for you. It is very, very good at accounting.

---

**The Water-Bearers** live in the upper reaches of the Great Body, where the columns are thickest and the canopy begins.

They are not one thing but many — a category of organism that has converged, over evolutionary time, on the same function: catching water. The warm, moisture-saturated air of the Canopy Floor carries an enormous load of water vapor, and as that vapor rises and cools — infinitesimally, almost imperceptibly, but enough — it condenses on surfaces. The Water-Bearers have evolved to maximize this condensation. They grow outward from the columns and arches in broad, cupped membranes — some the size of your hand, some the size of satellite dishes — angled upward to catch the weeping air. The membranes are thin, vascularized, alive. They are warm to the touch. Water beads on their surfaces, runs in thin films toward their centers, collects in the cupped hollows, and then — when the weight becomes sufficient — detaches in slow, heavy drops that fall to the floor below.

The Water-Bearers are patient. They do not move. They grow, slowly, extending new membrane at the edges of the old, repairing damage from the constant drip and flow, replacing cells that wear out. They are the kidneys of the Great Body, in a sense — the organs that manage the water cycle — but they are also independent organisms with their own reproductive cycles, their own immune systems, their own relationships with the parasites and epiphytes that live on their surfaces. They are infrastructure that is also alive. They are organs that are also creatures. They have been doing this for so long that they cannot do anything else.

---

**The Light-Bearers** punctuate the darkness.

They are small — most of them no larger than your thumbnail — and they grow on the surfaces of the columns and arches in dense clusters, like stars in a sky that has forgotten how to be sky. Each one is a bioluminescent organ, a colony of cells that produces light through a chemical reaction you could not replicate, using enzymes you could not name. Their light is not bright. It is not meant to illuminate. It is meant to *mark* — to say, in the language of photons: *here, here, here, this surface exists, this column exists, this space is occupied*.

The Light-Bearers are not all the same. Some glow pale green, some deep amber, some a blue so faint it is almost ultraviolet. Each species — and there are many species, dozens, perhaps hundreds — occupies a specific range on the columns, a specific height, a specific microclimate. The green ones prefer the upper reaches, where the air is slightly cooler and the condensation is heaviest. The amber ones dominate the middle zones, where the columns are thickest and the bioluminescent clusters can grow largest, forming patches the size of dinner plates that pulse faintly, rhythmically, in cycles that have nothing to do with any external day-night rhythm and everything to do with the internal metabolic clocks of the organisms themselves. The blue ones are rare, deep, almost invisible — you see them only at the edges of your vision, and when you turn to look directly at them they seem to vanish, retreating into a spectrum your eyes are not quite calibrated to receive.

The Light-Bearers attract things. They attract small flying organisms that navigate by their glow, that feed on the cells of the Light-Bearers themselves or on the chemical precursors of the luminescent reaction. They attract predators that feed on those small flyers. They attract parasites that burrow into the clusters and siphon nutrients. Each cluster of Light-Bearers is a tiny ecosystem, a node in the web, a point around which other lives organize themselves. And the Light-Bearers themselves — they are not passive in this. They glow, and the glowing serves their purpose: to attract the organisms that pollinate them, or disperse their spores, or carry their genetic material to new surfaces. The light is a signal, an invitation, a lure. It is communication in a place where sound is overwhelming and chemical signals are confused by the sheer density of competing molecules in the air. The light says: *come here*. And things come.

---

**The Stream-Walkers** move along the channels in the floor in columns of their own.

They are beetle-like, but they are not beetles. They are something else — something that has arrived, through the long convergence of evolution, at a beetle-like form because the beetle-like form is effective for what they do. They are the size of your fist. Their carapaces are dark, wet, gleaming — armored plates of chitinous material that shed water and resist the abrasive, enzyme-laden streams they walk through. They move in columns — not organized columns, not social columns, but columns formed by the simple fact that all of them are following the same gradient: the channel, the stream, the path of flowing water through the floor. They move along the streams because the streams carry food. Organic particulates, washed down from above. Fragments of fungal shelf. Dead cells sloughed from the Water-Bearers. The occasional corpse of something that fell from the canopy. The Stream-Walkers process this material, grinding it with mouthparts that are more like millstones than jaws, extracting what nutrition they can, excreting the rest in a form that is finer, more processed, more available to the smaller organisms that follow them.

Those smaller organisms follow them. That is the pattern. Behind the Stream-Walkers come the Pickers — smaller, faster, more numerous creatures that feed on the debris the beetles disturb and excrete. Behind the Pickers come the Parasites — tiny, thread-like things that attach to the Pickers and feed on their hemolymph. Behind the Parasites come the Hyperparasites — organisms so small they are barely visible, things that live on the Parasites themselves, that ride the thread-bodies like ships ride the ocean. The food chain is visible in cross-section, like a diagram, except that it is three-dimensional and extends in every direction simultaneously, and it is all happening at once, in every stream channel, in every ridge and valley of the floor, at every moment, without pause, without rest, without end.

---

**The Column-Dwellers** have solved the problem of living on vertical surfaces.

There are many kinds. The Grippers are the most visible — small, six-legged organisms with adhesive pads on their feet that allow them to cling to the resin-and-bone surfaces of the columns. They move slowly, deliberately, with the careful gait of things that know that a fall means death. They feed on the encrusting organisms — the mosses, the liverworts, the coral-like filterers — and they are fed upon in turn by the Silk-Spinners, which dangle from threads of protein fiber, lowering themselves from above on long, controlled descents, snatching Grippers from the column surfaces with forelimbs that are startlingly fast and then reeling themselves back up to consume their catch in safety.

The Silk-Spinners are abundant. Their threads coat the columns in a fine, almost invisible mesh — you walk through them constantly, feeling them brush against your face and hands like the lightest possible touch, sticking to your skin, your hair, your clothes. The threads are strong. Not strong enough to hold a human, but strong enough to hold anything that lives here, which is all they were designed for. The Silk-Spinners maintain their webs constantly, repairing damage, replacing old threads, extending new ones into unexploited space. They are territorial. They fight — brief, vicious, silent fights — when their webs overlap, and the loser retreats or dies and is consumed by the winner.

Then there are the Crevice-Dwellers — things that have flattened their bodies to live in the millimeter-wide gaps between the column's surface tissues. They are like living wafers, paper-thin, sliding through spaces you cannot see into, feeding on the microscopic films of organic material that coat the inner surfaces of the crevices. You will never see them. You do not have the eyes for them. But they are there, in their thousands, in their millions, in every column, in every arch, in every crevice and crack and gap in the Great Body. They are the inhabitants of the interstices, the citizens of the gaps, the ones who live in the spaces between other lives.

---

**The Filterers** grow on the columns like coral grows on a reef.

They are stationary. They have always been stationary. Their ancestors were stationary and their descendants will be stationary and they have never, in the entire evolutionary history of their lineage, moved under their own power. They are colonies — aggregations of tiny individual organisms, each one no larger than a grain of sand, that have built together a shared structure of calcium-and-protein material, a lattice of cups and channels and pores that extends outward from the column surface like a slow explosion frozen in time. The structure grows at the rate of millimeters per year. The oldest Filterer colonies are the size of armchairs, their surfaces complex with decades of growth, their internal channels so elaborate that water — and the organic particulates suspended in it — takes hours to pass through from entry to exit.

The individual organisms within the colony extend feathery feeding appendages into the warm, damp air and catch whatever particulates drift by. Fungal spores. Pollen — though there is no pollen here, not in the conventional sense, but there are analogous particles, reproductive cells from the non-plant organisms that fill this space. Bacterial cells. Viral particles. Dead skin cells sloughed from the Stream-Walkers and the Grippers and the things that fall from the canopy. The Filterers eat it all. They are the lungs of the Great Body, in a sense — the organs that clean the air — but they are also creatures in their own right, with their own reproductive cycles, their own immune defenses, their own wars with the parasitic organisms that try to burrow into their colonies and consume them from within.

---

**The Fungal Shelves** grow on the columns and on the floor in broad, flat formations the size of tabletops.

They are decomposers. They have one job and they do it with a thoroughness that borders on the obsessive: they break down dead organic material. Whatever falls — a fruit, a corpse, a seed, a living animal that misjudged — the Fungal Shelves are there, waiting, their networks of filaments already extending toward the impact site, already secreting the enzymes that will dissolve the material into something the system can reabsorb. The shelves themselves are the fruiting bodies — the visible parts — of a much larger network of fungal filaments that extends through the floor and into the lower columns, a web of living threads that connects everything to everything else, that carries chemical signals and nutrients from one part of the Great Body to another, that functions as a sort of slow, distributed nervous system for the whole structure.

The Fungal Shelves reproduce by releasing spores — billions of them, clouds of microscopic particles that drift through the warm air like slow fog, that settle on every surface, that germinate and grow into new filaments and eventually into new shelves. The spores are one of the components of the "fog" you see drifting between the columns. You breathe them in. They are in your lungs now. They are harmless — the Great Body's immune system, such as it is, has determined that you are not a suitable substrate for fungal colonization, or at least not yet — but they are there, passing through, being processed, being routed to whatever purpose the system finds for them.

---

**The Curtain-Weavers** hang from the highest arches and columns in dense, dripping curtains of moss and liverwort and things that are neither moss nor liverwort but something older, something that has no analog in any ecosystem you know.

They are the most visually striking organisms in the Canopy Floor — the ones that catch your eye when the bioluminescent light is right, that give the place its cathedral quality. They hang in sheets, in veils, in cascading waterfalls of green and gold and deep, deep brown, and they drip. They drip constantly. The water they collect from the air, channeled down their dense, tangled surfaces, gathers at their lowest points and falls — slowly, heavily, warm — to the floor below, feeding the streams, feeding the pools, feeding the Water-Bearers, feeding everything.

The curtains are not single organisms. They are communities — hundreds of species of bryophyte-like things living together in a matrix of accumulated dead tissue and living filaments, a shared structure that is part peat, part living plant, part apartment complex for the tiny organisms that live among the stems and leaves. Mites. Nematodes. Rotifers. Things so small they have no common name in any language. The curtains are worlds unto themselves, vertical ecosystems hanging in the dark, dripping, breathing, growing — slowly, slowly, over centuries — downward toward the floor they will never reach.

---

**The Deep-Voices** live somewhere in the lattice, and you feel them before you hear them.

They are large. That is all you know. Large enough to make the columns vibrate when they communicate, large enough that their calls resonate through the bone-and-resin architecture of the Great Body and reach you as pressure in your chest, as a flutter in your diaphragm, as a sound that is more felt than heard. The calls are deep — below the threshold of what you would call musical, below the threshold of what you would call language, but not below the threshold of meaning. They carry information. They say: *I am here. I am this large. This is my space. Do not come closer.* Or they say: *I am here. I am ready. Come to me.* Or they say something else entirely, something that has no translation, something that is meant for organisms whose sensory experience is so different from yours that you cannot even guess at the content.

What are they? You do not know. You have never seen one. They may be so large that they cannot move — that they are, in a sense, part of the structure itself, living columns, living arches, organisms that have grown so massive and so integrated with the Great Body that they have become indistinguishable from it. Or they may be mobile but reclusive, moving through the deepest parts of the lattice where you cannot go, where the columns are so dense and the passages so narrow that only something very large and very patient and very determined could navigate them. Or they may be somewhere else entirely — in the walls, in the ceiling, in the spaces between the Great Body and the substrate it grows on. You do not know. You feel them. You hear them. That is all.

---

**The Drifting Clouds** are not creatures in the way that you understand creatures, but they are alive, and they are numerous beyond counting.

They are the organisms that fill the air — the things so small that they do not fly so much as float, that ride the warm convection currents between the columns like plankton ride ocean currents, that form visible clouds of living matter drifting through the Canopy Floor like slow, warm fog. Some of them are spores. Some are bacteria. Some are tiny animals — mites, mostly, and the larval stages of things you have not seen the adult forms of — that have evolved to spend their early lives suspended in the air, feeding on the organic particles and microorganisms that share their aerial environment.

They are the things you breathe in. They are the things that settle on your skin, in your hair, on your lips. They are the first colonists — the ones that land on you within seconds of your arrival and begin the slow process of determining what you are, whether you are food, whether you are habitat, whether you are something to be ignored. They are harmless, mostly. The Great Body is too old, too stable, too thoroughly colonized for rampant pathogenicity — the organisms here have had millions of years to work out their relationships with each other, and the ones that cause disease without restraint have been selected against, have been outcompeted by organisms that take a longer view, that understand — in the blind, evolutionary sense of the word — that a dead host is a finite resource and a living host is a permanent one.

But they are on you. They are in you. You are breathing them in and they are passing through you — through your mouth, your nose, your lungs, your digestive tract — and they are being processed, killed, digested, excreted, and the material they are made of is being routed back into the system. You are already part of the inventory. You have been here for minutes and the system has already accounted for you — has already determined your mass, your water content, your caloric value, the composition of your breath, the temperature of your skin — and has begun to adjust itself to incorporate you into its ongoing calculations.

---

**The Parasites** live everywhere.

They are not a category. They are a way of life. At every level of the food chain, at every scale, in every niche, there is something that lives on or inside something else and takes its living from it. The Stream-Walkers have parasites. The Grippers have parasites. The Silk-Spinners have parasites. The Filterers have parasites. The Fungal Shelves have parasites. The Curtain-Weavers have parasites. Even the Great Body has parasites — organisms that burrow into its tissues, that siphon its nutrients, that consume it slowly from within. And those parasites have parasites. And those parasites have parasites. The chain extends downward into scales you cannot see, into organisms you could not identify even if you could see them, into a microscopic world of predation and consumption and parasitism that is as complex and as violent and as beautiful as anything happening on the surface.

The Parasites are the great regulators. They are the ones who keep the populations in check, who prevent any single species from dominating, who maintain the diversity that is the source of the system's stability. Without them, the Canopy Floor would be a monoculture — one species, one organism, one thing, growing unchecked until it exhausted its resources and collapsed. With them, it is this: a million species, a billion organisms, an incomprehensible web of consumption and being consumed, of eating and being eaten, of life that exists only because other life exists, of organisms so thoroughly dependent on each other that the removal of any one of them would send ripples through the entire system, would change it in ways that could not be predicted or controlled.

---
