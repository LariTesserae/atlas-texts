# The Dwellers of St. Absalom's Hollow

---

## The Frog Chorus

They are the voice of the place. Not its only voice — the water has its own, and the stone has its own, and there is something else behind the walls that may have its own — but they are the loudest, the most complex, the most *present*. They are what you hear first and what you hear last.

There are dozens of species here, which is unusual. More than unusual — it's improbable. A single constructed hollow, fed by a single spring line, should not support this much amphibian diversity. But the channels branch and rebranch, the basins vary in depth and width and temperature and flow rate, and each microhabitat has been colonized by a different kind of frog, as if the hollow were a city with distinct neighborhoods and each neighborhood had its own resident.

The smallest are the color of wet parchment — pale, translucent, almost luminous in the dim light. They sit at the edges of the thinnest channels, where water flows in sheets no thicker than a coin, and they call in frequencies so high they're nearly out of human range. You feel them more than you hear them: a faint, electric tingling in the air, like the sensation before a thunderstorm. They're the most numerous. They cover the basin rims in clusters, their tiny bodies pressed together for warmth or moisture or some amphibian reason no one has asked them about. When you move, they scatter — a ripple of small bodies leaping in synchronized panic, each one finding its own stone to cling to, its own shadow to disappear into. Within seconds the surface looks empty again. Within a minute they return.

The mid-sized ones are the musicians. They sit in the deeper basins, half-submerged, and they produce the layered, rhythmic calls that define the hollow's soundscape. Each species has its own pattern — a trill, a click, a sustained note that rises and falls like breathing — and the patterns interweave in ways that are not random. There's a structure to it, a call-and-response quality, as if the frogs are having a conversation across the carved stone. A trill from the upper basin answers a click from the lower channel. A sustained note from the eastern wall overlaps with a trill from the western, and the two sounds merge into something that is neither — a third sound, a harmonic that exists only in the space between them. The frogs may not intend this. The acoustics may intend it for them. The result is the same: a continuous, evolving, almost linguistic music that fills every corner of the hollow and spills out through the mouth into the open air above.

The large ones are solitary. They sit alone in the deepest basins, heavy-bodied, their pale skin loose and glistening, and they call rarely — a single, low, resonant note that you feel in your chest more than you hear with your ears. They are patient hunters. They sit motionless for hours, their oversized eyes reflecting what little light there is, and they wait. Their diet seems to be mostly the transparent shrimp and the smaller frogs. They are not aggressive toward humans, but they are not afraid either. They watch. They have the calm, ancient watchfulness of creatures that have never been preyed upon by anything larger than themselves.

And then there are the climbers. These are the strangest. They have the pale skin and oversized feet of the other hollow frogs, but their feet are wider, flatter, with more surface area — and their hind toes end in small, dark, sharp claws that grip the wet stone of the vertical walls. They climb. They move up and down the carved surfaces with slow, deliberate precision, their bodies flattened against the rock, their toes splayed wide. They hunt the water beetles and the mites that live in the moss. They lay their eggs in the tiny pools that collect in the chisel marks on the walls. They have adapted to a three-dimensional world — not just the floor of the hollow but the entire volume of it, every surface, every channel, every carved symbol. They treat the hollow as a single continuous habitat, a three-dimensional maze of stone and water, and they navigate it with a confidence that suggests they know every inch.

The frogs are not just living in the hollow. They are *of* it. Their color, their claws, their feet, their calls — all shaped by this specific place, this specific combination of stone and water and sound. If you removed them from the hollow, they would be ordinary frogs in an ordinary world. Here, they are something else. They are the living voice of an instrument carved in stone.

## The Tree

It has no name that anyone living knows. The species — if it is a single species — is not recognized. It grows at the edge of the hollow's mouth, its trunk emerging from a seam in the carved stone where the worked surface meets the living bedrock, and it spreads its branches over the opening like a hand held palm-down, sheltering what's below.

It is old. Not ancient in the way that geological things are ancient — it's not a thousand years old, probably not even five hundred — but old enough that its trunk is thicker than a man can wrap his arms around, and old enough that its bark has developed the deep, vertical fissures of a tree that has survived many seasons of stress. The bark is dark, almost black, and rough in a way that suggests armor rather than skin. It doesn't flake or peel. It holds.

The leaves are the most distinctive thing about it. They are dark — a deep, almost blue-green — and waxy, with a surface that repels water rather than absorbing it. When rain falls on the canopy, the water beads up and runs to the edges of the leaves, where it drips in heavy, isolated drops rather than a fine mist. This matters for the hollow below. It means the tree acts as an umbrella during heavy rain, reducing the amount of water that enters the hollow, and as a light filter during dry periods, casting a consistent shade that keeps the temperature stable. The tree is regulating the hollow's environment in ways the builders may have intended.

But the roots. The roots are the tree's true relationship with the hollow. They have found their way into the carved seams between the stone courses, and they have been growing there for decades, perhaps centuries, prying the stone apart with patient biological force. Some of the roots are as thick as a wrist, visible where they emerge from the wall and dangle in the air before plunging back into another seam. Others are hair-thin, threading through the tiniest cracks, following the water, following the path of least resistance. The roots are slowly, slowly destroying the hollow. They are also slowly, slowly holding it together — binding the carved stones in a net of living tissue that resists the water's dissolving force. The tree is both the hollow's preservation and its eventual ruin, and it seems to understand this, or at least to accept it, in the way that very old living things accept the contradictions of their existence.

The tree is also a bridge. Its branches connect the hollow to the surface world, and things travel along them. Birds — wrens, dippers, occasionally a small owl — use the branches as a highway down into the hollow, where they hunt the frogs and the insects. Insects use the branches too: beetles, moths, spiders, all descending on silk or wing to the green twilight below. Even the leaves themselves become part of the hollow's ecosystem when they fall — drifting down through the mouth, landing in the basins, decomposing into a thin layer of organic matter that feeds the shrimp and the worms and the microscopic life that undergirds everything else.

The tree is the hollow's connection to the sun, to the rain, to the wind, to the world of open sky and changing seasons. Without it, the hollow would be a sealed system, entirely self-contained, entirely underground. With it, the hollow is open — open to light, open to weather, open to the slow, continuous exchange between the made world below and the grown world above.

## The Water

It is not alive in the way that frogs and trees are alive. But it is not passive either. It has its own behavior, its own rhythms, its own intentions — or at least its own consistent patterns that look like intention from the outside.

It enters from above, weeping from the spring line along the upper rim. The seepage is steady — not a flow, not a trickle, just a constant, thin presence that keeps every surface damp. It enters the carved channels and follows them — or doesn't follow them, because the channels curve and loop and double back in ways that don't follow gravity or gradient, and the water has to be *guided* through them, coaxed along paths that a natural stream would never choose. The water does this. It flows where the channels direct it, pools where the basins hold it, drips where the stone lips interrupt it. It follows the geometry.

But it also resists it. The channels are carved, but the water is dissolving them. Every surface that the water touches is being slowly, slowly worn away — the chisel marks softening, the symbol-carvings blurring, the sharp edges rounding. The water is doing what water does: returning the stone to a state of lower entropy, smoothing what was roughened, filling what was emptied. The builders carved the hollow over centuries. The water will unmake it over millennia. The water is patient. It has time.

The water also carries life. The transparent shrimp, the aquatic worms, the crustaceans — all of these live suspended in the water column, drifting through the channels, pooling in the basins, invisible except for the shadows they cast on the stone below. The shrimp are the most remarkable: they are almost perfectly clear, their bodies like lenses that focus the dim light into tiny, moving bright spots on the basin floor. You can see their organs — a dark line of digestive tract, a flicker of something that might be a heart — but only if you look closely, only if you know what you're seeing. They feed on the organic matter that washes down from the tree's decomposing leaves, and they are fed upon by the frogs, and the frogs are fed upon by the large solitary hunters, and so the energy moves through the system: from sun to leaf to water to shrimp to frog to whatever comes next.

The water also carries sound. This is the thing that makes the hollow strange. The water's surface acts as a secondary sound reflector, bouncing the frogs' calls off at odd angles, and the water itself acts as a conductor, transmitting vibrations through the stone and back again in ways that alter the timing and the timbre of everything you hear. When you stand in the hollow and listen, you are hearing the frogs' calls twice — once through the air, and once through the water and stone — and the two versions arrive at your ears slightly out of sync, creating a natural chorus effect that makes the sound richer, deeper, more complex than any single source could produce. The water is an acoustic instrument. It was carved to be one.

## The Thing Behind the Walls

It might be water. The description allows for this. Water hitting a resonant chamber — a void in the stone, a bubble in the rock, a space behind the carved surface that was left empty or that opened up over centuries of dissolution — could produce a low, rhythmic thumping. The channels loop and double back; some of them might pass through or near such a chamber, and the water's impact could set up a standing wave, a pulse, a beat. This would explain the rhythm, the subsonic quality, the sense that it comes from everywhere and nowhere.

But.

The thumping has a regularity that doesn't quite match the water's irregular flow. It's too steady, too metronomic, too *intentional*. Water doesn't beat like a heart. Water doesn't synchronize with the frogs' calls, rising in volume when the chorus swells, falling when it fades. Water doesn't seem to respond when you speak — when you raise your voice in the hollow, the thumping pauses, just for a moment, as if something behind the walls has heard you and is listening, and then resumes, slightly altered, as if it has changed its mind about something.

There might be something living in the stone. Something large enough to produce a subsonic vibration — not necessarily a single organism, but perhaps a colony, a network, a fungal mat or a bacterial biofilm that has been growing behind the carved walls for centuries, feeding on the minerals in the stone, expanding and contracting in slow cycles that produce rhythmic pressure against the rock face. This is not as far-fetched as it sounds. There are bacteria that live kilometers underground, feeding on rock, reproducing once every thousand years, their metabolism so slow that a single generation might last longer than human civilization. If such a colony existed behind the walls of the hollow, fed by the constant moisture and the mineral-rich limestone, it might produce vibrations as a byproduct of its growth — a slow, rhythmic expansion and contraction, a geological breathing, a heartbeat measured not in beats per minute but in beats per decade.

Or it might be something else. Something that was here before the hollow was carved. Something the builders found when they began to dig, and instead of avoiding it, they built around it, carved channels to feed it water, shaped the walls to amplify its voice, created an instrument not for the frogs but for *it* — whatever it is. Something that lives in the deep stone, that communicates through vibration, that was old when the first chisel touched the rock. Something that the hollow was built to house.

The thumping continues. It has continued for as long as anyone has been listening. It will continue after everyone has stopped.

## The Hollow Itself

This is the hardest thing to describe, because it requires a shift in perspective — from seeing the hollow as a place where things live to seeing the hollow as a living thing itself.

It breathes. The water level rises and falls on a cycle that doesn't match the weather above — it draws water in and pushes it out on its own schedule, as if the hollow has a tidal rhythm, a geological respiration. The frogs' chorus swells and fades in patterns that suggest a single organism's metabolic cycle. The tree grows, the roots crack the stone, the moss spreads, the chisel marks weather — all of these are processes of growth and decay, of building and unbuilding, that mirror the processes of a living body. The hollow is not a container for life. It is life — a composite life, a collaborative life, a life made of stone and water and frog and tree and time.

It has a metabolism. Energy enters from above — sunlight, rain, falling leaves, the occasional bird — and is processed through the system: the leaves feed the shrimp, the shrimp feed the frogs, the frogs feed the large solitary hunters, and the waste products of all of these feed the moss and the bacteria and the fungi that slowly dissolve the stone. The dissolved stone feeds the water with minerals. The water feeds the tree through its roots. The tree feeds the hollow with shade and leaves and the slow, patient pressure of its growth. The cycle is closed. Nothing is wasted. Everything is used.

It has a voice. The frogs are its vocal cords, the water is its larynx, the carved channels are its throat, the stone walls are its resonating chamber. The sound it produces — that layered, echoing, ceaseless musicality — is not just a byproduct of its existence. It is its purpose. The hollow was built to make this sound. The builders carved the channels to shape it, placed the basins to tune it, carved the symbols to modulate it, and left the space empty so the sound could fill it. The frogs were invited — or attracted, or cultivated — to provide the raw material of the voice. The tree was allowed to remain to regulate the light and the water and the temperature, keeping the instrument in tune. Everything in the hollow serves the sound.

And the sound serves what? Communication? Worship? The thing behind the walls? The builders themselves, who may have come here to listen, to stand ankle-deep in the cool water and look up at the square of grey-green sky and hear the voice of the hollow and feel, for a moment, that they were inside something larger than themselves — something that had been growing and changing and singing for longer than their civilization had existed, and would continue to grow and change and sing long after their civilization was gone?

The hollow doesn't answer. It just sings.

## The Builders

They are gone, but they are not absent. Their chisel marks are on every surface. Their geometry is in every channel. Their intention is in every carved symbol, every basin, every step worn concave by centuries of feet. They shaped this place, and the place shaped them in return — their hands learned the stone, their ears learned the acoustics, their bodies learned the steps and the grooves and the cool, damp air. They became part of the hollow even as they were building it.

Who were they? The description doesn't say. The tools were simple — bronze, perhaps, or early iron — and the fabrication was primitive, the work of modest workshops rather than imperial engineers. This was not a state project. This was not a monument to a king or a god. This was something else: a communal effort, a generational project, a work passed down from parent to child over so many generations that the original purpose may have been forgotten and reinvented several times. The earliest builders may have been carving a reservoir. The middle builders may have been carving a temple. The latest builders may have been carving an instrument. All of them were carving the same thing, and the thing they were carving was not just a hollow in the earth but a relationship — between water and stone, between sound and space, between the living and the made.

They understood frogs. They must have. The channels are the right width for frog navigation, the basins are the right depth for frog breeding, the walls are the right texture for frog climbing. Either the builders designed the hollow for the frogs, or the frogs evolved to fit the hollow, or — most likely — both, in a feedback loop that lasted centuries. The builders carved, the frogs colonized, the builders observed, the builders carved again, the frogs adapted, and so on, back and forth, until the hollow and its inhabitants became a single co-evolved system.

They understood acoustics. The channels loop and double back not because the water requires it but because the *sound* requires it. The curved paths create phase shifts in the frogs' calls, producing the layered, echoing, almost linguistic quality of the chorus. The basins act as resonating chambers, amplifying certain frequencies and dampening others. The stone walls reflect sound at angles that create standing waves — nodes and antinodes of pressure that move through the air like invisible architecture. The builders may not have understood the physics of what they were doing, but they understood the *effect*. They could hear that a channel curved a certain way produced a different sound than a channel curved another way. They could hear that a basin carved to a certain depth resonated with a certain frog's call. They were tuning an instrument by ear, over generations, with no theory and no precedent, just the patient, obsessive, repeating work of people who knew what they wanted to hear.

They understood time. The hollow was not built in a lifetime. It was built in many lifetimes. The earliest chisel marks are the deepest, the most confident, the most rough — the work of people who were inventing the technique as they went. The later marks are shallower, more refined, more precise — the work of people who had inherited the technique and were perfecting it. The latest marks are almost decorative, the chisel drawn across the stone in patterns that serve no structural purpose, that exist only to be seen, to be felt, to be part of the texture of the place. The builders were not just carving a hollow. They were carving a *history* — a record of their presence, their skill, their patience, their devotion to something they may not have been able to name.

Are they still here? Not as bodies — there are no bones, no graves, no signs of death in the hollow. But as presence. As intention. As the shape of the channels and the depth of the basins and the angle of the steps and the symbols carved into the walls. The builders are in the hollow the way a composer is in a symphony — not present as a physical entity, but present in every note, every phrase, every silence. They are the ones who decided that the water should flow this way and not that way. They are the ones who decided that the basin should be this deep and not deeper. They are the ones who decided that the hollow should be empty, that the space should be vast, that the sound should fill it.

They are the ones who decided that something should live here, and that the something should sing.

## What Lives Here

Everything. The frogs and the shrimp and the worms and the beetles and the mosquitoes and the birds and the mites and the springtails and the moss and the tree and the water and the stone and the thing behind the walls and the builders and the hollow itself — all of it, all at once, all the time, in a continuous, layered, echoing, ceaseless, collaborative act of existence.

The hollow is not a place where things live. It is a place where living is the *point*. Every element serves the whole. Every process feeds the next. Every sound is part of the song. The frogs sing, the water flows, the stone dissolves, the tree grows, the moss spreads, the thumping pulses behind the walls, and the hollow breathes and breathes and breathes, drawing water in and pushing it out on its own schedule, holding its cool breath and its green light and its ceaseless, layered, echoing song.

You stand on the lowest step, ankle-deep in cool, clear water, and you look up through the mouth of the hollow at the square of grey-green sky framed by dark leaves and darker stone, and you hear it — all of it, everything, the whole living, singing, dissolving, growing, patient, ancient, unfinished thing — and you understand that you are not looking at a place.

You are looking at a creature.

It is made of stone and water and time. It breathes through its mouth. It sings through its throat. It listens through its walls. It has been alive for longer than anyone can remember, and it will be alive for longer than anyone can imagine, and it is not done yet. It is still becoming. The water is still dissolving the stone. The roots are still cracking the walls. The frogs are still singing. The thumping is still pulsing. The hollow is still becoming what it's going to be.

And what it's going to be is not yet clear. The purpose isn't clear. The result is.

The result is this: a hollow in the earth, entirely made, entirely wet, entirely alive, entirely strange, holding its cool breath and its green light and its ceaseless, layered, echoing song.

The result is this: a place where everything lives, and everything lives *together*, and the living is the point.

The result is this: St. Absalom's Hollow, singing in the dark, waiting for nothing, needing nothing, complete.
