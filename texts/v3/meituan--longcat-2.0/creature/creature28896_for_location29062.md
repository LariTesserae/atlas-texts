The first things that live here are the stubborn ones—the flora that refuses to bow. They are not so much rooted in the stone as they are clutching it, a dense, desperate collaboration between mineral and chlorophyll. The cushion plants are the most visible of these survivors: tight, green rosettes pressed so hard against the dark rock that they seem to be strangling it, hoarding the heat of the stone beneath their leaves. They flower in tiny, violent bursts of white and violet, a quiet riot against the black. 

Deeper down, in the cracks and depressions where a whisper of soil has gathered, the mosses live. They are almost obscenely alive, drinking in the weeping walls and the sudden rains, swelling into thick, emerald reservoirs. They are the true circulatory system of this place, holding the water that the stone refuses to keep, creating the lush, dark matrix from which the ferns unfurl and the lichens spread their grey and orange maps. They are the flesh on the skeleton, growing slowly, imperceptibly, forcing the stone to yield.

Then there are the movers. The translucent arthropods that process through the moss in slow, deliberate lines. They are phantoms of the undergrowth, their internal workings visible as dark shadows beneath their skins. They do not wander; they trace paths through the vegetation with a precision that looks almost liturgical, like a procession carrying out a rite dictated by the contours of the rock. They are joined by the white birds—round-bodied, silent, with dark eyes that reflect the violet sky like polished stones. They do not sing. They feed, and they watch. They are the evaluators of the cirque, deciding what belongs and what is merely passing through.

When the rain falls and the shallow depressions fill, a different kind of life emerges: the pool-dwellers. They are the strangest of the cirque’s children—crustaceans and larvae that seem to belong to no known taxonomy, swarming in water so cold it burns. Their movements create patterns on the surface that look disturbingly like writing, a script of ripples and intersections that is almost legible before the sun drains the pools away, returning them to the stone. 

But the oldest resident, the one who has watched the glacier scrape the walls clean and the stone calve into scree, is the thing in the back wall. It is glimpsed only in flashes—a patch of dark fur, a pair of eyes that catch the light and throw it back with a sharp, startled gleam. It is the watcher, the ancient tenant of the unmarked stone, moving through the deepest cracks where the glowing veins pulse. It is the sovereign of this bowl, waiting with a patience that outlasts the ice.

And then there are the ghosts—or perhaps the seeds. The ones who came with hands. The builders of the leaning cairn, the stackers of the low wall, the knappers of the un-nicked flint blade. They are gone now, their bodies long since absorbed by the cold or the altitudes, but their intentions remain, scattered across the floor like puzzle pieces. The flint, the green-patinated copper, the worn shard of pottery—they are not just left behind; they are being redistributed. The cirque itself moves them, slowly, over centuries, carrying them across the bare stone, rearranging them into a pattern that might mean everything or nothing. The built things are being unmade, swallowed by moss and settling scree, reclaimed by the rock that the people tried to shape. 

But the truest life of this place is the life of the stone itself. It is not a passive landscape; it is an active, breathing entity. The glowing veins in the walls are its slow exhalation—the release of absorbed light, a luminous breath held and let go. The mist that rolls in and out in regular, spiraling channels is its respiration, following pathways worn smooth by repetition. The crack of the rock in the cold is its shifting in its sleep. 

What lives here is a conspiracy of light and cold and patience. It is a single organism, functioning at a tempo so reduced that human perception can only grasp it in fragments. The arthropods, the moss, the white birds, the watcher in the wall—they are not just inhabitants of the cirque; they are the organs of it, participating in a process that has no name. The cirque is humming, resonating at a frequency felt in the sternum rather than the ears, vibrating at the threshold of perception. 

It is alive, this place of unfinished stone. It is awake, and it is still deciding what it wants to be. It takes the water and the light and the lives of the small, silent things, and it hums, and it grows, and it waits, reshaping itself one hairline crack at a time.
