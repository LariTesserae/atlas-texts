# What Lives in the Caldera of Sighs

---

The first answer is the small ones. The ones that move quickly, that live and die in the space of a season, that are born and grow and rot and are absorbed back into the green with a speed that makes the concept of individual life feel like a courtesy the forest extends but does not entirely believe in.

The beetles live here. They are not beetles in the way that the word beetle is usually meant — they are beetles in the way that a river is a river, which is to say they are a process that has taken a shape. Their carapaces are dark, volcanic, and they shimmer. Not with the oily iridescence of a scarab, not with the metallic gleam of a jewel beetle, but with something that shifts as you watch, as though the surface of their bodies is a screen on which something is being displayed — not images, exactly, but *information*, patterns that your eye reads as color because color is the only file format your brain has for this kind of data. They move through the undergrowth in loose aggregations, not swarms, not colonies, but something more like a conversation — they cluster, disperse, cluster again, and the pattern of their movement, if you could see it from above, would look like the patterns the water makes on the platform surface: slow, spiraling, deliberate, meaningful in a way that you can feel but cannot decode.

The moths live here. They are large — the size of a hand, the size of a small bird — and they are still. This is the thing about the moths: they do not flutter. They do not beat their wings in the frantic, stochastic way of moths elsewhere. They perch on the trunks of the tree-ferns, on the surfaces of the monoliths, on the warm smooth walls of the tech, and they hold their wings open, and they are still. Their wings are patterned with eyespots, but the eyespots are not eyespots. They are diagrams. They are maps. They are instructions. Each moth's wings are different — not slightly different, as in the variation of a species, but *fundamentally* different, as though each moth is carrying a different page of the same document, and if you could gather them all, if you could lay their wings side by side in the correct order, you would have something readable, something that would tell you what this place is, what it does, what it wants. But you cannot gather them. They are still, and they are watching you with their false eyes, and when you move toward them they do not fly — they simply *relocate*, appearing on a different surface a meter away, without any visible transition, as though the space between where they were and where they are is a space that they have chosen not to occupy.

The ants live here. They are translucent, amber, their bodies like small windows into a dark interior. You can see their organs working — the slow pulse of something that might be a heart, the darker mass of something that might be a brain, the branching filaments of something that might be nerves but might also be something else, something that has the shape of nerves but the function of — what? Antennae? Wires? Roots? They move in columns along the vine-ropes, and they carry their crystalline loads, and the crystals catch the light and throw it back in colors that the forest does not otherwise produce — sharp, clean, spectral colors, the colors of a prism, the colors of light that has been *sorted*. The ants carry the crystals down, always down, into the deeper parts of the caldera, into the places where the heat increases and the vegetation becomes so dense that the light does not penetrate, and they do not come back, or they come back empty, or they come back changed — it is difficult to tell, because they are so small and the columns are so constant that individual ants are lost in the flow, are subsumed into the larger pattern, which is the pattern of the place itself, which is the pattern of the water on the platform, which is the pattern of the moths' wings, which is the pattern of the beetles' shimmer, which is the pattern of the hum.

---

The small mammals live here. They are furred, quick, large-eyed, and they watch you from the branches with an attention that is not fear and is not curiosity but is something more like *assessment*. They are deciding something about you. They have been deciding something about you since before you arrived — since before you entered the caldera, since before you reached the rim, since before you first saw the green bowl from whatever vantage point you came from. They have been deciding something about you for a long time, and they will continue to decide something about you after you leave, or after you do not leave, which is also a possibility that they are considering.

They move through the canopy with a fluidity that suggests the canopy is not an obstacle but a medium — that the interlocking fronds and the vine-ropes and the orchid-laden branches are to them what water is to a fish, a substance they are so perfectly adapted to that movement through it requires no more thought than breathing. They eat the orchids, or they eat the things that eat the orchids, or they eat the things that eat the things that eat the orchids — the food web here is deep and recursive and loops back on itself in ways that make the concept of a web inadequate. They are part of the system. They are not separate from it. Their fur carries spores, their droppings carry seeds, their bodies carry the same faint luminescence as the moss, as though the glow is not a property of the moss but a property of the place, and the moss is simply the medium through which it is most visible.

---

The fish live here. The dark-scaled, bright-flecked, large-eyed fish that move through the terrace pools with a slowness that is not laziness but *deliberation*. They feed on the luminous algae, and they watch you, and their watching is different from the watching of the small mammals. The mammals assess. The fish *recognize*. They have seen you before. They have seen something like you before. They are seeing you now in a way that does not require your physical presence — they are seeing you the way the water sees you, the way the rock sees you, the way the tech sees you: as a disturbance, a pattern, a new variable in an equation that has been running for a very long time.

In the deepest pool, the one that half-submerges the eighth monolith, the fish are larger. They move more slowly. Their scales are darker, their flecks brighter, their eyes larger and more round and more aware. They have been here longer. They have been here since the monolith was not half-submerged but fully submerged, or since the pool was not a pool but a vent, or since the caldera was not a caldera but something else, something that had not yet decided what shape to take. They remember. Not in the way that memory is usually understood — not as a cognitive process, not as a retrieval of stored information — but in the way that water remembers, in the way that rock remembers, in the way that the tech remembers: as a physical state, a configuration of matter that encodes the past in the present, that carries forward not a record of what happened but the *fact* of what happened, the weight and the shape and the consequence of it.

---

And then there are the large things. The things in the deep green. The things you do not see.

They live here. They have always lived here. They are the reason the caldera is the way it is — or the caldera is the reason they are the way they are — or both are the reason for each other, a mutual causation that has been running for so long that the question of which came first is not unanswered but *irrelevant*, a question that belongs to a way of thinking about time that does not apply in this place.

You do not see them. You see the evidence of them: the tracks in the soft earth, wide and deep, with too many toes or too few or toes arranged in patterns that do not match any foot you know. You see the places where the undergrowth has been pressed down in long smooth curves, as though something heavy and sinuous has passed through — not a snake, not a worm, not anything that moves by contraction and expansion, but something that moves by *flowing*, that moves the way water moves through the vines, the way heat moves through the rock, the way the hum moves through the air: continuously, patiently, without urgency, without pause.

You hear them. In the hours when the light is failing and the forest's sound shifts from its daytime register to something lower, more resonant, more deliberate — you hear them. Not a call. Not a cry. Not a roar. A vibration. A pulse. A sound that moves through the ground and up through the soles of your feet and into the bones of your legs and into the bones of your pelvis and into the bones of your spine and into the bones of your skull, where it becomes not a sound but a *knowing* — a sudden, wordless, bone-deep understanding that you are in the presence of something that is not animal and is not machine and is not vegetable and is not mineral but is all of these things and none of these things and something else besides, something that does not have a name in any language that has ever been spoken, something that the tech was built to serve or that built the tech or that *is* the tech, something that the forest grows around or that grows the forest or that *is* the forest, something that the water feeds or that feeds the water or that *is* the water, something that the heat rises from or that produces the heat or that *is* the heat.

---

This is what lives in the Caldera of Sighs.

It is not a creature. It is not a colony of creatures. It is not an ecosystem, though it contains ecosystems. It is not a machine, though it contains machines. It is not a mind, though it thinks. It is not alive, though it lives.

It is the place itself. The caldera. The green bowl. The warm wet dense luminous patient strange thing at the top of the world. It is the rock and the water and the heat and the light and the air and the green and the tech and the hum and the moths and the beetles and the ants and the small mammals and the fish and the large things in the deep green and the eighth monolith in the deepest pool and the patterns in the water on the platform and the shadows that fall at wrong angles beneath the arch and the moss that glows and the orchids that shift color and the vines that pulse and the channels that spiral inward and the basins that hold water in perfect stillness and the overhangs that weep and the air that enters through the skin and the smell of growth and decay and the sound of water and the sound of insects and the sound of birds that may not be birds and the sound of the large things that may not be animals and the sound of the tech that may not be technology and the sound of the hum that is all of these sounds and none of these sounds and the sound of something breathing, something that has been breathing for ten thousand years and will breathe for ten thousand more, something that sighs.

It sighs.

This is what lives here. The Sigh itself. The long, slow, warm, wet, green, patient, ancient, aware, unknowable Sigh that is the caldera and that the caldera is, that breathes in heat and light and water and time and breathes out growth and density and strangeness and more of itself, always more of itself, more green, more warm, more wet, more patient, more ancient, more aware, more strange, more *here*, more present, more alive in its slow luminous patient way than anything that moves quickly, anything that fears, anything that hurries, anything that dies.

It lives here. It has always lived here. It will be here when the concept of here has changed its mean
