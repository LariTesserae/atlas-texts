They do not have names. To name them would require a distinction between the organism and its environment, and here, that distinction is a language that no longer speaks. 

To understand what lives in the Lathered Rise, you must first discard the idea of the individual. There is no animal and no plant. There is only the Process, and the Process moves through different vessels—some anchored, some crawling, some walking.

---

**The Crawlers**

You will not see them, but the mat is thick with them. They are the things with too many legs, or no legs, the burrowers and the weavers. But to call them "fauna" is to misread the grammar of this place. They are the mat’s sensory organs, its hands, its slow-moving teeth.

Imagine a creature the size of a large dog, but flattened, pressed into the damp by its own relationship with gravity. It has no eyes; the light here is an insult to eyes, a useless green glare. Instead, it wears a skin of fine, root-like hairs that taste the chemistry of the decomposing matter beneath it. It moves not by walking, but by a slow, peristaltic wave of its own bulk, rippling over the spongy substrate. It breathes through pores that weep the same warm, viscous fluid that beads on the bark of the great trees. 

When it eats, it does not bite. It extends a soft, phallic proboscis into the decaying wood and liquefies the cellulose within, drinking the rot. It is warm to the touch—warmer than the air—because it is a furnace, a mobile compost heap, accelerating the breakdown of the dead into the building blocks of the living. It leaves a track behind it, a shallow groove in the mat, but the mat is already filling it in, growing over the wound, erasing the evidence with the slow confidence of skin healing over a scratch.

**The Walkers**

Higher up, in the damp cathedral of the understory, are the things that walk. These are the ones that press the mat into slow dimples, that displace the heavy air with their passing. They are what you might call the herd, or the flock, or perhaps the platelets of the plateau’s blood.

They stand on limbs that are not quite legs. The limbs are jointed, yes, and they bend, but they are covered in the same rough, weeping bark as the trees. In fact, they *are* trees, or they were, or they are the mobile fruiting bodies of the subterranean network. They stand roughly two meters high, hunched, their backs a sprawling architecture of moss and epiphyte. Their heads—if you can call the frontmost bulk a head—are a cluster of sensory fronds that twitch and curl in the perpetual mist, reading the humidity, the temperature, the faint vibrations of the mat.

They do not graze. They do not hunt. They migrate. They walk in a slow, endless circuit around the plateau, their bark-legs carrying them from one zone of the mat to another. Why? Because the mat requires different things in different places. The Walkers are the plateau’s method of moving nutrients. They absorb water and minerals through the soles of their feet in one region, and when they reach a region where the canopy has thinned and the mat is hungry, they excrete the excess. They are the circulatory system, the slow, walking blood cells of the Rise, and their migration is the pulse you feel when you press your hand to the ground.

**The Anchored**

And then there are the great trees. The fused, buttressed, grafted giants that form the architecture of the world. They are not individuals. They are the skeleton, the lungs, the brain. 

If you were to cut into one of the massive trunks—though no one has, and the mat would likely swallow the blade before it bit deep—you would not find rings. You would find a dense, vascular knot of interwoven tissues, a thousand different species fused into a single, continuous organ. The sap that runs through them is not just sugar and water; it is a rich, warm slurry of hormones, electrical impulses, and genetic material. The trees are thinking. Not in words, not in images, but in the slow, chemical calculus of growth and decay. They are calculating the angle of the light, the weight of the clouds, the pressure of the mat.

When the trees lean toward you when you are not looking, it is not a trick of the light. It is curiosity. It is the forest extending its sensory apparatus, tasting the strange, dry, cold chemistry of your skin, the foreign bacteria that cling to your clothes. You are an anomaly. You are a piece of the outside world, and the forest is trying to decide if you are a nutrient or a threat.

**The Ghosts**

And what of the builders? What of the hands that laid the stones, that carved the faces, that raised the rotting posts?

They are here. They have not left. 

The plateau does not waste. The plateau does not allow for the concept of "dead." When the builders fell, when their hearts stopped and their brains went dark, the mat received them. It took their calcium, their phosphorus, their iron. It broke down their flesh and their wood and their stone, and it fed them to the roots. 

But the plateau is a record-keeper. It is a living archive. The chemical patterns of the builders' lives—their movements, their habits, their slow, deliberate hands—were absorbed into the vascular system of the trees. The low hum you feel beneath 20 hertz? That is the resonance of their bones, now calcium in the soil, vibrating in the wind. The slow, deliberate movement of the Walkers? That is the echo of the builders' gait, the memory of how they moved through the green dimness, laid down in the genetic memory of the mat.

The builders did not die. They were incorporated. They became the reason the trees lean. They became the slow, patient intelligence that watches you from the green-gray light. They are the plateau now, and the plateau is them, and it is waiting, with the patience of stone and the warmth of rot, for you to finally lie down and let the mat take you home.
