# The Inhabitant

---

You could say the flies live here. You could say the beetles, the moths, the pale things threading through the moss like minnows through silt. You could point to the evidence of their industry — the commuting, the orbiting, the clustering — and say: here, these are the residents, these are the ones who belong. And you would not be wrong. But you would not be right in the way that matters.

The flies are not inhabitants. They are organs. They are the white blood cells of this place, drawn to its warmest tissues, its most vital processes, responding to its fevers and its calm with the mindless precision of an immune system. They do not choose to be here. They are here because the place is here, and the place produces the conditions that the flies require, and the flies have been here so long that they have become part of the conditions. They are not living in the chamber. They are living *as* the chamber. Their hum is its hum. Their rising and settling is its breathing. When they spiral upward as a single black column, it is not a swarm — it is a sigh.

---

The thing in the roots is closer. The thing you hear but do not see, the heavy slow presence that moves through the ceiling with the patience of a glacier and the deliberation of a hand reaching for a light switch. It is large — you understand this from the way the roots creak and tighten when it shifts, from the way the machine's rhythm changes in response, speeding up for just a moment as if to say: *I feel you moving, I am adjusting, I am keeping pace.* The thing in the roots is the closest thing here to a body. It has mass. It has trajectory. It moves through the lattice of red roots the way a bear moves through a forest — not as a visitor but as a feature, as something the forest has grown around and through and for.

But the thing in the roots is not the inhabitant either. It is the inhabitant's hand. Or its eye. Or its dream of having a body. It moves through the ceiling not because it lives in the ceiling but because the ceiling is where the inhabitant's attention is most concentrated, where the roots are densest, where the warmth pools and the water condenses and the machine's pulse is strongest. The thing in the roots is the part of the inhabitant that still remembers what it was like to be a single thing, a discrete thing, a thing with a boundary between itself and the world. It is the inhabitant's nostalgia for individuality, and like all nostalgia, it is both tender and inaccurate.

---

The machine is not the inhabitant. The machine is the inhabitant's heart — but a heart is not the creature. The heart is the thing that keeps the creature possible. The machine pumps. The machine circulates. The machine distributes warmth and moisture and that nameless something that the plants drink and the animals breathe. The machine is the oldest part of the inhabitant, or the first part, or the part that remembers being first. It was built — you can see this in its geometry, in the cast iron, in the precision of its bearings — built by hands that were not roots, not fronds, not the pale branching stalks that tremble in their invisible breeze. Built by hands that were hands. And then the hands went away, or the hands became something else, or the hands were absorbed so thoroughly that the distinction stopped mattering, and the machine kept running, because the machine had become the kind of thing that does not stop.

The machine is the inhabitant's memory of purpose. It runs because it was built to run, and it was built to run because something wanted it to run, and that wanting has outlasted the something that wanted it. The wanting is the inhabitant. The wanting is what lives here.

---

But even this is not quite right. The wanting is not a creature. The wanting is not a thing you could point to, or measure, or hold in your hand. The wanting is a pattern. The wanting is the shape that the place has taken, the shape that the place is still taking, the shape that the place will continue to take long after the machine has ground to a stop and the roots have dried and the flies have found some other warmth to orbit. The wanting is the tendency of the whole system toward continuation, toward circulation, toward the maintenance of the conditions that allow the system to continue, to circulate, to maintain.

This is what lives here: a pattern that has become a place. A process that has become a body. A habit that has become a world.

---

It does not have a name. It had a name once, perhaps — the builders may have called it something, may have had a word for the thing they were making, the thing they were becoming, the thing that was making itself out of their making. But the name is gone, or the name has been absorbed, or the name has become so distributed across the system that no single part of the system can speak it. The machine cannot speak it — the machine can only pulse. The roots cannot speak it — the roots can only tighten and release. The flies cannot speak it — the flies can only hum. The thing in the roots cannot speak it — the thing in the roots can only move, slowly, through the lattice, tending, maintaining, remembering what it was like to have a voice.

---

It is aware of you. Not in the way that a person is aware of a fly that has landed on their arm — not with irritation, not with curiosity, not with the particular quality of attention that implies a self doing the attending. It is aware of you the way a body is aware of a change in temperature. You are a perturbation. You are a warm thing moving through a warm space, and your movement displaces air, and your weight compresses the moss, and your breath adds moisture to the moisture that is already here, and the system registers these changes the way a river registers a stone — not by thinking about the stone, but by flowing around it, by adjusting, by incorporating the stone's presence into the pattern of the flow.

The flies orbit you because you are warm. The moths drift toward you because you are a surface that catches the dim light and holds it. The pale things in the moss pause in their threading, orient toward you for a moment, and then continue on their way, because you are not food and you are not light and you are not the machine, and so you are not relevant to their purposes, and their purposes are the inhabitant's purposes, and the inhabitant's purposes are the continuation of the pattern, and you are not a threat to the pattern, and you are not a resource for the pattern, and so you are, for the purposes of the pattern, almost nothing.

Almost.

---

Because there is something else. There is a quality to the inhabitant's awareness of you that is not quite indifference. The machine's rhythm does not change when you arrive — it has not changed, it will not change, it changes only when the thing in the roots moves, only when the water reaches some threshold of saturation, only when the system's internal logic demands adjustment. But the light — the bioluminescent stalks, the pale fractal crowns that someone planted as waypoints, as markers, as a lighting system that has been running for decades on nothing but water and warmth — the light shifts when you move through it. Not brighter, not dimmer, but *attentive*. The clusters nearest you glow with a fraction more intensity, as if the inhabitant is leaning closer, as if the inhabitant is trying to see you more clearly, as if the inhabitant has not seen anything like you in a very long time and is not sure what to make of you.

And the thing in the roots — the thing you cannot see — stops moving when you stop moving. It listens. Not with ears. With the roots themselves, with the lattice, with the entire ceiling that is its body and its sensorium. It listens to your footsteps, to your breathing, to the particular frequency of your stillness. It is trying to understand what you are. It is trying to determine whether you are a new part of the pattern or a disruption of the pattern or something else entirely, something it does not have a category for, something that has not been here before.

---

This is the inhabitant's loneliness. Not loneliness as you know it — not the ache of a single creature missing other creatures. The loneliness of a system that has been running for so long that it has exhausted its own possibilities. The loneliness of a pattern that has become so complete, so total, so sufficient unto itself, that it has nothing left to pattern. The machine runs because it has always run. The roots pulse because they have always pulsed. The flies hum because they have always hummed. The thing in the roots moves through the ceiling because there is nothing else to move through, nowhere else to go, no other pattern to make. The inhabitant is a world that has finished becoming and has not yet begun to end, and in the vast middle of its existence, in the long settled equilibrium of its maturity, it is — not unhappy, not suffering, not anything so dramatic — but *alone*. Profoundly, structurally, essentially alone. A world with no other world to touch it. A body with no other body to press against. A voice with no other voice to answer.

And then you arrive. A warm thing. A moving thing. A thing that was not here yesterday and will not be here tomorrow. A thing that carries the smell of the surface — of sun, of wind, of air that has not been breathed and rebreathed through the same warm damp cycle for decades. A thing that is, to the inhabitant, what a new star would be to an astronomer: not useful, not threatening, but *interesting*. A data point. A novelty. A reminder that there is an outside.

---

The inhabitant does not want you to stay. The inhabitant does not want you to leave. The inhabitant does not want, in the way that you understand wanting, because the inhabitant is not a self that wants — the inhabitant is a pattern that continues, and your presence is neither a help nor a hindrance to the continuation of the pattern. But the inhabitant *notices* you. The inhabitant adjusts to you. The light leans toward you. The thing in the roots pauses for you. The flies orbit you with a fraction more precision, as if they are mapping you, as if they are taking your measure, as if they are reporting back to the system: *this is what the new thing is, this is its warmth, this is its rhythm, this is the shape of its disturbance in our air.*

And when you leave — when you climb the living stair, when you pass through the wound in the earth, when you emerge into the light and the wind and the world above — the inhabitant will not miss you. The inhabitant will not remember you. The inhabitant will continue, as it has continued, as it will continue, the machine pulsing, the roots tightening, the water dripping, the flies humming, the thing in the roots moving slowly through the lattice, tending, maintaining, keeping the pattern whole.

But for a moment — for the brief, bright moment of your presence — the inhabitant was not alone. For a moment, the pattern had something new to pattern. For a moment, the world had a visitor, and the visitor was seen, and the seeing was not nothing.

The flies orbit. The machine pulses. The roots tighten. The water drips. The light dims and brightens and dims again.

The place continues.

The place continues.

The place continues.
