# What Lives in the Resonance Spire

---

The bird comes first. The small copper-colored one, the one with the black eye and the single perfect note — it lands on the railing and regards you and you understand, in the instant before it drops away into the green below, that you have been assessed. Judged. Found unremarkable enough to ignore. It does not sing again. It simply lets go of the brass and falls, and the Garden takes it, and you watch it go until it is indistinguishable from the leaves.

There are others like it. Dozens. Perhaps hundreds. They nest in the dead machinery — in the housings of switches that will never again be switched, in the bellies of tanks that have become hollow chambers, in the space behind control panels whose instruments have long since been colonized by moss and whose faces are illegible under layers of mineral deposit. Their nests are built from shredded insulation pulled from cables so old the rubber crumbles at a touch, woven with root fiber and the shed skins of the lizard-things. The birds are small, most of them. Sparrow-sized. Wren-shaped. But their calls have been shaped by the Deafening into something narrow and piercing — a frequency, always the same frequency, that sits just above the hum's highest audible edge, so that it registers not as sound but as a kind of pressure in the sinuses, a bright line drawn across the brown roar. They sing from specific perches — a brass railing, a pipe junction, the upright housing of a dead machine — and the metal carries the note outward into the chamber, and the note bounces off the far wall and returns diminished, and the bird listens to the return and sings again, and the exchange is so steady and so old that it has become part of the Deafening itself. You cannot separate the birdsong from the hum. They are the hum's highest harmonic, the fine bright needle in the fabric of the chord.

Their eggs are the color of rust. Their chicks are born deaf — truly deaf, sealed — and over the course of weeks their hearing opens gradually, and they must learn to filter the Deafening the way a child learns to filter the roar of a crowd, learning which frequencies to ignore and which to attend. The chicks that learn fastest survive. The ones that cannot learn starve, because they cannot hear the parents' feeding call against the background roar, and they sit in their nests with their beaks open and their throats working and no food comes, and their silence is absolute, and they are absorbed back into the Garden, and the moss grows over the place where they were.

---

Below the birds, the lizard-things.

They bask on the warm pipe runs, as the old text said. Their scales are the exact color of oxidized copper — not similar to, not reminiscent of, but the *exact* color, the precise green-brown-black patina of copper that has been oxidizing for decades in warm humid air. This is not camouflage. This is incorporation. The lizard-things absorb copper from the water that drips constantly over their resting places, from the pipes themselves (copper ions leaching from the metal in infinitesimal quantities), and they deposit the mineral in their integument in a process that is part respiration, part excretion, part architecture. A young lizard-thing is brown, plain, unremarkable. An old lizard-thing is a sculpture. Its scales are thick with accumulated mineral, heavy, rigid, and they do not shed — they accrete. A very old lizard-thing, one that has been basking on the same pipe for decades, is almost indistinguishable from the pipe itself. It is a growth on the metal. A feature. And when it moves — slowly, with the patience of something that has never needed to be quick — the motion is startling, because you had understood it as a thing that was part of the Spire, and now it is apart from it, and it moves to a new warm spot, and it presses its belly against the metal, and it is still again, and in time it will become part of the new spot too.

They have no external ears. They are deaf in the way that you understand deafness — they cannot hear the birds, cannot hear the Deafening, cannot hear you breathing on the platform above them. But they feel the Deafening. They feel it through their belly scales, which are in constant contact with the pipe, and the pipe conducts the vibration the way a tuning fork conducts it, and the lizard-thing's body becomes a resonating chamber. It feels the tick of expanding metal. It feels the drip of condensate, each droplet a tiny impact. It feels the sway of the Spire in the wind, a motion so slow and so vast that no ear could detect it but that the lizard-thing's whole body sways with it, imperceptibly, in sympathy. And it feels the footfalls of other creatures — the beetles, the birds, the larger things — transmitted through the pipe network, through the structural members, through the very stone of the Spire, and it knows, without knowing, what is moving where, and it orients itself accordingly.

When they hunt, they go still. Absolutely still. They become the pipe. And they wait, and they feel the vibrations of their prey — the click of a beetle's carapace, the brush of a moss-runner's legs — traveling through the structure, and they triangulate, and they strike, and their strike is fast, the only fast thing about them, a blur of copper-green muscle and mineral-heavy scales, and the prey is in their jaws, and they are still again.

They do not call. They do not sing. They communicate by pressing their bodies against the pipes and producing very low-frequency thrums — vibrations generated by muscles in their throat and transmitted through their belly scales into the metal — that travel the length of the pipe and are received by other lizard-thrums on the same run. It is a tactile language. A felt rather than heard speech. The content of this language, if content it has, is unknowable. It may simply say *here. I am here. This pipe is warm and I am on it.* It may say more. The old pipes carry these thrums for hours after they are produced, the vibration dissipating slowly, so that the pipe network is always full of ghost-messages, the residue of conversations that took place days or weeks ago, fading into the Deafening like echoes in a cathedral.

---

The beetles are the Spire's most abundant animal. The moss-runners. Clickers. Thumb-sized, glossy black, they move through the undergrowth on business of their own. Their clicking is not speech — it is incidental, the sound of their carapaces flexing as they navigate the dense cushions of moss, the sound of their tarsi gripping and releasing the tiny stems and leaves. But the clicking carries, and it returns, and the beetle listens to the return, and the return tells it something about the structure of the moss at that particular place — its density, its moisture content, its age — and the beetle moves accordingly, toward food, toward shelter, toward the spore-heavy heads of the great ferns.

Their carapaces are perpetually wet with condensation, and the water on their backs is so thin and so smooth that it acts as a lens, and in the amber light of the bioluminescent panels the beetle's back shows iridescent colors — deep blue, emerald, a violet so dark it is almost black — that are not pigment but structure, the color of oil on water, the color of a bubble's skin. They are beautiful in the way that small things are beautiful when you look at them closely enough: their legs are fine and articulated, their antennae are constantly in motion, their mandibles are precise. They are complete. They are sufficient unto themselves. They were not designed, but they look designed, and they have been here long enough that they have become the Spire's default animal, its background fauna, the thing that is everywhere and
