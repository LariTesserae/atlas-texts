At first, you would say the place is empty. You would scan the pale, weeping stone and the shallow, mineral-laden pools and find nothing that announces itself as alive. There is no darting shadow, no sudden flight, no bright color breaking the monochromatic haze. The landscape seems to reject the very premise of biology, too wet to be a desert, too barren to be a marsh, too new to have learned the soft accommodations of moss or lichen. 

But if you stay—if you let your eyes adjust to the pearly gloom and your ears untangle the constant, layered roar of the water—you begin to see that the life here is not separate from the landscape. The life here *is* the landscape, expressing itself in ways that move at the speed of stone and water.

The first thing that lives here is the shimmer. In the deepest pools, where the water is warm and perfectly still, there are the iridescent films. They are not plants, nor are they simply chemical reactions, though they are made of both. They are the breath of the shelves. They spread across the surface of the water in oily, swirling mandalas—blues that bleed into violets, silvers that catch the diffuse light and hold it. When the wind disturbs the pool or a drop of water falls from a high ledge and breaks the surface, the film shatters into a thousand fractals. But it does not die. It waits. Over hours, the edges reach out, the colors realign, and the mandala reforms, healing itself like a living skin. It is the place tasting the air, pulling the dissolved minerals from the water and testing them against the sky.

Beneath the shimmer, in the warm and the wet, are the drifters. They are the pool things, the translucent commas that exist in a state of near-dissolution. To look at one is to look at water that has decided, for a moment, to hold a shape. They have too many segments, or perhaps the segments are merely ripples in their flesh; they have no eyes, no mouth, no organs that you would recognize. They are more water than body, and they move not by swimming but by yielding to the deep, slow currents that pulse through the basins. 

But they are not purposeless. If you stand very still for a very long time, you will see them gather near the warm seeps where the mineral-rich water rises from below. They lean into the heat. They pause there, vibrating at a frequency just below sight, and when they drift away, the water they leave behind is slightly clearer, slightly cooler. They are the metabolism of the pools. They are the place drinking. When one turns toward you—when it faces you with whatever it uses for regard—it is not an animal recognizing an intruder. It is the pool becoming aware of a shadow. It is the water, curious about the thing that blocks the light.

On the drier shelves, where the crust is thick and sharp and the constant weeping is just a dampness in the air, there are the crawlers. They are tiny, hard-shelled things, no larger than the nail on your smallest finger. They look like droplets of hardened salt that have sprouted legs. They move with an agonizing, geological patience. Their trails score the white surface of the shelves—thin, deliberate lines that catch the dim light. They eat the mineral films, scraping the microscopic fuzz from the stone with mouths that are like tiny, grinding plates. Or perhaps they eat the stone itself, grinding the soft, young salt into their bodies and excreting it as something slightly different. 

They are the only things here that seem to be going somewhere. But their destination is not a place; it is a state of being. They are the hands of the landscape, feeling their way outward, testing the edges of the shelves. Where they gather in numbers, the stone is worn thin. Where their trails cross and recross, shallow channels begin to form, directing the next rainfall into a new pool. They are building the architecture of the future, one microscopic grain at a time.

And then there are the things that were built, which are now becoming alive. The corroded metal stake, the half-consumed wall, the steps leading down into the risen pool. The mineral does not distinguish between the grown and the built. It deposits on the iron, the wood, the cut stone, just as it does on the natural salt. But as the mineral builds up—forming a sleeve around a stake, a ridge along a wall—it is changing the chemistry of the object. The iron is rusting into the salt. The wood is petrifying into the stone. The landscape is digesting the intrusions, breaking them down into its own flesh. The built things are slowly being adopted, their straight lines softened, their angles rounded, their foreign materials translated into the native tongue of the weeping shelves. They are becoming fossils of a future that hasn't happened yet. 

But the truest life of this place is the growth itself. 

Stand at the edge of a basin and watch the lip of the stone. The water sheets over it, and as it does, it leaves behind a microscopic layer of mineral. The lip extends itself by a fraction of a hair's width. It is building the ground it will eventually stand on. It is pushing itself out over the empty air, an act of pure, stubborn creation. The pools are not just holding water; they are waiting. They are filling, overflowing, carving the channels that will feed the next terrace, and the next, and the next. The landscape is accreting, reaching downward and outward simultaneously, a body that is endlessly constructing itself out of its own tears.

The roar of the water is its voice. The hum in the hollowed-out rooms is its resonance. The patient, shimmering films are its skin. The drifters are its blood, warming itself at the deep veins of the earth. The crawlers are its nervous system, firing in slow, deliberate impulses across the crust. 

It lives. It is alive in the way that a planet is alive, or a star, or a geological epoch. It does not know what it wants to be yet, so it is trying everything. It is weeping itself into existence, layer by layer, season by season, building a body out of the dissolved bones of the deep earth, and the water keeps coming, and the stone keeps growing, and the pale things drift and crawl in the gloom, and the process continues, and the point—the whole point, the only point—is that it never stops.
