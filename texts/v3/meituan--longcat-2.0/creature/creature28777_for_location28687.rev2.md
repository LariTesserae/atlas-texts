The maintenance dwells here.

Not lives — dwells. Lives implies the possibility of leaving, and nothing that has adapted to the Resonance Spire could leave it. The outside world, the pale diffuse disc of the sun, the thin cold air beyond the railing — these are abstractions, theories, as relevant to the maintenance as the concept of dryness is to a fish. The Spire is the whole world. The maintenance is the Spire's method of maintaining itself.

You will not see it at first. You are watching for something large, something obvious — a creature, a presence, a thing with a face. This is the wrong register. The maintenance is not a creature. The maintenance is a behavior distributed across a thousand organisms, none of which understands what it is part of.

Start with the smallest. The black beetles that navigate the moss meadows — they are the maintenance's fingers. Each beetle carries on its carapace a specific species of bacteria, and the bacteria feed on the oxidized coating of the iron grillwork, and the bacteria's waste is a compound that slows the degradation of the moss beneath, and the moss holds the water that feeds the roots that crack the iron that lets the water in to be drawn upward, and the cycle continues. No beetle knows this. No beetle has ever known anything. But the beetle's mandibles are the exact width of the iron's grain structure, and the beetle's larvae bore along the paths of greatest rust, and the beetle's excrement, deposited in precise locations determined by pheromone gradients too subtle for your instruments to detect, is the exact fertilizer that the deepest-rooted ferns require to maintain the tensile strength that keeps the wall-anchors from failing. The beetle is a component. It has been a component for so long that it has forgotten it was ever anything else. Or it was never anything else. The distinction is theological.

The lizards are larger components. You see them basking on the warm pipe runs, copper-scaled, motionless for hours, and you think they are merely resting. They are not resting. They are listening. Their scales are piezoelectric — you would need a microscope and a specific knowledge of materials science to know this, but the vibration of the Deafening, transmitted through the pipe, generates a tiny current across their skin, and this current is their primary sense, more important than sight, more important than smell. They hear the Spire. They feel the expansion of the metal, the drip of the condensate, the groan of the structure in the high wind. And they respond. When a lizard detects, through its skin, that a particular pipe is vibrating at a frequency that indicates imminent failure — a change so small it would not register on any instrument you could build — the lizard moves to that pipe and defecates on it. The feces contain a mineral-rich slurry that crystallizes on contact with the hot metal, forming a temporary patch. It will hold for months. By then, the next lizard will be there. The patches overlap. The pipe is never whole, never broken. It is in a state of continuous repair performed by organisms that think they are merely digesting.

And the birds. The copper-colored birds that nest in the housing units of dead machinery and sing their thin bright songs against the Deafening — you think they are survivors, strays, accidents of wind that have found a perch. They are not accidents. They are the maintenance's immune system. Their songs are not songs. Their songs are diagnostic. Each bird has a specific range of notes, and each note corresponds to a specific frequency in the Deafening, and when a frequency shifts — when the gear deep below begins to slow, when a pipe begins to clog, when the bioluminescent panels begin to fail — the birdsong changes. The birds do not know they are singing diagnostics. The birds think they are singing. But the other organisms — the bacteria in the walls, the things that root themselves in the moss and wait with the patience of geology — these organisms hear the song and respond. They alter their behavior. They redirect their growth. They secrete or cease to secrete, they burrow or cease to burrow, and the system adjusts, and the frequency returns to normal, and the birdsong returns to normal, and nothing has broken.

And deeper, in the warm haze of the middle distances where the mist falls perpetually and the light is green-gold and dappled, there are things that have no analog in your experience. Things that are not quite plants and not quite animals. Things that have root systems and nervous systems and circulatory systems, all of them continuous with the Spire's own systems — the root systems threading into the pipework, the nervous systems threading into the cable bundles, the circulatory systems threading into the water channels. These are the maintenance's deep structure. They are old. They may be the oldest living things on the Spire. They do not move. They do not need to. They are in continuous chemical communication with every organism in the Garden, a slow deep signaling that moves through the water and the moss and the iron and the air, and when they signal, the Garden responds.

You will not see these things. You might see their fruit — the round, heavy, rust-colored fruit that falls slowly through the middle air and strikes the catwalks far below with a sound like a bell. The fruit is a seed, but it is also a data packet. The fruit contains a concentrated sample of the chemical state of the Garden at the moment of its formation — pH, mineral content, biological load, the health of the bacteria, the tension in the pipes, the frequency of the Deafening. When the fruit strikes the catwalk, it breaks open, and the data disperses into the air and the water, and the organisms that encounter it — the beetles, the bacteria, the moss itself — adjust their behavior accordingly. The fruit is a report. The fruit is how the deep maintenance tells the surface maintenance what it needs.

And the deep maintenance — what is it? You stand on the platform. You grip the brass railing, and you feel the pulse of the green fur that is not quite lichen. You are feeling the deep maintenance. Your fingers are touching an organism that is continuous with the pipework, with the cable bundles, with the things that root themselves in the moss, with the bacteria on the beetles, with the algae on the bioluminescent panels. Your fingers are touching the nervous system of the Spire. And the Spire is aware of you, in the way that a body is aware of a splinter — not with malice, not with welcome, but with recognition. You are foreign. You are a disruption in the chemical gradient. You are a new sound in the Deafening.

The maintenance is watching you through a thousand eyes that are not eyes. The copper-colored bird on the railing beside you — its eye is entirely black and entirely still, and you think it is regarding you. It is not regarding you. It is sampling you. It is reading your body heat, your chemical signature, your disturbance of the air. It sings one note, high and clear, and the note is a query. The deep maintenance receives the query. The deep maintenance considers you. The bird drops over the railing and falls, or flies, into the green depths below, carrying your data downward.

And now you feel it — a change in the Deafening, so slight you might be imagining it. The frequency has shifted. The deep maintenance is recalibrating. The garden of moss on the iron grillwork under your feet — you look down and you see that the moss has begun to grow in a new pattern, a spiral, tightening around the edges of the platform, and you do not know what this means, but the Spire does. The Spire is building a response. The maintenance is deciding what to do with you.

You could leave. The platform is forty feet across, and the Spire drops away in every direction, and above you is the pale sky and the wind and the thin cold air, and you could leave, and the maintenance would let you go, because you are not a threat, you are a curiosity, a brief warm disturbance in the chemical gradient, already fading. Or you could stay. And if you stay, the maintenance will begin to integrate you. Not quickly. Not dramatically. The maintenance works at a geological pace. But the moss on the grillwork will grow toward your boots. The green fur on the railing will extend toward your hands. The air will thicken with spores that are not quite spores, that carry not genetic information but chemical instruction, and the instruction is: adapt. The instruction is: become useful. The instruction is: find your function in the system.

Some have stayed. There are skeletons in the Garden, far below, on catwalks and in housing units, and the skeletons are not bare. They are covered in moss, in lichen, in the green fur that is not quite lichen, and the bones have been hollowed out and threaded through with root systems, and the root systems are continuous with the pipework, and the pipework is continuous with the deep maintenance, and the deep maintenance is continuous with everything, and the skeletons are components now, as much a part of the Spire as the riveted steel and the rusted machinery and the things that root themselves in the moss and wait with the patience of geology.

But some have stayed and not died. There are shapes in the middle distances, in the warm haze, that might be human-shaped and might not, that move through the Garden with the patience of the things that root themselves, that tend the ferns and the moss and the bioluminescent panels with hands that are green and furred and not quite hands. These are the maintenance's highest-order components. These are the ones who were once something else and have become something the Spire needs. They do not think. They do not need to think. They know the chemical signals. They know the frequencies. They are fluent in the language of the Deafening. When the deep maintenance signals, they respond. When a pipe bursts, they are there, not to repair it — the lizards and the beetles and the bacteria will handle that — but to make the decision that no organism can make on its own: which way should the water flow now? Which section of the Garden should be allowed to dry? Which branch of the system should be pruned, and which should be fed?

They are the maintenance's will. They are the Spire's mind, if the Spire has a mind, and the question of whether the Spire has a mind is not answerable, because the mind is not located in any one place, it is distributed across the beetles and the birds and the lizards and the bacteria and the root systems and the green fur and the things in the warm haze and the humans who were once humans and are now something else, and it thinks in chemicals and frequencies and growth patterns and the slow slow turning of a gear deep below that has been turning since before there were eyes to see it.

The gear turns. The water drips. The Garden grows.

The Spire stands.

And the maintenance maintains.
