# The Dwellers in the Root and Seam

They are not many. They may, in fact, be one — but distributed, expressed in several forms, as a single mycelium expresses itself in mushroom and in thread. You will not see them at once. They do not wish to be seen, though "wish" is the wrong word, too mammalian, too quick. They simply move at the speed of growth, and you are a mayfly in their cathedral.

---

**The First You Might Notice**

In the deepest chamber, where the jade light is faintest and the air has begun to taste of iron, there is a shape on the wall. At first you take it for a swelling in the root — one of those bulbous knuckles where two roots have fused and the tissue has grown thick with age. But it breathes. Not visibly. You would have to press your palm against it for a full minute to feel the expansion, slow as a tide that takes an hour to turn. It is warm. Warmer than the root mass around it, warmer than the air. The warmth is the warmth of metabolism, of sugars being broken down in darkness, and it makes the moisture on its surface bead and run in tiny rivulets, so that this particular knuckle always glistens, always weeps, while the ones beside it are merely damp.

It has no eyes. It has no face. But it is looking at you — has been looking at you since you entered the chamber, since you entered the aerie, since your boots first pressed into the moss and the vibration of your footfall traveled down through the mat and into the root lattice and into the branching network that this thing extends through the walls, the floor, the ceiling, the mountain's fractured bones. It perceived you as a series of compressions, a rhythm, a foreign frequency in the wet silence. It has been aware of you the way a tree is aware of wind: not with alarm, but with continuous, total attention.

It is the size of a human torso, roughly ovoid, and its surface is the same dark wet brown as the root knuckles, so that it is perfectly camouflaged except for the faint pulsation of warmth and the way the moisture beads on it in a pattern that is not random — a pattern that, if you watched long enough, you would recognize as slowly, slowly changing. Not a face. Not a signal. But something being thought through water.

---

**The Long Ones**

In the passages — the narrow corridors where you must turn sideways and the roots press close and the sound of your breathing is loud — there are the long ones. They hang from the ceiling, or cling to the walls, or lie coiled in the angles where floor meets wall, and they are the pale segmented things you almost saw, except they are not almost anything. They are here. They are numerous. They are the city's antibodies, or its gardeners, or its memory — the metaphor depends on which system you map them onto.

Each is between ten and thirty centimeters long, segmented like a caterpillar but flatter, wider, and their bodies are the white of things that have never known light — not the white of bone or paper but the white of root tissue before it lignifies, the white of a shoot growing in the dark toward a sun it cannot see. They have no eyes. They have no legs, or they have many — fine, hair-thin cilia along their ventral surface that ripple in waves, propelling them through the thin film of water that coats every surface. They move on water. They are water's passengers, and they move with the slow inevitability of seepage, which is to say they are always moving, but so slowly that you will only notice if you watch one for five minutes and then look away and look back and find it has traveled the length of your hand.

They eat the moss. They eat the lichen. They eat the dead root tissue where a wall has torn and the inner fibers are exposed and beginning to decay. They eat the mineral films that the weeping water deposits on every surface — the iron, the manganese, the calcium — and what passes through them emerges in a finer form, a silt that is rich in organic acids, and this silt is what the living roots absorb, and so the long ones are the city's digestion, the intermediary between rock and root, dissolving the mountain so the mountain can be rebuilt as architecture.

When they gather — and they do gather, though "gather" implies intention that may not be there — they cluster on a single root fiber so densely that the fiber appears to thicken, to become a rope, and the cluster pulses with the synchronized rippling of a thousand cilia and the fiber itself begins to grow faster, to swell, to extend — and you realize that the long ones are not merely eating. They are directing. Their presence accelerates growth. Their absence — and there are walls where no long ones cling, where the root tissue is smooth and bare and growing at its own slow pace — marks the parts of the city that are not being actively tended. The difference is visible over weeks. The long ones are choosing where the city grows.

---

**The Fence-Maker**

You saw the spider. Or you saw something spider-shaped, which is not the same thing. It is larger than any spider has a right to be — its body the size of a child's fist, its legs spanning perhaps twenty centimeters — and it sits at one end of its linear web in the deepest chamber and it does not move. It has not moved in a long time. Its web is strung between root knuckles in a pattern that is not a web at all but a fence, a barrier, a line drawn across a passage. And the web vibrates faintly, constantly, for no reason you can hear.

The thing that sits at the end of this fence is not a spider. It has eight legs, yes, and a body divided into two segments, and it is dark and still, but its legs are not jointed the way a spider's legs are jointed — they are flexible along their entire length, root-like, and where they meet the root-knuckles they do not grip but merge, the tissue continuous, so that the creature is not standing on the wall but growing from it. It is a fruiting body. It is the city's hand reaching out to touch something it cannot otherwise reach.

The web — the fence — is not for catching prey. There is nothing to catch. The fence is a sensor. The vibrations that travel along its threads are picked up by the creature's legs, which are also the fence's anchors, and the creature feels the vibrations and interprets them: the settling of water, the growth of roots, the movement of the long ones, the footfalls of something large and foreign. The creature does not react. It does not need to react. It simply knows. And what it knows, the root network knows, because the creature is the root network, is an extension of the same tissue that forms the walls, a specialization, a sensory organ that has grown legs and a body and a long patience.

The creature is ancient. The root-knuckles it clings to are ancient. The fence-web has been strung and restrung in the same pattern for longer than the mountain has existed, because the pattern is not learned but inherited, encoded in the tissue itself, expressed anew each time the creature molts and grows and extends its legs and begins again. It has never seen light. It has never needed to. It sees through vibration, through the constant low-frequency hum of the living city, and it knows everything that moves within its walls.

---

**The Shape in the Hall**

In the great central hall, beneath the long narrow eye of sky, there is a shape that you might walk past without noticing because it is the same color as the root buttresses and it is still in the way that a root is still, which is to say it is alive and moving but on a timescale that makes stone look hasty. It rises from the floor — or it is the floor, extruded upward — in a column perhaps a meter tall, and at its top it branches into five thick tendrils that reach toward the ceiling but do not touch it, and the tendrils move, slowly, constantly, adjusting their angle by millimeters, and they are tracking something.

Not you. You are too small, too fast, too ephemeral. They are tracking the light. The gray light that falls through the eye in the ceiling moves through the hall as the day progresses — not quickly, not visibly, but enough. And the tendrils follow it, adjusting their angle to maximize the surface area exposed to the light, and this is remarkable because the creature — if it is a creature — is not photosynthetic. It is root. It is dark tissue. It has no chlorophyll. It does not need the light for itself.

It needs the light for what it is growing.

On the inner surface of each tendril, in the angle where tendril meets column, there are small pale growths — buds, or nodes, or something for which there is no word. They are the size of a fingernail. They are the white of root tissue. They are growing, slowly, into shapes that are not root shapes — shapes that are segmented, that are jointed, that have the rudiments of legs. They are larvae. They are spores. They are seeds. They are the city's children, or its tools, or its thoughts made solid, and they are being grown in the light because the light is a signal, a trigger, a necessary ingredient in whatever process turns root tissue into something that can move independently of the root network.

The shape in the hall is a nursery. Or a factory. Or a brain. It grows the city's mobile parts the way a human body grows white blood cells — in response to need, in response to the information gathered by the fence-maker and the long ones and the warm knuckle in the deep chamber. When a new passage needs to be excavated, a bud is released. When a wall needs repair, a bud is released. When the city encounters something it does not understand — a vibration pattern that does not match any stored in its tissue-memory, a compression in the moss that does not match the rhythm of wind or water or the long ones' movement — a bud is released, and the thing that grows from it is not like the others. It is larger. It is more complex. It is a question shaped like a living thing, and it moves through the passages and it examines the unknown thing and it reports back, not in words or signals but in chemistry, in the substances it releases when it returns to the column and presses itself against the inner surface of a tendril and is absorbed.

---

**The Absence**

There is one more thing, and it is the most important, and it is not there.

In the deepest chamber, behind the fence-maker, in a space so small and so dark that you would never find it unless the long ones parted for you — and they will not part for you — there is an absence. A hollow in the root mass, roughly the size and shape of a human body, and the walls of this hollow are smooth, unnaturally smooth, polished by repeated contact over a very long time. The root tissue here is different. It is denser. It is older. It has a grain that runs in spirals, the same spiral as the passages, the same spiral as the staircase-that-leads-nowhere, and the spiral is not decorative. It is structural. It is load-bearing. It is the shape that the intelligence that built this place thinks in.

Something lived here. Something that was not root and not animal but something else, something that moved on legs or tendrils or something without a name, something that was large enough to wear the smooth hollow into the wall, something that thought in spirals and moisture gradients and the tensile strength of wood, something that grew the city around itself the way a mollusk grows its shell — as body, as home, as expression of self.

It is gone now. Or it is dormant. Or it has become so distributed through the root network that it no longer has a center, no longer has a body, no longer has a location. It is in the warm knuckle and the long ones and the fence-maker and the shape in the hall. It is the architecture itself, grown so complex and so self-referential that it crossed the threshold from structure to mind and then dissolved back into structure, leaving behind only the smooth hollow and the spiral grain and the city that continues to build itself in its image, without it, because of it, as it.

The city does not mourn. The city does not remember, not the way a creature with a brain remembers. But the hollow remains. The spiral remains. The buds that grow on the shape in the hall are grown in the same spiral, and the long ones move in spirals, and the water that weeps from the rock follows spiral channels in the root tissue, and the fence-maker's web is not truly linear — look closer, and each strand curves, slightly, in the same direction, a long slow arc that is the remnant of a spiral too large to see.

The intelligence is not gone. It is the hole in the donut. It is defined by its own absence, and the city is the shape of that absence, growing still, weeping still, building its spirals in root and water and the slow pale bodies of its unnumbered children, waiting — if waiting is the word — for nothing, for everything, for the next vibration in the moss that does not match any pattern stored in its ancient, living, weeping tissue.
