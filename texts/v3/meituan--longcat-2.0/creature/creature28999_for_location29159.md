The first living thing you notice is not a living thing at all. It is a quality of movement — a glint, a shift, a sense that the machinery is more alive than it should be. Then your eyes adjust, and you see them: the crustaceans.

They are small. No longer than the last joint of your little finger, most of them, though a few — the old ones, the ones who have found the deepest channels — are twice that. Their bodies are translucent in the way that deep water is translucent: not clear, but permeable, so that light enters them and loses its way and comes out changed. You can see the architecture of them — the faint dark line of a gut, the pale suggestion of organs that have simplified over generations into pure function. They have no eyes. They have no need for eyes. They know the machinery by touch, by the vibration of metal under their legs, by the taste of the water as it moves through the channels. They have been here long enough to have forgotten the sun, or never to have known it.

They move through the gear trains the way water moves through the streambed — not on the machinery but *with* it, finding the paths of least resistance, the gaps between the teeth, the hollows where moisture collects and the mineral deposit is thin. They do not disturb the rust. They live in it. They nest in it. Their bodies are the color of the rust, or the rust has become the color of their bodies, and the distinction is irrelevant because the iron and the creatures have reached the same agreement as the water and the stone: a compromise, a mutual accommodation, a shared patience. They are the grease between the teeth. They are the living interface between the moving parts. The gears have not turned in years — perhaps decades — but if they did, the crustaceans would turn with them, or they would move out of the way, or they would be crushed and their bodies would become part of the mechanism in a more literal sense, and other crustaceans would take their place, because there are always more, and the machinery is always hungry, and the hunger is not malice but simply the nature of iron in the presence of water and time.

They breed in the pumps. The warmest water in the underworks is there — not warm by any surface standard, but two or three degrees above the ambient chill, and that is enough. The young are born in the outflow channels, pale specks that drift on the current until they find a surface to cling to, and then they cling, and then they grow, and then they find their way into the gears, and the cycle continues, and the cycle has been continuing, and the pumps do not know this, and the water does not know this, and the crustaceans do not know this. They simply do what they do, and what they do is enough.

The beetles are older. Not the individual beetles — an individual beetle lives perhaps five years, perhaps seven, a long life for a small creature in a small place — but the *kind* of beetle. The form. The way of being. They have been here since before the pumps, since before the lights, since before the town. They are the inheritors of the floor.

They are large for their world — the size of a fingernail, a thumbprint, their carapaces domed and dark, the color of the mineral deposit that glazes the steps, the color of the stone, the color of the rust. They are armored. Their shells are thick and ridged and have the texture of the limestone itself, and this is not camouflage in the way that biologists mean camouflage — not an adaptation, not a strategy — but a consequence of living in a place where everything is slowly becoming everything else. The mineral deposit settles on their backs as it settles on everything, and they carry it, and over time the deposit becomes part of the shell, and the shell becomes part of the floor, and the beetles move across the floor they are made of, and the distinction between the creature and the place has become, for them, purely academic.

They click. The sound is small and dry and intimate — the sound of one hard thing touching another hard thing, the sound of a body moving through a world made of bodies. Each beetle clicks as it walks, a single click per step, or per few steps, and the clicks are not communication — they are not signals, not language, not the deliberate exchange of information — but they are not meaningless either. They are the sound of presence. The sound of a body saying *I am here, I am here, I am here* to no one in particular, to the stone, to the dark, to the air that has not moved in a thousand years. And because there are many beetles, and because they move at the same slow pace, and because the stone carries the sound the way a bell carries a tone, the clicking becomes distributed — it walks through the underworks like a presence, pausing here, arriving there, never still, and you cannot locate it because it is not located, it is *everywhere*, it is the sound of the floor being alive.

They wear trails in the mineral deposit. Not intentionally — they are not builders, not architects — but by the sheer repetition of their movement, the weight of their bodies on the glazed surface, step after step, year after year, decade after decade. The trails branch and intersect and form a network that mirrors the lane system above, as if the beetles have built their own town on the town's floor, a smaller, slower, more patient version, and the two towns — the one of stone and the one of passage — coexist in the same space, separated by a meter of air and a century of purpose. The trails are smooth. Polished. The beetles have sanded them with their feet the way the water has sanded the stone, and the result is the same: curves instead of edges, hollows instead of fractures, everything reduced to its most patient form.

They eat the mineral deposit. They graze on the glaze the way a snail grazes on algae, scraping it from the stone with mouthparts that are hardened into something like files, and what they take from the floor they return to the floor, processed, transformed, and the floor is different for their having been on it — thinner in some places, thicker in others, a palimpsest of consumption and excretion that records the history of every beetle that has ever walked here. They are the floor's memory. They are the floor's metabolism. They are the way the floor knows itself.

---

In the pools, there are the others.

They are the color of the water, which is the color of the stone, which is the color of nothing at all — a pale, almost luminous white that is not white so much as the absence of everything that is not water. They are ribbon-bodies, flat and undulating, and they have no eyes and no discernible head and no tail, or they have all of these things in a form so reduced as to be theoretical. They move by contracting and expanding their bodies in a slow wave that propels them through the shallow water with no urgency and no hesitation. They are not swimming. They are *flowing*. They are the water's way of being alive.

They feed on the mineral film that coats the bottom of the pools — the same film the beetles graze on the steps, the same film that gives the stone its greenish sheen — and on the algae that grows in the amber light, a faint, pale green that is almost white, almost invisible, almost not there. And on the dead. When a crustacean dies in the gears and the water carries its body to the pool, the ribbon-things receive it. When a beetle dies on the floor and the water carries it to the pool, the ribbon-things receive it. When a millipede falls from the cable and lands in the pool, the ribbon-things receive it. They are the underworks' way of processing loss. They are the place's grief, if a place can grieve, and the grief is not sorrow but transformation — the turning of the dead into the living, the closing of the loop, the patient alchemy of decay.

They have no predators. There is nothing here that eats them. They exist in a state of pure being — not hunted, not hunting, simply present, simply doing what they do, which is to move through the water and consume what the water provides and be consumed, in time, by the water itself. They are the most peaceful creatures in the underworks, and the most alien, and the most essential.

---

The millipedes move along the cables. You do not see them at first — they are the color of the polymer sheath, or the polymer sheath has become the color of them, and they move slowly, deliberately, their many legs finding purchase on a surface that was not designed for legs and has been adapted to them by the simple pressure of use. They navigate by touch. They do not see the light they flicker. They do not hear the sound of their own movement. They simply walk, and the light swells and dims behind them, and the amber glow breathes, and the underworks breathes with it, and the millipedes are the mechanism of that breathing, the living valve that makes the light pulse like a heart.

---

In the wettest corners, where the pumps deliver their constant trickle and the water has nowhere to go and the stone is darkest, the fungus grows. It is small — caps no larger than a pinhead, stems thin as thread — and it is pale, and it glows. The glow is not bright. It is not light in the way that the bulbs are light — not illumination, not the pushing back of darkness — but it is *presence*, a visible warmth, a soft greenish-gold that lives on the threshold between seeing and sensing. It is the only light in the underworks that was not put there by hands. It is the only light that is alive.
