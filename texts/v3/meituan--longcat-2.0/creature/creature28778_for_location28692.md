They do not walk the Hanging Borough; they inhabit it, the way a parasite inhabits a host, or a memory inhabits a dream. To look upon the inhabitants of this weeping, frozen cathedral is to witness a species perfectly, terrifyingly adapted to a world that should not exist. 

At first glance, they are merely silhouettes against the blinding, fractured cobalt of the ice—dark, frantic shapes scaling the vertical labyrinth. But as your eyes adjust to the unnatural glare, the details of their existence emerge, sharpened by the absolute silence. 

They are small, compact, and dense, built low to the ice to resist the updrafts of static and cold that pull the water skyward. Their bodies are layered in thick, greasy pelts, not sewn, but bound to their flesh with a resin harvested from the phosphorescent slime-mold, giving them a hunched, bestial profile. Their hands are their most striking feature: broad, six-fingered, and heavily calloused, with flattened, spatulate tips. The extra digit is not a mutation, but an evolutionary necessity, allowing them to span the knotted sinew-ropes and grip the weeping ice with a friction that defies the slick, wet rime. Their fingers are constantly moving, twitching, reading the micro-vibrations of the glacier like a blind man reads braille.

Their faces are entirely obscured by the bone masks. Carved from the chitinous carapaces of the blind crickets, the masks are masterworks of primitive engineering. They lack eye-slits; the inhabitants do not rely on sight alone to navigate the shimmering, disorienting glare. Instead, the masks are fitted with delicate, hollow antennae of bone that resonate with the subtle frequencies of the ice. When they tap the walls with their stone mallets, the sound does not travel through the damp air—it travels up the mallet, through the bone of their arm, and into the mask, painting a three-dimensional sonar map of the Borough in their minds. They see the weeping water crawling up the walls; they feel the shifting weight of the Grovelers in the alleyways below. 

They move with an eerie, frictionless urgency. Because sound is swallowed by the fog, their communication is entirely tactile and visual. They speak in a rapid, complex sign language, their six-fingered hands dancing in the periphery of your vision. But more than that, they speak through the ice itself. A sharp rap of a mallet against a buttress is a warning; a slow, rhythmic scraping is an invitation to harvest; a staccato burst is a command to the Ice-Grovelers. The entire Borough is their instrument, and they play it ceaselessly.

The Ice-Grovelers are their partners in this silent symphony. The stocky, fur-covered beasts are not domesticated in the way of beasts of burden; they are symbiotes. The inhabitants braid the Grovelers' thick fur into the sinew-ropes that hold the city together, and in return, the Grovelers are fed the hard, mineral-rich ice chipped from the walls. The Grovelers' chisel-tipped claws are the only things capable of efficiently carving the ancient, pressurized ice, and the inhabitants direct them with sharp, precise taps of the mallet. You will see a Groveler hauling a massive block of frozen water up a sheer wall, its snowshoe-paws finding purchase where none should exist, guided by the silent, rhythmic tapping of a masked figure clinging to the rope above.

Their economy is a ballet of silent, ceaseless motion. They harvest the weeping water not by catching it as it falls, but by coaxing it. Using clay pots lined with the phosphorescent mold, they hold the vessels against the walls, and the static-charged, upward-crawling water shimmers and leaps into the pots as if drawn by a magnet. They scrape the slime-mold from the cracks, grinding it with ice-dust to create a luminous, glowing paste. This paste is their paint, their light, their currency. They smear it across the thresholds of their carved dwellings, creating intricate, glowing mandalas that pulse with a cold, violet light in the fog. 

In the narrow alleyways, you will see them weaving. They sit in the dark, damp hollows of the ice, their fingers flying as they spin the sinew of the Grovelers and the fibrous, hair-like filaments of the slime-mold into ropes and nets. The mold-thread glows faintly in the dark, so the weavers are cocooned in a soft, green luminescence, their masked faces bent in concentration, looking like ancient, chitinous spiders spinning webs of cold fire.

They are a people defined by paradox. They live in a freezing desert drenched in water, using primitive bone chisels to carve a metropolis out of a substance that is constantly trying to reclaim itself. They are surrounded by blinding light, yet they navigate by touch and vibration. They are a boiling cauldron of activity in a world of absolute, suffocating silence. 

To watch them is to feel the profound, unsettling truth of the Hanging Borough: they are not merely living on the ice. They are part of its metabolism. They are the antibodies in the blood of the sleeping god, the frantic, silent pulse that keeps the lung breathing, the water climbing, and the ancient, weeping ice alive.
