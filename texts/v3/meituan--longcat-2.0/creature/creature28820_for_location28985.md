They are called, in whatever passes for language here, something that translates roughly as *the patient ones* — though even that is a compression, a sharpening of a word that is less a name and more a long, slow description of a process. The closest you might come in your own tongue is: *those who have learned to move at the speed of the stone.*

They are tall. Taller than you, certainly, but not dramatically so — it is the *quality* of their height that strikes you, the way they seem to extend upward the way the towers extend upward, as though they are not so much standing as *growing* from the causeways, rooted in the warm, shallow water by feet that are broad and flat and slightly webbed, their toes spreading across the fused basalt with the slow deliberation of something that has all the time there is. Their legs are long and slender, and they move them the way the fog moves — not step by step, but in a continuous, gliding shift of weight that barely disturbs the water around them. They do not splash. They do not stride. They *migrate* forward, their bodies tilting at angles that should be off-balance but are not, their arms held slightly away from their sides, their fingers — long, long fingers, with an extra joint — trailing in the water like the drifting things trail their filaments in the air.

Their skin is the first thing you notice after the movement. It is damp. It is always damp. Not wet, not dripping, but *damp* in the way that the walls are damp — a persistent, ambient moisture that sits on the surface like a second atmosphere, and like the walls, it weeps. Fine beads of mineral water form on their shoulders, their forearms, the ridges of their collarbones, and they migrate slowly downward, following the contours of their bodies to drip from their fingertips, their elbows, the points of their hips. The water is warm. It is body-warm. It is, you realize, the same temperature as the water that covers everything here, and you understand that the boundary between their bodies and the city's body is, at best, a polite fiction.

The skin itself is smooth and slightly translucent, with a faint, grayish undertone that suggests something beneath it — not blood, not muscle, but something denser, something mineral. In certain lights — the glow of a bioluminescent patch, the dull orange pulse of a heated vent — you can see shadows of structure beneath the surface: not bones, exactly, but *scaffoldings*, frameworks of something crystalline or calcified that give their bodies their shape without the urgency of a skeleton. They have ribs, but they are wide and flat and inflexible, more like the pleats of a folded fan than the cage of a lung. They have spines, but they are segmented in ways that suggest they were assembled rather than grown, each vertebra a small, dense disc of something that is not quite bone and not quite stone.

Their faces are long and narrow, with high cheekbones and wide-set eyes that are large and dark and lack visible pupils — or perhaps the entire eye *is* pupil, a deep, absorbent black that takes in the diffuse light and gives nothing back. They do not blink. They do not need to; a thin, transparent membrane slides across the surface of each eye at long intervals — every several minutes — a slow, sideways sweep that clears the moisture and leaves the eye gleaming and fresh. Their mouths are small and lipless, more like a fine seam in the face than an opening, and when they part it — rarely, slowly — you see that their teeth are not teeth but *plates*, broad and flat and slightly translucent, the color of old ivory or milky quartz, suited not for tearing or cutting but for grinding, for pressing, for the slow, patient reduction of something hard into something soft.

They do not speak. Not with their mouths. But they *communicate*, and the medium of their communication is the water itself.

When two of them meet on a causeway — a rare event, for they are not numerous, and the city is vast, and their paths cross only when the slow currents of their separate purposes bring them to the same place at the same time — they do not stop. They do not turn to face each other. They simply *slow*, and as they pass, their trailing fingers brush the surface of the water in patterns that are deliberate and complex, and the water carries these patterns outward in concentric rings that intersect, overlap, and modify each other, and in this interference — this meeting of ripples — there is meaning. Not words. Not even language in the way you understand it. More like a slow, liquid emotion that passes between them: *I am here. I know you are here. The water is warm. The city continues. This is enough.*

You watch them navigate the city, and you realize that they do not navigate it so much as *participate* in it. They know the channels not by sight or memory but by *feel* — the temperature of the water tells them where they are, the mineral content, the faint electrical conductivity that varies from district to district, from street to street. They read the currents the way you might read a map, feeling for the subtle shifts in pressure and flow that indicate a junction, a depth change, the presence of a structure just below the surface. Their feet, broad and sensitive, pick up vibrations from the stone — the deep hum of the city's engines, the settling of a tower, the slow grinding shift of a wall finding its final position — and they interpret these vibrations the way you might interpret the expression on a friend's face: not as data, but as *feeling*, as mood, as the emotional state of the place they inhabit.

They eat. Of course they eat. But they eat the way the city eats — slowly, mineral-ly, without urgency. They stand in the deeper channels, their bodies half-submerged, and they open their mouths to the water, and they *filter*. Their plates grind. Their throats work. They extract from the water the things that are in it — the dissolved minerals, the microscopic organisms that swarm in the warm shallows, the fine particulate matter that the city itself sheds as it slowly, slowly breaks down. They are part of the city's digestive system, you realize. They are what the city has grown to *taste itself with*, to process its own substance through a metabolism that is slower and more patient than geology but faster than the stone.

And they are not separate from the other life. The things that cling to the walls, the things that drift in the air, the things that undulate beneath the surface — they are all part of the same continuum, the same slow, warm, wet process of living and dying and dissolving and crystallizing and living again. The patient ones collect the drifting things on their skin and in their hair — yes, they have hair, fine and filamentous, growing in sparse, dark strands from their scalps and the backs of their arms and the ridges of their spines, hair that moves in the water like seaweed, like the tendrils of the drifting things, and it is often difficult to tell where their hair ends and the drifting things begin. They carry colonies of the wall-clinging creatures on their shoulders and the backs of their hands, and these creatures feed on the minerals in their sweat and leave trails of iridescence on their skin that the patient ones seem to value — not as decoration, exactly, but as *information*, a record of where they have been, what waters they have passed through, what minerals they have processed.

Sometimes — and this is the thing that stays with you, the thing that you will remember long after the warmth and the wet and the overwhelming presence of life have faded into memory — sometimes a patient one will stop moving entirely. They will be standing in a channel, or leaning against a wall, or resting on a causeway with their legs folded beneath them and their hands floating in the water, and they will simply *stop*. Their eyes will close — or rather, the membranes will slide shut and not reopen. Their breathing, which was already so slow and shallow that you could barely detect it, will slow further, and further, until it is indistinguishable from the ambient movement of the water. And they will begin to *change*.

Their skin will darken. The moisture on their surface will thicken, will mineralize, will begin to form a crust — thin at first, then thicker, a shell of calcium and silica and iron oxide that spreads across their body like the patina on the walls, like the crystalline growths that bloom on the dark stone. Their hair will stiffen and become filamentous in a different way — not hair anymore, but *antennae*, sensory structures that extend outward into the water and the air, reading the currents, the temperature, the chemical composition of their environment with a precision that their living body never quite achieved. Their fingers will elongate, will branch, will become the root-like structures that you have seen anchoring certain walls to the mud below. Their eyes — the last thing to go — will cloud over, will become opaque, will take on the same dark, glassy sheen as the stone of the buildings themselves.

And they will become part of the city. Not dead. Not alive. Something between. Something that is still *sensing*, still *feeling*, still *processing* the slow, warm, wet information of the world around it, but at a different speed, on a different scale. They will become the walls. They will become the pillars. They will become the overhangs and the bridges and the causeways. They will become the structures through which the fog moves and the water flows and the other patient ones pass on their slow, patient, unhurried way.

This is not death. They do not fear it. They do not mourn it. It is simply the next phase of a process that began long before they were born — if *born* is even the right word for the way they emerge from the city's substance, growing slowly over years or decades from a mineral-rich patch of wall or floor, accreting mass and structure and complexity until they are complete enough, complex enough, *patient enough* to detach and begin moving through the world on their own. They are the city's children. The city is their parent, their home, their food, their purpose, and ultimately their body. They are what the city grows when it wants to move, to taste, to touch, to *know* something beyond the boundaries of its own stone flesh.

And there are so few of them. Perhaps a dozen in the vastness you can see from the causeway. Perhaps a hundred in the whole city. They do not need to be numerous. They are not a population; they are a *function*. They are the city's curiosity, its fingertips, its slow, patient way of reaching out and touching the world beyond itself.

You watch one now, standing at the edge of the causeway where the water deepens and the fog thickens and the city begins to slope downward toward the vast, wrong-curved horizon. It is very still. Its eyes are open, but the membranes are closed, and it is *listening* — not with ears, for it has no ears, but with its whole body, reading the vibrations of the water, the stone, the air, the deep hum that underlies everything. It is listening to the city's heart. It is listening to the slow, patient, mineral pulse that keeps the water warm and the air thick and the whole vast, wet, living machine turning over and over in its own unhurried rhythm.

After a long time — minutes? hours? time is different here — it moves. A slight shift of weight. A trailing of fingers in the water. A slow, deliberate turn back toward the city's interior, toward the tall, dense structures and the drifting fog and the clicking, scraping, wet percussion of ten million small lives.

It passes you. It does not look at you. But its fingers brush the water between you, and the ripples carry something — not words, not thoughts, but a feeling, a mood, a slow and patient recognition:

*You are here. The water is warm. The city continues. There is no hurry.*

*There is no hurry at all.*
