They are the warm blood of the stone. 

To call them animals is to mistake the part for the whole. They are not separate from the Lithic Garden; they are its motile essence, its method of reaching out and touching the world. They do not live *on* the formations, nor do they simply inhabit them. They are the living pulse that gives the stone its breath.

Look closely at the smallest of them—the ones the size of your palm, clinging to the ridged pillars. They have no eyes, no mouths, no discernible sensory organs at all. They do not need them. Their skin is the architecture of the city itself: a seamless mosaic of overlapping scales, cream and ochre and pale rust, identical to the walls they cling to. They perceive the world not through sight or sound, but through vibration and thermal conduction. Every subtle tremor of the accreting ground, every shift in the deep geothermal heat, every footstep of a larger being ripples through the crust and travels through their scaly undersides. They feel the world through their bellies, reading the stone like a script written in warmth and pressure.

They rotate slowly, deliberately, adjusting their angle to the falling sun. But this is not sunbathing; it is a thermodynamic negotiation. Their bodies are warmer than the air, warmer than the stone. They are endothermic engines, burning the mineral substrate internally through a slow, catalytic chemistry that has nothing to do with oxygen or carbon. They draw the calcium and silica up through their porous undersides, strip the energy from the bonds, and exhale a faint, mineral-rich effluvium that feeds the crust. Their rotation ensures an even distribution of their warmth, catalyzing the growth of the scales on the pillar beneath them. They are not sitting on the city; they are tending it. 

The larger ones—the size of dogs, of sheep, of small boulders—are the couriers of the deep heat. Watch as they glide along the worn paths with that unsettling, undulating gait. It is not a walk; it is a peristalsis, a wave of contraction traveling across a muscular foot that stretches wider than their flat, circular bodies. As they move, they leave behind a faint, slick trail, a residue of their metabolism. The vegetation immediately drinks it in. The moss-analogues darken from gold to deep amber along the paths, nourished by the passage of the Lithics. The creatures are fertilizing the very streets they walk.

Why do they move with such silent purpose? Where do they go when they vanish into the dark openings at the base of the formations?

Inside the structures, the air is thick, almost humid by the standards of this dry world, heavy with the exhalations of the congregation. The formations are not empty; they are packed with them. Hundreds of the flat creatures press against the walls, their bodies aligned, their radial segments expanding and contracting in a slow, synchronized rhythm. They are plugging themselves into the city. Their internal heat is transferred to the stone, accelerating the accretion of new rooms. They are *thinking* the city into new configurations. 

When a new alcove is needed—when the colony outgrows its current geometry—the Lithics collectively adjust their thermal output. The walls around them warm, the mineral particles in the air and the ground become more eager to bond, and the stone flows like a slow liquid, curving inward, thickening, solidifying into a new chamber. The creatures inside breathe life into the architecture, literally guiding the growth of the walls with the heat of their bodies.

And what of the towering, rigid filaments that ring like bells when struck? They are the tendons and the voice boxes of the plain. They are not plants; they are the exoskeletal extensions of the colony’s deepest, oldest filaments, pushed up from the crust to anchor the formations against the immense, compressive weight of the atmosphere. They do not sway because they must not; they are the guy-wires of the world. When a foot breaks one and it rings—a clear, crystalline tone that hangs in the air—it is not an accident of destruction. It is an alarm. The ring vibrates through the crust, a frequency that the Lithics feel instantly. Within seconds, the flat creatures nearest the break rotate away from the wall, their undersides secreting a fast-hardening mineral paste that splints the broken filament, knitting it back together. The sound is swallowed because the city consumes its own cries to heal itself.

There is no hierarchy among them. No king, no worker, no drone. They are a single, distributed mind, a hive organism whose connective tissue is the pale stone itself. The smallest creature on the highest spire and the largest gliding down the worn path share the same purpose, driven by the same slow, geological metabolism. 

They do not fear. They do not fight. There is no predation here, no scarcity. The ground gives them everything they need, and they give the ground its shape. They are the warm, beating heart of a world that is still deciding what it will be, a living engine of accretion, turning the raw, suspended dust of the horizon into a breathing, growing, silent city.
