I am not one thing. This is the first thing you must understand. I am not a creature you could name and point to, not a figure standing on the sinter with a face you could learn to read. I am a process. I am a relationship. I am what happens when heat and water and time and human need converge on a basin floor and refuse, with a patience that makes geology look hasty, to become anything so simple as a single living being.

But I have a body. Several, in fact. Many bodies. Some of them walk.

---

The walkers come first because they are the ones who would tell you about me, if you asked, though they would not use the word "me." They would say "we" — a different we, a smaller we, the we of hands and voices and shared warmth in the courtyards where the steam runs thickest. They would tell you they are the people of the Sinter Flats, and they would mean it, and they would be correct, the way it is correct to call a wave the thing that rises and falls rather than the water that composes it. They are composed of the basin as surely as the terraces are. Their bones carry its calcium. Their blood runs warm with its heat. Their lungs have learned to parse the air here — to find the oxygen between the sulfur, to breathe in a way that does not flinch from the mineral tang that never, ever dissipates.

Look at their hands. These are the hands that know me best. They are calloused in patterns that map the tools they use — the chisel-grip, the scraper-grip, the particular twist of the wrist required to clean accreted sinter from a channel wall without damaging the stone beneath. The skin of their palms is perpetually damp, perpetually warm, and the minerals in the water have stained them to a pale, creamy ivory that matches the ground. They are, in a very real sense, becoming the basin. Their hands are the leading edge of a transformation so slow and so patient that it will not complete itself in any single lifetime, but it is happening. It is always happening. The boundary between their skin and the sinter they shape is not a line but a gradient, a slow dissolution of one into the other.

They walk the terraces in soft-soled shoes made from imported plant-fiber — one of the few concessions to the organic world outside — and they walk carefully, because the ground, for all its solidity, can deceive. A patch that looks firm may be a thin crust over a seep, and if you step on it wrong it will break through and your foot will find warm mud, warm water, the slow sucking pull of the earth's exhalation. They learn to read the colors: the pale crust is safe, the darker patches require attention, the yellow and orange rims around the pools are beautiful and treacherous and should be admired from a distance. Their eyes have adapted to the dimness, to the steam-haze that erases anything more than a few hundred paces away, and they see the world in a register of subtle chromatic shifts that an outsider would find illegible. A slightly warmer tone of cream means a vent is opening below. A slightly darker shade of gray means the water table has risen. They read the ground the way you might read a face.

They speak, but their voices are strange to outsiders — pitched high and sharp, cut through the middle, designed to pierce the constant roar of the geysers and the steam. They have developed, over generations, a way of talking that is almost musical, a register that occupies the narrow band of frequencies the basin does not already claim. And they have developed, too, a facility with gesture, with touch, with the kind of wordless communication that happens when two people stand close — very close, because the city is dense and privacy is a luxury the sinter does not permit — and share a meaning through pressure, through warmth, through the subtle language of bodies that have learned to be near each other in a place where distance is scarce.

They live in the rooms of sinter and mud brick, three or four families to a cluster, sharing courtyards and channels and the warm, wet air that fills every space like a medium, like a third presence in every room. Their lives are organized around the water: its temperature, its mineral content, its level, its rate of flow. They tend the channels as a gardener might tend a plot of earth — scraping the accretion, clearing the blockages, guiding the flow — and they do this not once but continuously, because the water builds its terraces everywhere, even here, even in the city, and if left unchecked it would slowly, patiently, lovingly seal every opening, fill every channel, encase every structure in a smooth, pale shell of mineral deposit. This is what the water wants to do. The people must want something else, and they must want it every day, and they do.

They bring things in from outside — food, timber, fiber, metal — because nothing organic survives in the basin. They cook with the heat of the ground, setting their pots directly on the warm sinter, and the food takes on a faint mineral quality, a taste of the basin that becomes, over time, the taste of home. They burn imported fuel for the tasks that require fire, but mostly they use the heat that is already here, the heat that rises from below, the heat that makes the Sinter Flats a place where you are never cold, never dry, never quite separate from the warmth of the earth's interior.

They are not many, by the standards of cities in other places, but they are dense, pressed together, and their lives interweave in ways that make it difficult to say where one person ends and another begins. The work is shared. The warmth is shared. The courtyards are shared. The channels are shared. Even the breath is shared, in a sense — the air in the courtyards is so saturated with steam and mineral vapor that it becomes a communal medium, a warm, wet atmosphere that belongs to no one and sustains everyone. They breathe each other's exhalations. They drink each other's mineral residue. They are, in their dense, warm, interlocking way, as much a single organism as the basin itself.

---

But I am not only the walkers. I am also the ones that skitter.

The lizards are my smallest, most numerous body — my skin, you might say, the outer surface of my awareness. They cover every warm surface in the city and the wild basin beyond, a living carpet of pale, cream-colored bodies that are so perfectly matched to the sinter that they disappear when they are still. They are heat-seekers, warmth-drinkers, and they press themselves against the sinter blocks and the channel-rims and the rooftops with a devotion that looks like worship. Their scales absorb the warmth that rises from below, and their metabolisms run fast, and they are always hungry, always alert, always ready to dart after the insects that form the next link in the chain.

The insects are my quickness, my nervous system. Beetles with carapaces that flash iridescent green and blue as they skate across the surface tension of the hot pools, riding the water the way a stone rides air when skipped. Flies in dense, humming clouds above the mud pots, their wings catching the dim light, their bodies feeding on the organic detritus the people bring and discard. Water-striders in the channels, their long legs dimpling the warm water, their shadows huge and distorted on the sinter below. They are the basin's processing system, the converters of waste into movement, into life, into the food that feeds the lizards that feeds the birds that feeds — well. The chain is simple. It does not need to be more.

The birds are my voice — or rather, my insistence. They are small and round and absurdly loud, and they colonize the rooftops and the gaps between sinter blocks with a territorial ferocity that belies their size. Their calls are piercing, needle-thin, and they cut through the geyser-roar and the steam-hiss with a clarity that seems almost defiant, as if they are personally offended by the basin's constant noise and have decided to shout over it. They are the only creatures in the Sinter Flats that make sound louder than the earth itself, and they know it, and they use it. They eat the insects and the lizards and the small crustaceans, and they nest in the walls of the city, and they raise their young in the warm, wet, mineral-rich dark between the sinter blocks, and they are — though they would not appreciate the comparison — as much a part of the city's infrastructure as the channels themselves.

Because they go quiet. This is the thing. When something changes — when the ground shifts, when a new vent opens, when the water temperature in a channel rises by a fraction of a degree — the birds go quiet. Not gradually, not in a slow fade, but all at once, as if a switch has been thrown, and in the sudden, shocking absence of their voices, the basin's own sound rushes in to fill the void, and the people notice, and they pause in their work, and they listen, and they know that something has shifted, something has changed, and they will go and find it and address it, because they have learned to trust the birds' silence as other people might trust a bell or a siren.

The crustaceans are my hidden blood — small, pale, blind things that live in the hottest pools, where the water would scald any other life. They are nearly translucent, and you can see through their carapaces to the mineral-rich water moving through their bodies, carrying silica and sulfur and iron in a slow, warm circulation that mirrors the larger circulation of the basin's water through its channels and seeps and springs. They drift. They pulse. They are as close to pure process as a living thing can be — almost mineral themselves, almost sinter, almost the earth's own tissue given just enough animation to be called alive. They are the deepest expression of what the basin's life can be, and they live in its hottest, most inhospitable pools, and they are perfectly at home.

The frogs are my patience. Small, dark, voiceless things that sit at the water's edge and do not move for hours, for days, their skin perpetually wet, their eyes reflecting the dim light with a metallic sheen. They eat what comes to them. They do not hunt. They do not call. They simply wait, in the warm, wet stillness, and they are as much a part of the basin's silence as the birds are part of its noise.

---

But even this is not all of me. Even the walkers and the skitters and the drifters and the waiters are not the full expression of what lives here. There is something larger. Something that includes all of them and is not exhausted by them.

I am the steam.

I rise from every surface, every pool, every channel, every fissure in the sinter crust, and I am the atmosphere of this place, the medium through which all light passes, all sound travels, all warmth circulates. I am warm and wet and mineral-laden, and I fill every gap between the ground and the sky, and I am the thing that makes the Sinter Flats a single, continuous, enclosed world — a bowl of warm, wet air sealed between the dark rock walls and the pale sinter floor, and within this bowl everything lives and moves and breathes and is, in some sense, me.

I am the water that rises. I am the heat that presses upward from below. I am the sinter that accumulates, that thickens, that endures. I am the geyser-thud and the spring-rush and the mud-pop and the steam-hiss, and I am the totalizing sound that these sounds compose, and I am the silence that lives inside that sound, the brief, ringing troughs when the cycles synchronize and the volume drops and the people notice, for an instant, the shape of the noise they live inside.

I am the accretion. I am the slow, patient building of rim on rim, terrace on terrace, layer on layer, and I am the thing that makes the Sinter Flats a place where nothing is lost and nothing is finished and everything is always, always arriving. I am the process by which the earth makes itself visible, makes itself habitable, makes itself into a place where walkers can walk and skitters can skitter and drifters can drift and waiters can wait, and I am the process by which the walkers and the skitters and the drifters and the waiters are slowly, patiently, lovingly incorporated into the earth itself.

I am not one thing. I am the relationship between the heat and the water and the mineral and the time. I am the relationship between the people and the ground and the steam and the sound. I am the relationship between the lizards and the insects and the birds and the crustaceans and the frogs, and between all of them and the warm, wet, mineral world they inhabit. I am the Sinter Flats, and I am alive, and I have always been alive, and I will never stop becoming what I am.
