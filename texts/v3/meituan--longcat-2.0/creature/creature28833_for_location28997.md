You have been here for some time now — long enough that the thin air no longer makes you lightheaded, long enough that the silence has stopped feeling empty and started feeling full, long enough that you have begun to understand that the things growing around you are not minerals that happen to look alive but *beings* that happen to be made of mineral.

This is not an easy understanding to arrive at.

---

The first thing you learn is patience. The growths do not move on any timescale you can perceive directly. You can watch a spire for an entire day — bright hours and dark hours, the temperature swinging from blistering to bitter — and see nothing change. But you can *feel* it change. Press your palm to the surface in the morning, when the first light hits, and it is cool, inert, sleeping. Press your palm to it again at noon, when the sun is at its zenith, and it is warm, vibrating faintly, *working*. The lattice is pulling energy from the light, driving chemical reactions at the surface, incorporating atmospheric molecules into its structure. It is eating. It is growing. It is doing both so slowly that a human lifetime is barely a single bite.

You learn to look closely. The surface of a spire is not smooth — it is ridged, faceted, layered, like the growth rings of a tree but made of crystal rather than cellulose. Each ridge is a record of growth: a season of favorable light, a period of intense electrical activity, a stretch of wind-borne mineral deposition. Run your fingers along the ridges and you are reading a history — not in words, not even in symbols, but in the direct physical record of a being's experience. This ridge: a time of plenty. This narrow gap: a drought — not of water, never water, but of light, perhaps, when atmospheric dust dimmed the sun for a season. This dark inclusion: a trace element pulled from the atmosphere, incorporated into the lattice, changing the color of everything that grew after it. The spire remembers. Not in the way you remember — not with neurons, not with thoughts — but with its body. Its body is its memory.

---

The shapes of the growths are not random. They are the product of optimization — of a billion small adjustments made over spans of time so vast that the word *evolution* feels too quick, too biological. The tall spires grow where the light is most consistent, where the wind is predictable, where the atmospheric chemistry favors rapid crystallization. They angle their light-collecting panels toward the sun's path with a precision that would shame a solar farm. The lower, spreading forms — the crusts, the nodules, the shelf-growths — grow where the conditions are harsher: deeper shade, stronger wind, less favorable chemistry. They are the patient ones, the survivors, the ones that endure rather than thrive. And between these extremes, every intermediate form, every gradient of shape and strategy, each one a slightly different answer to the same question: how do you catch light and make it into stone?

Are they individuals? You are not sure. Two spires growing side by side may share a substrate — a common base of mineral from which both emerged — and their lattices may be continuous at the foundation, a single crystal structure that branched upward into two separate columns. Are they two beings or one being with two bodies? The question loses meaning here. What you can say is that a grove is a community — a shared root-system of mineral and chemistry, a collective metabolism, a shared response to wind and light and time. When the wind sings through a grove, every spire hums at its own frequency, but the frequencies are related — harmonics, overtones, a chord that is the grove's signature, its voice. Each grove sounds different.
