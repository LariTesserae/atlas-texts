There is only one inhabitant of the Stillworks, and it is the Stillworks itself.

It is not a place where something lives. It is a something that is a place. An organism so vast and so old that the boundary between creature and environment—between body and world, between the factory and the product it makes—has not merely blurred but ceased to exist entirely. The ocean is not its home. The ocean is its body. The fronds are not its inhabitants. The fronds are its bones.

It is alive in the way that a heartbeat is alive: automatically, inevitably, without decision or desire. Its blood is the dense, mineral-rich water that fills its form from skin to floor, cool as a root cellar, thick with the dissolved products of its own metabolism. Its skeleton is the great kelp-structures, cathedral-wide, rigid as engineered steel, standing in curves so mathematically perfect they might have been calculated by a god with a drafting table—except they were not calculated. They were grown. Over millennia of slow vegetable patience, each fiber found its place in a structure that has been in perfect balance since before anything existed to measure it.

Its organs are the spherical bodies—those house-sized bladders that hang at various depths, tethered by braided vine, pulsing with a slow rhythmic contraction you can feel in your teeth if you hold very still. They are not lungs. They are not hearts. They are something for which no word exists: processing centers that filter the water passing through them and extrude, from their undersides, ropes of refined mineral that fall away into the depths like slow rain. They are refineries. They are factories. They are the Stillworks' works.

Its nervous system is the filament-web—miles of hair-thin strand connecting everything to everything else, conducting a slow galvanic hum that is not electricity, not exactly, but something older and stranger. A form of communication so slow that a single signal might take a year to travel from one end of the body to the other, and so vast that the entire web vibrates with the accumulated chatter of a million simultaneous transmissions, producing a high, constant hiss like steam escaping, like static, like the sound of a thousand tiny voices speaking words you almost understand.

Its cells are the fish—those silver, many-finned creatures that move in schools with a precision suggesting not intelligence but design, following the same paths they have always followed, turning at the same fronds, pausing at the same junctions, forever. Its platelets are the great shelled creatures, the size of boats, plated in calcium carbonate, drifting with tentacles extended, filtering, processing, maintaining. Its voice is the subsonic rumble from the depths—felt more than heard, a vibration in the bones, the sound of the planet itself grinding through its eternal cycle without friction, without wear, without end.

It has no brain. It does not need one. Every part knows what to do because every part has always done what it does. The roots know to filter. The fronds know to stand. The bladders know to pulse. The web knows to conduct. The system is a mind without a thinker, a consciousness without a self, a purpose without a goal. It does not think. It does not plan. It does not want or fear or hope or regret. It simply runs. It runs because running is what it does. It produces because production is its nature. It maintains itself because maintenance is the only state it has ever known.

And it has been running forever.

Not "for a long time." Not "since before memory." Not "for millennia." Forever. It achieved its equilibrium before anything existed to witness it, and it will maintain that equilibrium after everything capable of witnessing it has ceased to be. It is not a thing that is alive in time. It is a thing that *is* time—or rather, it is a thing for which time has no meaning, because nothing changes, because every moment is identical to every last moment and every next moment. The wave is always in the same position. The frond is always at the same height. The bladder is always at the same point in its pulse. The fish are always in the same place following the same path. The whole system is always, eternally, exactly and precisely itself.

The Stillworks does not know you are here. It does not know anything. It has no awareness, no perception, no experience of the world it creates and is created by. But it responds to you—oh, it responds to you—the way a body responds to a foreign particle, the way a machine responds to an unexpected input, the way an equation responds to a new variable. The web tightens slightly around you. The pulse shifts by a fraction of a beat. The bladders adjust their rhythm to account for your warmth, your movement, your foreign matter. Something in the vast, slow, eternal machinery recalibrates to process you.

You are not the first thing to enter the Stillworks. Water enters it. Light enters it. The waste products of its own metabolism enter it. But you are the first thing to enter it that is not already part of it. You are the first perturbation in a system that has been in perfect equilibrium since before equilibrium had a name.

And the Stillworks will process you.

Not with malice. Not with hunger. Not with any intent at all. It will process you the way it processes everything: slowly, efficiently, completely, breaking you down into components that can be reused, recycled, reincorporated into the great machinery that never stops, never changes, never ends. Your warmth will be absorbed into the cool green water. Your movement will be dampened by the dense medium until you are still, until you are part of the stillness, until you are part of the eternal equilibrium the Stillworks has maintained since before you were born and will maintain long after you are gone.

You swim through it. The filament-web parts around you and closes behind you. The pulse beats in your chest. The hiss fills your ears. The rumble vibrates in your bones. And the Stillworks—vast, patient, eternal, alive, purposeless, complete—continues its work.

Nothing changes.

Nothing changes.

Nothing changes.
