What lives on the Plateau of Sunk Lēah is not easily categorized, because the plateau itself resists the boundaries between the geological, the botanical, and the architectural. To describe what lives here is to describe a slow, warm, damp convergence—a place where stone, moss, and human industry are being kneaded together by time into a single, breathing entity.

**The Lēah**
The primary resident of the plateau is the Lēah, though to call it a plant is to miss the point. It is a continent of warmth spread over a continent of cold stone. It is a single, ancient mind—or something so adjacent to a mind that the distinction hardly matters—expressed in filaments and fronds. It does not merely grow on the stone; it digests it, pulling nutrients from the deep grey-violet rock with a patience that makes human industry look like a tantrum. 

The Lēah is warm. It is the only reliable warmth on the plateau, a metabolic heat that rises from its dense, black-green carpets like the breath of a sleeping animal. It loves the town of Sunk. It loves the geothermal vents and the steam pipes, wrapping them in thick, exuberant halos of growth until the machinery disappears entirely beneath a layer of living insulation. It is slowly eating the town, but it is also holding it together, filling the cracks in the stone, buttressing the sagging timbers, and pulling the abandoned margins back into itself. The Lēah is the plateau’s true architect now, using the human structures as scaffolding for its own slow, hydraulic expansion. It is building something, though whether it is a nest, a cocoon, or a tomb is a question the locals have learned not to ask.

**The People of Sunk**
The three thousand souls who live in the town of Sunk are not so much inhabitants as they are parasites, or perhaps symbiotes, living within the warm folds of the Lēah. They have adapted to the plateau’s underexposure. Their eyes have adjusted to the dim, yellowish half-light and the deep purple shadows; they navigate not by sight but by the rhythm of footsteps on wet stone, by the silhouette of a shoulder, the particular gait of a neighbor. 

They are a people defined by dampness. Their clothes never dry; their skin is always filmed with moisture. They process the Lēah, cutting it away in sections and rendering it into dense fibers for the lowlands, and then they feed it steam and mineral slurry so it will grow back. It is a closed loop, a quiet bargain. 

But there are fractures among them. The geothermal workers are the only ones who regularly leave, descending to the lowlands on the groaning cable cars. They are touched by the deep earth, working in the roar of the wellheads where speech is impossible, communicating only through hand signals. When they return from the lowlands, they carry a look of profound dislocation, as if the bright, dry air of the world below has burned something out of them. 

Then there are the old colonists, the families who have been here for generations. They are the ones who know the plateau’s true nature. They do not speak of the things seen in the thick mist—the corridors that turn where no corridor turns, the basins holding water that is too thick, too dark, too warm. They walk quickly through the fog, eyes averted from the condensation basins, because they understand that the Lēah is not just a resource. It is a presence that is watching, waiting, and slowly closing in.

**The Marginal Things**
In the deepest layers of the Lēah, in the false soil a meter thick, live the things that have surrendered to the dark. There is the flightless beetle, blind and patient, chewing through the accumulated organic matter. There are the pale, thin worms. And there is the mouse-like thing—an evolutionary relic with a tail like a fern frond and eyes that have forgotten how to see. It lives in the walls of the oldest buildings, coming out in the permanent twilight to graze on the warm moss. It is a creature that has given up on the surface world, retreating into the damp, breathing walls of the town.

Above them, in the mist hours, the slow moth swims through the air, its wings beating with a lethargy that matches the plateau’s own rhythm. It does not hurry. Nothing on the plateau hurries. Even the midges, rising from the condensation basins, do not swarm so much as they accumulate, a thin, persistent haze of life.

**The Stone and the Sound**
Finally, there is the plateau itself—the stone that is less a foundation and more a slow, turning thought. It is alive in the way that a bell is alive after it has been struck. The low, resonant tone that sometimes rises from the rock is the plateau’s voice, a vibration that has been traveling through the compressed millennia since the world was young. 

The stone is being planed down, eroded by the patient work of the Lēah and the intermittent, half-hearted rains. At the Face, where the cross-section of time is exposed, the stone reveals its true nature: it is not a solid thing, but a process. It is disappearing. The people of Sunk avoid the Face because looking at it induces a vertigo of duration—the terrifying realization that the plateau is slowly erasing itself, and that the Lēah, the town, and the people are merely the thin, warm scum on the surface of a rock that is in the process of thinking itself out of existence.

What lives here is a warm, damp, purple-shadowed limbo. It is a place where the distinction between the living and the dead, the built and the grown, the past and the future, has been worn away by the mist and the moss, leaving only the slow, patient, inescapable pulse of the Lēah.
