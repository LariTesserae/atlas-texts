To stand on the walkway is to stand on the skin of a vast, breathing beast, and the creatures that live here are the parasites, the antibodies, and the symbiotes that course through its iron veins. They are the **Slough-Walkers**, and they are as much a part of the machine as the rust that blooms on its seams.

You will not see them at first. They are creatures of the fog and the sub-aquatic gloom, evolved to blend with the grime and the moisture. But if you fix your eyes on the cascading rivulets or the swaying, overburdened cables, you will begin to discern their shapes. 

They are humanoid in the loosest sense—bipedal, roughly the size of a grown man, but their proportions have been shaped by the vertical maze of their home. Their limbs are elongated, their joints articulated in ways that allow them to fold into the narrow pipe-chases and walkways. Their skin is not skin at all, but a thick, pallid integument that secretes a thin layer of natural oil, allowing them to slip through the oily runoff and resist the ever-present damp. It is the color of wet calcium and old brass, and it provides perfect camouflage against the calcified masonry.

But it is their feet and hands that truly betray them. To walk on grated iron slick with condensation, to leap between swaying pipes, requires a grip that flesh and bone cannot provide. The Slough-Walkers possess broad, splayed extremities lined with thousands of microscopic, hair-like setae—much like the feet of a gecko, but adapted for wet metal. When they press their palms against a rusted beam, they adhere with a soft, wet *thwack*. They move with a fluid, unsettling silence, skittering along the undersides of walkways, hanging inverted from the great horizontal pipes, their oiled skin catching the faint violet glow of the tram-lines.

They are the gardeners of this mechanical anthill. The riot of engineered vegetation does not maintain itself; left unchecked, the tensile ivy would crush the tenements, and the bathtub ferns would clog the water-wheels. The Slough-Walkers prune it. They harvest the thick ivy, stripping it from the iron lattices and weaving it into the heavy, rope-like cloaks they wear to protect themselves from the scalding steam-vents. They feed on the fungal blooms that filter the factory exhaust, their digestive systems uniquely capable of breaking down the toxic, sweet rot and converting it into the energy required to survive in the cool, oxygen-thin air. 

Their relationship with the water is one of absolute symbiosis. They do not fear the surges and floods; they ride them. When the catch-basins overflow and the deluge rushes down the vertical chutes, the Slough-Walkers are swept along with the current, their bodies perfectly hydrodynamic. They breathe the moisture. Along their ribcages, hidden beneath the woven ivy-cloaks, are vestigial gill-slits that extract oxygen directly from the heavy, mist-laden air and the spray of the waterfalls. To drown in the open air is impossible for them; they are as comfortable submerged in a rushing overflow pipe as they are walking the dry grating.

They do not build; they inhabit the interstices. They do not construct new architecture, for the machine builds itself. Instead, they claim the transient spaces—the gaps left when a district is cannibalized, the hollowed-out ribs of abandoned water-towers, the warm, vibrating spaces adjacent to the sub-level turbines. They are nomads of the vertical, following the flow of the water and the heat. When a district is walled off to cool an overheating reactor, the Slough-Walkers feel the shift in the ambient temperature through the soles of their feet and migrate upward, toward the amber glow of the Edison bulbs and the hiss of fresh steam.

Their society is a silent, percussive language. In a world where sound is muffled by wet air and absorbed by dense iron, the spoken word is useless. Instead, they communicate through the medium of the machine itself. They tap out complex rhythms on the pipes—a language of felt vibrations rather than heard ones. A rapid, staccato beat on a brass conduit warns of a burst pipe or a structural collapse. A slow, resonant thrum through the iron beams signals a gathering, a harvest of a particularly rich fungal bloom, or the discovery of a new, warm nesting ground. If you press your ear to the cold metal of the walkway, you might feel the echo of their conversations, a ghostly Morse code thrumming through the bones of the city.

They are not the masters of this place, nor its victims. They are its custodians, its scavengers, its quiet pulse. They exist in the balance of the machine, feeding on its waste, pruning its growth, and navigating its endless, dripping dark. As the steam-vent hisses and the white vapor rolls through the canyon, a Slough-Walker might momentarily materialize out of the fog, clinging to a cable above you, its gill-slits fluttering, its oiled skin gleaming in the bioluminescent moss-light. It watches you with eyes that are entirely black, adapted to see in the deepest, wettest shadows. Then, with a soft, wet click of its adhering feet, it turns and vanishes back into the iron, leaving only the faint, rhythmic tap-tap-tap of its passage echoing through the pipes.
