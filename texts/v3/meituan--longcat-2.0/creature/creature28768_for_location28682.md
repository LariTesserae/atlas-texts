# The Inhabitants of Aethren

---

## The People of the Basins

They are not many. Perhaps two hundred, perhaps fewer — the number is difficult to fix because they do not gather in groups large enough to count easily, and because the act of counting implies a desire to track change, and change is not a concept that has much purchase here.

They are small. Not unusually so, but compact, economical in their proportions, as if the thin air and the cool damp have encouraged a body plan that conserves heat and moves efficiently over sharp stone. Their skin is pale — not the pallor of illness or absence, but the clean, matte pallor of something that has never needed to darken. The light here does not demand melanin. Their hair is dark, straight, worn long by both sexes and usually tied back with cord made from the fibrous inner bark of the woody shrubs. Their eyes are dark as well, and they have a way of looking at things — at you, at the stone, at the water — that is attentive without being urgent. They see. They register. They do not react.

Their hands are their most remarkable feature. They are broad across the palm, with thick, blunt fingers and nails that are worn short and slightly ridged from constant contact with stone. The pads of their fingers are calloused in specific, diagnostic patterns — here from gripping a splitting wedge, there from the repeated abrasion of drawing a blade across a grinding stone, there from the precise pressure of fitting one rock face against another. Their hands tell the story of their lives with the specificity of a written record, and their lives are, almost entirely, about stone.

They dress in layers of woven plant fiber — not cloth, exactly, but something coarser and more deliberate, made from the long stems of the bamboo-grass and the inner fibers of the creeping vine. The garments are sleeveless, close-fitting, dyed in the muted tones that are available: grey from ironstone mud, pale green from moss, a deep rust-red from the ironstone itself. They wear soft-soled shoes made from layered fiber and the shed skin of the deep-crevice creatures, stitched with bone needles, and these shoes are replaced often, because the sharp rock destroys them, and because the people of the plateau understand that the interface between foot and stone is a thing that must be maintained.

They are quiet. Not silent — they speak to each other in low, unhurried voices, and what they say is practical, observational, grounded in the immediate: *the pool in the third basin is running clear today* (it is always running clear), *the grazers have moved to the eastern face* (they always move to the eastern face at this time), *the new wall in the lower settlement is nearly complete* (it has been nearly complete for a long time, and will be nearly complete for a long time more, because the work is not about completion but about the doing of it). They do not tell stories. They do not argue. They do not laugh, exactly, but sometimes one of them will make a small sound — a soft exhalation, a quiet hum — that indicates a state of satisfaction with the way a stone has split, or the way water moves through a channel, or the way the moss is growing on a particular wall.

They do not have leaders. They do not have hierarchy. They have *roles*, and the roles are assigned by aptitude and inclination and are as fixed as everything else here. There are the stone-workers, who maintain the walls and the channels and the buildings. There are the water-tenders, who monitor the pools and clear the channels of debris (there is almost no debris, but the monitoring is the point, not the clearing). There are the growers, who tend the vegetation pockets, transplanting moss, encouraging ferns, managing the slow, dense expansion of green into the spaces where green is welcome. There are the trackers, who watch the grazers and the birds and the deep-crevice things and report — to no one in particular, to everyone in general — on the state of the patterns. And there are the listeners, who sit beside the pools and place their hands on the stone and feel for the low, resonant vibration that comes from below, and who know — in a way that is not quite knowledge and not quite instinct — when the deep things are moving, and when they are still.

The listeners are the closest thing the settlement has to priests, though they would not use that word. They are the ones who maintain the relationship with what lives below, the ones who understand that the plateau's equilibrium is not self-sustaining but *negotiated*, a conversation between the surface and the depths that has been going on for longer than memory and will continue for longer still.

## The Grazers

They are called, by the people of the plateau, something that translates roughly as *the ones who walk the old lines*, though the word for *old* in their language is the same as the word for *always*, so the name might equally mean *the ones who walk the always-lines*.

There are thousands of them. Tens of thousands. They are the most numerous large creatures on the plateau, and they are, in a very real sense, the reason the plateau looks the way it does. Their paths — the smooth, worn tracks that cross the bare rock between the vegetation pockets — are the plateau's circulation system, its visible record of movement and time. The rock is hard, but the grazers are patient, and there are so many of them, and they have been walking the same routes for so long, that their feet — six each, small, broad, padded — have worn grooves into the stone that are deep enough to catch the mist and hold it, creating tiny channels of moisture that feed the moss and the lichen along the paths. The grazers do not merely cross the landscape. They *irrigate* it.

They are not social, exactly, but they are not solitary either. They move in lines — single file, evenly spaced, each one following the one ahead with a precision that suggests not obedience but *resonance*, as if they are all tuned to the same frequency and the frequency tells them where to walk and how fast to walk and when to stop and eat and when to move on. They do not jostle. They do not overtake. If one stops, the ones behind stop as well, and they wait, with the absolute patience of creatures that have never known urgency, until the one ahead moves again.

Their diet is moss, lichen, and the small woody shrubs, and they eat with a slow, methodical thoroughness that strips a patch of vegetation down to the rock and then moves on, and the vegetation grows back, and they return, and the cycle is as fixed as the water in the pools. They do not overgraze. They do not undergraze. They take exactly what the vegetation can afford to lose and no more, and the people of the plateau watch this with the same quiet attention they give to everything, and they do not interfere, because the grazers know what they are doing, and the people know that the grazers know, and this mutual understanding is one of the pillars on which the equilibrium rests.

They are not tame. They are not wild. They are *themselves*, completely and without apology, and they regard the people of the plateau with the same calm, dark-eyed attention that the people regard them. There is no fear. There is no aggression. There is only the shared understanding that they are both part of the same system, the same pattern, the same unchanging fact.

## The Birds

The small ones are called *commentators* by the people of the plateau, and the name is apt. They are not singers. They do not perform. They *observe* and they *report*, and their calls — soft, brief, conversational — are a running narration on the state of the world: *the moss is growing here, the water is clear there, the grazers have passed, the stone is warm, the stone is cool, the mist is gathering, the mist is lifting*. They are the plateau's sensory apparatus, its distributed awareness, and they are everywhere, in every pocket of green, on every wall, perched on every channel-edge, and their constant, quiet chatter is the sound of the plateau thinking about itself.

They build their nests in the moss, in the crevices of the buildings, in the hollow stems of the bamboo-grass, and their nests are small, precise, and identical — not because they are copied, but because the materials dictate the form, and the materials are always the same. They lay two eggs. They raise two chicks. The chicks fledge, mature, and take their place in the commentary, and the cycle continues, and the number of birds does not increase or decrease, because the plateau supports exactly as many as it supports and no more.

The raptors are different. They are larger — wingspan perhaps two meters — and they are silent, and they circle at the plateau's edges, riding the updrafts where the sharp rock meets the sky, and they do not hunt, exactly, because there is nothing to hunt. The grazers are too numerous to be prey. The birds are too small and too quick. The raptors circle because circling is what they do, because the updrafts are there, because the plateau's edge is a boundary and boundaries are meant to be watched. They are the plateau's sentinels, its border guards, and they have been circling for as long as anyone can remember, and they will continue to circle for as long as the updrafts blow, which is to say, always.

## The Deep Things

They are old. Older than the settlement, older than the grazers' paths, older perhaps than the plateau itself, or at least older than the plateau's current configuration. They live in the lower strata, in the caves and fissures that riddle the rock beneath the surface, and they are large — not monstrously so, but large enough that their passage through the underground spaces can be felt as a vibration in the stone, a low, resonant hum that rises and falls like breathing.

No one has seen them clearly. The listeners have felt them, have placed their hands on the stone and known, with a certainty that bypasses the intellect, that something vast and slow and *aware* is moving in the darkness below. The track-keepers have found their traces: the shed skins, pale and translucent, draped over rocks like discarded gloves, large enough to suggest a body the size of a human torso; the three-toed tracks in the damp clay beside the deepest pools, prints larger than a human hand, with a stride that suggests a creature that does not hurry because it has never needed to hurry, because nothing in its world has ever required haste.

They are not hostile. They are not benevolent. They are *other*, and their relationship with the surface is one of mutual avoidance maintained by long custom. They do not come up. The people do not go down. The water that seeps from the rock — the water that feeds the pools, that sustains the vegetation, that is the lifeblood of the plateau's equilibrium — passes through their domain, and they do not poison it, and they do not dam it, and they do not alter it in any way, and in return, the people of the plateau do not disturb them, do not send light or noise or intrusion into the depths, and the arrangement holds, and has held, and will hold.

The listeners say — when they say anything about it, which is rare — that the deep things are the plateau's *memory*. That they are the reason the plateau is still. That they hold the equilibrium in place not by force but by *inertia*, by the sheer weight of their existence, by the fact of their presence in the rock, the way a keystone holds an arch. Remove them, the listeners say, and the plateau would begin to change. The water would find new channels. The grazers would wander. The people would begin to vary. The mist would lift or thicken. The light would shift. Everything would become *eventful*, and the plateau would become, for the first time in its long existence, a place where things *happen*.

No one wants this. No one fears it either, because it is not a thing that can be wanted or feared. It is simply the alternative to what is, and what is, is sufficient.

## The Moss

It deserves its own entry, because it is, in a very real sense, the plateau's primary organism, the thing that ties everything together, the green thread that runs through the entire system.

It grows everywhere. On the rocks, on the walls, on the roofs of the buildings, on the channels, on the pools' edges, on the bare stone of the open plateau where the mist is thickest and the grazers have not yet reached. It is deep emerald and almost black-green, and it is dense — not the loose, casual growth of moss in other places, but a tight, interwoven mat that is almost structural, that holds the thin soil in place, that absorbs the moisture and releases it slowly, that provides food for the grazers and nesting material for the birds and a soft, damp surface for the people to walk on when they leave the sharp stone of the paths.

It is the plateau's skin. Its interface with the air. Its way of holding water, of moderating temperature, of converting the flat, directionless light into the chemical energy that drives the entire visible system. Without the moss, the plateau would be bare rock and open water and wind. With the moss, it is a place where life is possible, where the grazers can eat and the birds can nest and the people can grow their small, thick-leaved shrubs and their waxy ferns and their coin-leaved vines.

And the moss is still. Not dead — never dead — but still. It grows, but it does not spread. It photosynthesizes, but it does not increase in biomass. It is in perfect balance with the grazers, who eat exactly as much as it grows, and with the moisture, which provides exactly as much water as it needs, and with the light, which provides exactly as much energy as it can use. It is the equilibrium made visible, the plateau's central truth expressed in green.

---

## The Water

And finally, the water, which is not a creature but is, in some ways, the most alive thing on the plateau.

It emerges from the rock. It fills the pools. It overflows through the notches. It travels through the channels. It seeps away at the bottom. It returns to the rock. And the circuit is closed, and the volume is constant, and the rate is constant, and the temperature is constant, and the mineral content is constant, and the clarity is constant, and the water does not flow so much as *persist*, a single, continuous, unchanging presence that is the same water it has always been, that has been running through this circuit since before the settlement, before the people, before the grazers, before the moss, perhaps before the plateau itself rose to its current elevation.

The water is the plateau's consciousness. Its awareness of itself. The way it knows that it is here, that it is still, that it is complete. The people tend it not because it needs tending but because tending it is how they participate in the equilibrium, how they place their hands on the pulse of the world and feel it beating, steady and slow and unchanging, and know that they are part of it, and it is part of them, and the distinction between the two is, in the end, no more meaningful than the distinction between one drop of water and the next in a pool that has never known a ripple.

---

## The Plateau Itself

And beneath all of it — beneath the moss and the grazers and the birds and the people and the water and the deep things — the plateau.

The plateau is alive.

Not in the way that a person is alive, or a bird, or a grazer, or even a moss. It is alive in the way that a chord is alive — a single, complex vibration that contains within itself all the frequencies that are needed to produce the sound it makes. It is a system that has achieved consciousness of itself, that has resolved all its internal tensions, that has brought every variable to its optimal value and then *held* it there, not by force, not by will, but by the simple, total, unwavering fact of its own completeness.

It does not think. It does not plan. It does not want. It *is*. And in being, it sustains everything that lives on it and in it and through it, and it asks nothing in return except continuation, except the maintenance of the pattern, except the quiet, daily, unchanging act of being exactly what it is.

The people know this. They have always known it. It is the first thing a child learns and the last thing an elder forgets: that the plateau is not a place they live. It is a thing they are part of. A body they inhabit. A mind they contribute to. A stillness they maintain by being still themselves.

And the plateau, in turn, maintains them. Provides for them. Holds them in its cool, damp, changeless embrace and asks only that they continue — that they carry the baskets along the paths, that they work the stone with the tools, that they tend the pools and watch the grazers and listen to the deep things and let the moss grow on their roofs, and that they do these things not out of duty or fear or hope, but out of the simple, profound, unshakeable understanding that this is what it means to be here, to be part of Aethren, to be a cell in the body of a place that has solved itself and found the answer to be *stillness*.

The plateau is not waiting.

The plateau is not becoming.

The plateau is *finished*, and it is *still*, and it is *damp*, and it is *cool*, and the water beads on the dark stone like sweat on the skin of something that is not asleep but not awake but simply, perfectly, *here*.

And the small birds comment.

And the grazers walk their always-lines.

And the people work their stone.

And the deep things breathe in the dark below.

And the moss holds the moisture and the light and the thin soil in its green, patient grip.

And the water overflows its stone lips at exactly the rate it has always overflowed.

And nothing changes.

Nothing changes.

Nothing changes.
