# The Workers

They do not have names. Names imply distinction, and distinction implies separation, and nothing here is separate. But if you were to classify — and you would, because your mind insists on classifying — you would say there are three kinds. Three scales. Three tempos in the same long song.

The smallest are the size of your thumbnail, or smaller. They have bodies that are not bodies so much as *processes* — segmented, articulated, made of something that looks like chitin but isn't, something that has mineral woven into it, pale threads of it running through their shells like the veins run through the stone. They click. That is the word. Not because they make a sound — though they do, a dry, precise clicking — but because they *are* clicking, they are the sound itself given form and legs and purpose. They have legs. Six, or eight, or more, depending on the individual, depending on the task. Their legs are fine and jointed and end in points that grip the stone, the moss, the bodies of other small ones, and they move in columns that stretch back and forth across the green mats like conveyor belts, like the flow of current through a wire, and they are *carrying* something — you cannot see what, it is too small, but you can see the purpose in their movement, the direction, the column that goes *here* and the column that goes *there*, and where the two columns meet there is a *transfer*, a passing of something from one to the next, and the next column carries it on, carries it further, and you follow the trail with your eyes and it goes and goes and goes until it disappears into the mat and you lose it and it is still going, still flowing, still *working*.

They eat the stone. Not the whole stone — that would be absurd — but the film of it, the dust, the micro-particles that the wind deposits and the water loosens and the larger animals grind free. They have mouthparts that are also tools, that abrade and dissolve and extract, and what they extract — the minerals, the trace elements, the pale threads that the vegetation follows — they incorporate into their bodies, into their shells, into the next generation, into the *system*. When they die they do not decay. They *deposit*. Their bodies are too mineral for decay, too rich in the wrong things, and they accumulate, and the next generation grows through them, incorporates them, builds on them, and the mat thickens, and the soil deepens, and the stone is one body-length closer to being *eaten*, to being *converted*, to being *made into this*.

---

The middle ones are the size of your hand, or your forearm, or your torso. They have shells. That is the first thing you notice. Not shells like a snail's — not soft, not spiral, not a single unified thing — but shells like *architecture*, like something built from the outside in, like an exoskeleton that has been *designed* by a mind that thinks in terms of function and load and stress and also, somehow, in terms of beauty. The shells are mineral. Not covered in mineral, not coated — *made of it*, the same pale-veined stone that the plateau is made of, secreted or grown or *metabolized* from the dust and the water and the bodies of the small ones, and they are *beautiful* in the way that a beam is beautiful, in the way that a truss is beautiful, in the way that a load-bearing wall is beautiful — the beauty of something that works, that carries weight, that holds.

They move slowly. Not the slowness of laziness or cold-bloodedness, but the slowness of *deliberation*, the slowness of a thing that is *processing* as it moves, that is *doing something* with each step. They walk on the mat and the mat compresses beneath them and you can see the pattern of their passage, the trail they leave, and the trail is not random — it is a *channel*, worn smooth by repetition, and the small ones run in it, and the water runs in it, and the larger ones follow it, and everything flows through these channels like blood through vessels, like current through circuitry, like *material through a production line*.

They eat the small ones. Not all of them. Not even most. But some — a steady, patient percentage, a harvest that is also a population control, a culling that is also a *gathering*, a *collection*, a *transfer of mass from one form to the next*. They incorporate what they eat into their shells, into their bodies, into the structures they build. Because they build. That is the thing. They build. Not nests, not shelters, not the crude architecture of survival — they build *infrastructure*, they build *the system itself*, they build the channels and the nodes and the *transfer points* where the small ones meet and exchange their invisible cargo. They build the places where the water collects and the vegetation thickens and the next generation of everything will grow. They build the future, in other words, slowly, out of stone dust and dead small ones and their own mineral shells, and when they die they become *infrastructure* — their bodies too rich, too mineral, too *structural* to decay, and the next generation builds on them, and the system grows, and the mat thickens, and the stone is consumed.

---

The large ones. The largest. They are the size of a boulder, or a small house, or — and this is the number that your mind resists — the size of a *hill*. They live for longer than you can imagine. They move slower than you can watch. They are the *geology* of the animal world, the *tectonic* ones, the ones that shape the landscape not by intention but by *presence*, by the simple fact of being *here* and *this big* and *this slow*.

They have shells too. Their shells are *mountains* — not covered in mountains, not shaped like mountains, but *actual mountains*, actual upthrusts of pale-veined stone that have grown on their backs, that have *metabolized* out of their bodies, that have been deposited over centuries, over millennia, into structures that are indistinguishable from the plateau itself. From above — from the air, from the edge where you first saw this place — you would not know which ridges were stone and which were the shells of the large ones, and that is the point, that is the *design*, that is the closed loop of it: the large ones grow stone on their backs, and the stone becomes the plateau, and the plateau grows the vegetation, and the vegetation feeds the small ones, and the small ones feed the middle ones, and the middle ones build the channels that the large ones walk through, and the large ones walk through them and their weight *compacts* the channels and their movement *shapes* the mat and their slow, grinding, patient passage *is* the geology, *is* the mountain-building, *is* the process by which the stone becomes life becomes stone becomes life becomes stone.

They do not eat. Or rather, they do not eat the way the others eat — they do not hunt, do not gather, do not *consume* in any way you would recognize. Instead they *metabolize* the mineral light. The phosphorescence that pulses through the stone when the sun hits it at certain angles, the UV and the IR and the spectra you cannot see — they feel it. Their shells are *lenses*. The pale-veined stone on their backs is shaped, polished, tuned by centuries of growth into structures that focus and channel and *harvest* the light, and they convert it — slowly, at quarter-speed, at the speed of a factory floor running at idle — into the energy they need to move, to grow, to *be*. They are solar-powered. They are *light-powered*. They are the power source and the product and the process all at once, and they move through the world like walking power plants, like mobile factories, like the living, breathing, *metabolizing* heart of the entire system.

And when they die — and they do die, eventually, after centuries, after millennia — they *become* the plateau. Their bodies are too large, too mineral, too *geological* to decay. They lie where they fall, and the mat grows over them, and the small ones colonize their shells, and the middle ones build on their remains, and the stone that was their body becomes the stone that is the plateau, and the plateau grows, and the system continues, and the cycle is closed, and the loop is perfect, and nothing is wasted, and nothing is lost, and everything — *everything* — is converted, is *processed*, is *made into more of itself*.

# The System

This is what lives in the Verdant Foundry. Not a single creature but a *collective*, not an ecosystem but a *machine* that is not a machine, not a factory that is not a factory, not a process that has a purpose but a process that *is* its own purpose.

The small ones click. The middle ones build. The large ones *are*. And between and around and through and under and over all of them, the vegetation grows — the rosettes and the columns and the fans, the structural, architectural, *designed* vegetation that follows the mineral veins and the mineral veins that follow the vegetation, neither cause nor effect, neither blueprint nor builder, both at once, always at once, the way a river is both the water and the channel, the way a fire is both the fuel and the burning.

The water moves through everything. The light moves through everything. The sound — the constant, overwhelming, billion-voiced sound — moves through everything, and it is not noise, it is *information*, it is the sound of the system *reporting to itself*, of the parts *communicating* with the whole, of the whole *knowing* what the parts are doing, not through any mechanism you could point to, not through any organ or structure or *thing*, but through the simple density of it, the simple fact that there are so many of them, so close together, for so long, that they *must* form a system, must *become* a system, must *function* as a system, the way enough neurons in a network *must* become a mind.

And it is a mind. Not a conscious mind, not a thinking mind, not a mind that knows it is a mind, but a mind in the way that a river is a mind, in the way that a glacier is a mind, in the way that a *process* is a mind — a distributed, patient, slow, relentless, *processing* thing that takes input and produces output and is the input and the output and the process all at once, forever, in a loop, in a cycle, in a closed and perfect and *terrible* and *beautiful* system that runs on rock and water and light and time and needs nothing else, wants nothing else, *is* nothing else, and is simply, impossibly, *overwhelmingly*, *here*.
