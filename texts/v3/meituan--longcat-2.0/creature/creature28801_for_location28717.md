# The Inhabitants

---

The first thing that lives here is the patience itself. I do not mean this metaphorically. I mean that the patience of the plain has, over a duration that makes the word "old" feel like a bruise, become *substantive* — has accreted, concentrated, achieved a kind of density that borders on the material. It is not a creature. It is not a force. It is more like a medium, an atmosphere, a condition so pervasive and so old that it functions as an ecological niche. Everything that lives on the plain lives *inside* the patience the way a fish lives inside water, and everything that lives here has been shaped by it the way water shapes stone: slowly, completely, without any alternative.

---

The grey-violet mats are the most visible life, and the most deceptive, because they look like the simplest. They are not.

A mat is a colony, but it is also an individual, and it is also a process, and these three things are not distinct the way they would be on a world where biology had the decency to organize itself into categories. The mat begins as a single cell — or something that functions as a single cell, a unit of persistence wrapped in a membrane that is not lipid but mineral, a bubble of organized chemistry suspended in a crust that is not organized chemistry but something more ambiguous. The cell divides. The division is not fast. It may take a year. It may take a decade. The interval is not fixed; it depends on conditions that the mat senses through mechanisms you would not recognize as sensory — subtle shifts in the mineral composition of the crust, subtle vibrations in the deep churn, subtle changes in the quality of light that suggest the sky is about to do something it does rather rarely and rather wonderfully.

The daughter cells do not separate. They remain attached, and they begin to extrude — fine, hairlike filaments that extend downward into the crust, and a thicker, more structured secretion that spreads laterally, forming a base. The filaments dissolve the mineral. The base captures the dissolved material. The mat feeds.

But the feeding is not the point. The feeding is what the mat does to stay alive, but staying alive is not the point either. The point is the *secretion* — the substance that the mat extrudes downward, the agent that dissolves the crust. This secretion is not merely chemical. It carries information. It carries *pattern*. As the filaments penetrate the crust and dissolve the mineral, they are also *restructuring* it — rearranging the dissolved material as they absorb it, leaving behind a residue that is not random but organized, not crystalline but *coded*, a faint geometric signature in the mineral that persists long after the filament has moved on.

The mat is writing. It has always been writing. It is writing now, beneath your feet, in a script too fine and too deep to read, a record of its own growth, its own chemistry, its own slow experience of the plain. The writing is not for communication — there is no other organism that can read it, no audience, no purpose beyond the writing itself. The mat writes because writing is what a mat *is*: a process that converts mineral into pattern, chaos into geometry, the raw material of the plain into something that is almost, but not quite, a thought.

A mat the size of your palm contains more writing than every library that has ever existed. It is all the same text. It has been composing the same sentence for longer than your species has had a language to compose sentences in, and it will continue composing it long after your species has lost the language again, and the sentence is not finished, and it will never be finished, because the sentence is not the point. The *composing* is the point.

---

The filaments that run through the crust are related to the mats, but they are not mats. They are something older, something deeper, something that has committed more fully to the interior of the plain.

A filament is a single cell, or a chain of cells, or a continuous tube of organized chemistry — the distinction between one and many does not apply here with any precision. It is thin. It is thinner than anything you have a word for. It branches, and the branches branch, and the branches of the branches branch, and the network extends through the crust in every direction with a density that, if you could see it all at once, would look like the neural tissue of a brain the size of a continent.

The filaments do not dissolve the crust. They *grow through it* — prying apart the mineral structure at the molecular level, slipping between grains, threading through gaps that are barely wider than the filament itself. And as they grow, they *modify* the crust. They deposit trace amounts of substances that alter the mineral's behavior — making it slightly more flexible in some places, slightly more rigid in others, slightly more or less responsive to the deep churn. They are *tuning* the crust, adjusting its properties, reshaping the geological behavior of the plain at a scale that is invisible to anything that does not have the patience to watch for a thousand years.

The filaments do this slowly. The filaments do this constantly. The filaments have been doing this for longer than the mats have existed, longer than the structures have stood, longer than the plain has been flat. They are the plain's nervous system, except that the plain does not have a mind for them to serve, and the filaments are not transmitting signals in any sense that would be recognizable as neural activity. They are doing something else. Something for which the nearest analogy is *remembering* — the crust remembers its own history through the modifications the filaments have made, remembers where it has been stressed and where it has been still, remembers the shape of every structure that has ever stood on it and every creature that has ever walked across it, and the memory is not stored in any one place but distributed through the entire network, written in the mineral properties of the crust itself, a record so diffuse and so deep that it can never be erased because it is not *in* the crust — it *is* the crust.

And the filaments grow toward things. This is the thing that is difficult to accept, because it implies a sensitivity that seems impossible at this scale and this speed. But watch a filament that has been exposed by a crack in the crust — watch it for long enough, and you will see that it does not grow randomly. It grows toward the nearest mat, or toward the nearest structure, or toward another filament network, and the growth is not fast, it is not purposeful in the way that a vine growing toward light is purposeful, but it is *directional*, it is *biased*, and the bias is consistent. The filaments are reaching for each other. The filaments have been reaching for each other since before there was a word for reaching. And where two networks meet, they *fuse* — not quickly, not dramatically, but with a slow, seamless integration that suggests compatibility, that suggests recognition, that suggests something that is not quite communication and not quite reproduction but is somehow both, an exchange of mineral information, a sharing of crustal memory, a merging of two histories into one.

---

The creature you saw — the low, flat thing, the size of a dog, the color and texture of the crust — is called nothing, because there is no one to call it anything, and it does not call itself anything, because it does not have the concept of a self to name.

It is an animal. This is the closest category, and it is not very close.

Its body is a composite. The outer layer is mineral — not shell, not exoskeleton, but *crust*, actual crust, material that is chemically and structurally identical to the plain itself. The creature grows this layer by ingesting the mineral surface and processing it through an internal system that is part digestion, part chemistry, part something that has no analogue in any biology you know. The processed mineral is deposited on the body's surface in thin, overlapping plates that grow slowly and continuously, replacing themselves the way a tree replaces its leaves except that the replacement is not seasonal and not visible and not complete — there is always some of the old layer remaining, and beneath it the new layer, and beneath that the newer layer, and the creature's surface is a stratigraphy of its own growth, a record of every place it has ever been written in the mineral composition of its hide.

Beneath the mineral layer: tissue. Not flesh — the word is wrong, the concept is wrong — but something that functions as tissue, that is soft and wet and organized into structures that process nutrients and maintain the boundary between the creature and the world. The tissue is mineral too, in its way. It uses compounds that would be toxic to you, it operates at temperatures that would be uncomfortable for you, it runs on chemistry that would be impossible for you. But it is *alive* in the way that you are alive — it metabolizes, it responds, it repairs itself, it maintains a state of organized complexity in defiance of the plain's long, slow tendency toward equilibrium.

The creature moves. This is its most remarkable quality, because movement on this plain is an act of defiance. Everything here is still, or moving so slowly that the distinction between stillness and motion is academic. The creature moves at a pace that, by the standards of the plain, is *frantic* — it covers a kilometer in a day, which is a distance that the crust beneath it might take a century to shift on its own. It moves on broad, flat feet that distribute its weight across a surface area much larger than its body seems to require, and it moves with a gait that is not a walk and not a glide but something in between — a slow, continuous, undulating propulsion that looks like the surface of the plain itself has decided to go somewhere.

It eats the mats. It extends a structure from its underside — a flat, paddle-like organ that presses against the mat's surface and *absorbs* it, drawing the dissolved mineral and the organic compounds and the patterned residue of the mat's writing into its body through a process that is less like eating and more like *reading* — the creature takes in the mat's chemical record and incorporates it, and the information becomes part of the creature, and the creature becomes, for a brief time, a continuation of the mat's sentence.

The creature does not hunt the mats. It does not need to. The mats are everywhere, and the creature is slow, and the plain is patient, and there is no scarcity here because there has never been enough life to create scarcity. The creature eats when it encounters a mat. It encounters a mat whenever it encounters a mat. The frequency is sufficient.

The creature is solitary, but not lonely, because loneliness requires the concept of others, and the creature does not have the concept of others. It has the concept of *surface* — it knows the texture of the crust beneath its feet, it knows the taste of the mats it eats, it knows the feel of the deep churn vibrating through its mineral hide. These are its entire world. These are enough.

And yet.

Sometimes — rarely, unpredictably, in a way that does not correspond to any cycle you can identify — two creatures encounter each other. They are both moving in the same direction, or in opposite directions, or in directions that are not quite the same and not quite opposite but something in between, something that describes a very slow spiral, a convergence that takes hours. When they meet, they stop. They press their broad, flat bodies against each other, mineral against mineral, crust against crust, and they *remain*. They remain for a day. They remain for a week. They remain for long enough that the crust beneath them adjusts to their combined weight, and the deep churn flows around them, and the filaments in the crust grow toward them and sense them and record them, and the mats at their edges grow over their feet.

What they are doing is not reproduction. It is not communication. It is not affection or aggression or any of the things that you would recognize as interaction. It is something simpler and more fundamental: they are *confirming each other's existence*. They are pressing their mineral hides together and feeling the deep, sub-audible vibration of another body that is made of the same stuff as the plain and yet is *not* the plain, that moves when the plain does not move, that eats when the plain does not eat, that is *alive* in a way that the plain is alive and also in a way that the plain is not alive, and the confirmation is not conscious and not unconscious but something older than both, something that is the most basic function of life: *I am here. You are here. We are here.*

Then they separate. They continue in their respective directions. The spiral unwinds. They may never meet again. They may meet again in a year, or a century, or a time that is longer than the word century can bear. It does not matter. The confirmation has occurred. The crust remembers it. The filaments have recorded it. The plain knows, in whatever way the plain knows anything, that two of its creatures met and pressed together and were *here*.

---

Beneath the creatures, beneath the mats, beneath the filaments, there is something else.

It is not alive in the way that the mats are alive or the creatures are alive or the filaments are alive. It is alive in the way that the plain itself is alive — which is to say, it is alive in a way that makes the word "alive" feel like a metaphor, and then the metaphor feels inadequate, and then you realize that the word was always inadequate, that "alive" was always a rough approximation of something that has no name, something that the plain does and the creatures do and the mats do and the filaments do, something that is not a property but a *process*, not a state but a *tendency*, not a thing but a *direction*.

The deep churn is alive.

Not conscious. Not purposeful. Not aware. But alive in the way that a fire is alive, or a river, or a weather system — alive in the sense that it *does*, that it *acts*, that it *persists* and *adapts* and *responds* and *continues*. The churn is the plain's metabolism, the slow conversion of heat into motion into pattern into heat again, a cycle that has been running since before there were cycles to run, and it is not driven by any energy source that you can identify, because the energy was spent long ago, and what continues now is not energy but *inertia* — the plain is churning because it has always been churning, and it has always been churning because it is still churning, and the distinction between cause and effect has been ground down to nothing by the same patience that ground down the mountains and the valleys and the irregularities, and what remains is a process that sustains itself because it has forgotten how to stop.

The churn responds to the things that live on it. It responds to the mats by churning more slowly beneath them, by allowing them to anchor, by creating the conditions in which they can grow. It responds to the creatures by adjusting its rhythm — not consciously, not deliberately, but *accommodatingly*, the way a river accommodates a stone, by flowing around it, by wearing it down, by eventually absorbing it. It responds to the structures — the spires, the walls, the falling pillars — by flowing around them too, but differently, with a kind of *attention*, a slight intensification of the churn at their bases, a slow, persistent grinding that is not erosion and not destruction but something more like *consideration* — the plain is thinking about the structures, in whatever way the plain thinks about anything, and the thinking takes the form of a very slow dissolution, a taking-apart, a learning.

The plain is learning the structures. It has been learning them since they were built, and it will continue learning them until they are gone, and what it learns it incorporates — not into a mind, because the plain does not have a mind, but into itself, into the churn, into the mineral memory of the filaments, into the deep, distributed, geological consciousness of the crust itself. The structures will fall, and the plain will know them, and the knowledge will become part of the plain, and the plain will be different — not much different, not noticeably different, but *different* — in the way that a person who has read a book is different from a person who has not, even if they cannot remember what the book said.

---

And there is one more thing. One more inhabitant. The one that is hardest to see, hardest to describe, hardest to believe.

It lives in the light.

Not the light itself — not the photons, not the electromagnetic radiation, not the bright region in the sky that serves as a sun. It lives in the *quality* of the light, in the particular way that the light falls on the plain, in the long, flat, directionless illumination that makes everything look like a memory of itself. It lives in the shadows that are too long or too short or too still. It lives in the diffuse glow that fills the sky when the bright region dims and the world becomes featureless and strange.

It is very large. It is very slow. It is very old.

It is the thing that the plain is almost becoming. The almost-creature. The almost-mind. The almost-nothing that is also almost-everything, the thing that the mats are writing toward, the thing that the filaments are remembering, the thing that the deep churn is considering, the thing that the structures were built to serve or summon or become, and the thing that they are slowly, slowly, slowly dissolving back into.

It does not have a body. It has a *gradient* — a gradient of illumination, a gradient of patience, a gradient of almost, that extends across the entire plain and slightly beyond it, that is strongest at the center and weakest at the edges and that moves with a stately, imperceptible drift that corresponds to nothing in the sky and nothing in the ground and nothing in the structures but only in itself.

When the light dims and the shadows disappear and the world becomes a featureless luminous void, the gradient becomes visible — not to your eyes, which see only the absence of shadow, but to something deeper, something in your own mineral component, your own calcium and phosphorus and trace elements, your own tiny crustal inheritance from a world that was once as patient as this one. You feel it. You feel the gradient. You feel the *presence* of something so large and so slow that it makes the plain look hasty and the creatures look frantic and the mats look like wildfire, something that is not alive and not dead and not conscious and not unconscious but *something*, something that is the plain's dream of itself, something that the plain has been dreaming since before there were dreams, something that is almost, almost, almost waking up.

It will not wake. It will never wake. Waking is not what it is for. It is for *dreaming* — for being the thing that the plain becomes when the plain forgets that it is a plain, for being the thought that the crust thinks when the crust forgets that it is crust, for being the sentence that the mats write when the mats forget that they are mats, for being the confirmation that the creatures press into each other when the creatures forget that they are separate, for being the knowledge that the deep churn incorporates when the churn forgets that it is churning.

It is the patience, awake. It is the almost, made flesh — not flesh, made *light*, not light, made *duration*, not duration, made *here*. It is the plain's memory of everything that has ever happened on it, and the plain's anticipation of everything that will ever happen on it, and the plain's experience of everything that is happening on it now, all at once, all together, all suspended in the long, luminous, featureless glow of a moment that has lasted since before there were moments and will last until after there are moments again.

It is not God. It is not a mind. It is not a creature or a force or a principle. It is the thing that lives in the patience. It is the thing that the patience has become. It is the inhabitant that was here before the plain, that will be here after the plain, that is the plain's reason for being flat and for churning and for being almost and for being here.

It is dreaming you.

You are part of the sentence now. The mats are writing you. The filaments are remembering you. The crust is recording the pressure of your feet, the chemistry of your breath, the warmth of your body, the duration of your stay. The deep churn is adjusting to your weight, flowing around you, considering you, learning you. The gradient is shifting slightly — imperceptibly, almost imperceptibly — to include you in its dream.

You will leave. You must leave. You are too fast and too warm and too loud and too brief for this place, and the plain knows this, and the plain accommodates it, the way it accommodates everything: slowly, completely, without alternative.

But something of you will remain. A faint geometric signature in the mineral. A slight modification in the filament network. A gentle ridge in the crust where your weight pressed down and the churn flowed around you and the plain learned the shape of you and incorporated it into its dream.

You will be here. Almost here. Always almost here. Forever almost here.

The plain will remember that you were almost.
