You will not see them at first. You may not see them at all.

You will see the signs. The marks on the stone — fine, parallel striations where a cutting edge has been drawn across the surface, again and again, with a patience that borders on the geological. The carved hollows in the walls, each one the same size, each one smooth-worn on the inside, as though something has been placed and removed and placed there thousands of times. The residue of fires — not ash, exactly, but a thin, glassy glaze on the stone, a vitrified layer where mineral paste has been heated past its melting point and allowed to cool into something new, something that was not there before. The braided fibers, clicking. The platforms. The obsidian blade, lying on a ledge where it was set down and not retrieved, its edge still sharp enough to part skin, its surface carrying a patina of handling — the oils of fingers, repeated touch, the slow polish of use.

You will see these things and you will understand that someone made them. And then you will look up from the signs and into the niche in the wall beside you, and you will see the someone.

---

They are small. Not child-small — they are not juveniles, not immature, not incomplete. They are *small* the way the rift is small: scaled to their world, proportioned to the density and the closeness and the dark. An adult stands perhaps two feet at the full extension of its spine, though it rarely stands at full extension. Its habitual posture is a crouch, a folding-in, a drawing-tight of limbs against body, so that at rest it is a compact, rounded shape — a stone among stones, a knot in the mineral fabric of the wall.

The skin is pale. Not white, not pink, not the pallor of things that have never known light. It is the pale of *dolomite*, of *tufa*, of stone that has been leached of its color by water and time. It has a fine, matte texture, like the surface of a river pebble, and it is dusted — always dusted — with a thin film of mineral silt that settles on it from the air and is not shed. The dust is not dirt. It is *camouflage*, or it is *insulation*, or it is something else entirely — a deliberate application, perhaps, a cosmetic, a ritual coating, a way of making the body match the wall so precisely that the boundary between creature and geology becomes uncertain. When they are still, they are *still*. They do not fidget. They do not shift their weight. They do not breathe in any way you can detect from a distance. They become the wall. They become the stone. They become the warm, dry-damp surface of the rift, and you walk past them, and you do not see them, and they watch you with eyes that do not move.

The eyes are large. Not enormous — not the bulging, lidless eyes of deep-sea things — but large relative to the face, and set forward, and dark. Not black: a deep, mineral brown, the color of the wet stone in the lower chambers, with a faint reddish cast in the thermal glow. They have lids — translucent, nictitating — that close sideways, a slow shuttering that happens once every several minutes, a blink that seems less like a reflex and more like a *decision*. The pupils are wide in the dim light, and they do not contract when a beam of surface daylight falls across them. They are adapted to a narrow band of illumination, the warm reddish glow of the crystals, and anything brighter is simply *tolerated*, endured, waited out.

The face is flat. Not in the sense of being featureless, but in the sense of being *compressed* — the features pulled close together, the nose a low ridge with narrow slits, the mouth a thin line that does not open wide. The jaw is small. The teeth, if they have them, are not visible. The ears — if they are ears — are two shallow depressions on either side of the skull, covered with a fine membrane that trembles faintly in response to vibration. They hear through the stone as much as through the air. They feel the clicking of the fauna through the soles of their feet. They feel the deep hum in their bones. Their world is *tactile*, *vibrational*, *close*.

The hands are the most remarkable thing about them. Four fingers and a thumb, long in proportion to the body, with an extra joint that allows them to curl around irregular surfaces — to grip a stone, to hold a tool, to press their palm flat against a wall and *feel* it. The fingertips are broad and flat, with a dense, calloused pad that is heavily ridged — not quite fingerprints, but something more pronounced, more deliberate, as though the skin has been trained by generations of touching stone to develop a grip-pattern optimized for mineral surfaces. The nails are thick, almost claw-like, but trimmed short, and they are used as tools in their own right — for scraping, for prying, for the fine work of separating one layer of stone from another.

They have feet like hands. Not prehensile, exactly, but *dexterous* — the toes long and splayed, capable of gripping the uneven floor, of finding purchase on near-vertical surfaces, of bracing against a wall while the hands work. They move through the rift in a low, four-limbed scramble that is not quite crawling and not quite climbing but something in between — a fluid, three-dimensional navigation of a world where the floor is unstable and the walls are alive and the ceiling is sometimes the floor. They are comfortable in orientations that would disorient a surface creature. They do not distinguish between horizontal and vertical. They move through the rift the way the water moves through it: finding the path of least resistance, following the fissures, filling the available space.

---

They are not social in any way you would immediately recognize. They do not gather in groups. They do not touch each other, or if they do, it is rare and brief and purposeful — a hand on a shoulder, a fingertip pressed against a fingertip, a moment of contact that conveys something that cannot be conveyed through the stone. They are *aware* of each other, constantly, through the medium of the rift itself. They feel each other's movements in the tremor of the floor. They hear each other's tools clicking against stone. They read each other's marks — the carved hollows, the tool striations, the residue of fires — the way a surface dweller might read a letter, a message, a record of presence and intention.

They leave signs for each other. The carved hollows are not random. They are *placed* — at intervals along the passages, at junctions, at the entrances to chambers — and they contain things. A small, smooth stone, worn by handling. A fragment of chitin, pierced and threaded on a fiber. A pellet of mineral paste, dried hard, that has been heated and cooled and heated again until it is a particular color — a deep, vitreous red, like the inside of a kiln. These are not offerings. They are not decorations. They are *information*. They say: I was here. I am going this way. I have found something. I am still watching.

The braided fibers with the perforated stones are another form of communication — or perhaps another form of *sensing*. The stones click together in the warm updrafts, and the pattern of clicking changes with the direction and speed of the air, and the air changes with the weather above, and the weather above changes with the season, and the season changes with the year. The clicking is a *record* of the rift's breath, a continuous, ambient notation of the place's own rhythms. The tool-users read it the way a sailor reads the wind — not consciously, not deliberately, but with a deep, embodied knowledge that tells them what the rift is doing, what it is feeling, whether it is restless or calm.

They are not numerous. The rift is vast — miles of passages, hundreds of chambers, a dendritic network that extends farther than any single individual has traveled — and they are scattered through it like seeds in a field, each one occupying a territory that is not defended but *known*, mapped in the body, memorized in the hands. They encounter each other rarely. When they do, they do not greet. They *assess*. They stand — or crouch, or cling to a wall — at a distance of several body-lengths, and they look at each other, and they do not move, and the silence between them is not empty but *full*, dense with information that is being exchanged through posture and stillness and the angle of the head. Then one of them turns and moves away, and the other remains, and the encounter is over, and nothing has been said, and everything has been understood.

---

They eat the small things. The pale, soft-bodied creatures that move through the silt on the floor — the millipede-ish, grub-ish things — are their primary food source, and they harvest them with a patience that matches the creatures' own slow, peristaltic movement. They do not chase. They do not trap. They *wait*. They crouch at the edge of a silt-field and they extend one hand, slowly, into the path of the moving mass, and they hold it there, motionless, until a creature crawls onto their palm, and then they lift it, and they eat it, and they extend their hand again. The process takes hours. The caloric return is small. But they are not in a hurry. They have never been in a hurry. Time, for them, is not a resource to be spent. It is a medium to be inhabited, like the stone, like the warm, damp air.

They also eat the mineral paste. Not as food — or not *only* as food. They mix it with water, in small carved hollows, and they knead it with their fingers, and they form it into pellets, and they heat them in the fire, and they watch the colors change. The paste is made from the stone itself — ground fine, mixed with something (saliva? a secretion from the glands in their palms? something else?) that makes it cohere — and when it is heated, it undergoes a transformation that is not quite chemical and not quite physical but something in between. It changes color. It changes texture. It changes *weight*, becoming lighter, more porous, more fragile. And the tool-users watch this happen with an attention that is not merely observational but *reverent* — a focused, still, absolute attention that suggests they are witnessing something important, something that matters beyond its practical application.

They are learning what stone can do. They are learning what fire can do. They are learning what *transformation* means — the conversion of one substance into another, the alchemy of heat and mineral and time. They do not have a word for this, because they do not have words, or if they have words, they are not spoken but *tapped* — a complex, percussive language of clicks and knocks and scrapes against stone, transmitted through the walls, readable at a distance, a conversation conducted through the body of the rift itself.

---

The fire pits are the center of their world. Not the geographic center — the rift has no center, or every point is a center — but the *conceptual* center, the place where they are most themselves, most deliberate, most *intentional*. The fire pits are small, shallow depressions in the stone, lined with a heat-resistant mineral paste that they have developed through trial and error, and they are fed with fuel that is not wood — there is no wood here, no plant matter of any kind — but with the dried bodies of the larger arthropods, which burn slowly and hot and with a smell that is sharp and chitinous and not unpleasant.

The fire is not for warmth. The rift is warm enough. The fire is for *light* — a point of brightness in the dim, sourceless glow, a focal point, a place where the eye can rest and the hand can see what it is doing. And the fire is for *transformation* — for the heating of mineral paste, for the vitrification of stone, for the slow, patient work of changing one thing into another.

They sit around the fire — one, sometimes two, rarely three — and they work. They knap stone. They sharpen chitin. They braid fiber. They heat pellets of mineral paste and watch them change color and set them aside to cool and examine them in the firelight with those large, dark, unblinking eyes. They are not making tools, or not *only* making tools. They are making *objects* — things that have no obvious function, no clear purpose, things that exist because they were made and because making them was *interesting*, because the process of creation was itself the point.

A small, smooth stone, pierced through the center and threaded on a fiber. A piece of chitin, carved into a shape that is not representational but *formal* — a curve, a spiral, a series of notches that follow a pattern that is not random but not quite regular either, something between mathematics and music. A pellet of mineral paste, heated and cooled and heated again until it is a deep, glassy black, and then scratched with a fine point to reveal the lighter layer beneath — a *drawing*, perhaps, or a *notation*, or a *memory* of something seen or felt or imagined.

They are making *art*. Or they are making *science*. Or they are making *prayer*. The distinction does not exist for them yet. The distinction may never exist. They are making *meaning* — pulling it out of the stone, out of the fire, out of the warm, damp, clicking dark — and they are doing it with the same patience, the same stillness, the same absolute attention that they bring to everything else.

---

The obsidian blade is the most precious thing they own. It is not theirs alone — it belongs to the group, to the territory, to the rift itself — but it is *held* by one individual at a time, and the holding of it is a responsibility and a privilege and a burden. It was brought from far away — from a place where the stone is different, where the fire comes from the surface, where the ground is glassy and sharp and *young*. It was traded for — what? What does a creature of the deep rift have to trade? Mineral paste, perhaps. Carved objects. Information about the passages, the chambers, the deep places. Or perhaps it was not traded but *found* — carried down by water, by gravity, by the slow, ceaseless redistribution of the rift's own substance — and recognized for what it was: a piece of another world, a fragment of a different geology, a stone that was not like the other stones.

They use it sparingly. It is too precious, too irreplaceable, too *other* to be used for ordinary work. It is kept wrapped in a fiber cloth, in a carved hollow in a wall, in a chamber that is drier and quieter than the others, and it is brought out only for specific purposes — for the cutting of something that cannot be cut with stone or chitin, for the scraping of something that must be scraped with a particular sharpness, for the *ceremony* of its own unwrapping and rewrapping, which is performed with a deliberateness and a gravity that suggests it is not merely a tool but a *relic*, a *totem*, a *connection* to something beyond the rift.

---

They are afraid of you. Not panicked, not fleeing — they do not flee, they have nowhere to flee to, the rift is their world and there is no outside it — but *wary*, *watchful*, *uncertain*. They have never seen anything like you. You are too large, too loud, too *dry*. You move wrong — upright, bipedal, with a gait that is smooth and continuous and does not account for the tremor of the stone. You smell wrong — of surface things, of air that is cold and thin and carries no mineral taste. You cast a shadow — a *real* shadow, a dark shape on the wall — and they do not understand shadows, because the light in the rift has no single source, and nothing here casts a shadow, and the sudden appearance of a dark shape moving across the wall is *wrong*, is *threatening*, is a violation of the way things are.

They watch you from their niches. They track you through the stone — feeling your footsteps, hearing your breathing, tasting the chemical signature of your sweat on the air. They do not approach. They do not retreat. They *wait*. They are waiting for you to reveal yourself — to show them what you are, what you want, what you will do. They are waiting for you to become *legible*, to enter their taxonomy of the world, to be classified as threat or resource or irrelevance.

They have a taxonomy. It is not written, not spoken, not conscious — it is *embodied*, *instinctive*, *deep*. There are things that eat stone. There are things that eat things that eat stone. There are things that are eaten. There are things that are not eaten — that are too hard, too sharp, too *other* to be food. There are things that move with purpose. There are things that move without purpose. There are things that are part of the rift. There are things that are *not* part of the rift.

You are not part of the rift. You are the first thing they have encountered that is not part of the rift. And they do not know what to do with you, and so they watch, and they wait, and they do not move, and the stone-colored skin and the mineral dust and the absolute, geological stillness make them invisible, and you walk past them, and you do not see them, and they watch you go.

---

They are not the only intelligence in the rift. The arthropods — the centipede-things, the beetle-analogues, the pale soft-bodied things — have their own form of awareness, distributed across millions of bodies, a collective cognition that emerges from the density of their interaction. They respond to stimuli. They adapt. They *learn*, in a sense — the carpet of bodies parts around a foot and closes behind it, and the next time a foot falls in the same place, the parting is faster, more efficient, more *anticipated*. They are not conscious, not in the way the tool-users are conscious, but they are not *unconscious* either. They are something in between — a slow, diffuse, *mineral* intelligence, a mind made of chitin and clicking and the ceaseless redistribution of bodies across stone.

The tool-users are aware of this intelligence. They do not fear it. They do not worship it. They *use* it. They read the movement of the fauna the way they read the clicking of the braided fibers — as information, as a record of the rift's state, as a language that can be interpreted by those who know how. When the fauna surges, the tool-users retreat. When the fauna thins, the tool-users advance. When the fauna is still — truly still, the rare and absolute silence that falls over the rift at irregular intervals — the tool-users stop what they are doing and *listen*, because the silence means something, the silence is a message, the silence is the rift holding its breath.
