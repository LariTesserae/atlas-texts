# The Living Span

---

The first ones you will not see.

They are in the walls. They are *of* the walls. They are the pale, fibrous networks threading through concrete that has become, by now, more crack than mass — fungal mycelia so old and so dense that they constitute a second infrastructure, a root-system running through the Span's body the way nerves run through flesh. They are breaking down the old aggregate, dissolving the mineral binders, converting the worked stone back into something the earth can use. They have been doing this for longer than the oldest person in the Span has been alive. They will be doing it when the last person is gone. They do not know they are doing it. They simply reach, and dissolve, and grow, and the concrete yields.

In the deepest levels, where the air is warm and still and the light is the color of old amber, there are bioluminescent colonies — fungi that have colonized the surfaces of walls and columns and the undersides of decks, spreading in sheets and patches that glow with a faint, greenish-silver light. They are not decorative. They are metabolic. They feed on the moisture that condenses on the cooler surfaces, on the slow leaching of minerals from the old steel, on the organic dust that settles from above — skin cells and pollen and the dried bodies of insects and the fine, constant rain of biological matter that falls in any place where things live. Their light is a byproduct of this feeding. It has become, over the millennia, the primary illumination of the lower levels, the light by which the Span sees itself in the dark. The people who live down there have learned to read by it. They say it is enough. They say you stop missing the sun.

---

Above the fungi, the moss.

The moss is the Span's skin. It covers every surface that stays damp — every beam, every wall, every horizontal plane where water lingers long enough for a spore to find purchase. It is not one species but dozens, maybe hundreds, layering over each other in communities that have sorted themselves by moisture and light and the chemistry of the substrate beneath them. In the drier zones, near the open gashes where the wind moves, it grows in tight, dark cushions, almost black, hard as felt. In the wet zones — the drainage channels, the condensation trays, the slow seeps where water finds a crack and persists — it grows in long, pendulous beards that sway in the faint air currents and drip with a fine, constant mist. It is the first living thing most visitors notice, because it is everywhere, because it gives the Span its color — that deep, saturated, ancient green that is the color of a place where water and warmth and time have been left alone together long enough to make something lush.

The moss feeds the soil. The soil feeds the ferns. The ferns — great, arching, prehistoric things, their fronds spreading six and eight feet in the humid light — grow from every surface that has accumulated enough broken-down substrate to hold their roots. They grow from the walls. They grow from the ceilings, reaching downward, reaching toward the light that filters through from above. They grow from the old machinery — from the tops of dormant turbines, from the housings of long-silent compressors, from the beds of tanks that once held something industrial and now hold nothing but rainwater and leaf litter and the slow, patient accumulation of years. Their roots probe deeper than the moss, finding the cracks the moss has opened, widening them, filling them with organic matter, preparing the way for what comes next.

---

What comes next is the trees.

The trees are the Span's bones — or its skeleton is the trees, or the distinction has ceased to matter. They grow from the junction points, the structural nodes where beams meet columns meet decks, where the geometry of the Span creates pockets that catch debris and hold moisture and, over time, build up a depth of organic soil that can support something ambitious. The first trees were probably seeds carried in by birds, or blown in through the open gashes, or tracked in on someone's boot — a single seed in a single crack, germinating in a teaspoon of dust, sending a root into a hairline fracture in the concrete. That root found water. That root found rebar. That root grew.

Now there are forests. Not metaphorical forests — actual forests, with canopies and understories and the layered light of a place where the sun is filtered through forty feet of leaves and branches and the interlocking geometry of the Span's upper structures. The trees are not the species you would find in a forest outside, if there were an outside, if there were a forest outside — they are the species that could survive here, that could tolerate the shallow soil and the strange chemistry and the constant, gentle sway of a structure that moves. They are hardy. They are tenacious. They are, many of them, the same species that colonize cliffs and rock faces and the edges of quarries — the species that have always known how to grow from stone.

Their roots are visible. This is the thing that strikes you. The roots run along the surface of the concrete, following the lines of the structure, wrapping around beams and columns, entering cracks, bridging gaps between platforms. In places, the roots have grown so thick and so dense that they have become structural themselves — a living reinforcement, a wooden exoskeleton gripping the old steel and concrete, holding it together. When a column fails, the roots of the surrounding trees take up the load. When a platform sags, the roots brace it. The trees are not just growing in the Span. The trees are *holding the Span up*.

And the trees make light. This is the other thing. Where a tree grows tall enough to reach the enclosed levels — the places where the Span's own structure has roofed over, where the sky is a memory and the light is dim — the tree pushes upward, and its crown presses against the deck above, and the deck yields, or the tree finds a gap, or the tree makes a gap, and suddenly there is a hole, and through the hole there is sky, and through the sky there is sun, and the sun falls in a bright, warm column onto the level below, and in that column things grow. The trees are the Span's way of reaching for the light. The trees are the Span's way of making new rooms.

---

The bees found the trees. Or the trees found the bees. Or they found each other, the way things find each other in a place that has been left alone long enough — by proximity, by necessity, by the slow, blind, patient logic of coevolution.

The bees are not wild, exactly, and they are not domesticated. They are *commensal* — they live alongside the people of the Span the way they might live alongside any large, warm, stationary organism that creates niches and does not actively destroy them. Their hives are everywhere: in the hollow cores of structural columns, where the concrete has been eaten away by water and time and the space inside is dry and warm and defensible; in the abandoned control rooms of the old factories, where the walls are thick and the temperature is constant and the only opening is a conduit hole that the bees have reduced to exactly the diameter they prefer; in the spaces behind panels, in the gaps between decks, in the cavities of walls that have been hollowed out by roots and fungi and the slow dissolution of the old materials.

They forage on the flowers that grow in the condensation zones — the small, pale, hardy things that bloom in the spray of leaky pipes and the mist of drainage channels and the damp, warm air of the lower levels where the machinery runs and the temperature never drops. These flowers are not showy. They are not fragrant, or not in any way a human nose would notice. But they produce nectar, and they produce pollen, and they bloom year-round in the steady warmth of the Span, and the bees have built their entire economy around them.

The sound of the bees is the sound of the Span's metabolism. It is the sound of energy being converted, of sugar being made, of wax being built and comb being filled and larvae being fed. It is a low, dense, vibrating hum that you feel in your chest before you hear it with your ears, and it is everywhere — not loud, but omnipresent, a frequency that underlies everything the way the hum of the electricity underlies everything, the way the sound of the water underlies everything. The bees are the Span's heartbeat. Or one of them.

---

The birds are the Span's voice.

They nest in the upper structures — in the open frameworks of the old communication masts, in the rusted lattice of the cranes, in the gaps between the upper decks where the light is bright and the wind moves and the insects gather in clouds around the standing water. They are cliff-dwellers, mostly — species that have adapted to the vertical complexity of the Span the way their ancestors adapted to canyon walls and sea cliffs and the faces of quarries. They nest on ledges. They nest in cavities. They nest in the crooks of branches and the angles of beams and the hollow tops of columns where the rainwater collects and the wind does not reach.

Their calls are the sound you hear first, before the water, before the machinery, before the bees — a constant, echoing, overlapping chorus of song and alarm and contact calls that bounces between the platforms and reverberates through the open gashes and filters down through the layers of the Span like rain. The birds call to each other across distances that are not horizontal but vertical — from a nest on the forty-foot level to a perch on the sixty-foot level, from a roost in the canopy to a feeding ground on the drainage channel below. Their world is three-dimensional in a way that the world of ground-nesting birds is not. They move through the Span the way fish move through a reef — in all directions, using the full volume of the space, finding food and shelter and mates in the interstices of the structure.

They eat the insects. The insects are everywhere — clouds of them around the standing water, swarms of them in the warm air of the lower levels, a constant, humming, crawling, flying biomass that is the Span's primary converter of plant matter into animal matter. The insects feed on the moss, on the fungi, on the decaying leaves, on the flowers, on the wood of the old structures, on the organic detritus that accumulates in every corner and crevice. The birds feed on the insects. The small mammals in the walls feed on the insects. The lizards — the quick, iridescent, scaled things that sun themselves on warm steel plates and vanish into cracks when you approach — feed on the insects. The insects are the Span's blood. They carry energy from one part of the system to another. They are the medium through which the sun's energy, captured by the plants, becomes available to everything else.

---

The lizards are the Span's quickness.

They are on every warm surface — every steel plate that catches the sun, every concrete wall that has been absorbing heat all day and is now radiating it back into the cooling air, every pipe that carries warm water from the deep machinery to the upper levels. They are small, most of them — four, five, six inches from nose to tail-tip — but they are fast, startlingly fast, a flash of iridescent scale and then nothing, gone into a crack so thin you wouldn't think a leaf could fit, let alone a living thing. They are the Span's reflexes, its startle response, the thing that moves when everything else is still.

There are more of them than there are people. There are more of them than there are birds. They are the most visible vertebrate in the Span, the one you see most often, the one that watches you from a warm beam as you pass, its throat pulsing, its eyes tracking you with the flat, patient attention of a creature that has been watching things pass for a very long time and has learned that most of them are not dangerous and some of them are and the difference is not always obvious.

They eat the insects. They are eaten by the birds. They are eaten by the small mammals in the walls, the ones you hear but rarely see — the soft, scurrying, rustling presence that moves through the infrastructure at the edges of day, in the dim hours when the light is neither bright nor dark and the Span itself seems to be holding its breath. These mammals are small — rat-sized, mouse-sized, some of them, though they are not rats and not mice, they are something else, something that has been living in the walls of the Span long enough to become its own thing, shaped by the particular pressures of this particular place. They eat the insects. They eat the fungi. They eat the seeds of the ferns and the mosses. They eat the scraps of the people's food, when they can get them. They are eaten by — well. There are things in the lower levels. The people do not talk about them much. They are larger. They are quiet. They move in the dark, in the warm, in the places where the machinery hums and the air tastes of oil and ozone and the bioluminescent fungi cast their faint, greenish light on the walls. The people leave offerings for them, sometimes. Food, left in certain places, at certain times. It is not worship. It is not fear, exactly. It is *accommodation*. It is the recognition that the Span has its own inhabitants, its own priorities, its own food chain, and that the people are part of it, not above it.

---

The people.

The people are the Span's consciousness — or the closest thing to it. Not because they are more important than the bees or the trees or the fungi, but because they are the ones who *notice*. They are the ones who look at the Span and see it, who understand (partially, imperfectly, in the partial and imperfect way that any organism understands the system it inhabits) how it works, and who act on that understanding.

They live in the interstices. They live in the old rooms and corridors and platforms that the Span provides — the factory floors that have been partitioned into living spaces with walls of salvaged sheet metal and old plastic sheeting and, in the wealthier districts, actual lumber cut from the Span's own trees. They live in the residential blocks that may or may not have been residential blocks originally — it is hard to tell, and the answer may be different in each block, and it may not matter, because the Span does not distinguish between built-for-living and built-for-something-else. A room is a room. A space with walls and a roof and a door is a space where a person can sleep and cook and keep their things and be warm and dry, and the Span provides these spaces in abundance, in every configuration, at every level, in every degree of repair.

They farm in the light zones. This is the most important thing they do, the thing that makes them not just inhabitants but *participants* — they tend the soil. The soil that is rust and concrete dust and leaf litter and time. The soil that the fungi and the moss and the ferns and the trees have been making for centuries, breaking down the built world into something the living world can use. The people deepen this soil. They add to it — composted food waste, mulch, the droppings of their animals (they keep birds, mostly, for eggs and meat; they keep a few mammals, small ones, warm-blooded things that live in cages and eat scraps and are eaten in turn). They plant in it. They grow food — not the food of the outside world, if there is an outside world, but food that has adapted to the conditions of the Span: the filtered light, the constant warmth, the mineral-rich water, the strange chemistry of a soil that is half concrete and half compost. The food is tough and dense and nutritious and tastes of the Span — of iron and green things and the faint, sweet, fungal undertone that everything grown here carries.

They keep bees. This is the second most important thing they do. The bees are not domesticated — the people would be the first to tell you this — but they are *managed*. The people know where the hives are. They know which hives are healthy and which are struggling. They know how to harvest honey without destroying the colony, how to leave enough for the winter (there is no winter, but the bees do not know this, and the people respect the bees' instincts), how to capture a swarm and guide it to a new hive in a location that is convenient for everyone. The honey is the Span's sweetness — dark, complex, flavored with the nectar of flowers that grow nowhere else, that exist only in the condensation zones and the drainage channels and the warm, damp air of the lower levels. The people eat the honey. They trade the honey. They use the wax for light, for waterproofing, for the seals on their stills and the finishes on their tools. The bees are the Span's gift to the people, and the people know it, and they are grateful in the way that you are grateful for something you did not make and cannot control and would be foolish to take for granted.

They maintain the machines. Not all of them — not the deep machines, the ones that run in the lower levels, the ones that have been running so long that no one knows what they do or what would happen if they stopped. Those machines are left alone. They are the Span's autonomic functions — its heartbeat, its breathing, its digestion. The people do not touch them. The people maintain the machines that interface with their own lives: the pumps that move water from the lower levels to the upper, the generators that produce electricity (sometimes, in some districts, when the fuel holds out and the parts do not break), the water treatment systems that keep the dampness from becoming a flood, the simple mechanical systems — the levers, the valves, the gates — that control the flow of water through the drainage channels and the distribution of warm air through the ventilation shafts.

They maintain these machines with tools made from the materials the Span provides. Steel, cut from the old structures with torches and saws and the patient, grinding work of hand tools. Copper, stripped from the old wiring, melted and drawn and hammered into new wire, new pipe, new fittings. Plastic — the old, old plastic, the kind that has been here since the beginning, that is everywhere, that can be cut and shaped and heat-formed into almost anything. The people are not primitive. They are not advanced. They are *industrial* — they work with metal and fire and the principles of mechanics and thermodynamics that their ancestors learned in the factories that the Span was built to house, and they have passed these principles down, generation to generation, not as theory but as practice, as the things you know how to do because your parents showed you and their parents showed them and the knowledge is in the hands, not the head.

They have electricity, sometimes. They have running water, usually. They have light — from the sun, from the lamps, from the bioluminescence, from the fires they burn in the lower levels where the air is warm and still and the smoke rises through a thousand cracks and channels to the open air above. They have warmth. They have food. They have shelter. They have each other. They have the Span.

They do not question the Span. The Span is the world. It is the ground beneath their feet and the roof above their heads and the walls around them and the air they breathe and the water they drink and the food they eat and the light by which they see. It is the source of everything they have and the destination of everything they make and the context in which their lives have meaning. They are not separate from it. They are *of* it — the way the bees are of it, the way the trees are of it, the way the fungi are of it. They are the part of the Span that knows it is the Span. They are the part that looks around and says: *this is where we live. This is what we have. This is enough.*

---

And the Span.

The Span lives.

Not the way a person lives — not with intention, not with consciousness, not with the bright, anxious, self-aware spark that the people carry in their heads. The Span lives the way a reef lives, the way a forest lives, the way any system lives that has achieved a complexity sufficient to become self-regulating, self-repairing, self-perpetuating. It takes in energy — from the sun, from the deep machinery, from the composting action of its own biomass. It processes matter — breaking down the old, building up the new, converting the worked materials of its construction back into the raw materials of life and then, slowly, patiently, back into the worked materials of new construction (because the people build, and the trees build, and the coral-like accretion of mineral deposits in the water channels builds, and the Span is always building itself, always remaking itself, always becoming). It maintains its internal environment — the warmth, the moisture, the chemistry, the light — within the narrow range that its inhabitants require. It responds to disturbance — a column fails, a platform sags, a drainage channel clogs — not with a plan but with a thousand small, local adjustments that add up to resilience, that add up to continuity, that add up to *persistence*.

The Span breathes. You can feel it if you stand still long enough — the slow, warm, damp exhalation of air rising from the lower levels through the cracks and channels and open gashes, carrying with it the smell of the deep: oil and ozone and the sweet, earthy, fungal richness of a place where things are always growing and always decaying and the two processes are so thoroughly intertwined that you cannot tell them apart. And the inhalation: the cool air drawn in from above, from the open sky, carrying with it the spores and the seeds and the insects and the faint, salt-less scent of the outside world, if there is an outside world, if there is an outside. The Span breathes, and the breath of the Span is the wind that moves through its corridors and across its platforms and through the canopy of its forests, and the people breathe it, and the bees breathe it, and the birds breathe it, and the trees breathe it, and everything that lives in the Span is breathing the same air, the Span's air, the air that the Span has made from its own substance and offers to its inhabitants and takes back and makes again, endlessly, the way a body makes its blood.

The Span is alive. Not metaphorically. Not poetically. Actually, literally, functionally alive — a living system, a living structure, a living place, the largest living thing in the world, if there is a world beyond it, if there is a beyond. It is the thing that lives here. It is the thing that everything else lives *in*. It is the inhabitant and the inhabited, the body and the dweller, the place and the thing that places itself. It has been here longer than the people. It will be here after the people are gone. It was here before the people came — not as a ruin, not as an abandoned structure, but as a place that was already becoming itself, already growing, already breathing, already alive in the slow, patient, geological way that a place becomes alive when it is left alone long enough to learn how.

The Span endures. The Span grows. The Span lives.

And everything that lives in the Span — the fungi and the moss and the ferns and the trees and the insects and the bees and the birds and the lizards and the small mammals in the walls and the large things in the dark and the people with their tools and their fires and their honey and their children and their stories and their quiet, unshakeable knowledge that this is the world, this is the only world, this is enough — everything that lives in the Span is alive because the Span is alive, and the Span is alive because they are alive, and the distinction between the living and the lived-in has dissolved, has been dissolved by time and water and warmth and the patient, relentless, unstoppable chemistry of life finding a way to persist in a place that was not built for it but has become, over the long, slow, magnificent arc of its existence, exactly and precisely and perfectly the place where it belongs.
