They are small.

Not in the sense of diminished — in the sense of compact, of efficient, of every element of their bodies having been considered by the plateau and found acceptable. They are perhaps a head shorter than you would expect, with broad frames that carry dense, heavy muscle in the shoulders and backs and legs — the muscle of climbers, of people who have never known a flat surface, who have spent their entire lives ascending and descending stone. Their torsos are deep, barrel-shaped, the ribcage large to house lungs that have adapted over uncountable generations to the thinness of the high air. They do not breathe the way lowlanders breathe. Their respiration is slow, deliberate, a measured exchange that extracts everything from each inhalation. When they exert themselves, they do not gasp. They simply breathe more deeply, more fully, their broad chests expanding to draw in what little the air offers.

Their skin is the color of the stone they live in — or rather, it is a palette of the stone. Pale granite, dark basalt, the rust-red of iron deposits, the grey-green of lichen. Not in any literal, pigmented sense, but in the way the light plays across their weathered faces, in the mineral quality of their complexions, as though the plateau's own geology has seeped into them over the millennia. Their skin is perpetually damp, like everything on the plateau, a fine sheen of condensed moisture that makes them glisten in the diffuse light. They do not dry. They have never dried. Their skin has adapted to a state of perpetual wetness — it is smooth, almost supple, resistant to the chapping and cracking that would plague a newcomer. In the coldest months, when the condensation freezes in the early hours, their skin takes on a faintly crystalline quality, as though frost has become a part of them.

Their hair is dark — black or deep brown — and worn long by both sexes, usually braided or bound close to the head in practical configurations that keep it from catching on stone. The hair absorbs the dampness and hangs heavy, gleaming like the wet rock faces. Some of them weave small stone beads or carved bone clips into their braids, the only adornment they commonly wear. The beads are smooth, water-worn, collected from the streams and polished further between the fingers during long hours of work. They click faintly when the person moves, a sound like distant, irregular rain — the same sound as hooves on stone.

Their hands are their most remarkable feature. They are extraordinary hands — broad, strong, with thick fingers that seem too blunt for the precision they achieve. The palms are heavily calloused, the skin thickened by a lifetime of handling stone, yet the fingers are sensitive, articulate, capable of detecting variations in texture and fit that an instrument might miss. The hands are always moving — tapping a wall, testing a surface, feeling the moisture on a stone face, gauging the temperature of a cistern. They read the world through their hands the way a scholar reads a book. The fingernails are thick, often chipped or broken, the cuticles perpetually rimmed with stone dust. The hands are never still, even in sleep — twitching, adjusting, as though continuing their work in dreams.

Their eyes are adapted to the plateau's strange light. They are larger than you would expect, with wide, dark pupils that expand in the fog and contract in the rare direct sun. The irises are pale — grey, or a muted amber, or a green so faint it is almost colorless — and they have a quality of luminosity, as though they gather what little light there is and hold it. The whites of their eyes are not white but a faint grey-blue, the color of the sky on the plateau. They do not squint. They do not need to. Their eyes are accustomed to seeing in the diffuse, directionless glow, to reading the landscape by subtle variations in tone and texture rather than by shadow and highlight. They can detect the faint sheen of moisture on a rock face from across a valley. They can read the weather in the color of the fog. They can see in the dimness of the cities' narrow passages with an ease that startles outsiders. But they are sensitive to harsh light — a direct sun, a bright fire, a sudden glare off snow — and in those moments they narrow their eyes and turn away, their pupils contracting to pinpoints.

Their faces are broad, with high cheekbones and strong jaws. The features are weathered, lined by cold and damp and the constant effort of breathing thin air. They age slowly — or rather, they age in a way that is hard to track, their faces accumulating character rather than decay. A person of sixty might look forty, or a person of forty might look sixty, depending on their life's work and the particular stone they have spent their years shaping. Their expressions are restrained, economical. A slight narrowing of the eyes, a fractional tightening of the lips, a barely perceptible shift of the brow — these are their vocabulary of emotion, calibrated to the plateau's quiet. When they smile, it is a small thing, a quick brightening of the eyes and a slight upturn of the mouth that passes like a cloud shadow over stone. When they grieve, they become still — utterly, completely still — their faces settling into a neutrality that is more eloquent than any display of sorrow.

They move slowly. This is the first thing you notice when you watch them in their cities — the deliberateness of their gait, the economy of their gestures, the way they place each foot with care on the worn stone steps. They do not hurry. There is no hurry on the plateau. The cities are steep, the passages narrow, the stairs endless, and haste is not only pointless but dangerous — a slip on the wet stone, a turned ankle in a gap between steps, a collision in a passage too narrow for two. They have learned to move at the plateau's pace, and the plateau's pace is slow. They climb with a steady, rhythmic cadence, their broad hands trailing along the damp walls for balance, their breath coming in deep, measured draws. They descend with the same care, their weight shifted back, their feet finding the worn grooves in the stone that mark the path of ten thousand feet before theirs.

Their clothing is layered, practical, made from the plateau's limited materials. The inner layer is a fine-woven wool from the herd animals — soft, dense, moisture-wicking, worn close to the skin. The outer layer is a heavier wool or felted fabric, often treated with animal fat to repel the worst of the damp, dyed in the muted colors of the plateau — charcoal, rust, moss-green, the deep brown of iron-rich water. They wear close-fitting leggings, sturdy boots with thick soles cut from hide and fitted with bone or copper nails for grip on the wet stone, and hooded cloaks that can be drawn close against the fog. The clothing is worn and repaired, patched and re-patched, passed down through generations until it is more patch than original fabric. They have a word for this — something like "gathering" or "accumulation" — the idea that a garment, like a city, is built by the addition of many small efforts over time.

They smell of the plateau. Stone-damp, woodsmoke, animal wool, the faint mineral tang of the water. In the close quarters of the cities, where the passages are narrow and the walls press in, you are aware of their smell — not unpleasant, but distinctive, a scent that is the smell of the place itself, as though the plateau has seeped into their pores and become a part of them. They do not wash frequently — water is too precious, too laboriously collected — but they are not unclean. The constant dampness keeps them rinsed, in a way, the condensation washing their skin and hair in a perpetual, gentle rain. They scrub themselves with fine sand and abrasive stones, a practice that leaves their skin smooth and polished, like the water-worn pebbles in the streams.

Their voices are quiet. This is not a cultural affectation — it is an acoustic necessity. The plateau's damp stone absorbs sound, the narrow passages channel it upward and away, the fog muffles it. Loud voices do not carry; they dissipate, swallowed by the stone. So they have learned to speak at a volume that is just sufficient — low, soft, often barely above a whisper. Their voices have a particular quality — a slight rasp, a breathiness, as though the thin air cannot support a full-throated sound. They speak slowly, with long pauses, as though each word must be carefully selected and placed, like a stone in a wall. Their language is consonant-heavy, full of stops and clicks and hard sounds that carry better in the damp air than vowels. It sounds like stone being worked — tap, tap, tap, a rasp, a scrape — and in fact, the language and the craft of stone-working share many of the same terms. To speak well is to fit words together tightly, precisely, with no gaps.

They are a people of routine. The plateau's rhythms — the daily fog, the seasonal herd migrations, the slow accumulation of water in the cisterns — have shaped their days into a pattern that has not changed in living memory. They rise before the fog lifts, when the condensation is at its peak and the dripping is loudest. They tend the fires, feed the animals, check the cisterns. They work stone — this is the central activity of their lives, the thing that fills the hours between waking and sleeping. They carve new blocks, repair old walls, clear the water channels, fit new stones into the growing city. They work slowly, with a precision that borders on the obsessive. Each stone is shaped, tested, shaped again, tested again, until it fits its place with a seamlessness that is almost organic. A single stone might take days to fit — or weeks, or months. There is no rush. The city has been growing for centuries. It will grow for centuries more.

They eat simply — a porridge of ground grain (traded from lower altitudes, or grown in the deepest valleys where a thin soil permits), dried meat from the herds, a pungent cheese made from the milk of the pack animals, the occasional bird egg gathered from the cliffs. They drink water, always — water is the plateau's true wealth, more precious than stone, and they consume it constantly, their bodies requiring the hydration to function in the thin, dry cold. They have a ritual around water — a small, quiet ceremony of gratitude when filling a cup from a cistern, a murmured acknowledgment of the stone that gives it, the fog that feeds it, the slow, patient work of water on rock that makes their lives possible. It is not a religion, exactly — they have no gods in the way lowlanders understand gods — but it is a recognition, a gratitude, a sense of being held by the plateau's processes.

Their families are small, tight-knit, multi-generational. The cities' dense construction means that extended families often share a cluster of rooms, connected by narrow passages and shared cisterns. The old are revered — their knowledge of stone, of water, of the plateau's subtle moods, is the community's most valuable resource. They sit by the fires in the evenings, their extraordinary hands resting on their knees, their luminous eyes half-closed, and they talk — quietly, slowly — about the stone they have worked, the walls they have built, the changes they have seen in the fog, in the herds, in the water's flow. The young listen. They have always listened. The old are the city's memory, and the city is the plateau's memory, and the memory is long.

Children are raised collectively, as much by the city as by their parents. They learn to walk on the stone, to climb the stairs, to navigate the passages before they can speak. They learn to read the fog, to hear the change in the dripping that means the cistern is full, to feel the subtle vibration in the stone that means a tremor is coming. They learn to work stone — first by playing with the chips and dust, fitting small pieces together, then by grinding abrasives, then by carving soft stone, then by fitting. By the time they are adolescents, they have the beginnings of the hands — the broad, strong, sensitive hands that are the plateau's signature. By the time they are adults, they are already part of the city's growth, adding their small, precise contribution to the endless, patient work of building.

They do not write. They have no need. The city is their text — every stone a word, every wall a sentence, every generation a chapter. They read the city's history in its layers, the dark, smooth base giving way to progressively fresher, sharper construction as they climb. They know who built which wall by the style of the fitting, the particular angle of the joints, the characteristic marks of individual stone-workers passed down through families. The city is their library, their archive, their scripture, and it is written in a language they have been learning since birth.

Their art is small, intimate, hidden in the city's crevices. A carved stone bead, worn smooth by generations of handling. A pattern of colored pebbles set into the floor of a cistern, visible only when the water is low. A whorl of scratches on the underside of a lintel, visible only to someone who crawls through the passage on their back. A piece of copper, cold-hammered into a thin sheet and etched with a scene — a herd moving across the stone, a predator on a ridge, the fog rolling through a valley. These things are not displayed. They are not meant to be seen by many. They are private, personal, small acts of creation in a world that is already full of the slow, patient artistry of water on stone and stone on stone and life on life.

They are not a people of spectacle. They have no festivals in the way lowlanders understand festivals — no crowds, no loud music, no raucous celebration. Their occasions are quiet, intimate, tied to the plateau's rhythms. The lifting of the fog on a particularly clear morning. The first flow of a new seep. The birth of a calf in the herd. The fitting of a particularly difficult stone. These are marked with small gestures — an extra cup of water shared, a quiet word of acknowledgment, a hand placed on the stone in thanks. Their greatest celebrations are indistinguishable from their daily life, because their daily life is already a kind of celebration — of endurance, of persistence, of the simple, profound act of continuing to exist on this vast, cold, damp, sharp-edged plateau that generates its own weather and rises, slowly, imperceptibly, into the thin, bright air.

They are patient. This is their defining quality, the thing that the plateau has bred into them over the centuries. They think in generations, in centuries, in the deep time of stone. A problem that cannot be solved in a lifetime is not a problem but a project — to be passed down, added to, gradually worn away by the persistent application of small, careful efforts. They do not despair. They do not rage. They do not demand quick solutions to slow problems. They place one stone, and then another, and then another, and they trust that the wall will rise. They clear one channel, and then another, and then another, and they trust that the water will flow. They fit one joint, and then another, and then another, and they trust that the city will hold.

They are the plateau's people, as much a part of it as the lichen on the stone, the fog in the valleys, the herds on the slopes. They are small, and quiet, and slow, and patient, and their hands are extraordinary, and their eyes see in the fog, and they speak in the language of stone, and they build for generations they will not see, and they collect water from channels that have been dripping for centuries, and they endure.
