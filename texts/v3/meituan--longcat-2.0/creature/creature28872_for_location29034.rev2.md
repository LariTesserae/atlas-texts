# The Riven Hearth — Inhabitants

---

They are called, in the clicking, tonal language they use against stone, something that translates poorly as *those-who-watch-the-warmth-come-up*. They have other names for themselves, layered and situational, names that change depending on what they are doing and where in the rift they are doing it. A person mining mineral paste from a lower chamber is not the same person as the one who hangs perforated stones on braided sinew. A person who tends the fire pit is not the same person as the one who waits, still and stone-colored, to see what a stranger will do.

But they are not numerous enough to have developed many words for stranger. Stranger is still a new category.

---

Their bodies are small — most of them between four and four-and-a-half feet when they stand, which they do not do often or for long. Their spines are slightly different from a surface person's: the vertebrae a little more flexible, the ribcage a little narrower, the pelvis tilted in a way that favors crouching and climbing over walking. They move best on three points of contact — two feet and a hand, or two hands and a knee — and they can hold a crouch for hours without discomfort, their weight distributed through their long toes and the broad pads of their fingers. They have a lot of toes. Four on each foot, widely splayed, with rough calloused pads that can grip wet stone. Their hands have an extra joint in the thumb, which gives them a pincer-like precision that their otherwise crude tools don't quite deserve.

Their skin is pale, but it is not the pale of a mushroom or a cave fish. It is pale the way silt is pale — mineral, fine-grained, with a faint sheen that comes from the dust of the place settling into their pores and staying there. They don't wash it off. It is camouflage and also identity, a way of carrying the rift on their bodies, of being recognized as belonging to this geology rather than some other. A person who has been away from the rift for too long begins to look wrong to the others — too clean, too pink, too *exposed* — and they will be dusted with silt from the floor until their skin reads correctly again.

Their eyes are large, dark, and they do not squint in the dim thermal glow. They have a faint reflective layer behind the retina, not quite a tapetum, not quite a crystal deposit — something in between, something that gives them a muted shine when light catches their gaze and lets them see in the low, sourceless illumination the rift provides. They do not see sharply. They see *movement*, they see *texture*, they see the slight difference between one warm surface and another. They are not built for detail. They are built for reading a space that has no shadows.

They have no hair. They have, instead, a fine ridge of keratinous bumps along the scalp and the spine, more pronounced in some individuals than others, which may serve some sensory function — detecting vibration, or air movement, or the proximity of another body — but which they have not yet thought to question. They are not a people who ask *why* about their own bodies. They are a people who ask *what* and *how* and *what happens if*.

---

Their relationship with the fauna is not predatory. It is not symbiotic, exactly. It is closer to *commensal*, but with a twist: they eat what the fauna discards. The centipede-things shed their exoskeletons as they grow, and these shed carapaces — light, hollow, still smelling faintly of the creature that grew them — are collected and sorted and used for tools, for building material, for the curved awnings over their sleeping hollows. The beetle-analogues produce a resinous secretion that they use to glue their carapaces together when they cluster on the walls; the tool-users harvest this secretion, warm and tacky, and use it as mortar. The pale grub-things that move through the mineral silt excrete a fine, hardened pellet — a kind of fecal brick — that accumulates in the lower chambers and is gathered and stacked and used to build the low walls and platforms that define their living spaces.

They do not kill the fauna. The fauna is too dense, too numerous, too woven into the mineral cycle that sustains them. A person who killed a centipede-thing would be like a person who killed a river. It is not forbidden; it is simply incomprehensible. The one exception is the obsidian blade, which was used once, long ago, to cut a centipede-thing in half — not to eat it, but to see what was inside, to understand its structure, to use the hollow femur as an awl. The blade is kept wrapped in sinew cord and brought out only for specific purposes: cutting, dividing, opening. It is the only thing they own that came from somewhere else, and they do not know where. It was found, or traded, or carried in by someone who is no longer here, and it is treated with a kind of reverence that is not quite religious — they have no religion, not yet — but is something closer to *curiosity about the outside*, the way a person might hold a seed and wonder what it would become if they planted it.

---

Their structures are not permanent. They cannot be. The rift is still forming, still shifting, still depositing silt and crumbling and resettling. A platform built today might be buried in a month, or undermined by a new fissure, or overwhelmed by a surge of fauna responding to a pressure change from above. The tool-users build in anticipation of impermanence. Their platforms are low and wide, easily abandoned or reoccupied. Their carved hollows — the fist-sized ones in the walls — are not storage, exactly. They are *questions*. Each hollow is a test: can stone hold this shape? What happens if you carve here rather than there? How deep can you go before the wall gives way? Some of the hollows are arranged in patterns that might be decorative but might also be functional — a map, a tally, a record of where the silt has risen and receded over time, a way of reading the rift's growth like the rings of a tree (if trees existed here, which they do not).

The braided fiber strung between mineral spurs, hanging with small perforated stones that click together in the warm air — this is the closest thing they have to music. The clicking is not rhythmic. It is *responsive*: it changes with the air, with the heat, with the movement of bodies through the passage. A person who listens can read the state of the rift by the clicking of the stones. The stones are selected carefully — each one has a slightly different pitch, a different quality of sound, depending on the mineral it was carved from and the shape of the perforation. Hanging them is a kind of composition. Taking them down is a kind of editing. They do this constantly, adjusting, rearranging, trying new stones, removing old ones. The sound of the rift is always changing, and the clicking of the stones is their attempt to *harmonize* with it — not to dominate it, not to quiet it, but to find a voice within it.

---

The fire pit is in a side chamber that is slightly drier than the rest, slightly cooler, where the steam does not rise in columns but beads and slides. The fire is small — the wood-analogue they burn is a dense, resinous fungus that grows in the deeper shafts, and it burns slow and hot and smells of sulfur and iron. The fire is not for warmth. The rift is warm enough. The fire is for *transformation*.

They bring mineral paste to the fire — mixtures of silt and water and resin and ground carapace, blended in the carved hollows with their wide, four-toed hands — and they heat it over the flames and watch it change color. The paste goes from gray to pink to a deep, wet orange, and then, if the mixture is right and the heat is steady, it hardens into something that is not stone and not ceramic but something in between — a material that is slightly flexible, slightly warm to the touch, that holds its shape and can be carved further once it cools. They use this material to make the few things that they need and cannot find ready-made: small, curved bowls for mixing paste. Stoppers for the carved hollows. A single, palm-sized disk with a perforation in the center, which is worn on a cord around the neck of the person who tends the fire — not as ornament, but as a mark of role, a way of saying *this is the person who watches the color change*.

The fire-tender is not a leader. They are a specialist. The role is rotated, though some people are better at it than others — better at reading the colors, at knowing when to add resin and when to add silt, at judging the heat of the fire by the sound it makes. The fire-tender's disk is passed from person to person, and when it is not being worn, it hangs from a hook of carved stone near the fire pit, clicking softly against the wall.

---

They are not a society, not yet. They are a *community* — a loose, shifting association of individuals who share the rift and its resources and its dangers. They recognize each other by scent and by the specific pattern of mineral dust on each other's skin. They communicate in a low, clicking language that they make with their tongues against the roofs of their mouths, and also in a tactile language — a touch on the shoulder, a pressure on the hand, a specific arrangement of fingers that means *follow me* or *wait* or *the silt is rising* or *something is moving in the wall*. They do not speak often. The rift is loud enough.

They gather in small groups — three or four, rarely more — in the drier side channels, where they sleep in the carved hollows in the walls, curled into positions that look uncomfortable but are not, their spines flexible enough to fold into a tight crouch with their foreheads resting on their knees and their hands tucked under their thighs for warmth (though it is never cold — the warmth is for comfort, for the feeling of being held). They do not dream the way surface people dream. Their sleep is shallow and alert, broken into short segments, and they wake easily and completely, with no grogginess, as though their minds are always waiting for the next change in the stone.

They eat sparingly — mineral paste, raw or fired; the soft inner tissue of the grub-things, which they extract by squeezing the bodies between their long fingers; a thin, nutritious film that forms on the surface of the silt when it has been undisturbed for a long time, which they scrape off with their fingernails and eat directly, standing, looking at the wall. They do not hunt. They do not farm. They *harvest*, and they move through the rift the way a person moves through a garden — knowing where things grow, knowing when they are ready, taking only what is needed and leaving the rest to regenerate.

---

The person watching you now is not the only one. There are three of them, in a niche about fifteen feet above the floor of the passage you are standing in. They are motionless. Their skin is dusted with silt. Their large, dark eyes are open and tracking your movement with a focus that is not hostile but is not friendly either — it is the focus of a creature that is trying to solve a problem. *What is this? Does it eat? Is it dangerous? Is it going to stay?*

One of them has a small, curved blade of chitin in their belt — not for cutting you, but for cutting fiber, for scraping paste, for the thousand small tasks of living in the rift. Another has a cord of braided sinew over one shoulder, with a perforated stone hanging from it, clicking faintly against their collarbone as they breathe. The third is bare-handed, but their fingers are moving in small, subtle patterns against the stone of the niche — a tactile language, speaking to the others without sound. *Stay still. Let it pass. See what it does.*

They have seen strangers before, but only rarely — a lost surface-person who fell through a crack and wandered into the rift before the cold and the disorientation drove them back up; a centipede-thing from a deeper chamber, larger and stranger than the usual kind, that moved through their territory and left again without incident. They do not have a category for you yet. You are not surface-person — you moved too carefully, too deliberately, and you did not panic at the sound or the heat. You are not fauna — you are too large, too still, too *composed*. You might be something new, and if you are something new, they need to watch you for a long time before they know what you mean.

The person with the perforated stone shifts slightly, and the stone clicks once — a single, clear note in the warm air. Below you, in the silt, the pale grub-things pause in their peristaltic wave and then resume. The steam columns shift direction. The clicking of the fauna rises and falls in a wave that might be a response to the stone, or might be coincidence, or might be a conversation happening in a language you cannot hear.

---

They are not primitive. The word means *first*, and they are first — first tool-users, first builders, first people to ask what happens if you heat this paste or carve this hollow or hang these stones in this order. They are not simple. They are *early*. There is a difference. They are doing the work of invention, and invention is hard, and it takes a particular kind of mind to look at a rift full of clicking, seething, unfinished geology and think: *I could build something here. I could make something that wasn't here before.*

The obsidian blade, wrapped in sinew, is in a hollow near the fire pit. It is the only thing they have that is not of this place. It came from somewhere else — from a geology of volcanic glass and cooling lava, a place of sudden, violent formation, nothing like the slow, damp, patient becoming of the rift. It is an anomaly, an outside object, and they keep it not because it is useful (it is not, particularly — their chitin blades work fine) but because it is *evidence*. Evidence that there are other places. Other ways of being rock. Other worlds below or above or beyond this one. They do not know if they want to find those places. They do not know if those places would want to be found. But they keep the blade, and sometimes, in the quietest hours of the shallow-sleep cycle, one of them will take it out and hold it and feel the weight of another world in their hands, and wonder.

---

The rift is still forming. The walls are still crumbling. The silt is still rising. The fauna is still eating the stone, and the stone is still becoming something else, and the warm air is still clicking with the sound of ten million small bodies in motion. And they are here, in the middle of it — small, pale, four-toed, watching with dark eyes and saying to each other in clicks and touches: *What is this? What happens now? What does it become?*

They have not yet decided what you are. They are patient. The rift has taught them patience — the patience of stone, the patience of slow accretion, the patience of a place that is still deciding what it wants to be. They will watch you. They will wait. And eventually, when the time is right, when they have enough information, when they have a category for you that is not *stranger* but something more specific — they will come down from the niche and approach, and they will touch your hand with their four-toed fingers, and they will taste the mineral dust on your skin, and they will know whether you belong to this place or whether you are something the rift is still deciding about.
