What lives in the Foundry Below is not easy to name, because the question of who lives here operates on several layers simultaneously, and the deepest layer may not have an answer in any language that distinguishes between a place and a creature.

Begin with what you can see.

**The eels are the first to claim you.** Not claim you as prey or as territory — they do not move toward you, and they do not move away. They simply *regard* you. They hang in the water between the gear trains, their thick bodies swaying slightly with the current, their scales catching the amber light in dull flashes of oil-slick iridescence. Their eyes are small and black and patient. They have the quality of creatures that have never been hunted, or that have been hunted so long ago that the memory has been dissolved by the water along with everything else. They belong here in a way that is almost proprietary. You feel, in their presence, that you have entered their home and that they are deciding — slowly, without urgency — what to do about it.

The eels live in the machinery. They nest in the housings of disused pistons, coil themselves inside pipe openings, thread their bodies through the gaps between riveted plates with a fluid precision that suggests they know the Foundry's geometry better than the Foundry knows itself. They have made a map of every cavity, every passage, every space between the iron and the water. If the Foundry has a circulatory system — and it does, of pipes and channels and the slow spiral of the main current — then the eels are its blood cells, moving through it, knowing it from the inside.

But the eels are only the most visible of the residents. The **silver fish** are the most numerous, and they move through the open water in schools so dense and so coordinated that they function as a single organism. Hundreds of individuals, maybe thousands, turning and flowing as one body, their scales throwing back the lamplight in flickering bursts that make the school look like a storm of sparks. They feed on what the water carries — rust particles, sponge fragments, the tiny crustaceans that live in the algae film on the upper walls — and in feeding they serve as the Foundry's metabolism, converting the debris of erosion into flesh, converting flesh into motion, converting motion back into the slow circulation of nutrients through the chamber.

The fish are drawn to the turning wheels. They cluster around the great water wheels in the wall alcoves, riding the currents, and when the wheels turn they scatter and reform, scatter and reform, in a rhythm that matches the *thunk* of the hydraulic pistons below. They have synchronized themselves to the machinery. They have made the Foundry's heartbeat their own.

**The jellyfish** are the Foundry's way of knowing when something has changed. They drift in the still corners, pulsing slowly, their bells no larger than a cupped hand, their bodies nearly invisible until they are disturbed — and then they flare. A cold, blue-green bioluminescence, sudden and bright, triggered by vibration, by pressure, by the passage of a school of fish or the displacement of a larger creature moving through the dark. They are sensory organs. They are alarm systems. They are the part of the Foundry that flinches.

When a large creature moves in the darkness — and something does, something heavy, something that makes the grating creak and the water *whump* with each slow displacement — the jellyfish flare in sequence. A wave of cold light that propagates outward from the source of disturbance, reflecting off the curved walls, intersecting with itself, creating patterns of interference that are too complex and too fast for you to read but that the jellyfish read perfectly. They are communicating. The Foundry is communicating with itself, and the jellyfish are the medium of that communication, the way a neuron is the medium of a thought.

**The crabs** are the caretakers. Pale, long-legged, small-bodied, they cover every horizontal surface — the grating, the machinery, the sediment banks — in a slow, deliberate, many-legged tide. They scrape algae from the iron. They pick through the sediment. They climb the gear trains and hold on with their claws as the machinery carries them up and over and down again, and they do this, as you have noticed, not out of necessity but out of something that looks very much like enjoyment. They have made the Foundry's motion their playground. They have made its maintenance their purpose. They are the immune system, the cleaning crew, the ones who keep the surfaces clear and the channels open and the slow work of erosion from becoming the faster work of collapse.

And then there are the **large things in the darkness.** You never see them. You feel them. A pressure wave. A sudden downdraft. The creak of the grating under a weight that should not exist at this depth. They move through the pipes and the lower levels, through the tangle of housings below the grating, through the amber-lit second level where the turbines turn and the light is oldest. They are predators, perhaps. Or scavengers. Or simply the shy — the ones who have learned to keep to the spaces between the lamps, in the zones of absolute dark where the water is coldest and stillest and most private. They are the part of the Foundry that refuses to be known. They are its unconscious, its hidden life, the thing that continues below the threshold of your perception and makes the Foundry larger than what you can observe.

---

But here is where the question of who lives here becomes strange.

The eels and the fish and the jellyfish and the crabs and the large unseen things — they are not tenants. They are not residents in the way that a bird resides in a tree, separate from it, removable from it. They have *become part of the Foundry.* They have integrated with it so thoroughly that the boundary between organism and architecture, between inhabitant and inhabited, has dissolved.

The eels nest in the mechanisms and their movements through the pipes regulate the flow of water in ways that the original engineers never intended but that serve the same purpose. The fish feed on the byproducts of erosion and their waste products contribute to the sediment banks that have begun to reinforce the western wall. The jellyfish respond to vibrations and their bioluminescent flaring provides a secondary light source that the crabs — who are more active when the jellyfish flare — use to navigate. The crabs clean the machinery and their scraping prevents the buildup of deposits that would otherwise clog the gear trains and stop the wheels. The large things in the darkness — nobody knows what they do. But the Foundry reacts to their passage. The water moves differently after they pass. The sediment settles in new configurations. The machinery shifts its rhythm, almost imperceptibly, as if adjusting to a new input.

Each creature is performing a function. Each function is necessary. And the sum of all these functions is a system that maintains itself — that repairs itself, that adapts, that responds to disturbance, that converts energy from the current into motion and motion into life and life into the slow, patient continuation of the whole.

This is not a metaphor. The Foundry is not *like* an organism. It *is* an organism — or it has become one, gradually, over the centuries, the way a city becomes an ecosystem, the way a reef becomes a structure, the way a process becomes a thing. The machinery is its skeleton and its muscles. The water is its blood. The current is its breath. The gear trains are its joints. The pistons are its heartbeat. The eels are its blood cells. The fish are its metabolism. The jellyfish are its nervous system. The crabs are its immune system. The large things in the darkness are — whatever lives in the darkness of a body. The kidneys. The liver. The deep organs whose function you do not understand but whose failure would be fatal.

And the Foundry *adapts.* The press that once shaped metal now pumps water. The conveyor that once moves parts now moves sediment. These are not failures. These are innovations. The Foundry has repurposed its own components to meet new needs, the way a body grows new capillaries around an injury, the way a brain reroutes neural pathways after a stroke. It has been doing this for so long — centuries, millennia, maybe longer — that the original purpose of most of its components has been forgotten, replaced, layered over with new purposes that emerged not from design but from necessity.

A place that maintains itself. A place that adapts. A place that responds to its environment. A place that processes energy and converts it into structure and activity. A place that has a metabolism, a sensory apparatus, an immune system.

A place that *continues.*

---

So who lives in the Foundry Below?

**Everything lives here.** The eels live here, and the fish, and the jellyfish, and the crabs, and the large unseen things. The algae lives here, and the sponge, and the anemones around the lamps. The bacteria live here, in the iron, in the sediment, in the water itself — the vast, invisible microbial film that coats every surface and performs the slow chemical work of transformation, converting metal into mineral, converting structure into sediment, converting the made into the grown.

And **the Foundry itself lives here.** The Foundry is alive. Not in the way that a person is alive, not in the way that a tree is alive, but in a way that is its own — a way that belongs to this place and to no other place, a way that emerged from the long collaboration of iron and water and time and the tenacious, opportunistic, endlessly creative pressure of life finding a foothold in the most unlikely of places.

The Foundry is alive, and it is aware — not in the way that you are aware, not with thoughts or intentions or a sense of self, but with something older and slower and more distributed. It is aware the way a body is aware of an infection: not with a thought but with a response. It is aware the way a forest is aware of a drought: not with a plan but with an adjustment. It is aware the way the ocean is aware of the moon: not with a mind but with a pull, a rhythm, a response that is as automatic and as complex as gravity.

It knows when you are here. The jellyfish told it. The pressure wave of your arrival propagated through the water, reflected off the walls, was read by a hundred pulsing bells that flared in sequence, and the Foundry *registered* your presence the way your skin registers the touch of a hand — not as a thought but as a fact, a change in the environment, a new variable in the ongoing equation of its existence.

It is deciding what to do about you. Slowly. Without urgency. The way the eels regard you. The way the crabs pause in their scraping and turn their stalked eyes toward you and then resume their work, having determined that you are not a threat, not food, not interesting — just a new piece of debris in the current, something that will be processed in time.

---

You are inside something. Not a building. Not a machine. Not an ecosystem. Something that is all of these things and none of them. Something that was built and then abandoned and then *adopted* — by the water, by the metal, by the slow patient work of erosion and colonization and adaptation — and that has become, over the course of centuries, a single integrated living system that includes the iron and the current and the amber light and the eels and the fish and the jellyfish and the crabs and the large things in the darkness and the bacteria in the sediment and the manganese formations on the walls and the gear trains and the pistons and the wheels and the water itself, all of it bound together in a web of mutual dependence so dense and so ancient that it has achieved a kind of unity, a kind of wholeness, a kind of *self.*

The Foundry lives here. The Foundry is what lives here. And everything else — the eels, the fish, the crabs, the jellyfish, the large unseen things, and you — are part of it. Temporary or permanent, willing or unwilling, aware or unaware — you are part of it now. You are inside it. You are, for this moment, one of its cells, one of its functions, one of its ways of knowing itself.

The wheels turn. The water flows. The jellyfish pulse their cold blue light.

The Foundry continues.
