The wind is not weather. The wind is the place itself, breathing.

It has been breathing since before the stone beneath your feet had a shape, and it will be breathing after that stone has been scoured to nothing. It moves at the speed of a sustained shout, sometimes faster, and it never stops. It has never stopped. The sound is not a howl or a moan or a whistle, because those words imply variation. This is a single, unbroken, full-spectrum roar — the noise of air moving over rock at the limits of what rock can endure. It is so loud that after a few hours you stop hearing it as sound and start feeling it as pressure, as a physical thing pressing against your eardrums and your sinuses and the backs of your teeth.

And the stone endures it.

The stone is the second thing that lives here, though "lives" is a word that requires stretching. It is dark — a deep charcoal grey, almost black in the recesses, shot through with veins of something lighter, milky or rust-colored, that catch the light and gleam. It has been shaped by the wind into forms that are smooth and curved and strange: long sinuous ridges, deep bowl-shaped scoops, pillars and fins and delicate arches that look impossible but stand anyway, the hard grain of the rock holding against the soft persistence of the air. The erosion here is not dramatic. There are no fresh fractures, no loose scree piles, no evidence of recent collapse. Everything has been worn to its final form and then held there, suspended in a state of perfect equilibrium between the force of the wind and the strength of the stone.

The stone remembers every gust. Not consciously — the stone has no consciousness, or if it does, it operates on a timescale so vast that a million years is a single synaptic flicker — but physically, structurally, in the language of grain and fracture and polish. Each surface is a record. The mirror-finish slabs that reflect the sky like windows into an inverted world — those are the oldest surfaces, the ones that have been longest exposed, the ones that have had the most time to be worked. The rougher patches, where the grain is still visible and the wind has not yet finished its carving — those are younger, newer, places where some ancient impact or shift has revealed fresh stone that the wind has only begun to read.

The wind reads the stone the way a blind person reads a face: slowly, attentively, with a patience that has no bottom. And the stone, for its part, submits to the reading. It does not resist. It does not fight. It simply is, and in being, is slowly, slowly unmade.

---

In the lee of the stone, in the wind shadows where the air moves slow enough to hold a shape, there is water.

The water is the third thing that lives here, and it is the most improbable. It exists in the way that ghosts exist — not present, but detectable. The air is damp in a way that has nothing to do with humidity and everything to do with speed; the wind carries moisture ripped from distant valleys and lakes and seas, and when that fast-moving air hits the still air in the lee of a rock formation, the moisture condenses instantly, coating every sheltered surface in a film of cold water. Walk into the wind shadow of a large boulder and you will feel the air temperature drop ten degrees in a step.

The water beads on the stone, runs in thin threads down the polished surfaces, collects in the bowl-shaped depressions that dot the plateau like craters on a pale moon. Some of these pools are no bigger than your hand. Others are ten meters across and deep enough to swallow your arm to the shoulder. The water in them is black and still and so cold it hurts, and they have no inlet and no outlet — they are fed entirely by condensation, and they evaporate at exactly the rate they fill, and they have been doing this for so long that the stone beneath them has been worn into perfect smooth concavities, as if the water itself is a cutting tool.

The pools are the holiest things on the plateau. Not because they are sacred — nothing here is sacred, because nothing here has use for the concept of sacredness — but because they are the only things that are still. The wind never stops. The stone is always, imperceptibly, wearing away. The light shifts with the turning of the earth. But the pools are still. Perfectly, absolutely, unnervingly still. When the wind blows across them, they do not ripple — the wind is moving too fast, and the surface tension of the water is too strong, and the air is too cold, and the combination of these factors means that the surface of each pool remains a flawless, unbroken mirror even as the wind tears across it. The sky is reflected in these pools. The stone is reflected. You are reflected, if you kneel at the edge and look down, and what you see is not your face but a dark, distorted, wind-shimmered version of yourself that looks like it belongs to the plateau more than it belongs to you.

The pools are alive, in their way. Not with fish or plants or any visible organism, but with a slow chemical life, a mineral metabolism. The water dissolves the stone at a rate so slow it might as well be zero, but it is not zero. It is never zero. Over centuries, over millennia, the pools deepen by fractions of millimeters, and the dissolved minerals precipitate out in thin, invisible layers on the pool floors, building up a sediment so fine it is more like a memory of mud than mud itself. And in this sediment, things live — things so small and so slow that they make the lichens look hyperactive. Archaea, perhaps, or something older, something that has been here since before the stone was stone, since when this place was seabed or magma or the floor of an ocean that no longer exists. They metabolize the dissolved minerals. They reproduce on timescales of decades. They are the deepest life on the plateau, the foundation of everything else, and they have been here longer than anything.

---

Above the archaea, above the mineral sediment, in the thin film of water that coats every sheltered surface, there is moss.

Not moss as you know it. Not the soft, green, yielding moss of temperate forests and garden walls. This is something harder, darker, more mineral than plant. It grows flat against the stone in patterns that look almost like writing — tight, intricate, spiraling scripts in dark green and black and deep, deep brown, each frond no thicker than a human hair, each colony spreading outward from a central point in a pattern that is part fractal, part language, part something else entirely. It is so tightly adhered to the stone that you cannot peel it off without destroying it. It has, in a sense, become the stone — its root structures (if they can be called roots) penetrate the mineral grain itself, not breaking the stone but infiltrating it, occupying the microscopic spaces between crystals, binding the surface layer into something that is neither plant nor mineral but a third thing, a composite, a collaboration between biology and geology that has been ongoing for so long that the two parties have forgotten where one ends and the other begins.

The moss grows in the deepest wind shadows, where the air is stillest and the condensation is heaviest. It grows on the north sides of boulders, on the floors of wind-carved bowls, in the cracks between stone fins where the wind can only reach as a muffled whisper. It grows slowly — a millimeter a year, perhaps less — and it dies from the outside in, the oldest parts darkening and hardening and eventually becoming indistinguishable from the stone itself, so that each colony is a living thing built on the compressed remains of its own ancestors, a structure that is part organism, part monument, part geological feature.

And on the moss, and on the stone around the moss, and on every surface that the wind does not scour clean, there are lichens.

The lichens are the plateau's most visible form of life, and its most patient. They spread across the rock surfaces in slow, centuries-long fronts, consuming the mineral grain by grain and converting it into color. Pale orange. Pale green. A white so bright it seems to glow in the flat, shadowless light. A yellow like old bone. A grey that is almost silver. They grow in circles, in lobes, in crusts so thin they are barely there, in structures so raised and convoluted they look like tiny landscapes — mountain ranges and valleys and river deltas in miniature, each one a few centimeters across, each one decades or centuries old.

The lichens are the plateau's memory. Their growth rings — visible only under magnification, readable only by those who know the language — encode the history of this place in a chemical record: the composition of the atmosphere, the intensity of the wind, the frequency of the condensation, the slow, slow changes in temperature and light that mark the passage of centuries the way tree rings mark the passage of years. A lichen the size of your palm might be five hundred years old. A lichen the size of your head might be two thousand. The largest colonies, the ones that cover entire boulder faces in unbroken sheets of pale orange, might be older than your civilization. They were here before you arrived. They will be here after you leave. They are not waiting for anything. They are simply growing, simply being, simply converting stone into color at a rate that the wind can barely keep up with, so that the wind scours and the lichens grow and the wind scours again and the balance between them is so precise, so perfect, that it looks like intention.

---

In the lichens, and among the lichens, and in the tiny spaces between the lichen colonies and the moss colonies and the bare stone, there are insects.

They are pale. They are soft-bodied. They are almost translucent, and they have no wings, because wings are a luxury that the wind does not permit. They are condensation insects — the prompt calls them that, and the name is apt, because they exist in a way that is entirely dependent on the condensation, on the ghost-water, on the thin film of moisture that coats every sheltered surface. They emerge from cracks in the stone when the moisture comes — not when it rains, because it does not rain here, but when the condensation is heavy enough, when the wind-shadow dampness reaches some critical threshold that they can sense with antennae so fine they are barely visible even to each other. They emerge, and they feed on the moss and the lichens and the archaea in the pool sediments, and they mate, and they lay their eggs in the cracks, and they vanish back into the stone when the moisture dries, and they wait.

They can wait for years. Their eggs can wait longer — decades, perhaps, sealed in microscopic crevices with a drop of water that never quite evaporates, waiting for the next heavy condensation event, the next convergence of wind and temperature and humidity that will trigger hatching. The adults, when they are active, move quickly — not with the darting, nervous speed of lowland insects, but with a deliberate, purposeful gait, their pale bodies hugging the stone, their legs finding purchase on surfaces that look smooth but are, at their scale, as rough and varied as a mountain range. They are the most numerous animals on the plateau, and the least visible, and they are the foundation of everything else that moves.

---

Above the insects, above the moss and the lichens and the stone, there are birds.

They are large. They are dark-plumed — a deep, matte black that absorbs the light rather than reflecting it, that makes them look like holes in the sky, like absences, like the visual equivalent of silence. They use the wind the way fish use currents, soaring in wide, effortless circles without ever once flapping their wings, their eyes fixed on the stone below, watching for the small movements that mean food. They ride the updrafts that form on the windward side of rock formations, the thermal columns that rise from sun-warmed stone, the complex, invisible topography of moving air that is as real and as navigable to them as a river is to an otter.

They do not sing. There is no point in singing here — the wind would swallow the sound before it traveled a meter, and besides, these birds have no territory to defend, no mate to attract, no rival to warn. They are solitary, each one patrolling a territory that covers square kilometers, each one knowing the wind patterns of its territory with an intimacy that is almost geological — this updraft at this time of day, this downdraft in this season, this pocket of still air behind this particular boulder where the condensation insects will be emerging. They hunt by sight, plummeting from the sky in controlled dives that bring them to the stone at speeds that should be fatal but are not, because they know the wind so well that they can adjust their trajectory in the last instant, can flare their wings and brake and land with a precision that puts them exactly where they need to be — at the edge of a condensation pool, in the shelter of a boulder, on a moss patch where the insects are thick.

They eat the insects. They eat the small, fur-heavy burrowers when they can catch them. They eat each other's young, sometimes, in the rare years when the condensation is heavy and the insect population booms and the burrower population booms in response and there is, briefly, enough food to support a second brood, and the first brood is large enough and hungry enough to be a threat to the second. This is not cruelty. This is the plateau, where everything is food for everything else, where the margins are so thin that every calorie matters, where the difference between survival and death is measured in milligrams of body weight and fractions of a degree of body temperature.

The birds nest on the stone. They build no nests — there is nothing to build with, no twigs, no grass, no mud. They lay their eggs in shallow depressions that they scrape into the moss with their bodies, turning in tight circles, pressing down, wearing the moss and the stone beneath it into a cup-shaped hollow that is exactly the size of the bird and exactly the depth of the egg. The eggs are dark — a deep grey-green, speckled with black, nearly invisible against the dark stone. The chicks, when they hatch, are dark too, and still, and they sit in their scrapes and wait for food and do not move, do not call, do not do anything that might attract the attention of a neighbor who might be hungry.

---

And in the moss, in the tunnels they dig into the moss and the stone beneath the moss, there are the burrowers.

They are small. They are round. They are fur-heavy — covered in a dense, soft, grey-brown pelage so thick that it obscures the outlines of their bodies, so that they look less like animals and more like small, mobile cushions, or like stones that have accidentally grown fur. Their eyes are small and dark and set deep in the fur, protected from the wind. Their ears are tiny — just openings, really, covered with a fringe of stiff hair that filters the wind noise down to a manageable level. Their legs are short and powerful, ending in broad, flat feet with thick pads that grip the stone and sharp claws that dig into the moss and the cracks beneath the moss. Their tails are vestigial — just nubs, really, hidden in the fur, useless for balance in a place where there is nothing to balance on.

They live in tunnels. The tunnels are not deep — the stone is too hard for deep tunneling — but they are complex, branching networks that extend outward from a central chamber, following the moss patches, connecting one food source to another, always staying below the surface, always staying in the wind shadow. The central chamber is lined with shed fur and dried moss fragments, and it is warm — not hot, but warm enough, warm enough that the burrower's metabolism can maintain its body temperature without burning through its fat reserves too quickly. The burrowers huddle in their chambers during the worst of the wind, during the rare storms when the gusts exceed even the plateau's normal extremes, and they wait, and they sleep, and they metabolize the lichens and the moss they have eaten, and they survive.

They emerge to feed. They emerge at the times when the condensation is heaviest — not at any particular time of day, because time of day means nothing here, but at the times when the wind shifts and the moisture comes and the moss swells and the lichens soften and the insects emerge and the whole brief, improbable food web of the plateau's wind shadows comes to life for a few hours before the wind shifts again and everything goes dormant. The burrowers move quickly between food sources, their low, rounded bodies hugging the stone, their small eyes scanning for the dark shapes of the soaring birds. They eat the lichens. They eat the moss. They eat the insects, when they can catch them. They eat the hummock-grass, those tiny, dark-green islands of life that grow in cracks so small you can barely see them, each hummock rooted in nothing, sustained by nothing, enduring everything.

The hummock-grass is the burrowers' most reliable food source, and the burrowers are the grass's only pollinators and seed dispersers, and the relationship between them is so old and so tight that neither can survive without the other. The grass provides the burrowers with food and shelter — the hummocks create micro-wind-shadows that are even more sheltered than the lee of a boulder, tiny pockets of still air where the temperature is a degree or two warmer and the wind noise is a fraction quieter and the burrowers can rest and groom and nurse their young without expending quite so much energy on staying alive. The burrowers provide the grass with transportation — the seeds stick to the burrowers' fur, and the burrowers move between hummocks, and the seeds fall off in new cracks, and new hummocks grow, and the grass spreads across the plateau at the speed of a burrower's daily rounds, which is to say, very, very slowly.

---

The burrowers are the plateau's most improbable life. They are warm-blooded in a place that punishes warm-bloodedness. They are furry in a place that punishes fur — the wind catches fur, pulls at it, uses it as a lever to pull heat away from the body. They are round in a place that punishes roundness — the wind catches round shapes, pushes them, rolls them, sends them tumbling across the stone. And yet they persist. They persist because they are small enough to hide, and furry enough to insulate, and round enough to minimize their surface-area-to-volume ratio, and slow enough to survive on the meager calories that the plateau provides, and patient enough to wait out the worst of the wind in their tunnels, and social enough — barely, reluctantly, in the grudging way of animals that have no choice — to share their warmth and their food and their tunnels with their kin.

They are not intelligent, not in the way that word is usually meant. But they know things. They know the wind patterns of their territory — not the way the birds know them, from above, in broad strokes, but from below, in fine detail, the way a person knows the rooms of their home. They know which boulder to shelter behind when the wind shifts to the northwest. They know which condensation pool will be full and which will be empty. They know which moss patch is fruiting and which is dormant. They know the taste of every lichen species on the plateau, and they know which ones are nutritious and which are merely filling and which are mildly toxic and must be eaten only in small quantities and only when nothing better is available. They know the smell of a bird — not the sight, which is useless in a place where the birds are black and the sky is white and the contrast is too extreme for easy detection, but the smell, the faint, sharp, predatory musk that a bird carries on its feathers, which the burrowers can detect from fifty meters away, from inside their tunnels, through the moss and the stone and the still air.

They know each other. Not as individuals, perhaps — their eyes are too small and their lives are too dark and too sheltered for individual recognition by sight — but by smell, by the unique chemical signature that each burrower carries, which tells its neighbors its sex and its age and its reproductive status and its health and its kinship and its mood. They greet each other with a brief touch of noses, a exchange of scent, a moment of stillness in a place that has no stillness, and then they move on, each one back to its own territory, its own tunnels, its own slow, patient, ancient routine of feeding and resting and surviving.

---

This is what lives here: the wind, and the stone, and the water, and the moss, and the lichens, and the insects, and the birds, and the burrowers, and the grass, and the archaea in the pool sediments, and the minerals in the stone, and the light from the sky, and the cold, and the altitude, and the thin air, and the silence that is not silence because the wind never stops, and the stillness that is not stillness because everything is always, always changing, even if the change is so slow that a human lifetime is not enough to see it.

This is what lives here: a system. A single, self-sustaining, self-regulating system that has been running for so long that it has achieved a kind of perfection — not the perfection of design, but the perfection of persistence, the perfection of a thing that has been tested by every possible stress and has survived them all and has emerged not unchanged but unbroken, still running, still functioning, still converting wind and stone and light and water into life, slowly, patiently, at a rate that the plateau can sustain forever, or at least for as long as the wind blows and the stone endures and the water condenses and the sun burns and the world turns and the seasons that do not change continue their slow, indifferent, eternal round.

The plateau does not need you. The plateau does not need anything. The plateau was here before you arrived and it will be here after you leave, and your presence here is no more significant to it than the presence of a single condensation insect, a single lichen spore, a single burrower hair caught on a blade of hummock-grass. You are a visitor. You are a momentary perturbation in a system that has been running for longer than your species has existed. The wind will take your footprints. The stone will take your warmth. The water will take your reflection. And the plateau will remain, as it has always remained, as it will always remain: wind and stone and light and the slow, patient, ancient process of becoming less, which is also, in its way, the process of becoming more, because every grain of stone that the wind removes reveals a new surface, and every new surface is a new possibility, and every new possibility is a new beginning, and every new beginning is another chance for the moss to grow and the lichen to spread and the insect to emerge and the bird to hunt and the burrower to dig and the grass to root and the archaea to metabolize and the water to condense and the wind to blow and the stone to endure and the place to remain, to persist, to be, in its vast, indifferent, magnificent way, alive.

---

And if you stay long enough — if you camp in the wind shadow and drink from the condensation pool and eat the burrower that you caught (quickly, cleanly, with gratitude, because the plateau does not permit waste) and sleep in the lee of the boulder and wake to the same wind and the same light and the same stone — if you stay long enough, you begin to feel it. Not the wind, which you feel immediately, and not the cold, which you feel constantly, but something else. Something underneath the wind and the cold and the stone. A presence. An attention. The sense that the plateau is aware of you — not watching you, not judging you, not caring about you, but aware of you the way it is aware of everything: the way the wind is aware of the stone, the way the stone is aware of the water, the way the water is aware of the moss, the way the moss is aware of the lichen, the way the lichen is aware of the insect, the way the insect is aware of the bird, the way the bird is aware of the burrower, the way the burrower is aware of the grass, the way the grass is aware of the crack in the stone where it has rooted, the way the crack is aware of the wind that made it, the way the wind is aware of the sky that drives it, the way the sky is aware of the sun that lights it, the way the sun is aware of the stone that reflects it, the way the stone is aware of you, standing on it, breathing on it, warming it with your body for a few hours before the wind takes your warmth and the stone takes your footprint and the water takes your reflection and the plateau takes your memory and you are gone and the place remains and the wind blows and the stone endures and the water condenses and the moss grows and the lichen spreads and the insect emerges and the bird hunts and the burrower digs and the grass roots and the archaea metabolize and the system runs and the system runs and the system runs and nothing changes and everything changes and the plateau is alive, has always been alive, will always be alive, in its way, on its timescale, in its language of wind and stone and water and time, and you were here, you were part of it, for a moment, for a brief, bright, improbable moment, and now you are not, and the plateau does not mind, because the plateau does not mind anything, because the plateau is not a thing that minds, it is a thing that is, and in being, it is enough.
