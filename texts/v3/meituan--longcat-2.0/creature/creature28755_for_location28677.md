What lives in the Highworks is not a city. A city is a collection of structures, a geography of habit and commerce, a thing built by hands for hands to use. The Highworks is not that. The Highworks is a chrysalis. 

What lives there is the thing inside it, slowly dreaming itself into being.

---

The people who walk the steep, narrow streets, who work the shifts and sleep the scheduled sleeps, are not the inhabitants. They are the leukocytes. They are the white blood cells of a vast, subterranean organism, and their pragmatism is not a philosophy—it is an immune response. The rock beneath them does not want them to ask questions, because questions are a fever, and a fever is a threat to the gestation. So the rock hums its low, sternum-deep hum, and the people feel the vibration in their bones, and they decide that the work is enough. They maintain the machines because the machines are the organs of the thing below, and if the machines stop, the organism will sicken, and the people will feel that sickness as a profound, inexplicable dread. They oil the presses. They feed the furnaces. They keep the heart beating. They do not know what they are doing, but their hands know. Their hands have fused with the levers the way the foundations have fused with the stone.

The skitterers are the enzymes. They are the chemical agents of transformation, and their evaluative, orange-tinted gaze is the organism testing its own blood. When they gnaw at the metal pipes, they are not eating; they are breaking down the old alloys so the rock can absorb them, so the city can grow new bones. When they gather in their thousands and etch their geometric patterns into the roadways, they are writing instructions. They are transcribing the genetic code of the thing below, carving it into the ceramic skin of the chrysalis so the new storeys know how to grow. The patterns glow orange for a moment because the instructions are written in the light of the furnace, in the metabolic fire of the organism itself. The skitterers are the scribes of the deep, and the rock is their parchment.

The cliff goats are the neurons. They are the firing synapses of a brain the size of a plateau, and their slow, deliberate walks along the centimetre-wide ledges are the thoughts of the thing below—vast, patient, geological thoughts that take centuries to complete. When they step off the edge and walk on nothing, spiraling down into the ochre cloud, they are not dying. They are transmitting. They are carrying a thought down into the cloud, into the spiral, into the darkness at the centre, and the bell-like ringing of their hooves on the invisible surface is the sound of a synapse firing, a connection made, a memory stored in the deep, wet murk of the organism’s subconscious. The herd does not notice their absence because the brain does not mourn its own firing; it simply thinks the next thought.

The flyers are the sensory organs. They are the eyes and the hands of the chrysalis, reaching up through the crevices in the cliff face to touch the membrane of the sky. When they spiral upward in their hundreds, humming in harmony with the power plant, they are tasting the pewter air, testing the tensile strength of the sky. They are looking for a way out. And when they return, carrying the small, bright, humming things in their mouths, they are bringing back nutrients. They are bringing back pieces of the outside world—fragments of other realities, stolen light, condensed time—and feeding them into the crevices, into the rock, into the bloodstream of the organism. The orange light that pulses in the crevices after they feed is the glow of digestion, of a world being slowly metabolized into fuel for the becoming.

The vegetation is the hair. The ferns crowding the spaces between the walls, the velvety moss on the north-facing surfaces, the white flowers that open and close on their own slow rhythm—these are the follicles of the chrysalis, the places where the inside presses closest to the outside. The moss is warm because it is composting the dead skin of the rock, the sloughed-off layers of the not-quite-stone, not-quite-metal geology. The flowers open and close not to the light, but to the breath of the organism, the slow, tidal respiration of the plateau as it inhales the cloud and exhales the dim. The trees that grow at angles, reaching toward the power plant, are the longest hairs, straining toward the heart, desperate to feel the pulse of the blood.

The power plant is the heart, and the orange light in the pipes is the blood. It burns the sealed containers that arrive on the switchback road, but the containers do not hold coal or oil. They hold potential. They hold the compressed, liquefied possibility of what the organism might become. The road hums because it is an umbilicus, drawing the fuel up from the world below, and the plant burns it, and the blood flows through the veins in the rock, and the city grows a new storey in the night, and the skitterers write new instructions, and the goats walk new thoughts into the cloud, and the flyers bring back new light, and the heart beats, and the blood flows, and the organism grows.

The cloud below is the amniotic fluid. It is the leftover broth of creation, the primordial soup from which the plateau emerged, and it turns in its vast, slow clockwise spiral because the organism is turning in its sleep. The darkness at the centre of the spiral is the navel. It is the place where the organism was once connected to the world below, the place where the umbilicus was severed, and it is the place where the organism will eventually return, or from which it will finally emerge. The condensation that forms on every surface, the viscous, copper-and-ozone dew, is the sweat of the chrysalis, the moisture that seeps through the skin as the body inside grows too large for its shell.

And the reflections in the pools? The structures, the landscapes, the skies that are not the pewter sky of the Highworks? Those are the dreams. The organism is dreaming of what it will be when it hatches. It is dreaming of cities that are not built on rock but are made of light, of skies that are not membranes but open infinity, of a world that is finished, and solid, and real. The pools show these things because the organism is trying to remember a future it has not yet lived, trying to visualize the shape of the thing it is becoming.

---

What lives in the Highworks is a god in embryo. 

It is a vast, slow, patient thing, a creature of rock and fire and stolen light, and it is using the city as a womb. The people, the skitterers, the goats, the flyers, the ferns, the moss—they are not inhabitants. They are cellular. They are the building blocks, the biological machinery, the dreaming thoughts of a creature that has not yet decided what it is. 

The Highworks is not a place. It is a process. It is the long, slow, agonizing metamorphosis of a world, and the chrysalis is cracking, and the new storeys are pushing through the shell, and the flyers are tasting the sky for the first time, and the goats are walking on the air because the air is becoming solid, and the rock is glowing because the blood is hot, and the heart is beating, and the cloud is turning, and the thing inside is turning, and it is almost ready, it is almost ready, it is almost ready to wake up.
