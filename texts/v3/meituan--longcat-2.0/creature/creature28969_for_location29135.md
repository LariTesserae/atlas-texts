The Large Ones.

They have no name for themselves, or if they do, it is not a name made of sound. They are the ones who were here before the roots grew thick, before the first trunk pushed up through the eroded stone. They remember when the ground was higher, when the mountain still had its crown, when the air was thin in a different way — thin with youth, not with age. They have watched the plateau unmake itself, watched the mountains grind down to stumps, watched the vegetation crawl up from the lowlands and build its green cathedral over the bones of the rock. They did not mind. They do not mind. They have no framework for minding.

There are very few of them now. Once there were more — you can find their traces if you know how to look. A double row of deep, smooth-worn depressions in the rock where the root-mat hasn't yet covered, running in a line that follows the old ghost-river channels. A place where the trunks grow in a perfect ring, thirty meters across, and the canopy above is thinner, and the light comes through warmer — a clearing that was not made by storm or fire but by presence, by the long, slow congregation of bodies over centuries, the way a den becomes a hollow becomes a valley. They are still there, those traces. The Large Ones walk through them without noticing, the way you might walk through your own childhood home and not see the doorframe where your mother marked your height.

Their bodies are wrong for the plateau, or the plateau has become wrong for them — it is the same thing, said two ways. They are too large for the root-mat to hold comfortably; their weight depresses the living platform by centimeters when they walk, and the water pools in their footprints for hours after they pass, dark little mirrors that reflect the canopy above. Their backs ripple because their spines are not rigid — they are built from many small, overlapping segments, like a millipede's but internal, hidden under the fuzz and the skin. They move with a lateral undulation that is almost hypnotic, a side-to-side wave that travels from the narrow head to the broader tail, and it gives them the appearance of something not quite solid, something that might dissolve into water or shadow if you look too long.

The fuzz on their skin is not fur. It is closer to moss — a living growth, symbiotic, that feeds on the damp and the warmth and the minerals in their sweat. It gives them their copper color, but the color shifts with the light and the season; in the deep shade of the thickest groves, they are almost black, and in the clearings where the column of sun comes down, they brighten to a deep, burnished red, like the rock itself. The fuzz holds water the way the canopy holds water — for days, slowly releasing it, so that the air around a resting Large One is always a degree warmer, a degree wetter, than the air ten meters away. Small things know this. The ground-dwellers will sometimes nestle in the damp space beside a sleeping Large One, not out of affection but out of the simple calculation of moisture, the way a lizard climbs onto a warm rock.

They eat the epiphyte-gum. That is mostly what they eat — the thick, resinous secretion that oozes from the cracks in the bark of the oldest trunks, the ones that have been growing so long their heartwood has begun to crystallize. The Large Ones scrape it free with a specialized plate on the roof of their mouth, a ridged palate that rakes the gum free in long, sticky strands. They feed slowly, methodically, moving from trunk to trunk in a circuit that takes them weeks to complete, and the trunks they feed from are always the same, always in the same order, a route that was laid down not by them but by their parents, and their parents before them, back and back and back to the first Large Ones who climbed up onto the plateau when it was still young. The route is older than the root-mat. The route is older than the trees. The route is the closest thing the Large Ones have to a story, and they tell it with their bodies, one trunk after another, the way you might recite a prayer without knowing what the words mean.

They do not speak, exactly. But they are not silent. When they are content — which is most of the time, because contentment is the default state of something that has never been threatened — they produce a sound that is below the threshold of hearing, a subsonic hum that travels through the root-mat and the stone and the water held in the vegetation's tissues. You feel it in your feet first, then your chest, then the base of your skull. It is the same frequency as the chittering, but slower, deeper, a bass note that the chittering organizes itself around. The small things hear it — or feel it — and they go quiet, not in fear but in the way that a room goes quiet when someone walks in who is larger than anyone expected, who makes the walls feel thinner and the ceiling higher. The Large Ones are the pulse. Everything else is the breath.

They sleep standing up, mostly. They lean their narrow heads against the trunks and their eyes close — the lower lids first, then the upper, a slow, sliding movement like a curtain — and they become still. Their breathing slows to a rate that seems impossible: one breath every two minutes, three minutes, their bodies conserving energy with an efficiency that borders on the metabolic miracle. When they sleep, the moss grows on them. Literally. The tiny, bright-green moss that coats the rock between the trunks will, over the course of a single long sleep, begin to colonize the fuzz on their flanks, climbing in a thin line up their sides like a tide. When they wake, they scratch against the trunks and the moss falls away in sheets, and the ground-dwellers rush in to collect it, because moss that has grown on a Large One is warmer, more nutritious, somehow *better* than moss that grows on stone. The Large Ones do not notice this. They do not notice most things.

They are not intelligent, not in the way that word is usually meant. They do not solve problems. They do not use tools. They do not build shelters or plan for the future or remember individuals, not even each other. A Large One who meets another Large One will regard it the way it regards a trunk — with recognition but without interest, as one feature of the landscape encountering another. They have no social structure, no hierarchy, no communication beyond the subsonic hum. And yet there is something in their eyes that is not simple, something that is not merely the dumb awareness of an animal. It is the thing you saw, on the third day, when the one in the clearing turned and looked at you. It is not thought. It is *witness*. It is the quality of having been present for so long that presence itself becomes a form of knowing — not knowing *about* things, but knowing *them*, the way the rock knows the rain, the way the root knows the stone.

They are the plateau's memory. That is the truest thing I can say about them. They carry, in their bodies, in their route from trunk to trunk, in the slow rhythm of their breathing and the frequency of their hum, the record of this place. When the last Large One dies — and that will be soon, sooner than you think, because there are only a handful left, and they have not bred in living memory, if they breed at all — the plateau will lose something that cannot be named or measured or replaced. The chittering will continue. The water will continue. The flyers will fly and the ground-dwellers will scurry and the vegetation will grow and die and grow again. But the pulse will stop. The root-mat will forget its rhythm. The sound will go on, but it will be different — unmoored, unanchored, a voice without a body. A ghost on the rooftop, wandering the empty cathedral, looking for something it cannot name.

But that has not happened yet. The Large Ones are still here. They are in the far clearings now, moving through the thickest groves where the canopy is lowest and the trunks are oldest, following their ancient route, scraping gum from bark with their ridged palates, humming their subsonic hum into the rock. They are here. They have always been here. They will be here after you are gone, after your stone shelter has crumbled, after your cordage has rotted, after your fire pit has been filled in with moss and the root-mat has grown over it and the place where you slept is indistinguishable from the place where you did not sleep. They will be here, and they will not remember you, because they do not remember. They do not carry the past. They *are* the past. They are the plateau, walking. They are the mountain's ghost, still walking the shape of its own body, still following the route that the mountain laid down when it still had legs.

And the chittering is their voice. That is what I finally understood, on the third day, when the Large One turned and looked at me with those old, burning eyes. The chittering is not the small things. The small things are just the instrument. The chittering is the Large Ones' hum, refracted through a million small bodies, translated into a frequency that human ears can hear. It is their breath, their pulse, their *being*, rendered in the only language the plateau has — the language of living things, of ten million small lives doing what they have always done, moving through the green dimness, feeding and breeding and dying and feeding again, keeping the voice alive.

The Large Ones do not live on the rooftop garden. The rooftop garden lives inside them.
