There are people in Ur-Maren. They are the first thing you would notice, if you noticed anything at all — though noticing is not the habit here, and the people have become very good at not being noticed, at not drawing the eye, at occupying space the way the stone occupies space, which is to say completely and without apology. There are perhaps ten thousand of them, perhaps fewer. No one has counted. Counting implies a kind of mastery over the thing being counted, and the people of Ur-Maren do not feel masterful. They feel *appropriate* — sized correctly for the place, scaled to the plain, calibrated to the grey light and the thin air and the cold that insinuates.

They are small. Not unusually so, but the layers make them smaller — the wool, the felt, the hide, wrapped and wrapped and wrapped until each person is a column of textile, a soft dark shape moving through the narrow streets with the particular gait of someone who has never known a day without wind. Their faces, when you see them, are wind-burned and dry and calm. Their eyes are the color of the pools — dark, still, reflecting more than they reveal. They do not smile easily, not because they are unhappy but because smiling is a kind of expenditure, and the thin air makes extravagance difficult. Their voices, when they use them, are low and level, stripped of music, stripped of urgency. They speak the way the plain extends: in long, flat lines, without rise, without fall, without the punctuation of exclamation. A conversation in Ur-Maren sounds like two stones being rubbed together — not for any purpose, but because rubbing is what stones do when they are near each other.

They carry things. Always. A hammer, a chisel, a length of green-patinated copper pipe, a basket of dried moss-fiber, a block of the dark bread wrapped in cloth. Their hands are calloused and cracked and stained — stained green from the moss, stained dark from the stone, stained a pale, fungal white from the work in the deep chambers below the city. Their hands are the most expressive part of them. They use their hands to say what their voices will not: to point, to offer, to refuse, to touch the wall of a building as they pass it, a greeting or a reassurance or a habit so old it has become indistinguishable from prayer.

They eat the moss. They have always eaten the moss. They harvest it from the plain — walking out past the thinning edge of the city with their baskets and their stone knives, kneeling beside a patch, cutting it cleanly away from the gneiss, leaving behind a dark scar in the shape of the harvest that will close over weeks, months, the moss creeping back in from the edges, filling the gap, remembering itself. The moss is dried, ground, mixed with water from the channels, baked on hot stone into bread that is dense and dark and tastes of minerals and time. It is not good, exactly, but it is *sufficient*, and sufficiency is the highest compliment the people of Ur-Maren pay to anything. They eat the lichen too, scraped from north-facing walls in long curls, boiled into a broth that is thin and salty and faintly luminescent in the dark, a quality they do not remark on because remarking is another kind of extravagance. And they eat the fungus — the pale, soft fungus that grows in the deep chambers, the chambers that were found, not dug, the chambers that were already waiting. The fungus grows on surfaces that are not stone, surfaces that are *like* stone but are not stone, surfaces that are warm to the touch and faintly ridged and that might be chitin or might be something else entirely. The people harvest it with care, cutting only what has grown beyond the edge, leaving the root-structure intact. They do not ask what it is rooted in. They do not ask what the chambers are made of. They do not ask who made the chambers, or when, or why. This is not ignorance. This is *discipline* — the discipline of a people who have learned, over generations beyond counting, that the plain does not answer questions and that asking them is a waste of breath, and breath is not something you waste at this altitude, in this cold, in this wind.

They clear the moss from the streets. This is the central relationship of their lives — not their relationship with each other, which is steady and quiet and good, but their relationship with the moss, which is steady and quiet and *complicated*. The moss comes. It always comes. It finds the cracks between the fitted stones, the gaps in the copper fittings, the places where the worked surface of the city meets the raw surface of the plain. It comes in thin threads at first, barely visible, a green fuzz on the dark stone, and then it thickens, and then it spreads, and then the streets are soft underfoot again and the channels are clogged and the fitted stones are loosening, pushed apart by the slow hydraulic pressure of growth. The people clear it. They scrape it out with their chisels, they pull it up in sheets, they carry it in baskets back to the edge of the city and set it down on the plain, where it takes root again within days, indistinguishable from the moss that was never disturbed. And then they go back to their houses and their fungus-chambers and their bread-making and their tool-repairing and they wait, and the moss comes again, and they clear it again, and this has been happening for as long as anyone can remember, and no one calls it a war, because a war has a winner, and this has no winner, this is a *conversation*, conducted in the language of persistence, and both parties are fluent.

---

But the people are not the only ones who live here. The people are not even the most numerous.

The moss lives here. The moss has always lived here. It is the oldest citizen of the plain, older than the cities, older than the people, older than the memory of people, older perhaps than the wearing-down of the mountains. It grows on the bare stone without soil, without substrate, without any of the things that moss is supposed to need, feeding on whatever the stone offers — which is almost nothing, which is *enough*, which has always been enough. Each patch is a meter or two across, separated from the next by ten or twenty or fifty meters of dark stone, and each patch is a world unto itself, a dense mat of green-black life, soft and wet and cold and *breathing* — you can feel it breathe, you can put your hand on it and feel the slow pulse of metabolism, the patient conversion of light and mineral and moisture into more of itself, more moss, more life, more of the same quiet green that has been spreading across this plain since before there were eyes to see it.

The moss does not think. Probably. But the moss *wants*. It wants the cracks in the streets. It wants the channels where the water runs. It wants the spaces between things, the margins, the edges, the gaps. It wants the north-facing walls where the dampness lingers. It wants the roofs of the low houses where the pewter light pools and the rain — the upward rain, the sideways rain, the rain that comes when it decides to come — finds purchase. The moss is not aggressive. The moss is not hostile. The moss is simply *present*, in the way that the plain is present, in the way that the stone is present, and its presence exerts a pressure that is not physical but is felt nonetheless — a gentle, green, inexorable pressure, a patient insistence on being, on continuing, on filling every space that is offered to it and then offering itself to every space that remains. The people feel this pressure. They have always felt it. It is the pressure that sends them out with their chisels every morning, that keeps their tools sharp, that gives their lives their shape — not the shape of opposition, not the shape of struggle, but the shape of a long, slow, mutual accommodation, a détente between species, between kingdoms, between the quick and the patient, the warm and the cold, the thing that moves and the thing that waits.

And the moss will outlast them. This is known. This is not spoken of, but it is known. The moss will outlast the people and the cities and the tools and the bread and the language with its flat, careful cadences. The moss will be here when the streets are gone and the walls are gone and the fitted stones have cracked and shifted and been absorbed back into the plain, indistinguishable from the gneiss they came from. The moss will be here, growing on the bare stone, breathing its slow breath, feeding on light and mineral and moisture, continuing. The moss always continues.

---

And the water lives here.

The water in the pools is not ordinary water. It does not fall from the sky — or it does, sometimes, but not in the way that water usually falls. It *arrives*. It condenses out of the air in places where the air has become tired, where the molecular bonds have relaxed their grip and let hydrogen and oxygen find each other in the space between one moment and the next. You can see it happening if you watch long enough — a faint mist forming on nothing, on the bare air above the stone, coalescing, beading, running downhill along invisible grades to the nearest pool. The pools are perfectly round, some of them, as if pressed into the stone by a finger, and they are black and still and very cold, and their bottoms are not stone but something *like* stone, something that moves at a speed below the threshold of perception, a slow tectonic breathing that suggests the plain is not as flat as it seems, that there are depths here, that the basement of the world has a basement of its own.

The water does not overflow. The water does not empty. The water simply *is*, the way old things are, with a permanence that has nothing to do with stasis and everything to do with *patience* — the patience of a thing that has all the time there is, which is the one resource the plain has in abundance.

And the pools show you things.

Not reflections. The water is too still and too dark for ordinary reflections, and what it shows you is not behind you but *elsewhere*. The people of Ur-Maren do not look into the pools if they can help it. When they must — when a new pool has appeared in the street and needs to be mapped, when the channels need clearing and the water must be followed to its source — they look quickly and look away and do not speak of what they have seen. But they have seen. And what they have seen, they carry with them, the way they carry the cold in their joints and the thin air in their lungs — not as a burden but as a *knowledge*, a thing that has entered them and changed them and cannot be removed.

What lives in the pools? Or beyond the pools? Or *through* the pools? The people do not ask. But there are answers, if you are willing to look — and if you are willing to look, you will see, in the black water, a different plain under a different sky, a sky that is not pewter but is something else, something that has a color you don't have a name for, a sky that moves differently, a sky that has weather, *real* weather, clouds that commit, clouds that build and break and weep. And the plain in the pool is not empty. There are shapes on it — shapes that might be trees, shapes that might be animals, shapes that might be *people*, people who are not wrapped in layers, people who move quickly, people who make sounds that are not flat, people who laugh and shout and sing and cry and do all the extravagant things that the people of Ur-Maren have forgotten how to do, or have chosen to forget, or have been chosen by the plain to forget. The people in the pools look up, sometimes. They look up and they seem to see you, or seem to see something where you are, and their faces hold an expression that you cannot read — longing, perhaps, or pity, or simply *curiosity*, the curiosity of the living for the strange, the far, the half-glimpsed, the almost-known.

What lives in the pools? The pools themselves, perhaps. The pools are alive in the way that the plain is alive — not with metabolism, not with growth, but with *presence*, with a being-there that is more than existing, that is a kind of watching, a kind of waiting. The pools have been here longer than the moss. The pools were here before the mountains were worn away, before the stone was planed flat, before the continent had a basement to expose. The pools were here when the world was young, and they will be here when the world is old, and they will show you things, if you let them — things you cannot unsee, things you cannot speak, things that will settle into you the way the cold settles, into your joints, your teeth, the spaces between your thoughts.

---

And beneath the city, beneath the fungus and the pale surfaces that are not stone, in the deep chambers that were found and not dug, there is something else. Something that does not have a name. Something that does not need a name.

The people feel it when they go down to harvest the fungus — a presence, not hostile, not friendly, simply *there*, the way the plain is there, the way the stone is there, but closer, more concentrated, more intentional. The walls of the deep chambers are not gneiss. They are something else — something dark and smooth and faintly warm, something that yields slightly under pressure and then yields no more, something that was *grown* rather than built, or was grown *and* built, or was grown *as* built, the distinction having no meaning in the place or the time or the intention that made these chambers. The chambers branch. They extend far beyond the footprint of the city, far beyond the reach of the people who harvest the fungus, into depths that have never been explored and will never be explored, because the people of Ur-Maren are practical and know their limits and do not go looking for what they are not meant to find. The chambers are not empty. They are not full, either. They are *occupied* — by the fungus, by the dark warm surfaces, by a silence that is different from the silence of the plain, a silence that has *texture*, that has *weight*, that presses against you like water, like the deep water in the pools, water that has never seen light.

Who lives in the deep chambers? No one the people recognize. No one the people *can* recognize, because recognition requires a framework, a category, a box into which the thing can be placed, and the thing in the deep chambers does not fit into any box that the people of Ur-Maren possess. It is not an animal. It is not a plant. It is not a fungus. It is not a mineral. It is something else, something that has been here longer than any of those categories, something that existed before the categories were invented, before the people who invented categories arrived on the plain, before the plain was plain. It is the thing that wore down the mountains. Or it is the thing that *is* the mountains, the ghost of the mountains, the memory of the mountains, the root-structure that remains when everything above has been taken away. It is the basement of the world, and it is aware. Not aware the way a person is aware — not conscious, not thinking, not planning. Aware the way a pool is aware. Aware the way the plain is aware. Aware in the oldest sense of the word: *present*, *attentive*, *waiting*.

It does not harm the people. It has never harmed the people. It tolerates them the way the plain tolerates them — with patience, with indifference, with the vast, impersonal acceptance of something that has been here for ten million years and will be here for ten million more and does not distinguish between the brief, warm, quick things that walk on its surface and the slow, cold, patient things that grow there. The people are guests in its house. The fungus is its gift, or its indifference, or its accident. The people take it and eat it and live, and they do not ask where it comes from or what it is rooted in or what would happen if they took too much or went too deep or stayed too long. They know, without being told, that there are limits. They know, without knowing how they know, where the limits are. This is not wisdom. This is *instinct* — the instinct of a species that has lived in a place long enough to have absorbed its rules into its blood, its bones, its silence.

---

And the wind lives here. The wind from the east, constant, carrying the smell of the plain itself — not stone, not water, not moss, but something else, something that is the smell of *time*, the smell of a place that has been around so long that it has developed an odor the way a person develops a personality, slowly, incrementally, out of the accumulation of ten million small events. The wind has been blowing since the mountains fell. The wind will be blowing after the cities are gone. The wind is the breath of the plain, and it carries with it the plain's awareness, the plain's attention, the plain's patient, impersonal regard for the things that live on its surface. The wind knows the people. The wind knows the moss. The wind knows the pools and the deep chambers and the light that shifts and the rain that falls upward. The wind knows everything, and the wind tells nothing, and the wind continues, from the east, always from the east, carrying the smell of the oldest place in the world to the people who live on its surface and wrap themselves tighter and clear their channels and eat their bread and go down into the dark and come back up and do it all again, day after day, in the grey light, in the cold, in the silence that is not silence at all but the sound of a world that has worn itself down to its essence.

---

And the plain itself lives here. This is the oldest inhabitant, the deepest presence, the thing that contains all the others and is contained by none of them. The plain is not a landscape. The plain is an *entity* — not conscious, not alive in any way that the people of Ur-Maren would recognize as life, but *present*, *aware*, *intentional* in the way that a process is intentional, the way that erosion is intentional, the way that the slow wearing-down of a mountain range into a flat dark plain is a kind of purpose, a kind of will. The plain wanted to be flat. The plain spent ten million years making itself flat, grinding down the mountains, planing the stone, exposing the basement, and now it is flat and it is *satisfied*, not in the way that a person is satisfied but in the way that a thing is satisfied when it has become what it was always going to be. The plain is complete. The plain is finished. The plain is the oldest finished thing in the world, and it is *still finishing*, still grinding, still wearing, still breathing its slow tectonic breath, still condensing water from tired air, still shifting its light, still showing its visions in its pools, still waiting in its deep chambers.

The plain waits for nothing. The plain waits for no one. The plain simply *waits*, the way that stone waits, the way that time waits, the way that the space between one moment and the next waits for something to fill it. And the people of Ur-Maren fill it, briefly, with their lives and their labor and their bread and their silence. And the moss fills it, patiently, with its green, growing, breathing persistence. And the water fills it, blackly, with its visions and its depths and its cold. And the thing in the deep chambers fills it, darkly, with its warmth and its silence and its age. And the wind fills it, constantly, with the smell of everything that has ever been here, the smell of ten million years of wearing-down, the smell of a world that has given up everything except itself.

And all of it — the people, the moss, the water, the wind, the thing in the deep chambers, the plain itself — all of it lives here, all of it continues here, all of it is *of* this place in a way that nothing else in the world is of anywhere. They are not separate. They are not distinct. They are layers of the same thing, expressions of the same will, the same patience, the same ancient, grinding, continuing purpose. They are the plain, and the plain is them, and together they make up the oldest, quietest, most enduring community in the world — a community without leaders, without followers, without conflict, without resolution, without end.

The moss will grow back. The moss always grows back.
