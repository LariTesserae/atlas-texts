# The Inhabitants of the Smoldering Heights

---

## The Mats

They are the first thing you notice, because they are the first thing you touch. Your hand comes away stained—not with color exactly, but with *presence*, a residue that is part moisture, part slime, part something finer than either, a living film that clings to your skin and takes hours to dry. If you press your fingers into the mat, it yields. Not like soil. Like flesh. Like something that has thickness and warmth and a slow pulse you can feel if you hold still long enough.

The mats are the foundation. They are what happens when the stone decides to become something else.

They grow where the seepage comes closest to the surface—those wide, flat expanses where the rock is perpetually slick and the mist hangs in the air like a held breath. The mats are not a single organism. They are a consortium, a collaboration between things that are fungal and things that are bacterial and things that are neither, layered in strata that record decades or centuries of growth. The surface is the youngest: a dark, almost black skin that is smooth and slightly rubbery, interrupted by pores that open and close with a rhythm too slow to watch but visible if you mark them and return hours later. Below that, the mat deepens into colors—deep reds, purples, burgundies—each band a different consortium, a different chemistry, a different relationship with the stone beneath. And below that, at the bottom, where the mat meets the rock, there is a zone of pure digestion, where the root-like structures have penetrated so deeply into the stone that the boundary between mat and rock becomes theoretical, becomes a gradient rather than a line. The mat is eating the stone. Slowly, slowly, over spans of time that make the word "patience" feel adolescent. It is dissolving the minerals, extracting what it needs, and the waste products of that dissolution are the chemistry that feeds the next layer up, and the next, and the next.

In some places the mats are ancient. Meters thick. And they have built their own topography—hummocks and ridges and valleys, a landscape in miniature, rising and falling across the surface of the stone in patterns that follow the hidden water below. The hummocks are warmer than the valleys. The hummocks are where the mat is thickest, where the metabolic activity is most concentrated, where the digestion of the stone is most vigorous. The valleys are where water collects, where the mat is thinner, where the consortium shifts toward species that tolerate immersion. And on the boundaries between hummock and valley, where the gradients are steepest, the mat is most diverse, most active, most *alive*—a frenzy of chemistry and growth that is almost visible, a shimmer of heat and moisture and metabolic urgency.

The mats smell. Not badly. The smell is mineral, organic, complex—like wet stone and old blood and something sweeter underneath, something almost floral, the smell of processes you don't have words for because they happen in darkness and in warmth and in the slow, patient digestion of rock by life.

## The Grazers

They are the first animals you see—or rather, the first animals you *detect*, because seeing is not quite the right word for how you encounter them. Your eyes are mostly useless here. You detect them by sound: a faint, rhythmic scraping, like a brush being drawn across a rough surface, repeated and repeated and repeated, and then a pause, and then the sound of movement, and then the scraping again. You detect them by touch: the vibration in the stone, a faint tremor that travels through the rock and up through the soles of your feet. And you detect them by smell: a sharp, ammoniac odor that cuts through the mineral smell of the mats, the unmistakable signature of concentrated waste.

They are low-slung. Six legs, arranged in three pairs, the front pair shorter than the rear, giving their bodies a profile that slopes upward from front to back, like a sled. Their bodies are flattened—dorsoventrally compressed, pressed close to the mat—and covered in a skin that is smooth and dark, almost black, with a faint iridescence that you can only see when your face is very close and you angle your head to catch the faint glow from a nearby pool. Their heads are broad and flat, wider than their bodies, and their mouths are on the underside, hidden from above, a wide, rasping structure that works side to side like a file, scraping the surface of the mat and drawing the loosened material in. They have no visible eyes. They navigate by touch—by the long, mobile antennae that sweep the mat ahead of them in slow, arcing movements—and by the chemical signatures in the air and on the mat surface, reading the landscape the way you might read braille, with a sensitivity that is almost unimaginable.

They move in loose aggregations. Not herds, exactly—there is no leader, no coordination, no apparent communication between individuals. They simply move in the same direction, for a time, following the same gradients of warmth and moisture and mat density, and then something shifts—a difference in the mat chemistry, a change in the temperature of the stone—and they begin to disperse, each individual following its own path, and the aggregation dissolves.

The grazing is selective. You can see this if you watch long enough—and you will watch long enough, because there is nothing else to do, and the quiet is so deep that even this small activity becomes compelling. The grazers do not eat everything. They move across the mat in patterns that are not random—they follow the hummocks, avoiding the valleys. They linger in certain areas, scraping deeply, and pass quickly over others, barely touching the surface. They are selecting for something—a particular species in the mat consortium, a particular chemical signature, a particular stage of growth—and what they leave behind is not what they don't want but what they *do* want it to become, because their grazing stimulates growth, prunes the mat, keeps it at the stage of succession that is most productive, most nutritious, most useful.

And their waste. Their waste is the product. They defecate in specific places—not randomly, not wherever they happen to be, but in specific places, at the edges of the mat, where the grazing trails converge, where the stone is bare. The waste accumulates. It is rich in concentrated minerals—iron, copper, manganese—that the grazers have extracted from the mat and from the stone beneath it, that have passed through their bodies and been chemically transformed, oxidized or reduced or complexed into forms that are more soluble, more mobile, more *available* than they were in the raw rock. The waste piles are small—no larger than a fist, no taller than a finger—but they are numerous, and they are ancient, and they are the raw material for everything else.

## The Predators

You will not see a predator for a long time. You may never see one at all. You will see their effects.

The grazers are not afraid. Fear is a response to a threat that can be seen or heard or smelled from a distance, and the predators of the Heights do not announce themselves. They are ambush hunters of a purity that is almost abstract—they do not pursue, do not chase, do not stalk. They *wait*. They find a place where the grazers pass—a convergence of trails, a narrow channel between two hummocks, a spot where the mat is particularly dense and the scraping sounds of the grazers are loud and frequent—and they settle into the stone and they become stone. Their skin changes color, changes texture, matches the rock so perfectly that even if you know where to look you cannot find them. They fold their limbs beneath them. They lower their metabolisms. They stop breathing, or they breathe so slowly and so shallowly that the movement is undetectable. And they wait.

They have a heat sense. Not vision—not a image-forming sense—but a sensitivity to infrared radiation, to the warmth of living bodies against the background warmth of the stone, that allows them to detect a grazer at a distance of several body lengths. They feel the grazer's warmth the way you might feel the heat of a fire across a room: not as a picture, but as a presence, a direction, an intensity. And when the grazer is close enough—when its warmth fills the predator's sensory field—the predator moves.

The movement is explosive. There is no other word for it. From absolute stillness to full-speed strike in a fraction of a second, the predator unfolds, extends its forelimbs—those terrible forelimbs, longer than the rest, jointed in too many places, ending in structures that are part hand, part jaw, part something that has no analog in the sunlit world—and grasps the grazer and pulls it in and the mouth, which is large and opens wide and is lined with serrated plates rather than teeth, closes and the grazer is dead or dying and the predator is feeding.

But the feeding is not the point. Or rather, it is a point, but not the only point, and perhaps not the most important one. The predators are not numerous—the system cannot support numerous predators—and they do not need to kill often. A single grazer provides enough energy and nutrients to sustain a predator for days, sometimes weeks. And when they are not hungry, they still hunt. They kill grazers and they do not eat them, or they eat part of them and leave the rest, and the carcass is consumed by the decomposers in the mat, and the minerals return to the mat, and the cycle continues.

The predators are managing. That is the word that keeps coming to you, the word that feels wrong because it implies consciousness and intention and these animals are neither conscious nor intentional, but it is the only word that fits. They are managing the grazer population. They kill the weak, the slow, the ones that graze in the wrong places, the ones that wander too far from the aggregations. They kill in patterns—more in some areas, less in others—that correspond to the productivity of the mat, that keep the grazer density at the level that maximizes the harvest without destroying the resource. They are doing this because their hunting behavior has been shaped by natural selection to produce this outcome, because predators that hunted this way left more descendants than predators that hunted differently, because the system has been tuning itself for so long that the behavior and the outcome are perfectly aligned, and the result is a population of grazers that is healthy and productive and sustainable, and a population of predators that is small and efficient and perfectly adapted, and a mat that is grazed at the optimal intensity, and waste that is deposited in the optimal places, and the whole thing *works* like a machine that no one built.

## The Swimmers

The rivers are invisible for the most part—you feel them before you hear them, a vibration in the stone, a coolness in the air—and the swimmers are invisible entirely, until they choose otherwise.

They are elongated. Laterally compressed, thin as a hand, with bodies that are translucent in their youth and increasingly opaque with age, the opacity spreading from the core outward like a stain, like ink diffusing through water, until in the oldest individuals the body is a deep, uniform black that is indistinguishable from the water itself. They have fins—long, continuous fins that run the length of their bodies, dorsal and ventral, that undulate in waves so fine and so rapid that the fish appears to flow rather than swim, to move through the water the way smoke moves through air, without effort, without visible propulsion.

They are warm-blooded. This is unusual, and it matters. The water in the channels is warm but not uniformly so—there are gradients, places where the water rising from below is hotter, places where the water near the surface is cooler, places where two currents meet and create a boundary that is sharp enough to feel against your hand. The swimmers regulate their body temperature internally, and they move through the water not randomly but along these thermal gradients, seeking the temperatures that suit their life stage, their activity, their metabolic needs. The young stay in the warmer water, near the bottom, near the heat source. The adults range more widely. And when it is time to spawn, they gather in specific places—the spawning beds, the places where the mineral deposits from the grazers' waste accumulate—and the water there is exactly the right temperature, and the chemistry is exactly right, and the spawning happens.

The bioluminescence is faint. Blue-green, the color of deep-sea creatures in sunlit worlds, but here the color is not a response to sunlight—there is no sunlight—it is a product of the swimmers' own chemistry, produced in specialized organs along their flanks, controlled by their nervous systems, modulated by their physiological state. The young glow steadily, a faint, constant light that marks their position in the dark water. The adults pulse—patterns of light and dark that vary in frequency and intensity and duration, that carry information the way a bird's song carries information, except that the information is not "I am here, this is my territory" but something more complex, more nuanced, something that has to do with readiness, with identity, with the state of the body and the state of the water and the state of the minerals in the spawning bed. The spawning beds, when the adults gather, are fields of light—dozens or hundreds of individuals pulsing in patterns that synchronize and desynchronize and synchronize again, a display that is also a communication, a communication that is also a *display*, a showing of presence and vitality and genetic quality that is beautiful in a way that has nothing to do with beauty and everything to do with function, because the females choose their mates by the quality of the light, by the steadiness and the intensity and the complexity of the pattern, and the males who pulse best spawn most, and the next generation inherits the capacity for light, and the system selects for the signal, and the signal becomes more elaborate, more refined, more *itself*, over generations that number in the thousands or the millions.

And the spawning itself is the industry. The swimmers filter the water as they feed—they strain it through fine structures in their mouths, extracting dissolved minerals, concentrating them in their bodies, and what they excrete is not waste but *product*: mineral deposits of extraordinary purity, laid down in the spawning beds, building up layer by layer, season by season, year by year, century by century. The spawning beds are the refineries. The swimmers are the refiners. And the product—concentrated copper sulfides, rare earth oxides, manganese nodules—accumulates in forms that are, if you know what you are looking at, *useful*.

## The Pale Growths

In the deepest channels, where the water is warmest and the currents are slowest, there are structures that rise from the riverbed like folded hands, like elongated bells, like the pipes of an organ built for a cathedral that has no walls and no roof and no worshippers. They are pale—white, cream, the faintest yellow—and they are translucent, and they are warm, and they pulse.

They are alive. But they are not plants, and they are not animals, and they are not fungi, and they are not any of the categories that a sunlit-world biologist would reach for. They are something else. A kingdom unto themselves, perhaps. Or a consortium, like the mats, but organized differently, structured differently, operating on different principles.

They are anchored to the stone by structures that are part root, part holdfast, part cement—a mineralized base that glues them to the riverbed and extends into the stone, extracting warmth, extracting dissolved minerals, extracting the energy that drives their chemistry. Above the base, the body rises: a central stalk, hollow, flexible, surrounded by folds or pleats or fronds that increase the surface area, that interface with the water, that do the work of filtration and extraction and transformation. The stalk is warm to the touch—warmer than the water, warmer than the stone—because the chemistry inside it is exothermic, releases heat as a byproduct, and the heat keeps the internal fluids warm and mobile and reactive.

The pulse is the movement of fluid inside the stalk. A slow, rhythmic contraction—once every few seconds, or once every few minutes, depending on the size of the organism and the temperature of the water—that pushes fluid up from the base and into the folds, where it is exposed to the water, where the chemistry happens, and then the fluid drains back down, and the cycle repeats. The pulse is not a heartbeat—there is no heart, no circulatory system in the way that an animal has a circulatory system—it is more like breathing, like the slow inflation and deflation of a bellows, a movement that drives exchange, that brings the inside and the outside into contact, that does the work of living without muscles or nerves or a brain.

They are doing chemistry. They are filtering the water, extracting specific elements—sulfur, selenium, arsenic, elements that are toxic to most sunlit-world life but that these organisms require, that they incorporate into their tissues, that they transform into compounds that serve as catalysts, as energy storage, as structural materials. And they are releasing other compounds—oxidized sulfur, reduced iron—into the water, changing the chemistry of the river, creating conditions that favor other organisms, that precipitate other minerals, that build the deposits that accumulate in the channels.

Some of them are as tall as a person. Some are no taller than your finger. The tall ones are old—centuries old, perhaps, or millennia, their stalks thickening and their folds deepening as they grow, their bases extending deeper and deeper into the stone, tapping into warmer and warmer sources, into richer and richer mineral streams. The short ones are young, newly settled, just beginning the slow work of growth and chemistry and transformation.

And they are not passive. They respond to the water—to its temperature, its chemistry, its flow rate—by adjusting their pulse, by changing the permeability of their folds, by altering the rate of their chemistry. They respond to each other—when two growths are close together, their pulses synchronize, falling into a shared rhythm that is more efficient, that moves the water between them in ways that enhance the exchange. They respond to the swimmers—when the swimmers come to spawn, the growths change their chemistry, release compounds that trigger the spawning, that signal the water is right, the time is right, the conditions are right. They are part of the system. They are a component in the factory. They are the processing vats, the reaction chambers, the slow and patient engines of transformation.

## The Large Ones

You will never see one. Not fully. Not clearly. You will see evidence.

The paths. In the damp fields, in the regions where the mats are thickest and the stone is worn smooth, there are paths—grooves, really, channels carved into the mat and into the stone beneath it, meters wide, centimeters deep, running in straight lines or gentle curves for distances that are impossible to measure because they begin in darkness and end in darkness and you cannot see far enough to trace them. The paths are polished. The stone in them is smooth as glass, and warm—warmer than the surrounding stone, warmer than the mats, warm the way a living body is warm—and the mats grow differently along the edges of the paths, thicker, denser, a different consortium, as though the passage of the large ones changes the chemistry of the stone, changes the seepage, changes the conditions for growth.

The gouges. In the mineral columns—those enormous, banded structures that rise from the floor of the great channels like the pillars of a cathedral—there are marks. Deep, parallel grooves, spaced meters apart, that score the surface of the columns and expose the fresh stone beneath, the bright colors of newly deposited mineral that have not yet been darkened by time and moisture. The grooves are old. The columns are still growing, and the edges of the grooves are already being coated with new mineral, already being softened and rounded by the slow accumulation of deposit. But the grooves are deep enough, recent enough, to tell you that something passed this way, something massive, something with appendages or projections or *parts* that are hard enough to score stone that has been accumulating for millennia.

The resting places. In the deepest channels, where the water is warmest and the stone is thinnest and the heat from below is a physical pressure that you can feel against your face like an open oven, there are depressions. Vast, smooth depressions in the stone, tens of meters across, shaped like shallow bowls, polished to a finish that is almost mirror-like. The depressions are warm—hot, almost, hot enough to make the water above them shimmer, hot enough to make the air above the water ripple and distort. And they are worn. Not by water, not by time, but by *contact*, by the repeated pressing of something enormous and heavy against the stone, over and over, in the same place, for a long time. The depressions have a shape. They are not random. They are the imprint of a body, the negative space of something that rested here and pressed the stone into a mold of itself and then moved on and returned and rested again and pressed the stone deeper and smoother and the stone remembered.

The large ones move through the deepest channels. They are in the water, or they are *of* the water—they are so large and so integrated with the water and the stone and the heat that the boundary between the animal and the environment becomes unclear. They are warm. They generate heat—not just metabolically, not just as a byproduct of living, but *geologically*, as a consequence of their chemistry, as a product of the reactions happening in their bodies, reactions that involve the minerals they consume, the water they process, the energy they extract from the heat gradient between the deep stone and the surface water. They are living reactors. They are furnaces. They are the heavy industry of the Heights.

Their waste. Their waste is vast. It accumulates in the deepest channels, in the places where they rest, in the warm water above the polished depressions. It is not feces, not urine, not any kind of waste that a sunlit-world animal would produce. It is *slurry*—a thick, mineral-rich suspension of processed stone, chemically altered, biologically transformed, deposited in layers that build up over time into deposits of extraordinary chemical complexity and extraordinary practical value. The slurry contains rare earth elements in concentrations that would take a sunlit-world refinery enormous energy to achieve. It contains sulfides and oxides and native metals in forms that are directly usable, directly *refined*, the product of a process that is biological and geological and chemical all at once, that happens at temperatures and pressures that no sunlit-world factory could economically reproduce.

They are old. The evidence of age is everywhere—in the depth of the paths, in the accumulation of the waste deposits, in the sheer *patience* of their movements, which you can infer from the worn stone even if you never see them move. They are slow. They are deliberate. They are part of the system in the way that a river is part of a landscape—not separate from it but *of* it, not acting upon it but *expressing* it, a process rather than a creature, a function rather than a being, and yet alive, alive in the way that the mats are alive, in the way that the stone itself might be alive if you define life broadly enough and wait long enough and listen carefully enough in the dark.

## The System

This is what lives in the Smoldering Heights. Not a collection of species. Not an ecosystem in the way that word is usually meant—a community of organisms interacting with each other and with their environment. It is a single thing. A single, integrated, self-sustaining, self-regulating process that produces, refines, manufactures, distributes, and recycles, that takes raw materials from the stone and energy from the heat and labor from the bodies of living things, and transforms them into products that accumulate in forms that are useful, that are concentrated, that are *pure*.

The grazers harvest. The predators manage. The swimmers refine. The pale growths process. The large ones smelt. And the mats hold it all together, digest the stone, feed the grazers, filter the water, build the substrate that everything else depends on.

No one designed this. No one built this. No one runs this. It runs itself. It has been running itself for longer than you can imagine—longer than the oldest sunlit-world forest, longer than the oldest coral reef, longer than any ecosystem that has ever existed in the light. It has been running in the dark, in the warmth, in the damp, in the silence, doing what it does with the quiet confidence of a system that has found its equilibrium and will maintain it forever, or until the heat from below fails, or until the stone runs out, or until something changes that is larger and slower and more patient even than this.

And it is not conscious. It is not aware. It does not know what it is doing, because it is not *doing* anything—it is simply *being*, and the being has consequences, and the consequences are industry, and the industry is life, and the life is a process that has been happening for so long that the happening has become the thing itself, indistinguishable from the stone and the water and the warmth and the dark, a living factory the size of a continent, humming quietly in the vast and ancient deep.
