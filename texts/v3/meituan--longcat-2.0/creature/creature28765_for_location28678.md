It lives in the damp. Not *on* the damp, not *near* the damp — *in* it, the way a thought lives in the back of a mind that has stopped trying to think. The watchers in the shallows do not dwell on the Sallow Plain. They are continuous with it. Their bodies are made of the same mineral-saturated water that presses up through the compacted earth, the same pale, patient substance that has forgotten it was ever anything else.

They are small. The largest of them, a full-grown adult at the end of a long and quiet life, would fit on your palm with room to spare — but "fit" is the wrong word, because nothing about them suggests containment. They are built for emergence. Every line of their body speaks of the moment they break the surface: the enormous hind legs, folded tight as clockwork, loaded with the kind of tension that makes the ground itself seem to hold its breath; the eyes, set on top of the head like twin periscopes, bulbous and dark and glistening, designed to ride the meniscus of a pool and see the sky without exposing anything else. They are watchers. That is what they do. That is what they are.

Their skin is the color of the plain — that particular grey-brown that is not quite either, the hue of wet earth that has been worked and reworked by water until all the brightness has been leached out of it. Smooth, always smooth, because roughness is a luxury of dry places, and there is no dryness here for them to afford it. The skin is moist in the way that stone is moist in a cave — not wet, exactly, but never quite not-wet either, a thin film of humidity that clings to them like a memory of the water table from which they came. When the light catches them at the right angle, you can see faint patterns beneath the surface: darker lines, almost like the veins of a leaf, tracing paths across their flanks that might be vessels, or might be something else entirely. Something older. Something that maps, in some indecipherable way, the hidden hydrology of the plain itself.

They breathe through their skin. Of course they do. There is no need for lungs in a world where the air is so heavy with moisture that it is practically a liquid, where the boundary between water and atmosphere has grown soft and permissive. Oxygen dissolves into that thin film of dampness on their flanks and passes directly into their blood, and carbon dioxide passes out, and the exchange is so quiet, so perfectly calibrated to the pace of this place, that they do not need to move to sustain it. They can sit for hours — days, even — without a single visible motion, just their eyes riding the surface of a pool, watching.

And what do they watch for?

Everything. Nothing. The plain.

The dragonflies are the most dramatic thing that moves through their world, and the watchers track them with the patient, mechanical focus of a security system that has never once detected a threat. The dragonflies are not predators of the watchers — the watchers are too small, too quick, too deeply embedded in the damp earth for the dragonflies' aerial hunting to be a meaningful danger. But the dragonflies are *indicators*. Where the dragonflies gather, the midges are thick. Where the midges are thick, the water film is rich. Where the water film is rich, the microorganisms are blooming. And where the microorganisms bloom, the nutrients rise, and the sedges send out new roots, and the ground becomes, by the standards of the Sallow Plain, *lush*. The watchers eat the midges. Not the adults — they are not built for aerial pursuit — but the larvae, the wriggling, thread-thin grubs that hang just below the surface of the pools, suspended in the iridescent film like ornaments in a shop window. The watchers extend a tongue — short, sticky, surprisingly fast for a creature otherwise defined by its stillness — and the larvae vanish, and the watchers return to watching.

But this is only their surface life. This is only the life you can see, if you know to look, if you are patient enough to stand motionless in the warm, humming air and let your eyes adjust to the scale of the plain. The watchers' true life happens below.

They burrow. They burrow the way the plain itself erodes — slowly, persistently, without drama. Their hind legs, those great coiled springs that can launch them a dozen body lengths in a single explosive instant, are also digging tools, and they use them to excavate tunnels in the damp earth just below the mineral crust. The tunnels are narrow — no wider than a finger — and they run horizontally, just below the surface, connecting one pool to the next in a network that can extend for dozens of yards. You cannot see these tunnels from above. They are hidden beneath the pale, compacted earth, in the zone where the ground is neither quite solid nor quite liquid, where the water table seeps and pools in the interstitial spaces between grains of mineral. This is the watchers' highway system, their communication network, their home.

They are not social, exactly. They do not gather in groups, do not cooperate, do not build shared structures. But the tunnels intersect, and at the intersections, the watchers encounter one another, and something passes between them — not touch, not sound, but something. A chemical signature. A taste of the water on each other's skin. Information about where the midges are thick, where the ground is firm, where the water has risen or fallen by the fraction of an inch that means the difference between abundance and scarcity in this spare world. They do not acknowledge these encounters. They simply absorb the data and move on, and the plain adjusts itself by imperceptible degrees in response.

When the water table rises — and it does rise, slowly, seasonally, in response to rains that fall somewhere beyond the horizon, somewhere outside the plain's vast, flat reach — the watchers rise with it. They surface from their tunnels through the neat, lip-ringed holes that pock the earth between the pools, and they sit at the edges of the new water, and they watch. The rising water brings new nutrients, new microorganisms, new larvae. It also brings the heron-birds, standing motionless on their long, thin legs, coiled and waiting. The watchers know the heron-birds the way they know the dragonflies — not as threats, exactly, but as *indicators*, as features of the landscape to be read and responded to. When a heron-bird stands in a particular pool, the watchers stay in their tunnels. When the heron-bird moves on, they surface.

This is the rhythm of their lives: a rhythm of watching and waiting, of rising and retreating, of explosive movement followed by long stillness. They are creatures of the threshold, the boundary layer between water and earth, between surface and depth, between the seen and the unseen. They exist in the place where the plain is most itself — that thin, damp, living interface where mineral becomes organic, where water becomes life, where the slow erosion of the land produces, paradoxically, an abundance of small, brief, watchful beings.

They do not live long. A single season, perhaps two — it is hard to say, because no one has stayed on the plain long enough to count their generations, and the watchers do not leave traces that persist beyond the season that made them. When they die, they sink into the damp earth, and the microorganisms that film the pools take them apart, and their substance seeps back into the water table, and the water table rises, and new watchers emerge from the tunnels, and the watching continues.

But here is the thing about the watchers, the thing that makes them more than just another small, obscure inhabitant of an overlooked landscape: they are aware. Not in the way that you or I are aware — not with narrative, not with memory, not with the anxious, forward-leaning consciousness of a creature that knows it will die. They are aware the way the plain is aware. Which is to say, they perceive. They register. They respond. Their periscope eyes track the movement of every dragonfly, every heron-bird, every shift in the light as the clouds pass overhead. Their skin registers every fluctuation in the water table, every change in the mineral content of the damp earth, every tremor of the burrowing life that moves around them in the tunnels. They are, in a very real sense, the plain's sensory organs — the means by which the Sallow Plain knows itself.

And what does it know?

That it is warm. That it is wet. That the water moves, slowly, through the compacted earth, and the sedges grow in their tight, patient rosettes, and the dragonflies hunt the midges, and the heron-birds stand and strike and coil back into stillness, and the microorganisms film the still water with their iridescent sheen, and the iron-rich stone knobs jut from the plain like the knuckles of a buried hand, softening by fractions, by imperceptible degrees, over millennia that the plain does not count because counting implies an end, and there is no end here, only the watching, only the rising and falling of the water table by inches, only the slow, warm, wet, patient business of being what remains when everything dramatic has been taken away.

The watchers know all of this. They know it the way you know the weight of your own body in a chair — not as information, but as presence. They are the plain's consciousness of itself, distributed across a thousand small, moist, watchful bodies, each one riding the surface of a pool with its eyes above the waterline, each one waiting for the next dragonfly, the next rise in the water, the next moment of explosive movement that will send it bolting back into the earth, back into the damp, back into the dark and the quiet and the slow, slow time of the Sallow Plain.
