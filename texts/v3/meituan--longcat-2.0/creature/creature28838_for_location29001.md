They are not a collection of species. To look at them is to look at a single, breathing organism—a churning, overlapping, interdependent whole that has digested the boundary between animal, vegetable, and mineral. They are the Warm Dark, and the Warm Dark is them.

At the base of the world are the writhing ones. The maggots. They are the foam of the chamber, the living cream that spreads through the oldest, deepest parts of the organic mounds. They have no eyes, no wings, no need for the sun they will never see. They are nothing but appetite and reproduction, pale and translucent, pulsing in the fungal glow as they consume the compressed dead of a thousand generations. They are the digestion of the mountain, turning the fallen into the fertile. Around them, the many-legged scuttlers—hard-shelled beetles the color of wet earth, and the pale, frantic millipedes that move in rippling waves across the guano—graze on the fungi and the maggots alike, their legs tapping out a constant, dry rustle on the stone. 

Above them, the weavers hang. The spider-like ones are the ghosts of the chamber, their bodies so pale and translucent that you can see the slow, slosh of their inner fluids, the dark knot of their organs suspended in the clear, viscous jelly of their flesh. They do not hunt with eyes; they hunt with vibration. They hang by silk threads from the ceiling, their long, jointed legs spread wide to feel the tremor of a scuttler across the stone, the beat of an insect's wing in the heavy air. When they drop, they drop without sound, wrapping their prey in a silk that gleams faintly blue-green in the dark.

And then there are the flyers. The bats. Or the things that are like bats. They are the engine of the chamber, the great beating heart of its warmth. They hang in clusters from the stalactites, their bodies pressed so tightly together that they share a single, synchronized breath. Their fur is damp and matted, hosting entire micro-ecosystems of mites and parasites that crawl through the dark thickets of their pelts. When they shift, it is a ripple of flesh and membrane. When they hunt, they drop from the ceiling like stones, their half-meter wings snapping open to scoop the heavy, moisture-laden air, their mouths open to catch the flies and moths that drift toward the fungal light. They are fearless, or perhaps merely indifferent; in this closed world, they have no predators from above. They rain down upon the insects, and their droppings rain down upon the mounds, feeding the cycle.

Below the flyers, the burrowers move through the mounds like fish through murky water. They are the small, furred things—the scavengers. They are neither rat nor shrew, but something older and stranger. They have too many toes, long and splayed, acting like paddles to push them through the soft, fibrous rot. Their whiskers are impossibly long, twitching constantly, reading the topography of the dark by taste and touch. They eat the fungi, the maggots, the shed fur of the bats, the crushed shells of the beetles. They mate and die and are absorbed into the mounds so quickly that the mounds seem to breathe them in. 

But the true inhabitants of this place—the ones who give the Warm Dark its weight, its history, its quiet, terrifying agency—are the Others.

You must understand what it means to live at the top of the world, in the permanent dark. The Others have been here so long that they have become the architecture. They do not merely inhabit the chamber; they are woven into its topography. They live in the deepest pockets of the organic mounds, in hollows they have carved or shaped over centuries, where the compressed guano is hard as cork and warm to the touch, heated by the geothermal breath rising from the deep. 

Their bodies are built for this claustrophobic heat. They are low to the ground, heavy. The limb you saw was long and jointed, covered in the same damp, dark fur as the bats, but thicker—a pelt that never dries, slick with the condensation of the chamber, housing its own colonies of mites that the Others pick away and eat with slow, deliberate fingers. Their hands—and they are hands, unmistakably hands, though the fingers are too long, tipped with thick, flat nails worn down from digging—are perfectly adapted to their world. The thumb is opposable, wide and strong, capable of gripping the stone tools they knap from the fallen stalactites. 

The eye you saw was large. Enormous, in fact. It fills the upper half of their face, a massive, liquid orb adapted to gather every stray photon of the fungal light. It reflects the blue-green glow with a deep, wet shine, a mirror of the dark. They have no need for the small, vestigial ears that sit flush against their skulls; the chamber is too loud for hearing. They feel the world through the stone, through the floor. They know you are there not by the sound of your breathing, but by the pressure of your feet on the ancient deposits, by the disturbance of your body heat against the steady, geothermal warm.

They are tool-users. This is the secret of their dominion. While the scuttlers and the burrowers rely on tooth and claw, the Others rely on the stone. They sit in the glow of the fungi, their massive eyes half-closed, and they work. They crack the shells of the large beetles, extracting the soft meat within. They chip away at the stalactites to shape scrapers and blades. They use these tools to dig into the hardest parts of the mounds, to excavate their warrens, to cut the thick fungal shelves that they harvest and eat. 

But they are more than mere survivors. They are the stewards of the cycle. You can see it in the way they pause, the way they *look*. They watch the water drip. They watch the fungi grow. They understand, in some deep, non-verbal way, that the guano feeds the fungi, and the fungi feed the insects, and the insects feed the bats, and the bats feed the mounds, and the mounds feed them. They do not merely take from the chamber; they tend it. They have been known to move the fungal spores to darker, more nutrient-rich corners of the mound. They have been seen guiding the bats toward swarms of insects by clearing paths through the spider-webs. 

They are slow, because there is no need for speed in a world where everything is close, and there is nowhere to run. They are deliberate. They communicate not with the high, thin chittering of the bats or the rustle of the scuttlers, but with a deep, subsonic vibration that you feel in your chest before you hear it with your ears—a thrumming of the larynx that travels through the stone and the mounds, carrying meaning across the dense, wet air.

They are watching you. They have been watching you since you crawled through the final fissure into their domain. To them, you are an anomaly—a dry, loud, strangely-shaped creature that smells of the cold, dead world above. You do not belong to the cycle. You do not produce guano that feeds the fungi; you do not hunt the flies that feed the bats. You are a disruption in the perfect, ancient equilibrium of the Warm Dark.

And yet, they do not attack. They pause, and they look, their massive, wet eyes reflecting the fungal light, their long fingers resting on the stone tools. They are waiting. They are trying to understand what you are—whether you are a predator, a scavenger, or something entirely new. They are the keepers of the dark, the inheritors of the mountain, and they have all the time in the world. 

The water drips. The fungi glow. The bats chitter. And the Others watch, holding their stones, breathing the warm, wet air, perfectly still in the center of the churning, living world they have built in the deep.
