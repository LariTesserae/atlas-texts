# What the Flats Host

---

You kneel beside a pool. This is the first thing you do — not because you have seen anything, but because the warmth draws you. The pool is small, no wider than your two hands placed side by side, and the water is so clear it barely seems to exist. You have to look for the surface: find the faint meniscus where water meets air, the slight distortion of the pale crust beneath, the way the light bends at the boundary. And then you see them.

Filaments. Threads. The finest imaginable strands of something that is not quite plant and not quite animal and not quite anything you have a word for. They drift. That is the only verb that fits — they do not swim, do not crawl, do not propel themselves with any visible mechanism. They drift, the way dust drifts in a sunbeam, the way smoke drifts in still air, except they are in water, and the water is warm, and the warmth is what moves them: convection currents, the slow circulation of water heated from below, rising in the center of the pool and sinking at the edges. The filaments go where the water goes. They have no say in the matter.

They are pale. Some are white, or nearly white — the color of nothing, the color of something that has never needed pigment. Others carry a faint rose tint, a blush, as though they have been stained by the mineral water they live in. They are so fine that you cannot tell where one ends and another begins. They gather in loose tangles, in wisps, in clouds so diffuse they are more absence than presence — a slight thickening of the water, a texture, a suggestion of something there.

You watch them for a long time. Minutes pass. They do not do anything. They drift. They are moved by the water and they drift. And yet they are alive — you know this the way you know most things about the Calcine Flats, not by seeing it demonstrated but by being told, by having it explained to you in a voice that is patient and precise and does not expect you to fully believe it. They feed on chemistry. They take in dissolved minerals — iron, sulfur, whatever the water carries — and they extract from these the energy they need to maintain themselves, to hold their shape, to continue being filaments in warm water. They do not need light. They do not need oxygen. They do not need anything you would recognize as food. They need only the warmth and the water and the dissolved earth, and the Calcine Flats provide all three in abundance.

There are others in the pool. Smaller things. Specks. You cannot see them as individuals — they are below the threshold of your vision — but you can see their effect: tiny disturbances in the water, minute flickers of movement at the edge of perception, the kind of thing you might dismiss as a trick of the light or a fatigue of the eye. But they are there. They dart. They pause. They dart again. They have purpose, or something that looks like purpose — a direction, a goal, a reason for moving that is their own and not the water's. They are motile in a way the filaments are not. They go where they want to go, or where something inside them — some imperative, some drive, some chemical gradient they are following — tells them to go.

You will never know what they look like. You will never know if they have parts, organs, structures. They are too small. They are the fauna of this place, and they are barely there.

---

And then there is the clicking.

You hear it first as an interruption — a single, sharp sound that cuts through the continuous murmur of the water and is gone before you can locate it. A click. Like a pebble tapped against stone. Like a fingernail against enamel. Like something small and hard striking something else small and hard, once, in the dark beneath your feet.

You stop. You listen. The water murmurs. The warmth rises from the ground. The haze diffuses the light. Nothing clicks.

You walk on. Ten steps. Twenty. And then: another click. Not the same place. Somewhere else. Somewhere to your left, or behind you, or below you — you cannot tell. The sound is too sharp, too brief, too unlocated. It does not echo. It does not reverberate. It simply occurs and then does not occur, and the murmur of the water fills the space where it was.

You stop again. You wait. You listen with your whole body — with your feet on the crust, with your palms open, with your jaw slightly unclenched. You are trying to feel the click before it happens, to sense the moment of its occurrence in the mineral beneath you. But there is nothing. No vibration. No warning. Just the click, when it comes, sudden and isolated and impossible to place.

The question — the question you cannot stop asking, the question the Calcine Flats will not answer — is whether the clicking is alive.
