# The Residents of the Underworks

---

## I. The Gear-Reef

They are not named, the small pale ones, because naming requires a mouth and they have none — only a cluster of appendages near the ventral surface that work the water, taste the mineral film, sort the edible from the not. They have been here longer than the light. Longer than the bulbs, which hang from the ceiling like the rotting fruit of some subterranean tree, yellowing and dimming on a timescale that means nothing to them. The crustaceans do not measure time by light. They measure it by the chemistry of the water, which changes on a scale of years, by the temperature, which changes on a scale of decades, and by the movement of the gears, which is the most reliable clock in their world and which stopped, once, and the stopping nearly killed them all.

This was long ago. Long enough that no living crustacean remembers it directly, though something in their bodies remembers — a stress response, a contraction, that their descendants carry like a scar in the genetic text. The pumps had stalled. The water had stopped moving. The channels in the floor had gone still, and the still water had warmed, and the warmth had killed the algae, and the algae had rotted, and the rot had poisoned the first layer of the reef, and the crustaceans had died in heaps in the dead gear-trains, white bodies piled in the gaps between the teeth like drifts of pale, fragile snow.

But the pumps restarted. Or something restarted them. And the water moved again. And the reef grew back, not in the same pattern — the pattern had been broken — but in a new pattern, one that incorporated the memory of the break as a zone of avoidance, a place where no egg is laid, no larva settles. The dead zone is still there, deep in the reef, a graveyard compressed into the mineral substrate, and the living grow around it the way a tree grows around a nail.

The reef is the crustaceans. The crustaceans are the reef. The distinction, as with the buildings and the stone, has stopped mattering.

They live in the gaps. This is the first principle of their existence: the gap is home. The gap between gear teeth, the gap between frame and flywheel, the gap between the housing and the floor — these are the streets, the alleys, the rooms of their world. The gaps have dimensions, and the dimensions change as the gears move (they move rarely, but they move), and the crustaceans have learned the timing. They feel it in their legs before it arrives — a shift in the vibration of the iron, a change in the way the water presses against the housing — and they retreat to the safe gaps, the ones that do not close, and they wait, and the gear turns, and the gap reshapes, and they emerge into a slightly different world and they rebuild their routes.

They are not intelligent. Not in the way that word is usually meant. But they are *sensitive*. Each one is a small node in a network of touch and taste and vibration, and the network is not conscious but it is *responsive*, and the response is not random. When the water changes, they move. When the temperature drops, they cluster. When the tone sounds from below — that deep, felt vibration that comes from the stone itself — they do not react, but their eggs do. The eggs, which are carried in a brood pouch on the ventral surface, which are translucent and barely visible, which contain a larva curled like a comma — the eggs respond to the tone with a slight acceleration of development, as if the sound is a signal that conditions are right, that the deep systems are functioning, that the place is stable and it is safe to hatch.

This is not communication. The tone is not a message. But it functions as one, and in the Underworks, function is meaning.

They eat the mineral film. They eat the algae that grows in the amber light. They eat each other, sometimes — the dead, the slow, the ones trapped in closing gaps — and this is not cruelty, this is cycling, this is the reef feeding itself. They are small. They are pale. They are the color of the stone, or the color of the water, or the color of nothing at all. They are the dominant life. They are the bones of the place now, the way the iron was the bones once, and they will outlast the iron, and when the iron has rusted to nothing and the gears have collapsed into heaps of orange dust, the reef will still be there, smaller, slower, finding new gaps in new configurations, and the place will still be the Underworks, and it will still be at peace.

## II. The Beetles of the Lower Town

Below the reef, below the lanes, on the floor itself, the beetles move.

They are large by the standards of the place — the length of a thumb, armored in elytra that are the color of wet limestone, dark grey with a faint greenish cast from the mineral deposit that crusts them. They move slowly. Their legs are short and strong, designed not for speed but for *purchase* — the floor is slick, the film of deposited minerals as smooth as glass in places, and the beetles have adapted. Their feet are broad, almost paddle-like, with thousands of tiny setae that grip the surface by molecular adhesion, the same principle that lets a gecko climb a wall. They do not slip. They have never slipped. Slipping is a surface-world problem.

They wear trails. This is the most remarkable thing about them. Over years — over decades, perhaps — the beetles have worn a network of paths in the mineral film, shallow grooves that branch and intersect and form a map of their movement. The map mirrors the lane system above, but it is not a copy. It is a *translation*. Where the lanes are straight, the beetle-paths curve. Where the lanes branch at right angles, the beetle-paths branch at angles that are not right but not quite oblique — angles that correspond to the geometry of the beetle body, to the turning radius of a creature that is wider at the rear than at the front and must swing wide to change direction.

The trails are visible. They catch the amber light differently than the surrounding surface, a slightly different sheen, and if you stand at the edge of the space and look down, you can see the whole network spread out below you like a diagram of something — a circuit board, a root system, the neural map of a very simple brain.

They do not communicate. This has been established. The clicks they produce — that small, dry sound that moves through the Underworks like a presence — are not signals. They are the sound of the elytra closing, of the mandibles working, of the legs moving over the mineral film. The sound is *incidental*. But the effect is not. The clicks arrive at your ear from everywhere and nowhere, and they arrive in clusters and silences, and the clusters correspond to the beetles' activity — more beetles moving, more clicks; fewer beetles moving, fewer silences — and so the sound maps the beetle population in real time, and you can *hear* where they are, and the hearing creates the impression of intention, of coordination, of a distributed intelligence clicking its way through the dark.

There is no intelligence. There are beetles. They are eating — the mineral film, the dead crustaceans that fall from the reef, the fungal spores that drift down from the glowing caps on the walls. They are mating — slowly, carefully, a process that takes hours and involves no display, no ritual, just a gradual alignment of two bodies in a groove-worn trail, a pressing together, a stillness. They are dying — of age, of desiccation if they stray too far from the wet zones, of being crushed by a falling flake of limestone from the ceiling. They are being eaten — by the crustaceans, by the ribbon-things in the pools, by each other. The trail network persists. It is the most permanent thing in the Underworks, more permanent than the buildings, more permanent than the machinery, and it will be here when the beetles are gone, a fossil record of beetle movement, a map of a town that no one built.

## III. The Ribbon-Things

In the pools, the pale ones move.

They are not fish. They are not worms. They are something that has no surface equivalent, no analogue in the world above, and this is because they have never been above and they have never needed to be. They are shaped like ribbons — flat, undulating, perhaps as long as a hand and as wide as two fingers, with no visible features. No eyes. No mouth that you can see. No segmentation, no shell, no structure. They are pale. They are translucent. In the amber light, they are almost invisible against the pale limestone of the pool floor, and you can only see them by the way they disturb the surface — a faint ripple, a slow spreading ring, as if the water itself is breathing.

They feed on the dead. On the mineral film. On the algae. On the small bodies that fall into the pools from above — the dead crustaceans, the beetle larvae, the millipede fragments. They wrap around their food and they *press*, and the pressing is a kind of digestion — external, slow, enzymatic — and the food softens and is absorbed through the ribbon-body, and the process takes hours, and the ribbon-thing does not move during it, and it looks like a ribbon of pale water, like a piece of the pool itself that has taken a slight form and is resting.

They reproduce by breaking. A ribbon-thing, grown large enough, will simply *separate* — a clean break somewhere along its length, and the two halves will drift apart, and each half will continue to feed and grow, and the missing end will regenerate, slowly, over weeks, and the two halves will become two wholes, and there is no violence in this, no urgency, no search for a mate. The breaking is a response to size, to the ratio of surface area to volume, to the efficiency of nutrient absorption, and it happens when the ribbon-thing has reached a length at which the ends are too far from the center to receive enough food, and the break solves this, and the two new ribbon-things are the right size, and they continue.

They are the only creatures in the Underworks that seem to have no purpose beyond living. The crustaceans maintain the reef. The beetles wear the trails. The millipedes navigate the cables. The ribbon-things simply *exist* in the pools, feeding, growing, breaking, and their existence is so complete, so self-contained, that watching them induces a kind of stillness in the watcher — a slowing of thought, a quieting of the mind, as if the ribbon-things are demonstrating a way of being that requires nothing from you and offers nothing to you and is, in its absolute sufficiency, a kind of instruction.

## IV. The Light-Bearers

In the wettest corners, where the pumps deliver their constant trickle, the fungus grows.

It is small. Pale caps on thin stems, clusters of them, no larger than a fingernail, and they glow. The glow is faint — not bright enough to read by, not bright enough to photograph by, but bright enough to *navigate* by, and this is the point. The fungus is the only source of light in the Underworks that was not installed by hands, and the creatures here — the crustaceans, the beetles, the millipedes, the ribbon-things — have oriented to it. Not consciously. Not deliberately. But the creatures that can detect the glow are the creatures that stay close to the wet zones, and the creatures that stay close to the wet zones are the creatures that survive, and so the glow functions as a beacon, a signpost, a small voice saying *water here, life here, stay*.

The fungus feeds on the stone. On the mineral deposit. On the organic detritus that the pumps carry through the water. It grows slowly — impossibly slowly, by surface standards — and it does not spread, not in any way that can be detected over the course of a human visit. It has been in its current positions for years. Decades. Longer. It is a fixture. It is as much a part of the architecture as the buildings, the machinery, the cables, the bulbs, and its light is the only light in the place that is not failing.

The amber bulbs are failing. They yellow and dim, and some of them have already gone dark, and the dark zones are growing, and the creatures are retreating from them, and the fungus is holding its ground, and in a hundred years the bulbs will be gone and the fungus will still be here, and the Underworks will be lit — dimly, faintly, in small patches in the wettest corners — by biological light, and the creatures will have adjusted, and the place will be the same, and the place will be different, and the difference will be too small to measure.

## V. The Millipedes of the Upper Dark

Along the cables, in the spaces between the bulbs, the millipedes move.

They are the navigators of the vertical world. The crustaceans own the gears, the beetles own the floor, the ribbon-things own the pools, and the millipedes own the cables — the thin polymer sheaths that hang from the ceiling in loose loops, sagging with moisture, slick with condensation, and the millipedes traverse them with a confidence that borders on indifference. Their legs are long and fine, and they find purchase on the polymer by touch, and they move in a continuous, sinuous flow, and as they pass in front of the bulbs, the light swells and dims, swells and dims, and this is the flicker that you see — not electrical, not mechanical, but *biological*, the shadow-play of a thousand small bodies moving through the amber glow.

They eat the polymer. Slowly. Over years. The polymer sheaths are thin and the millipedes' mandibles are weak, but they work at it constantly, and the sheaths are thinning, and in places they have worn through, and the bare wire is exposed, and the wire corrodes in the damp air, and the connection fails, and the bulb goes dark. The millipedes are, in their slow and mindless way, dismantling the electrical system. Not deliberately. Not with intent. But they are eating the infrastructure, and the infrastructure is losing, and the loss is slow, and the darkness is growing, and the millipedes move through it, unbothered, feeding, navigating, casting their shadows on a world that is slowly going dark around them.

They do not fall. This is the remarkable thing. They navigate cables that sag and loop and cross, and they do not fall. Their grip is absolute, and even when they pause — to feed, to rest, to mate — they pause with a fraction of their legs still attached, and the rest of their body hangs suspended in the dark, and they are still, and they are patient, and they are the ceiling's own, the dark's own, and when the last bulb goes out, they will not notice, because they navigate by touch, and the dark is their medium, and they were here before the light and they will be here after it.

## VI. The Unseen

At the edges, in the darkness beyond the amber pool of light, things move.

You can hear them. Clicks and rustles, the small sounds of bodies navigating stone, and the sounds are distributed the way the beetle-clicks are distributed — everywhere and nowhere, a presence that walks the margins, a population that is present but invisible. They are not the beetles. The beetles are on the floor, and these sounds are higher — on the walls, on the ceiling, in the crevices where the limestone pocks and rounds into shapes that provide cover. They are not the millipedes. The millipedes are on the cables, and these sounds are lower — on the stone, on the metal, in the gaps between the buildings and the cave wall. They are something else.

They may be juveniles. They may be a species that has no name, no classification, no analogue in the surface world. They may be the dark-adapted descendants of surface creatures — insects, arachnids, isopods — that colonized the Underworks generations ago and have since changed, adapted, become something new. They may be the true natives of the cave, the ones who were here before the town, before the machinery, before the light, and who retreated to the margins when the town was built and who have never returned to the center and who will never return, and who do not care about the town or the machinery or the light or the dark because they have their own world, the dark world, the wet world, the world of stone and stillness and silence, and it is complete, and it does not need them to be seen.

The sounds they make are not for you. They are for each other, or for themselves, or for nothing at all, and the distinction is irrelevant. They live at the edges. They live in the dark. They are the Underworks' own shadow population, and they will outlast the light, and they will outlast the machinery, and they will outlast the town, and when the buildings have crumbled and the pumps have stopped and the stone has reached its final patient form, they will still be here, at the edges, in the dark, clicking and rustling, unseen and unbothered, and the place will still be the Underworks, and it will still be at peace.

## VII. The Tone

Below everything, below the floor, below the pumps, below the stone, there is a vibration.

It arrives at intervals. Long intervals. Minutes apart, sometimes longer, and it lasts for perhaps three seconds, and it is low, so low that it is felt more than heard, a pressure wave that travels through the earth and through the water and through the stone and through the bodies of every living thing in the place, and it is felt, and then it stops, and in the silence after, everything seems more itself.

The crustaceans do not react. The beetles do not react. The millipedes do not react. The ribbon-things do not react. The fungus does not react. The unseen ones in the dark do not react. And this means either that they are accustomed to it — that it has been happening for so long that it has become part of the background, the way the hum of the pumps is part of the background, the way the dripping is part of the background — or that it is not a sound to them, that it is something else, something that travels through the earth the way a thought travels through a mind, and what you hear is only the part of it that happens to intersect with air.

No one knows what causes it.

The pumps are not the right frequency. They hum below hearing, a continuous vibration, and the tone is discrete, separated by silence, and the frequency is wrong — lower, deeper, older. The water is not the right medium. The water carries sound well, but the tone does not travel through water the way sound travels through water; it travels through *stone*, through the limestone bedrock, through the deep structure of the earth, and the water only transmits it indirectly, the way a drumhead transmits the blow of a stick. The stone is not the right shape. The cave is small, bounded, and the tone does not resonate in it the way a tone resonates in a chamber of the right size; it passes *through* it, the way light passes through glass, unchanged, uninterested.

Something is moving below. Something large. Something slow. Something that has its own rhythm, its own interval, its own duration, and the rhythm is not related to anything in the Underworks, and the interval is not related to any cycle that the creatures here can perceive, and the duration is not related to any process that anyone has measured. The tone is not a message. It is not a signal. It is not a warning or an invitation or a call. It is a *fact*. It is the fact of something vast and slow and deep, something that exists below the Underworks the way the Underworks exists below the surface, and the two facts intersect at intervals, and the intersection produces a vibration, and the vibration is felt, and the feeling is the closest thing to a prayer that the Underworks will ever know.

## VIII. The Pumps

They run.

This is their purpose, their function, their entire existence. They sit in their housings at the edges of the space, four of them, iron and rust and mineral deposit, and they pump water from one part of the underworks to another, and the water cycles, and the cycle sustains everything — the reef, the beetles, the ribbon-things, the fungus, the millipedes, the unseen ones, the tone, the silence after the tone. The pumps are the heartbeat. They have been pumping for longer than anyone knows, and they will pump for longer than anyone will know, and the pumping is not a choice, it is not a decision, it is not a conscious act, it is a *mechanical inevitability*, and the gears inside them turn, and the water moves, and the world continues.

But the gears are rusting. The housings are corroding. The seals are failing, slowly, and water is seeping into places water should not be, and the bearings are wearing, and the vibration is changing — subtly, almost imperceptibly, but changing — and the change is accelerating, and the pumps will fail, eventually, and when they fail, the water will stop cycling, and the water will warm, and the algae will die, and the reef will die, and the beetles will die, and the ribbon-things will die, and the fungus will die, and the millipedes will die, and the unseen ones will die, and the Underworks will become a cave, just a cave, a wet and dark and silent cave, and the tone will still sound from below, and the tone will still pass through, and no one will hear it, and the place will not be at peace, the place will be *dead*, and the difference between peace and death is the difference between a place that has found its rhythm and a place that has lost it.

But this is not now. Now, the pumps run. Now, the water cycles. Now, the reef grows, and the beetles wear their trails, and the ribbon-things feed and break and regenerate, and the fungus glows, and the millipedes navigate the cables, and the unseen ones click in the dark, and the tone sounds from below, and the silence after the tone is full of everything that lives here, everything that has found its niche in the wet and the dim and the cool and the quiet, and the place is complete, and the place is strange, and the place is at peace.
