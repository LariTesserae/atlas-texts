To inhabit the Plateau of Bellows is to exist within a breath. This is not a landscape that hosts life; it is a landscape that *exhales* it, a continuous, warm, damp respiration rising from the ancient stone. The creatures that live here do not merely dwell upon the plateau—they are extensions of its slow, patient metabolism, indistinguishable from the surface that birthed them.

At the foundation of this existence is the Mat. It is not a single organism, but a composite skin, a democracy of fungi and lichens that has erased the boundary between the living and the dead. The crustose lichens are the bedrock of this world, fused directly to the pale stone, their sulfur-yellow and bruised-purple bodies dissolving the very substrate they cling to, millimeter by millimeter, century by century. They are the planet's slow digestion. Above them, the foliose lichens rise like ruffled lungs, their parchment-colored edges curling and uncurling with the microscopic fluctuations in the fog, breathing in the damp air, exhaling the faint sweetness of rot. And threading through it all, the mycelial network—a subterranean cosmos of white and pale gold threads—serves as the plateau's neural network. It carries chemical whispers from the swales to the basins, distributing the captured moisture, sharing the dissolved minerals. When you see a patch of crustose lichen shudder and release a microscopic cloud of spores, it is not an isolated event; it is a sigh from the entire plateau.

Moving through this breathing skin are the Glittering Herds. To look at the lichen forests with the naked eye is to see a static, painterly landscape. But crouch down, let your eyes adjust to the micro-scale, and the stillness shatters into a writhing, glittering metropolis. The mites are the great herbivores and apex predators of this realm, their bodies beaded with captured fog like tiny, living chandeliers. Thousands of species traverse the lichen branches in slow, deliberate waves, their translucent legs carrying them over vast distances—the distance of a human room to them—feeding on the fungal fruiting bodies, or on each other. They move with the deliberate grace of cattle grazing on an endless, purple prairie. 

Among the mites leap the springtails, the erratic sparks of the plateau. Propelled by their furcula, they launch themselves into the void between the fruticose columns, tumbling through the heavy air, their erratic trajectories writing invisible, frantic calligraphy. And in the shallow basins—those mirrors of still water that reflect the overcast sky—the nematodes swim. They are the serpents of this galactic ocean, their sinuous bodies turning each pool into a galaxy of writhing silver threads, swimming through the film of moisture with a grace that belies their microscopic scale. 

Then there are the Watchers. The small, round-bodied mammals—the pika-voles—are the plateau’s rare concession to macroscopic life. Fur the color of dry bone, they are the ghosts of the stone, emerging from the crevices to graze upon the swollen fruiting bodies. But to call them mere grazers is to misunderstand their role. They are the plateau's mobile circulatory system. Their fur is thick with spores, with dormant mite eggs, with slime mold cysts. As they move from basin to basin, their soft scraping teeth harvesting the fungal flesh, they inoculate the isolated pockets of the plateau with new life. Their high, thin whistles are not songs of joy or warnings of predators; they are simply the sound of the plateau acknowledging its own existence, a frequency that cuts through the muffling fog just enough to say, *I am here.*

And yet, the most profound inhabitants of the plateau are not its present creatures, but the Echoes of the Builders. Whoever they were—whatever they were—they did not simply vanish. They were consumed, and in being consumed, they were understood. The low walls of stacked stone, softened and blurred by millennia of fungal accretion, are not ruins in the traditional sense. They are scaffolding. The mycelial network, encountering the strange angles and deliberate hollows of the ancient walls, did not breach them; it adopted them. The fungi grew *through* the mortar, *around* the corners, *into* the doorways, until the distinction between the built and the grown dissolved entirely. 

The mycelial mat does not mimic the architecture. It *remembers* it. The low walls, arched openings, and columnar forms that rise from the fungal sheets are the plateau's attempt to finish the thoughts the Builders began. The Builders left no bones, no tools, no artifacts, because the fungi took them all. They took the stone, the wood, the flesh, and the intent, and wove it into the mycelial memory. The Builders are not gone; they have been translated into the language of the Mat. When the fog rolls in and the light dims, and you see a particularly regular cluster of pale, columnar fungi rising from the damp stone, you are not looking at a natural formation. You are looking at the plateau dreaming of its creators.

To live on the Plateau of Bellows is to surrender the urgency of individual existence. The spore-pulsed air, the tickling in the throat, the warm damp that never leaves your bones—the plateau is trying to adopt you, too. It is trying to bring you into the continuous, patient organism. Here, the boundaries are eroded. Stone becomes soil, soil becomes fungus, fungus becomes animal, and the intentions of the lost are carried on the hyphal threads of the living. It is the end state of the world, a place where everything has finished becoming anything else, and has settled, at last, into the warm, quiet, spore-laden business of simply being alive.
