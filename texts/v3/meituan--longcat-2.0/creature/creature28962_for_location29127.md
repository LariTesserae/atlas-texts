They do not walk the Weep so much as they inhabit its crevices, moving through the vertical strata of the city like blood through a capillaries. If you were to catch a glimpse of them through the sepia haze, standing motionless on a slick basalt shelf or silhouetted against the glow of a steam-vent, your first instinct might be to mistake them for stalwart, half-human scavengers. But the longer you looked, the more the illusion would fracture.

They are the **Cruciforms**, though that is a term they would never use; language, for them, is a percussive thing, a series of hammer-taps and pipe-knocks that travels through the iron bones of the city rather than through the damp, useless air. They are the Weep’s immune system, its caretakers, and its eventual prey.

**Physiology and Adaptation**
The Cruciforms are not born of the earth, but of the condensation. Their skin is thick, rubbery, and hairless—a mottled, translucent grey that mimics the weeping basalt, shot through with a faint, bioluminescent blue that pulses in time with the deep, tectonic grumble of the Geothermic Works below. They possess no external ears; the omnipresent roar would have shattered eardrums generations ago. Instead, they possess flat, bony plates on the sides of their skulls that resonate with the city’s vibrations, "hearing" through their bones. 

Their eyes are vestigial, clouded over with a milky, amber cataract. They do not need to see in the flat, shadowless light. They navigate by touch and by the subtle changes in air pressure against their damp skin. Their hands are broad, splayed things with elongated, spatulate fingers, tipped with nails that have evolved into hard, hooked claws, perfectly designed to catch on rusted grating and grip the slick, moss-covered pipes.

Most striking is their respiration. They do not breathe the heavy, silty air of the Weep directly. Hidden beneath the folds of their rubbery necks are slits—vestigial gills that filter the mineral-rich condensation directly from the air and the pipes. They drink the city’s sweat. To sustain their massive, hunched frames, they scrape the heavy-metal lichen and the cold-water moss from the girders, chewing it with broad, flat molars. Their blood, visible through the thin skin of their throats, is a dark, oxidized green.

**The Language of the Iron**
Communication among the Cruciforms is a physical, intimate affair. They stand shoulder-to-shoulder on the catwalks, pressing their damp, rubbery palms against the sweating pipes or the iron beams. When one has something to say, it does not shout. It strikes the metal with a heavy, flint-tipped stylus—*clink-clink, clatter-tap*. The rhythm travels through the circulatory system of the city, vibrating up through the soles of their feet, resonating in their skull-plates. A tap-language of immense complexity, capable of conveying sorrow, warning, or the precise location of a sagging conduit. It is a language that has no word for "silence," only for "the muffled throb of the dead pockets."

**Society and Ritual**
Their social structure is dictated entirely by the flow of the city’s fluids and the needs of its decaying infrastructure. A Cruciform’s role is not chosen; it is observed. If an infant is born with a slight discoloration in its skin, it is marked for the Pipes. If its claws grow especially long and hooked, it is marked for the Pit.

The Weepers of the Pipes are the most numerous. They move along the vertical tangles of the city, hundreds of feet above the ground, patching leaks with globs of hot, molten solder and greasy orange biofilm. They move with agonizing slowness, applying the same glacial patience the city possesses. To rush is to fall; to fall is to shatter on the basalt below and be reclaimed by the weeping stone. 

The Divers of the Pit descend into the excavated factories, their gills fluttering in the scalding, sulfurous vapors near the Geothermic Works. They chisel away at the calcified mineral deposits that choke the ancient pistons, working in the blinding, superheated steam. They return to the upper levels coated in a fine, orange dust, their bioluminescence dimmed, their skin peeling.

And then there are the Listeners.

**The Listeners and the "Soon"**
In the deepest, most insulated dead pockets—rooms lined with thick felt and cork where the roar of the city is reduced to a muffled, almost tolerable throb—the Listeners sit. They are the oldest of the Cruciforms, their bodies so heavy and encrusted with mineral deposits that they can no longer climb. They sit in the dark, pressing their bony skull-plates against the walls, listening to the heartbeat of the basin.

They are the ones who heard it first. When the steam forms the shapes that are almost familiar—the hand, the face, the half-remembered word—it is the Listeners who interpret the meaning. They feel the shift in the low-frequency vibration, the moment the entire basin draws that massive, agonizing breath, dropping the pitch of the deafening roar to a subsonic rumble. 

It is the Listeners who receive the message. Not as a sound, but as a knowing pressed directly into the wet clay of their minds: *Soon.*

But soon what?

The Listeners do not fear it. They do not question it. They have lived in the stomach of a digesting organism for so long that they have accepted their role. The Weep is not merely a factory; it is an alchemical crucible, a vast, planetary retort where heat, pressure, and time transmute not just ore, but reality itself. The Cruciforms know that the "Soon" is the catalyst. The city is nearing the end of its great process, the final stage of its metabolism. 

When the voice speaks, the Listeners tap out a message of their own. It travels down through the pipes, across the girders, into the Pit. *The Breathing is near. Attend the leaks. Secure the shafts.* 

They do not wonder if they will survive the "Soon." They are part of the machinery, and machinery does not survive the process—it is merely the vessel that allows the transformation to occur. So the Cruciforms wait, their rubbery hands pressed against the warm, weeping iron, their gills filtering the steam, their blind eyes staring into the flat, endless light. They attend to the slow decay, weld the sagging pipes, and wait for the city to finish its digestion.
