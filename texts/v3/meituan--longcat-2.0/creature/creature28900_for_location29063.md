The Resonant Expanse.

To ask who lives here is to ask who lives inside a lung. The answer is everything. The answer is too much.

Begin with the ground. The crust is alive — you know this from the description, but the description does not tell you what it feels like to be the crust, to be that mat of root-hairs and fungal threads and shed skin that stretches in every direction like a hide laid over the bones of something unimaginably old. The crust does not think, exactly. But it processes. It sweats. It drinks. It holds the memory of every rain that has ever fallen on it in the faint dampness that beads on its surface each morning, and it releases that memory back into the air as the heat rises, and this cycle — dampness and dryness, dampness and dryness — is its respiration. The creatures that walk on it, the people who press their clay vessels into it to catch the condensation, the Ones Who Walk Between who press their flat bodies against it and disappear — they are all part of its respiration too. They are the things it breathes in.

Above the crust: the Pillars.

The Pillars are the first thing. They are the thing that defines every other thing. A Pillar is not a mountain that happens to be alive. It is life that happens to be geological. It is warm. It is slow. It grows at a rate that can only be measured in the deepening of its furrows, in the widening of its breathing-pores, in the accumulation of the papery skin that it sheds and that becomes the crust that becomes the ground that becomes the floor on which everything stands. A Pillar does not have a face, but if it did, it would be looking inward, not out. It is absorbed in itself. It is a process, not a thing — a process that has been running for so long that the distinction between the process and the thing has collapsed, and what remains is something that is simultaneously a single organism, an ecosystem, a landscape, and a kind of weather.

The surface of a Pillar is where the first layer of life lives. The Swarm-Wings. They are not insects. They are something else — something that has converged on the shape of insects the way a river converges on the shape of its channel, not because it intends to but because that is the shape that works. They emerge from the furrows in the morning, when the Pillar's own heat has warmed them enough to move, and they rise in columns that spiral upward, and the sound of their wings is the Pillar's voice, or one of its voices, a drone that is not a single frequency but a chord, a dense overlapping of millions of individual wingbeats that blend into a tone so constant that the creatures that live on the Pillar — the Crawlers, the bright lizards, the pale eyeless things in the deep cavities — have evolved to communicate in the silences between the Swarm-Wings' notes, in the tiny gaps where the drone thins and a click or a chirp can be heard. The Swarm-Wings themselves do not eat the Pillar. They eat the air, or the microorganisms that live in the air, or the tiny droplets of moisture that condense on the bark — the accounts differ, and the truth may be all three. What is certain is that they rise and fall in patterns that correspond to the Pillar's breathing, expanding outward when the Pillar contracts and settling inward when it expands, as if the Swarm-Wings are the Pillar's exhalation, as if they are what the Pillar breathes out into the world.

Below the Swarm-Wings: the Crawlers.

The Crawlers are the Pillar's metabolism made visible. They move through the furrows like blood through veins, eating the bark and the moss and each other, converting the Pillar's substance into their own substance and then, when they die or are eaten, returning it to the crust below. They are segmented, many-legged, and they come in forms so varied that a single furrow can contain a dozen species that share nothing but their environment and their habit of moving slowly and constantly, without apparent purpose, except that the purpose is the furrow itself — they are the furrow's function, they are what the furrow does. Some of them are armored in chitin that matches the crust so perfectly that they look like moving pieces of the ground. Some are soft and pale and eyeless, living in the deep furrows where the Pillar's heat is strongest, and these ones are the strangest — they have no need for eyes because the world they live in is defined by temperature and texture, by the gradient of warmth that rises from the Pillar's core to its surface, and they navigate this gradient with a precision that suggests they are sensing something the other creatures cannot, some quality of the Pillar that is invisible and perhaps inaudible, a signal that the Pillar sends out through its own body like a heartbeat sent through blood.

And then there are the Ones Who Walk Between.

They are the prey. They are also the connective tissue. They live on the open crust, moving between the Pillars in their groups of twenty or thirty, and their flat, wide bodies are covered in the same papery substance as the crust so that when they stop they become the ground, they become texture, they become nothing — they are the negative space of the Expanse, defined entirely by their edges, by the thin dark line where their bodies meet the air. When they move, they move fast. Their six legs are low and splayed, and they cover the ground with a scuttling urgency that suggests they know — in whatever way a creature without eyes as we understand them can know — that the open steppe between the Pillars is dangerous, that the Flyers nest in the crowns and descend on thermals, and that the only safety is in motion or in stillness, in being either too fast to catch or too invisible to see. The Ones Who Walk Between eat the crust itself, scraping at it with mouthparts that are adapted to its particular composition, and in doing so they aerate it, they loosen it, they prepare it for the next rain, and so they are not just the Expanse's prey but its gardeners, its maintainers, the creatures that keep the ground ready to receive whatever moisture comes.

The Flyers.

They are the raptors of the Expanse, and they are terrible. They nest in the Pillar crowns, in the aerial root-curtains that hang like veils from the highest ridges, and they descend on thermals with wingspans that the description says blot out the sun, and this is not an exaggeration. A Flyer in descent is a shadow that arrives before its source, a sudden darkening that makes the Swarm-Wings scatter and the Ones Who Walk Between freeze and the very light seem to dim. The Flyers hunt by silhouette — they look for the dark line, the edge where a One Who Walk Between meets the air — and they strike with a speed that is almost instantaneous, and the sound of a strike is a single sharp crack, like a branch breaking, and then the Flyer is rising again with its prey in its talons, and the Ones Who Walk Between are running, and the ground is full of dark lines, and the shadow passes, and the light returns, and the Expanse continues.

The Flyers do not eat only the Ones Who Walk Between. They eat the large Crawlers. They eat the Swarm-Wings when the columns are dense enough. They eat, occasionally, the small lizards that dart in the furrows. They are the Expanse's restraint, its check on abundance, and without them the Ones Who Walk Between would overrun the crust and the Swarm-Wings would cloud the sky and the Crawlers would strip the Pillars bare. With them, there is balance. With them, there is the particular quality of attention that comes from being watched by something that can kill you — the Ones Who Walk Between are not mindless. They are alert. They are responsive. They have a social structure that is based on the distribution of attention, on the way the group spreads out so that some are always moving and some are always still, so that the Flyers never find the whole group in one state, so that some always survive.

And then there are the people.

The people are small. This is the first thing to understand about them. Not just physically — though they are physically small, adapted to the thin air and the thick heat, their bodies compact and efficient — but in their relationship to the Expanse. They are small the way a single thought is small inside a mind. They are small the way a single note is small inside a chord. They live in the lee of the Pillars, in shelters half-buried in the crust, and their structures are so low and so covered in the papery bark-substance that from a distance they look like natural formations, like hummocks of the crust that have grown up around the Pillar's base. You could walk past a settlement and not see it. You could walk past a settlement and see only the Pillar, only the ground, only the shimmer of heat and the columns of Swarm-Wings rising into the sky.

The people harvest the resin from the Pillars' wounds. This is their central act, the thing that defines their relationship with the living world around them. The resin is amber-colored and smells of honey and something sharper, something almost medicinal, and it is the basis of everything they make — their glue, their waterproofing, their preservation. They use it to seal their shelters against the rare but violent rains. They use it to coat their nets and their spear-hafts. They use it to preserve the Swarm-Wings they gather in the evening, when the creatures are torpid with the cooling air, and the preserved Swarm-Wings are their primary food source, roasted or dried, and the taste is described — by the people themselves, in the stories they tell — as something between smoke and sweetness, something that tastes like the Expanse itself, like cinnamon and ozone and the particular quality of light that comes through the Swarm-Wing columns at midday.

The people do not build high. They do not build large. Their tools are bone and stone and, in the settlements near the thermal vents, simple copper — native copper, found in the vents' mineral deposits, worked cold or with gentle heat, hammered into blades and hooks and needles. They have no writing, or if they have writing it is not on any surface that would survive — it is in the patterns of their woven root-fiber, in the arrangements of their stone cairns, in the songs they sing that describe the walking routes between Pillars, the distances measured not in units but in the number of condensation cycles, the number of mornings, the number of times the ground sweats and dries between one Pillar and the next.

The people have a relationship with the Pillars that is not worship and not husbandry. It is something else. It is a relationship of mutual awareness, or at least of one-sided awareness that might be mutual. The people know the Pillars are alive. They know the Pillars are aware — slowly, chemically, through networks of signal and response that operate on timescales of days and weeks. They know that if you cut too deeply into a Pillar's bark, the Pillar will react — not with pain, but with a change in the local ecosystem, a shift in the creature populations, a closing-off of the wounded area that can take years to heal. And so they harvest carefully. They ask permission, or they used to, or they still do in some settlements — a ritual touching of the bark, a spoken word, an offering of water poured into a furrow. The ritual is not for the Pillar's benefit, exactly. It is for the people's benefit. It is a way of remembering that the Pillar is not a resource but a being, that the resin is not a product but a secretion, that the relationship between the people and the Pillar is not extraction but exchange.

What do the people give back? This is the question that the people themselves disagree about. Some say they give back their attention — that the Pillar is aware of them the way a forest is aware of the animals that move through it, and that this awareness is a form of nourishment, that the Pillar feeds on being known. Some say they give back their labor — that by harvesting the resin they are performing a service, clearing the Pillar's wounds, preventing infection, the way a cleaner fish clears parasites from a larger fish. Some say they give back nothing, and that the Pillar does not know they exist, and that this is the most frightening possibility of all — that they are not in a relationship with the Pillar at all, that they are simply there, like the Crawlers, like the Swarm-Wings, like the Ones Who Walk Between, a part of the Pillar's ecosystem but not a partner in it, and that the rituals are stories they tell themselves to feel less small.

The people have different names for the pulse they feel in the ground. The word in the description translates roughly as "the dreaming," but this is a simplification. In the settlements near the Throat — the largest Pillar, the one that generates its own weather — the pulse is called something that means "the breathing of the deep." In the settlements on the eastern edge of the Expanse, where the Pillars are smaller and farther apart, it is called something that means "the voice below." In the settlements near the thermal vents, where the heat from below is strongest and the ground is warm even at night, it is called something that means "the blood." These are not just different words for the same thing. They are different interpretations of the same sensation, different theories about what lives in the deep substrate, what the Pillars grow from, what the crust is the skin of.

The people who live near the Throat say the pulse is the Throat's own heartbeat, that the Throat is so vast that its metabolic processes are felt across the entire Expanse, that all the other Pillars are extensions of the Throat, branches of a single root system that stretches under the entire plateau. The people who live on the eastern edge say the pulse is something older than the Pillars, something that the Pillars grow from the way a tree grows from soil, and that the Pillars are not the source of the Expanse but its fruit. The people who live near the thermal vents say the pulse is the Expanse itself, that the plateau is a single living thing and the Pillars are its organs and the creatures are its cells and the people are — what? They disagree. Some say the people are the Expanse's awareness of itself. Some say they are a fever. Some say they are nothing, and the Expanse does not know they exist, and this is the most frightening possibility of all.

The people do not go into the deep caves. This is the one thing they all agree on, across all the settlements, across all the distances. The deep caves — the cavities in the Pillars that are large enough to shelter a village, the ones where the air is cooler and damper and the walls are smooth and polished and marked with patterns that might be natural and might not — these places are avoided. Not because of the creatures that live there, though the creatures there are strange and pale and slow and some of them are large enough to be dangerous. Not because of the darkness, though the darkness in the deep caves is absolute, a darkness that has never been touched by the aggressive clarity of the Expanse's light. But because of the dreaming. The pulse is stronger in the deep caves. The pulse is louder. And if you stay too long, if you press your ear to the smooth polished wall and listen, you can hear something that is not a pulse, that is not a heartbeat, that is not a voice, but that is all of these things and none of them — a sound that is below sound, a vibration that is felt in the bones and the teeth and the space behind the eyes where thoughts begin, and the people who have heard it say that it is the sound of the Expanse thinking, and that its thoughts are not for minds as small as theirs, and that to hear them is to be changed by them, and that the change is not always survivable.

There are stories about people who went into the deep caves and did not come out. There are stories about people who came out but were different — who spoke in languages that no one else could understand, or who could not stop moving, or who lay on the ground and pressed their ears to the crust and would not get up, or who walked into the open steppe between the Pillars and kept walking until they were swallowed by the heat shimmer and the distance and were never seen again. These stories are told in every settlement, and they have the quality of myths — possibly true, possibly not, impossible to verify. But they are told with a seriousness that suggests the people believe them, or at least believe that they could be true, and that this possibility is enough.

The people are not the only ones who avoid the deep caves. The Ones Who Walk Between do not go near them. The Flyers do not nest in them. Even the Swarm-Wings, which rise from every furrow and cavity on the Pillar surface, do not enter the deep caves — their columns spiral upward and outward, away from the dark openings, as if the caves are places where the Pillar's exhalation does not reach, where the Pillar's breath turns inward, where something else is breathing.

What lives in the deep caves? The description says: pale things, slow things, things that have never seen the sun. This is accurate but incomplete. The things in the deep caves are old. They are older than the Swarm-Wings, older than the Crawlers, older than the Ones Who Walk Between. They may be older than the people. They are the things that were here before the Pillars grew tall enough to cast shadows, when the Expanse was young and the crust was thin and the heat from below was the only heat and the only light was the faint bioluminescence of organisms that have since been buried under millennia of shed skin and fungal threads and the accumulated weight of a living world. They are the Expanse's memory, or its unconscious, or its dream of what it was before it became what it is. They move slowly because they have never needed to move quickly. They are pale because they have never needed color. They are eyeless because they have never needed to see. They sense the world through the pulse, through the vibration that travels through the crust and the Pillars and the air, and they respond to it with a deliberation that makes the Crawlers look frantic, that makes the Swarm-Wings look like a blur. They are the Expanse's deep time, its geological patience, and they are the reason the people do not go into the caves, and the reason the Ones Who Walk Between do not go near the caves, and the reason the Flyers do not nest in the caves, and the reason the Swarm-Wings spiral away from the cave mouths as if the caves are the one place in the Expanse where the living world ends and something else begins.

This is who lives in the Resonant Expanse.

The crust lives. It sweats and drinks and breathes and holds the memory of rain.

The Pillars live. They grow and shed and breathe and sing in frequencies that are felt in the bones and the teeth and the space behind the eyes.

The Swarm-Wings live. They rise and fall in columns that are the Pillars' exhalation, and their wings make the sound that is the Expanse's base note, the drone that never stops.

The Crawlers live. They move through the furrows like blood through veins, eating and being eaten, converting the Pillars' substance into the world's substance and back again.

The Ones Who Walk Between live. They move across the crust in their groups of twenty or thirty, flat and wide and invisible when still, and they are the Expanse's prey and its gardeners, the creatures that keep the ground ready.

The Flyers live. They descend from the Pillar crowns on thermals, and their shadows are the Expanse's restraint, its check on abundance, the sudden dark that keeps the world in balance.

The people live. They are small. They are faint. They harvest resin and gather Swarm-Wings and press their clay vessels into the crust to catch the condensation, and they tell stories about the other settlements and the deep caves and the pulse in the ground, and they disagree about what they are — awareness, fever, nothing — and this disagreement is itself a form of life, a form of the Expanse's ongoing argument with itself about what it means to be alive.

And below all of this, in the deep substrate, in the places where the pulse is strongest and the dreaming is loudest, something else lives. Something that the Pillars grow from, or feed, or are part of. Something that the people have many names for and no name for. Something that is not a creature and not a place and not a process but all of these things at once, something that is the Expanse itself, the living plateau, the warm and breathing ground, the thing that was here before the Pillars and will be here after they fall, the thing that dreams the Pillars and thinks the creatures and perhaps — perhaps — knows the people, or does not know them, or knows them in a way that is so vast and so slow that it is indistinguishable from not knowing them at all.

This is who lives in the Resonant Expanse.

Everything.

All of it.

At once.
