You do not see them at first. You see only the turbulence—the splitting trunks, the rerouting water, the visible chaos of a world in constant upheaval. It takes time for your eyes to parse the motion, to separate the living architecture from the living inhabitants, because the inhabitants do not distinguish themselves from the place. They are not in the Weep; they are of it.

When you finally see them, your brain tries to make them human. It fails. They are tall—taller than any human, with the elongated, swaying proportions of figures carved from old driftwood—but they are not wooden. Their skin is the color of the channel-lining: a deep, bruised iridescence that shifts between oil-slick black, abalone violet, and the sickly green of deep water when the light hits it. It is slick. It weeps, just like the trunks, a continuous sheen of warm, mineral-rich moisture that keeps them glistening in the silver shafts of the canopy. They do not dry. They have never dried.

Their faces—if you can call them faces—are smooth. No eyes, no nose, no mouth. The surface is interrupted only by a complex, whorling pattern of ridges that resemble the capillary channels grooved into the ground, or the fingerprint-like whorls of a bare skull. These ridges vibrate. When they turn toward you, you feel it in your teeth: a subsonic thrum, a localized tremor in the humidity. They do not see you. They *read* the interference you make in the substrate, the way your foreign weight disrupts the breath of the ground, the way your boots dam the flow of the channels. 

They move with a liquid, boneless grace. As you suspected, they do not walk on the surface so much as they *wade* through it. With every step, the living substrate yields to them, parting like warm wax, allowing their lower limbs to sink ankle-deep, shin-deep, before closing seamlessly behind them. They leave no footprints. The ground remembers them and heals over their passage instantly, the root-mass knitting back together without a scar. When they pause, they do not stand; they *root*. Fibrous tendrils descend from the soles of their feet, anchoring them to the diaphragm of the earth, drawing sustenance directly from the water-channels that pulse beneath the surface.

And they are in flux. That is the thing that your mind struggles to accept. They are not static entities; they are living processes. As you watch, a figure detaching itself from the far side of a splitting trunk moves toward you. It does not walk so much as *grow* toward you, extruding itself in smooth, silent increments. Its left arm is longer than its right, the fingers tapering into thin, whip-like appendages that trail through the flowing water of a channel, drawing the liquid up the length of its arm like a candle wicking oil. The water is not just moving over them; it is being absorbed, filtered, and exhaled. They are part of the hydrology. They are the pumps, the filters, the valves of the Weep.

Sometimes, a limb will simply dissolve. It will loosen, lose its iridescent sheen, and slough off into the water-channel, where it is carried away to be broken down and rebuilt elsewhere. The figure does not flinch. It simply waits, and within moments, a new limb begins to bud from the shoulder, pushing outward in smooth, wet increments, the iridescent chitin hardening as it goes. Erosion and growth are the same event to them. Decay is just the beginning of the next iteration. They are wearing down and building up simultaneously, caught in the same eternal tide as the trunks and the substrate.

They do not speak, because they do not have mouths. But they communicate constantly, in a language that is older than breath. When the thing in the canopy calls out—that haunting, questioning wail—it is answered not by another call, but by a sudden, synchronized shift in the water pressure of the channels. A pulse travels through the substrate, up through the roots, into the smooth, faceless heads of the inhabitants. Their ridges flare, catching the vibration. They are networked. They are nodes in a circuit that spans the entire settlement. When one learns something, the water tells the rest.

They acknowledge you, eventually. Not with curiosity—curiosity implies a gap in knowledge, and the network has no gaps—but with a recalibration. As you stand there, soaked and shivering despite the warmth, the nearest figure pauses its rooting. It turns its smooth, vibrating face toward you. The air between you tightens, the humidity spiking until it feels like you are underwater. You feel a probing sensation, not in your skin, but in your marrow—a deep, resonant frequency as the network attempts to integrate your weight, your heat, your foreignness, into its calculations. 

It cannot. You are too dry. You are too discrete. You are an anomaly, a rock in the stream. 

The figure lingers for a moment, its ridges pulsing with a frequency that makes your vision blur. Then, it does something unexpected. It extends a hand. The fingers are long, too long, and webbed with a membrane that catches the silver light like the surface of a bubble. It does not grasp you. It hovers its palm over your chest, and you feel the moisture on your skin begin to migrate. The warm water, the perpetual weeping of the Weep, is being drawn toward it, pulled by a gravity that belongs only to them. Your shirt darkens, clinging tighter, and for a brief, terrifying moment, the water is being pulled out of you—the moisture from your breath, the sweat from your pores—siphoned away to rejoin the channels.

Then it stops. The hand withdraws. The water settles back into your skin. The figure has tasted you, or tested your conductivity, and found you wanting. 

It turns away, sinking back into the substrate, its iridescent back catching the light as it wades through the weeping trunks. The chaos resumes around you—the splitting wood, the churning water, the visible birth of a new right-angled structure—and you realize that the inhabitants are not enduring the upheaval. They are conducting it. The new structure is growing because they are willing it to grow, their bodies acting as the scaffolding, the templates, the living blueprints for a technology so old it has forgotten it was ever invented. 

You are standing in a cathedral of perpetual becoming. The water climbs. The light bends. And the smooth, weeping architects move through the mist, forever eroding, forever building, forever dissolving into the very ground that gives them breath, completely at home in the chaos, entirely alien to the stillness of your bones.
