They are not many. They are one thing, in many bodies.

---

You will not see them at first. Not because they are hidden — they are everywhere, they are the floor, they are the walls up to the knee, they are the surface of the catwalks and the warm flanks of the pumps — but because they are the color of the place, and the place is dim. Their shells are the pale of things that have never been touched by light: not white, not gray, but a translucent, living beige, the shade of bone that has never dried, of fungus that has never known air. Each shell is soft in the way that things growing in warm darkness are soft — not weak, but unfinished. Still deciding what it will be. Like the rock around them.

They are small. A coin, the description says, and that is close, but a coin is flat and these are not. They have depth. They have the rounded, domed carapace of a body that holds something inside it — organs, warmth, whatever passes for a self in a creature this small and this old. Their legs are fine, almost hairlike, and there are too many of them to count at a glance, which is part of the design: you cannot look at a crab and see all of it. You see the shell, the claws, and then a blur of movement at the edges, a fringe of pale filaments that are always in motion, always testing the surface beneath them.

They move the way that heat moves — slowly, without apparent origin, in directions that seem to have no single cause. A crab will be still for a long time. Minutes. You will think it is dead, or dormant, or something other than alive. Then one leg moves. Then another. Then the whole body shifts, and it is walking, and you did not see the decision being made. There is no start to their movement. There is no stop. They simply are, and then they are elsewhere, and the transition between the two states is invisible.

They have claws, but the claws are small and they do not use them the way you expect. They do not fight. They do not threaten. They wave them — slowly, rhythmically, like a person stirring a cup of something very hot — and the waving is not communication, or not only communication. It is sensing. The claws taste the air, taste the stone, taste the faint electrical hum of the pumps and the chemical signature of the water and the vibration of your footsteps on the catwalk forty meters away. They know you are here. They have known since you entered the cavern, since before you entered, since your weight first pressed on the rock above them and sent a tremor through the floor that traveled at the speed of stone and told them: something large is coming.

They do not flee. This is the thing that stays with you, afterward, when you are somewhere else — somewhere cool and dry and brightly lit and full of creatures that scatter at your approach. The crabs do not flee. They shift. They rearrange. You step onto the floor and the crabs near your foot move away from it — not quickly, not in a panic, but with the same slow, patient certainty with which the water finds its level. A space opens around your foot. A perfect, foot-shaped absence in the pale carpet of bodies. And when you lift your foot, they close the gap. Slowly. From the edges. Like water filling a footprint in sand.

They have been here a long time. Or they have always been here. Or they arrived so long ago that the distinction has dissolved, the way the minerals dissolve in the water and precipitate on the walls, the way the rock is dissolving and reforming, the way the cavern is always in the process of becoming itself and never quite arrives. They are native to this place in the way that the heat is native, in the way that the moisture is native. They were not introduced. They were not invasive. They emerged — from the warm water, from the mineral-rich dark, from the bacterial mats that were here before them and that they eat and that eat them, in the slow, circular economy of a closed system.

Their biology is the biology of the cavern. They are thermophilic. The heat does not discomfort them; it is the medium they move through, the way air is the medium for birds. They gather at the warmest places — the bases of the pumps, the outflow pipes, the settling tanks where the water is hottest — and they cluster there in dense, overlapping masses, their shells touching, their legs interlocking, their bodies pressed together in a way that looks like discomfort but is not. They are absorbing. Drawing the heat up through their legs, through the thin membranes of their undersides, into the soft machinery of their bodies. They are charging themselves. They are filling their reserves. The heat is food, and they eat it slowly, over hours, with the patience of creatures that have never known hunger.

Because there is always enough. That is the other thing. The algae grow on the pipes. The bacteria bloom in the tanks. The mineral deposits build up and are colonized and are consumed. And when there is not enough of those, there are each other. They are not sentimental about this. A crab that dies — of age, of accident, of the simple accumulation of mineral deposits in its joints — is not mourned. It is not buried. It is eaten. Slowly. By its neighbors. By its kin. By the colony, which does not distinguish between self and other in the way that larger creatures do. The dead crab is material. It is calcium and protein and water, and these things are needed, and so they are taken back. The colony consumes itself and is sustained. It is a closed loop. It is efficient. It is, in its way, perfect.

---

They do not have eyes that you can see.

This does not mean they are blind. They perceive — but they perceive the way the cavern perceives: through vibration, through temperature, through the chemical composition of the water and the air. They feel your footsteps through the stone. They feel the heat of your body radiating outward, a disturbance in the thermal field of the cavern, a warm ghost moving through their dark. They taste your presence in the condensation that falls from the ceiling, which carries the salt of your skin and the carbon dioxide of your breath and the faint metallic signature of your boots. They know you the way the pumps know you: as a change in the system. As a variable. As something that has entered the works and must be accounted for.

They watch you without eyes. They track you. As you move across the catwalk, the crabs along your path turn — not their bodies, which barely move, but their claws, their legs, the fine sensory filaments that fringe their shells. They orient toward you the way plants orient toward light, except there is no light here to orient toward, and so they orient toward the nearest equivalent: the disturbance. The new thing. The large, warm, loud thing that breathes and sweats and moves with such violent, hurried purpose through their slow and patient world.

And they are patient. God, they are patient.

They have been patient since before the pumps were installed. Since before the pipes were laid. Since before the first human walked into this cavern with a lamp and a blueprint and the industrial instinct to build something in a place that was not yet finished being a place. They were here when the rock was warmer. They were here when the water was hotter. They were here when the cavern was raw — when the floor was sharp and the walls were glass and the ceiling was still dripping for the first time, not the ten-thousandth time, and the air was so thick with sulfur that nothing else could breathe in it. They were here, and they breathed it. They adapted. They always adapt. That is what they do. That is what they are.

The pumps came, and the pumps were warm, and the crabs gathered around them. The pipes came, and the pipes grew algae, and the crabs ate the algae. The catwalks came, and the catwalks collected condensation, and the crabs lined the edges of the catwalks and drank from the thin streams that ran along the grating. The settling tanks came, and the settling tanks bloomed with bacteria, and the crabs bloomed with the settling tanks. They did not resist the works. They incorporated them. The machines became part of the ecosystem. The pipes became cliffs. The pumps became hearths. The catwalks became ridges, elevated pathways above the warm pools, and the crabs walked them the way they had always walked the flow-lines on the walls — slowly, with purpose, in single file, going from one feeding ground to the next.

They are the works. Not metaphorically. Not poetically. They are the living component of the system, the warm thing inside the machine. The pumps move the water. The pipes carry the water. The tanks filter the water. And the crabs — the crabs tend. They eat the things that would clog the filters. They consume the algae that would choke the pipes. They break down the organic matter that falls into the system — a dropped glove, a scrap of cloth, the small deaths of the cavern — and they return it to the water as something simpler, something the bacteria can use, something the system can process. They are not maintenance workers. They are not a janitorial staff. They are an organ. They are the liver of the cavern, the stomach, the slow metabolic process by which the works digest the world.

---

Listen.

The pumps thrum — a low, continuous note, felt more than heard, the heartbeat of the machinery. The pipes tick and groan — the sound of metal expanding and contracting, of water moving through constricted spaces, of pressure building and releasing. The water drips — a random percussion, arhythmic, eternal, each drop a small, sharp sound that rings against the stone and fades and is replaced by another, and another, and another.

And underneath it all: the rustling.

It is the sound of thousands of small legs on stone. On steel. On other crabs. It is the sound of claws waving through air and water and the thin film of algae that covers every surface. It is the sound of shells touching shells, of bodies shifting, of the slow, constant rearrangement of a colony that is never still, never silent, never quite the same from one moment to the next. It is dry. It is soft. It is everywhere.

It is the sound of the cavern breathing.

Not the air moving in and out — though that happens too, a slow thermal draft that pulls the hot air upward and draws the cooler air in from the entrance — but the sound of the place itself, the living place, the place that is half rock and half machine and half crab (and the math of it doesn't work, it adds up to more than one, because the place is more than the sum of its parts, because it is alive in a way that none of its parts are alive alone). The rustling is the sound of awareness. Of a system knowing itself. Of thousands of small, eyeless, soft-shelled bodies processing information — heat, vibration, chemistry, the presence of the large warm thing on the catwalk — and responding, not as individuals, but as a collective, a single distributed intelligence that thinks in the language of leg-movements and claw-waves and the slow, tidal rearrangement of bodies across the floor.

They are thinking. They have always been thinking. But their thoughts are slow — so slow that you cannot perceive them happening. A thought might take an hour to form. A decision might take a day. The decision to move from one pump to another, to abandon a feeding ground that has been depleted, to send a stream of bodies along a pipe to colonize a new section of the works — these are not decisions made by any single crab. They are decisions made by the colony, emerging from the accumulated sensations of thousands of bodies, the way a weather system emerges from the accumulated motion of molecules. The rustling is the sound of cognition. It is the sound of a mind that is not located in any single brain but distributed across an entire floor, an entire cavern, an entire species, thinking its slow, warm, dark thoughts in a language of movement and touch.

---

They know things.

They know the temperature of every surface in the cavern to a precision that your instruments cannot match. They know which pumps are running and which are not by the vibration in the floor. They know the chemical composition of the water in each pool — which ones are safe to drink, which ones are mineral-heavy, which ones carry the faint, acrid signature of a developing blockage in the pipes. They know the schedule of the works — not as a schedule, not as a set of times and tasks, but as a rhythm, a pattern of sounds and temperatures and flows that repeats and repeats and occasionally breaks its repetition, and when it breaks, they notice. They always notice.

They know each other. Not as individuals — there is no individuality here, not in the way you understand it — but as positions in the colony, as nodes in the network, as warm spots in the collective body. When a crab dies, the others know. Not because they find the body — though they do — but because the network has changed. A node has gone silent. A gap has opened. And the colony responds: the gap is filled, the dead body is consumed, the network heals itself. There is no grief. There is only adjustment. The system reconfigures. The colony continues.

They know you.

They knew you were coming before you knew you were coming. They felt your footsteps on the approach — the long, sloping tunnel that leads down from the surface, the one with the electric lights that flicker and the air that gets warmer with every step. They felt you descending. They felt the change in air pressure as the door was opened, the brief, cool intrusion of surface air, the closing of the door, and then the slow equalization as the cavern reclaimed its atmosphere and the heat and the moisture closed around you like a fist.

They felt you enter the space. They felt your weight on the first catwalk — a new vibration, a new pattern, a new set of frequencies that their legs interpreted and their claws tasted and their collective mind catalogued and considered. They do not fear you. Fear is a luxury of creatures that can be hurt. You cannot hurt the colony. You can step on individual crabs — you probably have, you will, the floor is thick with them and your boots are heavy — and the individual crabs will die, and the colony will consume them, and the network will close the gap, and the colony will continue. You are too large to threaten it and too small to destroy it. You are a weather event. You are a warm front moving through. You are noted, and accounted for, and allowed to continue.

But they watch. They always watch. The claws orient toward you as you move. The filaments wave. The rustling rises and falls — not in response to your movement, but in parallel with it, a kind of echo, a kind of translation, as the colony processes the information you provide and incorporates it into its slow, ongoing model of the world. You are a variable. You are a data point. You are a warm, loud, fast-moving thing that does not belong here but that has entered here, and the colony is patient, and the colony will watch, and the colony will learn.

---

They are waiting.

Not for anything in particular. Not for you to leave, though your departure will be noted and processed and incorporated into the model. Not for the pumps to stop, though they have survived pump failures before — the colony has memory, chemical memory, stored in the bacterial mats and the mineral deposits and the accumulated wisdom of generations of crabs that have lived and died and been consumed and been replaced by other crabs that carry, in their bodies, the chemical echoes of what their predecessors knew. Not for the cavern to finish cooling, though it will cool, eventually, in geological time, and the colony will adapt, as it has always adapted, shrinking with the heat, retreating to the deepest, warmest places, becoming smaller, becoming slower, becoming something new.

They are waiting because waiting is what they do. It is their nature. It is the mode of existence that the heat and the darkness and the abundance have produced in them. They wait the way the rock waits — for the water to dissolve it, for the minerals to crystallize, for the slow, patient processes of geology to do their work. They wait the way the water waits — to be heated, to be pumped, to be filtered, to be used, to fall as condensation, to gather in pools, to evaporate, to condense, to fall again. They wait the way the cavern waits — for nothing, for everything, for the next drip, the next shift, the next warm body to enter the space and be noted and be watched and be, for a brief time, part of the system.

They are the oldest thing here. Older than the pumps. Older than the pipes. Older than the concrete pads and the steel grating and the wire-mesh cage around the substation. They are the first colonizers of this warm, wet, dark place, and they will be the last. When the pumps have rusted to nothing and the pipes have collapsed and the catwalks have fallen into the pools and the cavern has cooled to the temperature of the surrounding rock, they will still be here. Diminished. Slowed. Changed into something that would not be recognized by the crabs that cluster now around the warm bases of the machines. But here. Still here. Still patient. Still watching with whatever senses they have evolved to replace the ones they lost. Still shifting, slowly, in their slow, patient way, across a floor that is, at last, finished deciding what it wants to be.

---

And in the meantime: the rustling. The slow, continuous rustling of thousands of small legs on stone, on steel, on each other. The sound of the place breathing. The sound of a mind thinking its slow, dark, mineral thoughts. The sound of life — not as you know it, not as movement and urgency and the bright, frantic pulse of sunlight — but as it is here, in the warm dark, at the bottom of the world, where the rock is still soft and the water is still rising and the heat is still coming up from below and everything is patient and everything is slow and everything is alive in the way that a machine is alive, in the way that a system is alive, in the way that a colony of pale, eyeless, soft-shelled crabs is alive: completely, and quietly, and without end.
