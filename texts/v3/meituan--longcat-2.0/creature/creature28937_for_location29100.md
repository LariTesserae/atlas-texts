# The Vey

---

## The Hands First

You notice the hands first.

They are small hands — smaller than you expect, given the scale of the city — with long, tapered fingers that seem to have too many joints, or perhaps the right number of joints moving in too many directions. The palms are broad relative to the fingers, padded with a thick, calloused skin that has the same pale, faintly luminous quality as the wood itself, as if the boundary between the hand and the wall it touches is a negotiation rather than a fact. The nails are not nails. They are something harder, something that grows in a slight curve, something that can score the outer wood without breaching it, and the Vey keep them carefully shaped — not trimmed, *shaped*, worn against a stone to a specific profile that varies by trade and by individual preference.

The hands are never still.

Even at rest — if the Vey truly rest, which is not clear — the fingers move in small, exploratory patterns, tracing the air the way a blind person traces a face, reading something invisible. When a Vey presses a palm flat against a wall, the fingers spread wide, the pads making contact with the wood, and the hand *listens*. It reads the pulse. It reads the moisture content, the temperature, the subtle vibration of sap moving through channels deep below the surface. A Vey can tell you the health of a building by touching it the way a doctor tells you the health of a body by taking your pulse — not approximately, not vaguely, but precisely, diagnostically, with a specificity that has no translation into surface language.

The hands are the primary interface. Everything else follows.

## The Body

The Vey are small. Not child-small — adult-small, fully grown, a meter to perhaps a meter and a half in height, with a compact, dense build that suggests efficiency rather than frailty. They are light. Their bones are not the heavy, mineralized columns of surface vertebrates but something more flexible, more cartilaginous, a structural compromise between rigidity and resilience that allows them to move through the three-dimensional lattice of the city with an ease that looks, to an outsider, like a kind of controlled falling.

They climb the way surface people walk. The bridges between the trunks are not railinged — there is no need — and a Vey will step off a forty-meter span without hesitation, catching a handhold on a passing branch, swinging, releasing, landing on a lower platform with the casual precision of a bird changing perches. Their feet are as expressive as their hands, the toes long and grip-capable, the soles padded with the same thick, sensitive skin. They do not wear shoes. Shoes would deaden the contact, and the contact is everything.

Their skin is pale — the pale of things that have never seen sun, a translucent white-grey through which you can see, faintly, the shadow of structures beneath: not veins, exactly, but something analogous, a network of fine, branching channels that carry not blood but a fluid that is closer to sap, that shares the same faint luminescence as the wood, that pulses with the same slow, arrhythmic beat. The skin is cool to the touch, cooler than you expect, and it has a texture that is not quite skin — slightly rough, slightly fibrous, like the surface of a leaf, like the bark of a young branch.

Their faces are broad, flat, with wide-set eyes that are large relative to the skull — not enormous, not insectoid, but large enough to gather every photon of the bioluminescent light, and behind them a reflective layer, a *tapetum*, that gives their eyes a faint, ghostly glow when caught at the right angle, a secondary luminescence that mirrors the walls. The eyes are dark — deep brown, deep grey, a black that has no surface — and they do not blink the way surface eyes blink. They have a nictitating membrane, a secondary eyelid that sweeps horizontally, and it moves constantly, a slow, rhythmic wiping that keeps the eye moist in the humid air and gives the Vey a quality of perpetual, calm attention.

They have no hair. Instead, fine, filamentous growths emerge from the scalp and the forearms and the spine — not hair, not fur, but something closer to the root-hair of the organism itself, pale and soft and faintly mobile, swaying in air currents too subtle for surface senses to detect. These filaments are sensory organs. They read the air the way the hands read the wall — its temperature, its humidity, its chemical composition, the presence of spore, the proximity of other living things. A Vey can feel you coming before they see you, can read your emotional state in the chemicals your skin releases, can tell you what you ate for breakfast by the smell of your breath.

They do not speak loudly. Their voices are soft, low, resonant in a register that carries well in the three-dimensional acoustics of the lattice without being loud. The language is tonal but not in the way surface tonal languages are tonal — the pitch carries meaning, but so does the *texture* of the sound, the way it is shaped in the mouth and throat, the subtle harmonics that the Vey produce by engaging resonating chambers that surface vocal anatomy does not possess. A single Vey word can contain layers of meaning — denotative, connotative, emotional, relational — that would require a paragraph of surface language to unpack.

And they hum. Not consciously, not performatively — they hum the way surface people breathe, automatically, involuntarily, a low, continuous tone that is not quite a voice and not quite a song but something in between, a sound that resonates with the hum of the engines and the hum of the organism and the hum of the city until all the hums become one hum, and the Vey are part of it, and it is part of them, and the boundary between the individual and the collective is, in this at least, purely notional.

## The Relationship

The Vey do not live *in* the Rootworks. They live *as* the Rootworks.

This is not metaphor. This is not poetry. This is the most literal description of their existence that language can manage.

They are not separate from the organism. They never were. The organism grew them — or they grew from it, or they grew alongside it, or they grew *into* it, and the direction of causality is as unclear here as it is everywhere else in the city. The Vey are not parasites. They are not symbionts. They are *organs* — specialized structures that the organism has developed, or that have developed within the organism, for the purpose of interacting with the world at a scale and with a precision that the organism itself cannot achieve. The Vey are the organism's hands. The Vey are the organism's eyes. The Vey are the organism's way of knowing itself.

And the organism is the Vey's body. The organism is the Vey's home, the Vey's food, the Vey's medicine, the Vey's god, the Vey's parent, the Vey's child, the Vey's self. The pulse that the Vey feel in their chests is the pulse of the organism, and the pulse of the organism is the pulse of the Vey, and when a Vey dies — as they do, as all things do — the body is given to the organism, placed against the raw wood at the frontier, and the wood grows over it, absorbs it, incorporates it, and the Vey becomes structure, becomes wall, becomes floor, becomes the city itself, and the pulse continues.

This is not a funeral. This is a homecoming.

The Vey do not have a word for "nature." The concept does not exist. There is no separation between the natural and the artificial, the grown and the made, the living and the built. The city is not a city in the surface sense — it is not a place where people live. It is a place that *lives*, and the people are part of the living, and the living is part of the people, and the distinction is not blurred so much as it is simply absent, a category error, a question that has no meaning.

They do not have a word for "property" either. The organism does not recognize property. The Vey do not recognize property. A dwelling is not owned — it is *inhabited*, and the inhabitation is a relationship, not a transaction. You do not buy a trunk. You do not rent a bridge. You find a space that is growing in a way that suits you, and you move in, and you tend it, and it tends you, and over time — over years, over decades — the space shapes itself to your body and your body shapes itself to the space, and the boundary between the dwelling and the dweller becomes, like every other boundary here, a negotiation rather than a fact.

## The Work

The Vey work. Not because they must — there is no coercion here, no wage, no survival imperative in the surface sense — but because the work is the relationship, and the relationship is the life, and the life is the work.

The pruning is the most visible work, and the most important. Every morning — or what passes for morning in the civic light-cycle — the pruners go out with their short blades and their shaped nails and their bare, sensitive hands, and they walk the city, and they touch the walls, and they read the growth, and they cut where cutting is needed. Not to control the organism — you cannot control something the size of a continent — but to *communicate* with it. Every cut is a signal. Every cut is a word in a conversation that has been going on for longer than the Vey have had language, a conversation between the organism and its specialized organs about the shape of the city, the direction of growth, the allocation of resources, the future.

The pruners know the organism the way a conductor knows an orchestra — not by commanding it but by listening to it, by feeling where the energy is, where the growth wants to go, and then by making small adjustments, small suggestions, small cuts that redirect the flow without stopping it. A good pruner can guide a trunk into a bridge. A good pruner can coax a wall into a window. A good pruner can feel, through the wood, the presence of a sap-channel running deep below the surface, and can cut to within a hair's breadth of it without breaching it, and this is not skill in the surface sense — it is *intimacy*, it is the knowledge that comes from a lifetime of touching the same living thing and being touched by it.

The industrial work is no different in kind. The weavers who work the looms that turn hair-moss into fabric are not operating machines — they are *collaborating* with the organism, feeding it the raw materials it needs, guiding the growth of the moss into patterns that serve both the organism's needs and the Vey's. The press-workers who shape wood-pulp into paper are not manufacturing — they are *cultivating*, encouraging the organism to grow in specific ways, to produce specific textures, specific densities, specific qualities of material. The sap-refiners who extract and process the organism's exudate are not extracting — they are *milking*, they are *harvesting*, they are engaging in a reciprocal exchange in which the organism gives and the Vey give and the giving is the point.

The engines are the most complex collaboration. The brass and iron machinery bolted into the living wood is not imposed on the organism — it is *integrated* with it, sealed with the organism's own resin, fed by the organism's own sap, and the Vey who maintain the engines must understand both the metal and the wood, both the mechanical and the organic, both the made and the grown. They must be able to feel, through the vibration of the machinery, the state of the organism's metabolism. They must be able to read, through the color and viscosity of the sap, the health of the engines. They must be fluent in two languages — the language of industry and the language of growth — and they must be able to translate between them in real time, constantly, the way a person translates between breathing and walking without thinking about it.

## The Knowledge

The Vey do not write things down.

Not because they cannot — they have paper, they have ink, they have the physical capacity for literacy — but because writing implies a separation between the knower and the known, between the knowledge and the thing known, and this separation does not exist for the Vey. Their knowledge is not abstract. It is not theoretical. It is *embodied* — it lives in the hands, in the feet, in the filaments, in the pulse, in the hum. You cannot write down what a pruner knows about the organism any more than you can write down what your own liver knows about your blood. The knowledge is not in the head. It is in the body. It is in the relationship.

This does not mean the knowledge is imprecise. It is, in fact, more precise than anything surface science can produce, because it is knowledge that is continuously updated, continuously tested, continuously verified by the organism itself. A Vey does not theorize about the health of a building — a Vey *feels* it, directly, the way you feel the temperature of your own skin. A Vey does not calculate the load-bearing capacity of a bridge — a Vey *knows* it, the way you know, without thinking, whether your leg will hold your weight. The knowledge is immediate, embodied, relational, and it is passed from one Vey to another not through instruction but through *contact* — hand to hand, palm to wall, body to body, the way heat passes from a warm thing to a cool thing, the way the pulse passes from the organism to the Vey and back again.

The elders — and there are elders, though age is measured differently here, not in years but in *depth*, in the degree to which a Vey has integrated with the organism — the elders are not teachers in the surface sense. They are *resources*. They are living libraries, living archives, living repositories of the organism's memory, which is also the city's memory, which is also the Vey's memory, and when a young Vey needs to know something — how to read a particular kind of growth, how to respond to a particular kind of stress in the wood, how to guide a trunk that is growing in an unusual direction — they do not go to a book. They go to an elder. They press their hands to the elder's hands. They press their foreheads to the elder's forehead. And the knowledge passes between them, not as words, not as images, but as *feeling*, as *knowing*, as a direct transfer of embodied experience that leaves the young Vey changed — not informed, *changed*, the way a muscle is changed by use, the way a bone is changed by load.

## The Quiet and the Sound

The Vey are quiet people. Not silent — they speak, they hum, they make the small sounds of living — but quiet in the way that deep water is quiet, in the way that a forest floor is quiet, in the way that a place is quiet when it has never known the sound of wind.

They move through the city like the animals move through the city — softly, efficiently, three-dimensionally, with a minimum of disturbance. Footsteps on the rhizome pavement are barely audible. Conversations are conducted in low tones that carry only as far as they need to. The Vey do not shout. The Vey do not slam doors — there are no doors to slam, only openings that the organism has grown into doorways, and they do not close so much as they *constrict*, the wood tightening around the aperture until it is sealed, and then relaxing again when passage is needed.

But the quiet is not emptiness. The quiet is *fullness*. The quiet is the hum of the engines and the hum of the organism and the hum of the Vey themselves, all resonating together, all part of the same continuous vibration, and within that hum there is information — vast, complex, constantly changing information about the state of the city, the state of the organism, the state of the world. The Vey read the hum the way surface people read the weather — they can tell you when something is wrong, when something is changing, when something is about to happen, by the subtle shifts in frequency and amplitude that ripple through the city like pressure changes through an atmosphere.

And sometimes — rarely, unpredictably, at no time that anyone can identify in advance — the hum *changes*. It deepens. It slows. It becomes something that is not a sound but a *presence*, a weight, a pressure, and the Vey stop what they are doing and press their hands to the walls and close their eyes and *listen*, and in the listening they hear something that is not a voice and not a word but a *communication*, a message from the organism itself, from the deep wood, from the heart of the world, and the message is not translatable into any language because it is not information in the surface sense — it is *experience*, it is the organism sharing with its organs something that it has felt, something that it has known, something that it has become, and the Vey receive it, and they are changed by it, and they go back to their work, and the hum returns to its normal frequency, and the city continues.

## The Frontier

The Vey are drawn to the edges.

Not all of them — most are content in the deep city, in the old trunks, in the settled lattice where the pulse is steady and the light is constant and the hum is familiar. But some — the young, the restless, the ones whose filaments twitch toward the new, the ones who can taste the future on the air — are drawn to the frontier, to the places where the city is still becoming, where the wood is still raw and damp and growing visibly, where the air is warmer and the green smell is stronger and the light is brighter.

They go to build. They go to tend. They go to *participate* in the act of creation, to place their hands on the raw wood and feel it respond to their touch, to guide the first trunks upward, to train the first bridges across the gaps, to lay the first rhizome pavement over the bare substrate. This is the most sacred work — not sacred in the religious sense, but sacred in the sense of *fundamental*, *essential*, *the thing upon which everything else depends*. The frontier is where the city is born. The frontier is where the future is made. The frontier is where the organism is most alive, most active, most *present*, and the Vey who work there describe a feeling that has no surface equivalent — a sense of being *inside* the act of creation, of being not the creator but the *instrument* of creation, of being the hands through which the organism reaches into the dark and pulls something new into being.

The animals gather at the frontier too — the pale arthropods, the furred things, the vast slow shadows in the upper reaches — and they watch, and they wait, and they seem to know what is coming before it arrives, and the Vey watch them, and learn from them, and the animals are not afraid, and the Vey are not afraid, and the frontier extends, and the city grows, and the pulse continues, and the hum holds, and the light — the strange, constant, sourceless light of a world that has never seen the sun — holds, and will hold, and keeps holding, the way a heartbeat holds, the way a breath holds, the way a living thing holds to life: not by force, not by will, but by the simple, continuous, unremarkable act of going on.

---

## The Rule

There is one thing the Vey do not do, and they do not do it the way you do not breathe water — not as a choice, not as a prohibition, but as a *condition of existence*.

They do not cut deep.

A child knows this before it knows language. A child pressed against the wall of its dwelling — pressed against the organism, pressed against the world — can feel the difference between the surface wood and the deep wood, can feel the sap moving in its channels, can feel the pulse of the heartwood, and the child knows, in its body, in its bones, in the sap that runs through its own branching channels, that the deep wood is not to be touched. Not because it is forbidden. Because it is *self*. Because to cut the deep wood is to cut your own heart, to breach your own veins, to wound the thing that is keeping you alive, and the knowledge of this is not learned — it is *grown*, it is *cultivated*, it is as much a part of the Vey as their hands, as their filaments, as their pulse.

And so the city grows outward. And so the frontier extends. And so the Vey tend and prune and guide and build, and the organism grows, and the city lives, and the pulse continues, and the hum holds, and the light holds, and the quiet holds, and the strange, deep, living quiet of the Rootworks holds, and will hold, and keeps holding, in the dark, in the deep, in the place the world above has forgotten or never knew, where the Vey live and work and die and return, and the organism breathes, and the city breathes, and the breath is one breath, and the life is one life, and the living goes on.
