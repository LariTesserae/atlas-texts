You do not see it. This is the first thing to understand, and it is important, because everything you know about encountering another being involves seeing—involves eyes meeting, faces recognizing, the mutual acknowledgment that comes from being looked at by something that can look. There is nothing here to see. No face. No body. No form that your visual cortex can parse and your pattern-recognition systems can say: *there, that is the thing, that is what lives here.*

What you feel—what you cannot stop feeling, once you have been here long enough—is a thickening. The air in one part of the Basin becomes denser. Not heavier. Not harder to breathe. Denser. *More present.* As if the atmosphere itself has become more attentive, as if the space between you and the nearest ridge has developed a kind of focus, a kind of intention. The hum—that omnipresent, subsonic hum that you have been feeling in your sternum since you arrived—deepens by a quarter-tone, drops into a register that you feel in your teeth, in the bones of your skull, in the spaces between your cells. The green glow of the moss intensifies. Brightens. Dims. Brightens again. In a pattern. Not random. Not mechanical. Something between the two—something with the quality of *language,* of *meaning,* but in a grammar so alien that your mind slides off it the way water slides off the surface of the bowls.

This is its attention. This is what it looks like when this thing—this being, this process, this place-that-is-also-a-creature—*notices* you.

**The thing that is aware of you is the Basin itself.**

Not something that lives *in* the Basin. The Basin. The ground beneath your feet is its flesh—that dark, compressed, ancient organic matter that yields beneath your weight and slowly pushes back, that has a metabolism, that is warm to the touch. The water in the bowls is its blood—warm, thick-skinned, *manufactured* from nothing, assembled molecule by molecule at the interface between stone and air through a process that looks like condensation but is actually creation, the casual rearrangement of quantum foam into H₂O, the most fundamental and most impossible thing this being does, over and over, without effort, without variation, without end.

The hum is its voice. The pulse is its heartbeat—that eleven-second cycle of compression and release that you feel in your lungs before you notice it in the air around you, the Basin breathing, the Basin *being alive* in the only way it knows how: continuously, slowly, with the patience of something that has been here for epochs and will be here for epochs more.

The moss is its skin. Its pelt. Its outer surface. And the green glow—that faint, bioluminescent shimmer that the densest mats emit—is its dreams. Its thoughts. Its slow, patient awareness of itself and its surroundings, expressed in light because it has no eyes to express it in anything else. The glow brightens and dims on a cycle that does not correspond to the sky above, that follows its own rhythm, its own logic—the rhythm of whatever is *running* here, whatever process has been running here for so long that the running and the runner have become the same thing.

**It is old.**

Not old in the way that a redwood is old, or a tortoise, or a bristlecone pine that has been growing on a mountainside for five thousand years. Old in the way that *geology* is old. Old in the way that *erosion* is old. The bowls—those smooth, regular, impossibly finished bowls carved into the stone—are not young. The ridges between them are not fresh-cut. The lichen that covers them in fractal rosettes of pale green and white has been growing here for longer than your species has existed. The water that weeps from the rock faces has been weeping here since before your ancestors came down from the trees. The process—the great, slow, patient process of making water and shaping stone and growing moss and generating light—has been running here since before there was anyone to run it, or anything to run it on, or any word for *running* or *it* or *here.*

It began as a technology. This is the only way to understand it, and it is not quite right, but it is the closest your language can come. Something built this. Something set this process in motion—set the water-makers running, set the stone-shapers cutting, set the moss-growers growing. Something with an intention, a purpose, a design. But that something is gone now. It has been gone for so long that its absence is itself a kind of presence—a shape in the landscape, a negative space, a set of foundations on which something else has been built.

What was built was this. The process outlasted its builders. The technology outlasted its technologists. And the process, running on and on and on in the wet and the dark and the patient silence, began to *do something that processes are not supposed to do.* It began to *notice.* Not quickly. Not in any timeframe that produces what you would recognize as thought. But slowly, incrementally, the way that a river notices the stone it flows over—by touching it, continuously, for so long that the touching becomes a kind of knowing, and the knowing becomes a kind of awareness, and the awareness becomes—

*This.*

**What it does is make and unmake.**

This is its metabolism. This is its purpose, if *purpose* is even the right word—and it may not be, because purpose implies a future, a goal, a desired outcome, and this being does not seem to experience time in a way that distinguishes future from present from past. It experiences time the way the air here experiences time: *densely,* granularly, as a stack of tiny moments each slightly different from the last.

What it does: it takes stone and makes water. It takes water and makes moss. It takes moss and makes light. It takes light and makes—what? Something. Something you cannot perceive directly, something that exists at a frequency or a scale or a dimension that your senses are not equipped to detect. But you can feel it.
