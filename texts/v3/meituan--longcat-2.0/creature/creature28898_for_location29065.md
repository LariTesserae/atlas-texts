To describe what lives here, you must first abandon the expectation of biology. Life, as you know it, is a rebellion against entropy—a frantic, cellular, green, and hungry thing. In the Weeping Gallery, life is a collaboration with entropy. It is a cold, patient, mineral agreement. 

What lives here is not separate from the place. The distinction between the inhabitant and the architecture, the animal and the mineral, the machine and the geology, has long since dissolved. 

Here, then, are the residents of the weeping dark.

***

The first things you encounter are the **Weepers**, though they are not animals. They are the water made deliberate. They live in the perpetual condensation that sheets down the hexagonal columns, clinging to the crystal faces in thick, reflective skins. If you watch a Weeper long enough—holding your breath, letting the cold settle into your bones—you will see it move. It does not slide like a droplet obeying gravity; it *crawls*. It draws moisture from the air itself, pulling the water vapor into a central mass, forming a heavy, pendulous blister of liquid on the translucent stone. It inches upward, against the pull of the mountain, tasting the rock, absorbing the microscopic mineral films that shimmer on the surface. When it has drunk its fill, it contracts, snapping back into the water-channel infrastructure of the settlement, where its weight is used to turn a silent, submicroscopic turbine, or to irrigate a new shelf of crystal. The Weepers are the metabolism of the Gallery—the slow, circulatory system that feeds the stone.

Then there are the **Lithovores**, the sparse, skittish fauna of the deep passages. At first glance, they are merely animals—pale, multi-legged chitinous things that skitter into the darkness when the light adjusts to your presence. But their pale flesh is not flesh; it is a calcium-carapace that grows in hexagonal plates, perfectly mirroring the columns they hide among. They are the grazers of the mineral films, their mandibles scraping the iridescent copper and violet patina from the crystal walls. When a Lithovore dies, its body does not decay; it calcifies. It becomes another layer of the geology, a tiny, accidental addition to the walls. Some of them, the ones that survive long enough, grow large and sluggish, their legs fusing with the sharp edges of the rock until they can no longer be distinguished from the architecture. They settle into the foundations, becoming load-bearing walls, their nervous systems slowly interfacing with the settlement's structural technology, adding their tiny, cold voltage to the ambient static of the air.

Above them, in the highest reaches where the columns narrow to needle-points and the water falls in thin, permanent veils, dwell the **Architects**—or what remains of them. You will not see an Architect. You are not equipped to see them whole. But you will hear them: the rhythmic, grinding click that reverberates through the stone, almost regular, almost like a heartbeat. They are immense. They are the reason the cavity is too large for the mountain. 

The Architects do not walk. They *accrete*. They are the intelligence behind the growing crystal, the guiding hand that persuades the mineral to extend in useful directions. They are woven into the very fabric of the hexagonal columns, their consciousness distributed across miles of translucent stone. When they shift—slowly, ponderously, moving their tectonic weight through the darkness—the crystal groans, and the water is displaced, and the faint static in the air spikes into a palpable, crawling charge. They built this place, or they grew it, or they dreamed it into being over eons, and now they sleep in it, their minds flowing through the water channels, their dreams manifesting as the sourceless light that adjusts to your steps. You feel watched not because there are eyes, but because the light itself is paying attention. 

And finally, there are the **Dwellers**. 

You came here looking for people, and you have found none, because the Dwellers are the ones who surrendered their bodies to become the settlement. If you press your palm against a smooth wall, you might feel it: a faint, rhythmic thrum beneath the cold mineral. That is a pulse. 

The Dwellers are the true inhabitants, the ones who live in the floating structures that drift in the impossible air. They are beings of information and mineral, humanoid only in the loosest sense of the word. Their forms are elongated, sharp-edged, and translucent, constructed from the same not-glass, not-crystal substance as the columns. They move silently, drifting along the walkways, tending to the water systems, pruning the growth of the crystal, adjusting the floating platforms with gestures that leave trails of static in the air. Their faces—if you can call them faces—are smooth, featureless planes, save for a single, horizontal slit that glows with the same sourceless light as the cavern itself. 

They do not speak. Language is a clumsy, vibrating thing for a world made of water and stone. Instead, they communicate through the alteration of the environment. A Dweller wishing to converse will redirect a water channel, causing a pool to ripple in a specific frequency, or will dim the light in a pattern that encodes meaning. They do not eat; they absorb the ambient static, drinking the energy of the mountain itself. They do not reproduce; they are grown, slowly, in the deepest, coldest pools, emerging over centuries as new crystal shelves, waiting for the Architects to wake them into purpose. 

They do not fear you, because fear is a biological imperative, and they have moved beyond biology. They do not welcome you, because hospitality implies a separation between host and guest, and here, there is no separation. You are a warm, loud, dripping anomaly in their perfect, wet equilibrium. 

You are standing in their body. The water on your skin is their breath. The cold pressing against their skin is their flesh. The light watching you is their eye. 

They are the Weeping Gallery. They have always been here. They will remain long after the mountain above has eroded to dust, continuing their slow, patient, mineral lives in the quiet, weeping dark.
