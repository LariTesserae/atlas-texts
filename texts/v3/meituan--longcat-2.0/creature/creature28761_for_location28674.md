To live in the Loom-Caverns of Suth-Vael is to live as a whisper inside a breathing lung. The inhabitants—both the human and the otherwise—do not occupy the space so much as they are permitted to exist within its slow, damp metabolism. 

The people of Suth-Vael are the quietest residents, though they are the ones who build. They are small in number, a few dozen souls who have learned that survival here requires a radical diminishment of the self. Their bodies have adapted to the deep stone: their skin is pale and perpetually slick with condensation, almost translucent in the amber glow, as though the darkness is slowly claiming them. Their eyes are wide, adapted to the near-absolute dark, and they move with a fluid, gliding gait, their feet barely lifting from the moss, their hands trailing along the breathing walls. They wear the cavern—layered cloaks of processed moss-fiber and iridescent beetle-shell that rustle softly, like dry leaves, making them indistinguishable from the shifting shadows. 

They are not primitive; they are hyper-attuned. They read the cavern the way a sailor reads the sea. They know the moods of the beetle-swarms, reading in their clicking, scattering movements the approach of the wet season or a shift in the deep cold. They know which roots carry pure water and which carry something else—something warmer, something that hums. They do not bury their dead; they offer them to the pale, many-legged things that move in columns along the root networks, the sanitation system of the pocket, returning their flesh to the cavern's endless cycle. They live in deference to the architecture, weaving their wisteria-membranes and harvesting only the moss that offers itself, leaving the root-throne untouched save for their small, desperate offerings of water and shell.

But the people are merely the consciousness of the place, not its bulk. The true citizens of Suth-Vael are the arthropods. The beetles are the first wave—small, dark-shelled, and innumerable. They are the living carpet, a clicking, rustling tide that flows around intruding feet like water around a stone, their legs moving in patterns too fast for the human eye to track. They are the sound of the pocket, a dry, papery whisper that fills the silence between the dripping water. 

Beneath them, in the walls and the root-veins, live the pale, translucent centipede-things. They are the custodians, the recyclers, moving in endless processions along paths worn smooth by generations of passage. They consume the dead, the shed, the rotting, keeping the closed system pristine. They are the immune system of the pocket, and they do not distinguish between a fallen moth and a fallen person.

Then there are the moths—*the forgetting*. They are the most ethereal inhabitants, living only a few days, doing nothing but reproducing. When the conditions are right, they rise in silent, fluttering clouds, their wings dusted with a bioluminescent powder that glows like sparks when disturbed. They are the pocket’s dream-state, a luminous confusion that blurs the edges of reality. To follow them is to be unmade, to wander into the deep places where the roots converge and lose the tether of the world above. They are the siren-song of the dark, beautiful and deeply dangerous.

And beneath it all, woven into the very stone and water, is the flora. The architecture is alive. The massive, cordyceps-like fungal bodies that form the pillars and beams are not dead wood; they are slow, woody giants, their caps spreading overhead to catch the weeping condensation. The wisteria-vines that form the walls are breathing membranes, filtering the thick air, hosting tiny bromeliads that cup the water like chalices. In each of these tiny pools lives a microcosmos—rotifers, larvae, crustaceans—entire worlds existing in a thimble of cold water, oblivious to the greater world that holds them.

But the most pervasive inhabitant is the moss. It is the skin of Suth-Vael. It carpets the floor in deep, spongy mats that sigh underfoot, and it climbs the walls in deliberate, strange formations. It grows in directions that defy gravity and water flow, forming columns and ridges that look almost like writing. It pulses with a warm, amber bioluminescence that follows its own, unknowable time. The old people say the moss is trying to speak, that it is the medium through which the pocket thinks. 

And what thinks in the pocket? 

Deep below, in the warm rock where the root networks converge, lives the entity that the cavern grew around. It is not plant, not animal, not fungus. It is something older. It is the pulse that the people feel in the stone, the low rhythmic thrum that might be water moving through the earth, or might be a heartbeat. It is the intelligence that keeps the water sterile and the cold uniform. It is the voice that speaks from the root-throne on certain nights—a sound that is not a voice but carries the shape of one, speaking a language that dissolves upon waking. 

The people of Suth-Vael live in the palm of this entity. They are its nerve-endings, its way of knowing the surface world. The moths are its dreams. The beetles are its pulse. The moss is its slow, deliberate attempt to articulate the unarticulateable. To live in Suth-Vael is to be a cell in a vast, subterranean organism, breathing its air, drinking its water, and listening, always listening, to the deep, rhythmic pulse of the thing that dreams in the roots.
