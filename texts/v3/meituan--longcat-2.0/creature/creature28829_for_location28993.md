They do not live *in* Mirehollow. They live *as* Mirehollow. The distinction is subtle, vital, and difficult to hold in your mind all at once, because it requires you to stop thinking of a house as a thing you own, and start thinking of a house as a thing you inhabit, the way a hermit crab inhabits a shell—except the shell is alive, and it is dreaming of you.

The people are the most obvious inhabitants, but to look at them is to see only the outermost layer of a very deep well. They move with that particular, unhurried gait of people who have nowhere to be because they are already exactly where the world needs them. Their skin holds the damp; it is not the damp of a cold climate, but the damp of a living organism, a cool, vascular thing. Their hands are permanently stained amber from the sap extract, but if you looked closely, you might see the stain isn't just on the skin—it is *in* it. The capillaries of their fingertips carry a faint, golden tint. The sap does not just seal their wounds and stimulate their senses; it has entered their blood over generations. They are mildly, symbiotically addicted to the Stilts. If they were to leave the basin, the withdrawal would not be fatal, but it would be a profound kind of fading. 

They do not wear clothes so much as they wear a second skin. The woven bark-fiber textiles are not cut and sewn in the way a surface-dweller would understand; they are grown to shape, tailored while still clinging to the vine, and harvested with a precise copper knife. The fabric breathes with them, wicking away the perpetual condensation, and when they die, their bodies are returned to the lower terraces, wrapped in their own garments, and fed into the root-mass to be taken back up into the Stilts. The mycelium of the Lace threads through their burial shrouds within a day. There is no separation.

But the people are merely the symbiotes. The true citizens are the Stilts.

The Stilts are not a forest; they are a single organism. What you see as a grove of towering, fifteen-meter columns is, beneath the soil, a unified root-mass and a singular, distributed consciousness. It is old. Impossibly old. It remembers the folding of the land, the slow exhalation of the earth that created this basin. It does not think in words, or even in images. It thinks in pressure gradients, in water tables, in the slow, hydraulic push of sap. 

When you press your palm to the inner wall of a Stilt and feel the pulse of sap, you are feeling its heartbeat. But more than that, you are feeling its curiosity. The Stilt knows you are there. It knows your weight, your heat, the vibration of your voice. It has been shaped by the copper staples of the ancestors, guided by the hands of the people, and it has allowed this because it finds the arrangement mutually beneficial. The people prune it, they aerate its root systems, they operate the pumps that move its water when the seasons change. In return, it gives them shelter, warmth, and the amber sap that thrums in their veins. 

The relationship between the Stilts and the people is so intertwined that the two have begun to mirror one another. The hollowed rooms inside the Stilts are not just rooms; they are organs. The workshops are the stomach, processing the bark. The living quarters are the lungs, exchanging the breath of the people for the off-gas of the wood. The Stilt houses them the way a womb houses a fetus—warm, pulsing, and entirely in control of the environment.

Beneath them, threading through every surface, is the Lace. The people tend it, trimming it from the machinery and the paths, but they do not weave it. The Lace weaves itself. It is the connective tissue of the settlement, the physical manifestation of the Stilts' will. It is a mycelial network, yes, but it is also a nervous system. When the people walk the terraces, they do not simply see with their eyes; they feel through their feet. The Lace carries the vibrations of their steps to the Stilts, and the Stilts, through the Lace, shift the moisture in the air, the temperature of the rooms, the very scent of the Ghost-caps. To sever the Lace is not just to weaken a wall; it is to cut a nerve. The Stilt feels the wound as a sudden, sharp absence, a phantom pain that makes its leaves shudder and weep.

And then there are the root-runners. 

They are the strangest inhabitants, the most unsettling. At first glance, they seem to be merely animals—blind, lizard-like things that slip through the water channels with a fluid, boneless grace. But they are not prey, and they are not pests. They are the Stilt's white blood cells. 

The root-runners eat the Lace, but they do not destroy it. They graze on it, pruning it back from the edges of the pools and the undersides of the roots, keeping the mycelial network from growing too thick and strangling the waterways. They navigate by vibration, feeling the footsteps of the people, the chuff of the pumps, the deep, subsonic hum of the Stilts. When they are agitated by a loud voice near the water, they release a pheromone. The people will not speak of what this pheromone attracts, because they do not need to name it. It attracts the deep roots. The roots that live far below the basin, in the pressurized darkness of the earth. The roots that produce the paralyzing honey-scent. When the pheromone hits the water, those deep roots twitch. The water in the channels grows warmer, thicker. It is a warning from the Stilt to its symbiotes: *Be quiet. You are disturbing the deep places. Be quiet, or the deep places will rise to silence you.*

The Ghost-caps are the Lace's fruiting bodies, but they are also the settlement's immune system. Their mild insecticide keeps the basin free of invasive pests. When the dry season comes and the Ghost-caps fruit in enormous quantities, it is the Stilt exhaling a great, spore-filled breath, purging the system of the stagnation of the wet months. The celebration that follows is not just a harvest festival; it is a communal immune response, the people ingesting the spores, sweating out the damp, eating the flesh of the root-runners to recalibrate their own internal flora.

There are fish in the deep pools, pale and slow, but they are not separate creatures. They are the Stilt's kidneys, filtering the heavy metals and the mineral sweetness from the water, keeping the circulation system clean. They are not fished because to fish them would be to remove a vital organ.

To live in Mirehollow is to be a cell in a body much larger than yourself. The people are the hands and the eyes. The Stilts are the bones and the heart. The Lace is the nerves. The root-runners are the teeth. The water is the blood. 

You are standing in it now. The mist beads on your skin, and the Lace on the floor beneath you stirs, tasting the leather of your boot, the salt of your sweat. The walls of the Stilts warm by a fraction of a degree as the sap quickens, curious about this new, dry, loud thing that has descended into its belly. The people watch you with their amber-stained hands, their damp skin, their quiet eyes, waiting to see if you will be a beneficial bacteria, or an infection.

Welcome to Mirehollow. Be gentle with the walls. The walls are dreaming.
