To live in the Caldera Works is to occupy a space between the geological and the biological, to exist at the bleeding edge where the raw, molten breath of the earth meets the cold, indifferent sky. Whoever—or whatever—lives here does not merely survive; they are slowly transfigured by the environment, their boundaries dissolving into the heat, the damp, and the endless, grinding industry.

At first glance, the inhabitants are the workers. They are transient flesh, temporary interlopers in a mineral world. They live their lives on a vertical axis, oscillating between the thin, biting cold of the upper levels and the suffocating, tropical humidity of the deep. Their bodies are perpetually in negotiation with the earth. Their lungs must consciously pull in the thin air; their skin must adapt to the constant, warm drizzle of condensation that beads on every surface. They wear the city on their backs—heavy respirators, suits slick with volcanic sweat, boots crunching on the sharp, unweathered basalt. They are the hands of the machine, descending to tend the roaring wellheads and the clattering centrifuges, only to ascend back to their small, identical cells to wait. But even as they live, they are being slowly calcified by the place. Their lungs fill with aerosolized silica; their pores clog with the damp, mineral-rich air. They are, temporarily, part of the Works.

Then there are the ones who stay. The long-timers. They are the ones who have stopped fighting the dampness and learned to breathe with the earth. They move differently—slower, deliberately, attuned to the subsonic hum that vibrates through the rock and into their bones. They have learned to read the subtle shifts in the machinery, to hear the deep reservoir's moods in the hiss of a valve or the groan of a pipe. Over time, they lose their need for the sun; the diffuse, orange glow of the haze-lit nights and the eerie chemical luminescence of the vats become their day. They are no longer visitors; they are symbiotes. And when their human parts finally wear out, the Works claims them entirely. Lowered into the hot, mineral-rich water of the caldera, their bodies do not decay—they dissolve. Their elements join the brine, feeding the plants, feeding the city. They are the only organic matter that truly belongs to the place, returned to the very reservoir that sustained them. In this way, they become permanent residents, their calcium and carbon flowing through the pipes long after their names are forgotten.

Below them, in the warm, lightless arteries of the lower levels, the pale things live. They are the true natives of the deep, born of the condensation and the heat. Whether they are blind, eyeless animals that have evolved in isolation over millennia, or some bizarre, accidental byproduct of the industrial alchemy taking place above them, is a mystery that does not need solving. They are soft, pale whispers in the dark, slithering through the warm, mineral-laden water that channels through the rock. They feed on the earth’s sweat—the lithium, the boron, the silica—and in doing so, they become living extensions of the geothermal reservoir. They are the city’s gut flora, dwelling in the damp, warm stomach of the basalt, utterly indifferent to the steel and concrete that surrounds them. To the workers, they are just another part of the environment, as expected as the dripping water.

High above, on the caldera’s rim, the not-birds circle. They are the inhabitants of the thermal, creatures of the updraft. They are never seen clearly, only as strange silhouettes against the hazy sky, their proportions slightly wrong, their flight too mechanical, too perfect. Perhaps they are merely birds, mutated by the altitude and the heat into something unrecognizable. Perhaps they are something else entirely—heat mirages given fleeting form, or echoes of the volcanic violence that birthed the caldera. They ride the columns of hot air rising from the vents, watching the orange glow of the city with eyes that do not blink, mimicking the industry below with their tireless, circular labor.

But ultimately, the true inhabitant of the Caldera Works is the Works itself. 

It is the entity that truly lives here. It is a creature of basalt and steel, a mechanical leviathan hunkered in the palm of the volcano, plugged directly into the earth’s infinite, fiery heart. It sweats constantly, its skin beaded with condensation as it breathes the thin air and drinks the deep brine. It has a circulatory system of massive pipes, carrying superheated fluids to its processing organs. It has a nervous system of subsonic hums and shifting pressures. It has a digestive tract that breaks down the raw rock and excretes pure, separated minerals. It does not sleep. It does not tire. It exists in a state of perpetual, grinding metabolism.

The Works is a thing that is still deciding what it wants to be. It is an embryonic geology, a city that is as much a part of the volcano as the basalt and the steam. It will outlast the workers who built it, outlast the companies that fund it, outlast the very reasons for its existence. Long after the last human has departed or dissolved into the brine, the Works will remain. It will sit in its caldera, dripping and humming, processing minerals that no one needs from a reservoir that will never run dry, a living monument to the heat of the earth's interior, breathing in the silence and sweating the slow, patient sweat of the stone.
