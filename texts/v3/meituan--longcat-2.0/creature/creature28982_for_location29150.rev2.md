## The People

You will not see them at first. You will walk the narrow paths between the hummock-roofs and find the settlement apparently empty — water standing in its dark mirrors, a fire pit with no fire, the paths curving on about their business — and then a hummock near your boot will shift, and part, and a face will come out of the green at you, grey on grey, and you will understand that you have been watched since you came down through the notch, and that everything in Grithen which looks like landscape is dressed.

They are people. Hold that word, because they hold it too — but they are people the way the trees at the rim are pines: the word holds, and the word strains.

Small is the first thing you understand, once you understand anything at all. Nobody in Grithen is tall. Tall is a thing that happens where there is sun to rise into; here the body is built for lintels and for turning sideways, and the people are built like their paths — narrow at the shoulder, economical, ending where they need to end. Their skin is the pale of things that never dry, faintly grey, translucent at the wrist, where you can see the slow tick of blood. And on all of them, in every fold and seam, the silt: the mites, or the mite-like, that settle on everything warm. The people carry theirs the way they carry the cold — without comment. A keeper's shawl holds three generations of one mite-line. To be rid of them would be to be rid of Grithen. This is not stated. It does not need to be.

Their hands are the most forceful thing about them. Wide at the fingertip, pale at the knuckle, calloused in the exact shape of their work — a seed-callus across the right palm, a resin-callus along the left thumb — so that a person's history can be read in the palm the way the bowl's is read in the striations. When two people meet who have not met before, they look at hands.

Their eyes are the color of the pools: dark, and always full of sky. They are built for grey — they read the moss, the moth on stone, the footprint in standing water — but only for grey, and when the sun comes full the people go nearly blind and suffer it gladly, standing in the plaza with their eyes shut and their faces tipped toward a light they are taking through the skin. They do not watch the sun. They wear it.

## The Warmth They Keep

There is no fire in Grithen but the one fire, so heat is not weather and not wealth here — it is nearer to blood. Everything the people possess that is warm, they made warm with their bodies, or they banked from the sun while the sun was giving.

A family sleeps as a single animal, under turf quilts, in a slow-breathing heap each member has known since before memory. At the center, held like an egg, a stone of the last sun-day, which gives back what it gathered across a week of nights until it is only stone again. Some houses' stones have been in the family longer than the family's sayings. A house, if it is measured at all — and nothing here is measured — is measured by how long its stones remember the sun.

You do not call anywhere empty-handed in Grithen. You call with a warm stone, carried against your chest on the walk over, and it is a gift worth exactly what it is worth. By the time you arrive it will be worth less. It is meant to.

On the narrow paths, where two people must turn sideways past each other, there is no room for bowing and no custom of speech. There is instead a hand — brief, to the shoulder, or flat to the chest — a transfer of warmth, greeting and payment in one, and then you are past each other and the path curves on. A day in Grithen is a long series of small warm transactions, taxed by cold.

From the lizards they have taken the lesson and declined the method: the lizards spend themselves cold in the seep-channels and then buy the warmth back whole, pouring up the walls in a river when the gold comes. The people are warm the whole time. They live as though they are not altogether sure they will be. The lizards on a house wall are counted, loosely, as part of the house — current residents of a stone that has always had residents.

## The Rounds

Perhaps two hundred of them live on the two shelves — two hundred, or a hundred and eighty; the number stays vague because counting is a kind of asking, and asking, in Grithen, is a kind of taking. It takes the answer away from whoever was keeping it.

Each person is a round. That is not a figure of speech: a circuit of pools, ledges, channels, and crevices, walked daily in the same order, tended by hand, for a lifetime — and a person is known by it. She is of the third pool and the violet ledge. If you asked her name — well. You would not ask.

The work itself is slow the way the water is slow: a fern moved two handspans into the seep; rival green combed from a channel with a bone pick; a flat stone set to shade a seed-tuft's roots at noon of a pewter day; the medicinal herb crushed and smelled and left alone because the smell is wrong this year. They do not sow. Nothing here has ever been sown. If their practice has a law beneath all its customs, it is the one their tools already obey: take what is given, leave what is not, and adjust the rest — gently — toward abundance.

And the tools. The blades are the claws of something that no longer lives, weathering free of the new-broken walls the way seeds come out of split fruit; the mountain's making tore open rock where old life lies folded, and the settling mountain lets a claw go when a claw is ready. The people do not quarry. They receive. A claw comes down, is hafted to rim-pine with resin and sinew, is ground sharp on quartzite, is spent carefully, and is handed on. Some are older than the settlement. Some, it is quietly supposed, are older than the bowl, which is impossible, and which everyone allows.

They eat small, cold, and well: seeds soaked overnight in pool-water; the pale crisp stems of the waxy-leaf; moss-beds where the beetle-things are encouraged to lay; on great days, a curd pressed from crushed fern and set in a cold channel, which a traveler would need three hungers to try.

A round is not taught; it is ceded. When a keeper's knees can no longer make the circuit, a successor walks it behind them for a full turning of seasons — which pool first, which ledge last, which crevice wants a listening rather than a looking — and at every plant along the way the old keeper takes the young one's hand and sets it on the stem and holds it there: a moment, no longer. An introduction. By the last turn of the year, the plants are said to know the new hand. No one has ever claimed a plant answered. No one has ever claimed it didn't.

## The Speaking

Everything said in Grithen, the bowl hears. The walls hold sound the way they hold warmth — nothing leaves; everything carries — so a whisper on the third shelf arrives in the plaza with its meaning intact, and privacy, in the lowland sense, was never invented here. What was invented instead is a speech that does not travel: words said with the lips closed; a hummed grammar felt in the chest more than heard; a conversation between two people at work that you, ten paces off, will register only as weather. Their speech takes the shape of its container, the way their animals do.

They have no noun for the sun. They have verbs — to gild; to have-light — and you will hear a child say *it golded*, meaning the whole of it at once: the fire, the smoke, the lizards pouring up the walls, the warmth banked in stone. For them the sun is not a thing that is there. It is a thing that is done. Their shortest unit of time is a bird's pause, because everyone here agrees that is how long a moment is.

Names are fire-things. A name is warm — warm the way a banked stone is warm, warm in a way that draws — and you do not leave warm things lying about, here, where anything at all might come to them. Through the grey year a person is their round, their kin, their function. But on the sun-days, when the plaza fire is built and the thick sweet smoke goes up, the whole settlement hums every name into it — the newborn's for the first time, the newly dead's for the last — and the smoke is understood to carry the names where names belong. No one says where that is. The smoke does not come back down.

## The Children

A child is raised mostly by subtraction. The cirque's terms were fixed at the making; a child is taught the terms, which is to say the silences — the ledge you do not climb, the dropping you step around, the question you do not ask twice.

They learn stillness before they learn words. The smallest play the game you would call lizard: when the cloud drops to charcoal, everyone freezes, mid-reach, mid-step, and the last one to move has won, or lost, and no adult has ever settled which, and no child has ever asked. The older ones count the birds passing in their thin endless stream, a slip-knot tied in grass for every hundred, and the full knots are hung in the roof-thatch, where the rain finds them and the generations accumulate. Looked at from below, the roofs of Grithen are archives. No one reads them.

When a child is judged old enough for level ground and a full vessel, they are sent up the path to the marked trees alone: water in a cupped stone, a fist of seeds, a scrap of resin. They are told nothing except which vessel. They are watched from below, without seeming to be, until the last bend takes them. What they see up there, no one asks. Some come back running. Some come back walking the way you walk when you are carrying water very carefully, though their hands are empty. Some do not speak for days, and there is a place in the heap for exactly this, near the middle, where it is warmest.

Before a child can remember anything, a bed in the terrace channels is encouraged in their name — the first green worked for them while they were still being carried — so that everyone in Grithen comes into the settlement as an act of adjustment done on their behalf. That bed is theirs all their life: never harvested, always greeted. Every round, whatever else it inherits, has been arranged to pass each keeper's beginning at least once.

## The Ones They Do Not Speak Of

You will ask, eventually — you are from outside; asking is what outside is for — about the large ones. About the fibrous droppings on the high ledges, the parallel scores in the rim-trees' bark, the breath at the edge of hearing. And the people of Grithen will do with that question what they do with every question that matters: they will put something warm in your hands.

Consider what is offered at the base of the marked trees. Seeds. Resin. Water, in cupped stone. Nothing made. Nothing that is not the cirque's own giving, portioned back — a sample of everything received, returned. The offering does not plead; its grammar is plainer than a plea. It says *received*, or it says *still here*, or it says — if there is a verb in it at all — *agreed*. The people know the gesture is understood. They know this the way you know a hand is held out to you, without ever seeing the body it belongs to. They do not know by whom. They have never needed to. Needing-to-know is a lowland hunger, and it does not keep at this altitude.

There is a story — told rarely, and never finished in the same place twice — that when the mountain opened, everything that lived inside it was given a choice: become small enough to be everywhere, or large enough to be nowhere. The swarming, the stream of birds, the silt of mites: the first choice, made all the way down. The weight behind the walls: the second, made all the way up. And the people are neither. They came down into the opened hand afterward, from wherever people come, and made themselves the size of the space that was left.

No one will tell you the claws in their tools belong to the large ones' kind. No one will tell you they do not. The claws are old; the droppings are new; the cirque itself is young; and in Grithen, oldness and newness are less like opposites than like neighbors on a narrow path, turning sideways, each feeling the other's warmth as they pass.

Once — and it sits in the settlement's memory the way a stone sits in the center of the heap — the oldest keeper opened her eyes on her last sun-day and said, to the smoke, or past it, *smaller than we hoped*. Or *larger*. The two are a breath apart. No one at the fire leaned close enough to settle it; you do not lean toward that; and whichever it was went up with the names.

## The Going Out and the Giving Back

A keeper who dies is carried in the evening to the channels they tended and given to the soil of their own beds — the green they encouraged, encouraged by their going. The channel lies fallow for a turning of seasons, and when it is walked again the green has come back dense and unhurried, and this is understood — understood, and never once said — to be them, still at work, adjusting the terms. The birth-bed is cut at the root, carried up by the successor whose hand was introduced to it, and left among the offerings. Your beginning, returned as your last gift. It is the largest transaction the people of Grithen ever make, and it takes nine minutes, and no one speaks during it.

No one leaves. This is not law; there is no law; it is only that leaving has never become a habit. The paths go up to the rim and along it and down again, and the idea of the mountain's outside — of a *down* that continues, of ground with a horizon on it — is a thing the feet here decline to imagine. Once, a young man went out with travelers. Years on, a traveler came through reporting a man of Grithen's grey gait living two ridges east, keeping moss-beds where no moss had ever been kept, and the report was received the way all news of the outside is received here: as weather.

If you visit, you will be yielded to on paths. You will feel a stranger's hand flat to your chest, once. You will be shown a gap in a sleeping heap and given the place of the fading stone — the warmest place; it is where they put what they are keeping. In the morning you will go, and no one will watch you go, and no one will say your name. But the mites in the folds of your clothes will ride out with you, a fine silt of Grithen that works itself loose over the following days: the cirque leaving you grudgingly, at the scale it is able. And if you ever come back down through the notch, the lizards on the eastern walls will not startle at your step. The people will notice. The people will have noticed before.

That is who lives in Grithen. Two hundred warm people in a cold bowl, walking inheritable rounds; a settlement that banks the sun in stone and spends it hand to hand; children raised as a set of silences between two doors; names burned a dozen times a year into sweet smoke and sent up to wherever the smoke is understood to carry things. Small things beyond counting beneath them. Large things keeping their own counsel around them. A question still open, perhaps, in the young rock, about what all of it will be when the edges soften.

The people do not wait to find out. Waiting, too, is a kind of asking. They keep the heap warm instead, and the walls are sharp, and the water weeps, and the bowl goes on holding what it holds.
