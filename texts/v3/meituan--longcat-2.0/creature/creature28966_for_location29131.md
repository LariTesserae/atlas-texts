To describe what lives here, you must first abandon the idea of a boundary between the place and the thing that inhabits it. There are no tenants in this cathedral of wet walls, no guests paying rent to the flesh that holds them. What lives here is an extension of the Gland itself—a manifestation of its intent, given just enough autonomy to crawl.

They are not animals. They are not machines. They are *articulated functions*.

At first, your eye refuses to separate them from the amber gloom and the dripping walls. They are the color of raw meat and old bone, their bodies smooth and glistening, catching the dim light the way a drop of plasma catches the edge of a microscope slide. They are small—roughly the size of your fist—but they move with a fluid, jointed economy that makes them seem larger, as though they displace more space than their physical forms actually occupy. 

Their shapes are asymmetrical, evolved not for aesthetics but for the specific geometries of the ridges and valves they attend. Most have between six and nine limbs, though "limbs" is a generous word; they are more like specialized appendages that phase in and out of existence. At rest, a creature is a low, flattened ovoid, its flesh pressed flush against the breathing wall. When it moves, limbs unfold from its sides—long, spindly, and tipped with tools that range from delicate, hair-like filaments to rigid, chitinous scrapers. Some limbs end in what look like suckers, allowing them to anchor themselves as the Gland contracts around them. Others split into fine, pincered digits that tremble with minute adjustments, reading the vibration of the membrane beneath them.

They do not have faces. Where a head might be, they have a sensory plateau—a dense cluster of nodules that taste the chemical gradient of the fluid on the walls, that feel the electrical intention in the salts. They do not see as you see; they perceive the world through a constant, echolocating vibration, their bodies acting as tuning forks that resonate with the churning roar of the Gland. 

You watch one unfold itself from a crevice in the raw new tissue. It moves with the slow, deliberate patience of a growth, stretching one leg after another, testing the wetness of the wall. It finds the accumulation—a thick, glittering paste that has wept from the seams of a newly formed valve—and it goes to work. Its front limbs, sharp and precise, scrape the deposit loose. The substance is heavy, dense, smelling of iron and a cloying, almost fermented sweetness. As it scrapes, its back limbs cup the falling flakes, funneling them into a pouch-like fold in its own abdomen. 

It is not eating. It is not waste collection. It is *harvesting*.

When the pouch is full, the creature detaches from the wall and drops—not falling, but drifting with a strange, purposeful grace. It lands on a lower ridge, its limbs immediately gripping the wet surface, and it scuttles toward a narrowing in the wall. There, it is met by others. A congregation of them has gathered around a darker, throbbing patch of membrane. They are feeding the harvested paste back into the Gland. Their limbs work in a frenzy of synchronized motion, massaging the glittering substance into the pores of the flesh, packing it down as the membrane tightens and absorbs it. 

They are the Gland's digestive tract, but inverted. They take what the body has sweated out, and with a chemical process of their own—a slight warmth radiating from their pale bodies—they re-digest it, breaking it down into a state the tissue can reabsorb. Without them, the Gland would choke on its own accumulation. They are the reason the chamber exists.

And yet, there is something else about them that unsettles you. It is in the way they pause. 

The work is relentless, but occasionally, in the spaces between the grinding contractions of the chamber, the swarm will go still. The roar drops to a simmer. The creatures flatten themselves against the wall, their limbs retracting, their bodies going perfectly rigid. In these moments, they are indistinguishable from the tissue itself. They are waiting. 

You realize, with a slow, creeping horror, that they are listening to the Gland. Not just perceiving its mechanical rhythm, but *interpreting* it. The irregular tempo of the valves—the "deliberate" adjustment of the rhythm—is a language. It is an instruction. The creatures are the Gland's hands, but the Gland's mind is elsewhere, deep in the branching network, directing the flow of product. These shapes are the local syntax, translating the vast, unknowable desire of the larger body into micro-adjustments of pressure, temperature, and chemistry. 

Sometimes, the Gland's instructions are demanding. When the great contraction comes—the moment when the space tightens, the roar rises, and the amber light brightens—the creatures do not simply clean. They *arch*. Their bodies lift from the walls, their limbs splaying wide, and they vibrate. The thin, harmonic shriek that threads through the roar is them. They are pushing back. They are lending their own bioelectricity to the Gland's surge, acting as living capacitors, amplifying the signal so that the valves seal completely and the product is forced along the branching network.

They are dying in these moments. The surge is too great. When the contraction passes and the walls relax, you see the casualties. Creatures caught in the open, their bodies ruptured by the pressure, spilling a viscous, clear fluid that is immediately lapped up by the nearby survivors. They do not mourn. They do not pause. They absorb the fallen, breaking them down into their component salts and sugars, returning the raw material to the wall. There is no death here, only reabsorption.

If you were to reach out and touch one—if you were foolish enough to disrupt the flow of this endless, wet industry—it would not bite you. It would not sting. It would simply stop. Its sensory nodules would brush against your skin, tasting the foreign salts of your sweat, reading the entirely wrong electric signature of your mammalian flesh. It would not know you are alive. It would simply know that you are an obstruction, a wrong texture on the smooth membrane of its world. And it would call the others. They would come with their scraping limbs and their cupping digits, and they would try to break you down. They would try to harvest you, to pack your glittering essence into their pouches, and feed you back into the walls to be made into product. 

But for now, they ignore you. You are too large, too dry, too still. You are merely the landscape. You stand in the center of the roar, breathing the iron-sweet air, watching the swarm flow over the walls like living coolant, like the very thoughts of this vast, subterranean brain made flesh. They do not know they are beautiful. They do not know they are cruel. They only know the rhythm, the scrape, the harvest, and the endless, grinding work of making.
