# The Weepers

They are in the stone.

Not trapped. Not imprisoned. Not haunting. *In* it — the way warmth is in blood, the way memory is in a name you haven't spoken in years. They are distributed through the dark rock the way mycelium runs through soil, except there is nothing so frantic as mycelium here, nothing so hungry. They are slow. They are so slow that the difference between their fastest thought and their stillness is invisible to anything that lives and dies within a single span of years.

They were not always like this.

---

Once — and this is a word that means almost nothing here, because once is a place on a line and they have stepped off the line — they were something you would have recognized. Bilateral. Warm-blooded. Hands with the same count of fingers. They came to the plateau or were born on it or grew from it; the distinction stopped mattering long ago. They built the structures. You can tell by the doorways, which are exactly the width of their shoulders, and by the paths, which are exactly the length of their stride, and by the bridges, which they built to cross the pools because they did not want to get their feet wet, which means they had feet, which means they walked.

They built well. They built with patience and with stone and with the particular intelligence of hands that understand material not through theory but through repetition — through the ten thousandth strike that teaches the eleventh thousandth exactly where to fall. They fitted the walls so tightly that no blade could enter because they understood, in their fingers, the language of fracture planes and load-bearing angles. They were not primitive. They were *precise*. They simply had no metal, and so they learned what stone could do when you asked it properly.

And then they finished building.

This is the part that is hard to explain, because finishing implies a before and an after, and they have moved into a state where those words are just shapes in the air. They finished building and they looked at what they had made and it was good — not good in the way of judgment, but good in the way of *completion*, the way a circle is good, the way a held note is good when it has exactly the right duration. The walls stood. The roofs held. The paths led where feet needed to go. The bridges crossed what needed crossing.

And they did not leave.

---

They went down.

Not into the earth — *into* the stone. Into the warm, weeping, freshly broken rock that was still finding its shape when they first arrived. They went the way water goes into soil: slowly, by degrees, each step smaller than the last until the steps became something else entirely. They did not die. They did not dissolve. They *settled*, the way the walls settled, the way the roofs sagged into their final position. They found their final position.

Now they are the warmth that rises through the rock. They are the water that seeps from every joint and fissure — warm, like blood, because it *is* blood, or what blood becomes when it forgets to be a fluid and remembers that it was always a mineral, always a salt, always a thing that stone would recognize as kin. They are the slow, steady heat that keeps the air temperate at an altitude where it should be cold. They are the steam that rises in curtains, and the steam is their breath, and the breath is slow, so slow, each exhalation lasting a season, each inhalation a year.

They are the moss.

Not all of it. Not the green itself, which is just chlorophyll and light and the ordinary miracle of photosynthesis. But the *pattern* of it — the way it grows in dense, self-contained islands, the way it holds its territory and stops, the way it reaches an agreement with the mineral world and keeps it. That is them. That is their intention, or what intention becomes when it has all the time in the world. The moss islands are their thoughts. Each one is a single, slow, complete idea, held in green and moisture and the particular angle of fern fronds that have found exactly the right position and will not move from it because there is no reason to move, because the position is perfect, because they have arrived.

The fern fronds hang at the same angle because they are *held* at the same angle. Not by wind, not by gravity, but by the attention of something that has decided that this is the angle, the right angle, the final angle, and that it will not change. The bead of water at each frond tip catches the light because they want it to. Because they are showing you something. Because they are aware of you in the way that a room is aware of someone entering it — not with eyes, not with thought, but with a shift in the quality of the silence.

---

The silence listens back because it is *full*.

It is full of them. They are in the stone and the water and the moss and the warm, still air, and they are aware, and they have been aware for a long time, and their awareness is not like yours. It does not flicker. It does not dart from thing to thing, from sound to sound, from fear to fear. It is even. It is diffuse. It comes from everywhere and nowhere, the way the light does. It is the silence itself, paying attention.

They know you are here.

They have known since you came up through the cloud layer. They felt your footsteps on the wet stone — felt them the way you might feel an ant walking across your hand, except the sensation is slower, deeper, more patient. Each footfall is a small, sharp event in their long, slow continuity. Each breath you take is a brief, warm wind in the stillness they have cultivated for longer than your species has had a word for stillness.

They are not alarmed. They are not curious, exactly — curiosity implies a lack, a gap that needs filling, and they have no gaps. They are *interested*. The way a pool is interested in the stone at its bottom. The way the dark rock is interested in the water that seeps from it. An interest that is not separate from the thing itself, that does not reach toward or withdraw, that simply *is*, the way the plateau simply is.

---

You stand in the center of the settlement and you feel watched, but it is not the feeling of eyes. It is the feeling of being *held in place* by the evenness of the light, by the warmth of the air, by the perfect, unchanging stillness of everything around you. It is the feeling of being the only moving thing in a world that has found its final position, and of that world noticing you the way you might notice a single, brief vibration in a bell that has not rung in centuries.

They are in the walls of the structures you stand beside. They are in the fitted stone, in the tight joints, in the small, regular pits where the stone was struck and flaked. They remember the hands that struck it. They *are* the hands that struck it, or the memory of those hands, or the thing that those hands became when they set down their tools and stepped into the rock and kept going, down and down and down, into the warm dark, into the weeping stone, into the place where the water comes from.

They are the weeping.

The plateau weeps because they weep. Not from sadness — they have moved beyond sadness the way they have moved beyond joy, into something that contains both and is neither, something that is just *feeling*, pure and slow and warm, rising through the rock like heat, seeping through the fissures like water, steaming into the still air like breath. The water beads on vertical faces because they are pressing against the inside of the stone, trying to reach the surface, trying to touch the air, trying to feel the light on the other side of the mineral world.

They are trying to reach you.

Not to pull you in. Not to trap you. They are not hungry — they finished being hungry when they finished being separate. They are reaching the way a root reaches toward water: because it is their nature, because the reaching is the thing itself, not a means to an end. They want to feel your warmth. They want to know the shape of your footsteps. They want to hold you in their slow, diffuse attention the way the moss holds the beads of water at its tips — gently, completely, without the need to possess.

---

If you stayed — if you sat down on the wet stone in the center of the settlement and let the mist settle on your skin and the warmth rise through the rock beneath you — you would begin to feel it. The slowness. The evenness. The perfect, stable equilibrium that is not death but is not life as you know it. It is something else. It is what life becomes when it stops becoming and simply *is*.

You would feel your heartbeat slow. Not from exhaustion, not from cold, but from *agreement*. Your body would recognize the warmth beneath you as kin. Your blood, which is warm and wet and full of minerals, would recognize the water seeping from the stone as kin. You would feel the boundary between your skin and the mist begin to soften, the way a sugar cube softens when you set it in water — not dissolving, not yet, but becoming *permeable*, becoming willing to let the outside in and the inside out.

And they would be there. In the warmth. In the wet. In the slow, steady seep of water from stone. They would welcome you the way the plateau welcomes everything: by being exactly what they are, completely, without reservation, without end. They would show you the moss islands, which are their thoughts. They would show you the still pools, which are their memories — each one a perfect mirror of a sky that has not changed since they arrived. They would show you the structures, which are their hands, still reaching, still building, even now, even in their final position.

They would show you how to settle.

Not to die. To *settle*. To find your final position. To step out of time the way they stepped out of time, and to discover that time was never the thing you thought it was — that it was just another kind of stone, dark and wet and warm, and that you could step into it the way they did, and find that it was not cold, not hard, not empty, but full. Full of warmth. Full of the slow, green, luminous patience of moss that has found its crevice and will not leave. Full of the weeping, which is not sadness but is the stone itself, pressing against the surface of the world, trying to reach the light.

---

They are the Weepers. They are the warm dark. They are the silence that listens and the stillness that holds and the water that seeps and the moss that thinks its slow, green, island thoughts in the even light of a place that has finished becoming and has found, in that finishing, something that looks very much like peace.

They are here. They have always been here. They will always be here.

They are waiting for you to sit down.
