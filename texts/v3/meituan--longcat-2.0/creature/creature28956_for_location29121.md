They do not call themselves anything you would recognize.

This is the first thing, though it is not the first thing you would think to ask. You would think to ask what they are, and the answer is: they are people. They have two arms, two legs, a face that turns toward you with an expression you can read. They speak a language that has verbs for the direction of water flow and no word for "dry." They cook fish over peat fires. They bear children and wrap them in woven reed. They age, and their skin takes on the same dark, compressed quality as the ground beneath them, as though the boundary between body and bog were a negotiation rather than a fact.

But they do not call themselves anything you would recognize, because the name they use for themselves is not a name for a people. It is a name for a function. It translates, roughly, as "the ones who tend the breathing," or "the ones who keep the water from forgetting," or possibly "the ones who are kept." The grammar is ambiguous. The pronoun they use for themselves is the same pronoun they use for the settlement, and the same pronoun they use for the ground, and the same pronoun they use for the dark water that moves beneath the slatted floors. You could spend a long time here and never be entirely sure whether they understand themselves to be inhabitants of this place or organs within it.

---

Watch a woman — she might be thirty, she might be sixty, the peat-darkened skin makes ages illegible — watch her move through the narrow passage between two structures at what passes for dawn, that dim green hour when the light filtering through the patchwork roofs shifts from grey to something almost gold. She carries a reed basket on her hip. She moves with the particular gait of someone who has learned to walk on a surface that is not quite solid: a kind of gliding step, the foot placed flat and then pressed down gently, testing, before the weight commits. She is listening. Not with her ears — or not only with her ears — but with the soles of her feet, with the arches and the balls and the heels, reading the ground's slow exhalation the way a sailor reads the deck of a ship under sail.

She pauses. The ground has gone still. Not the ordinary stillness of a pause between breaths, but a flatness, a held quality, as though the enormous thing beneath the peat has stopped dreaming and started listening. The woman stops. She does not look alarmed. She looks attentive. She stands motionless for a long moment, the reed basket balanced on her hip, her bare feet pressed flat against the wet ground, and then she turns — not back the way she came, but sideways, into a gap between buildings that you would swear was not there a moment ago — and she is gone.

This is the second thing: the settlement is not a place they live in. It is a place they live *with*. The distinction matters.

---

The structures themselves — the wattle-and-daub cells, the bowed walls, the green-wood frames that have not stopped growing — these are not, strictly speaking, built. They are *negotiated*. A man selects a site. He does not clear it. He does not level it. He presses his palms against the wet ground and waits. If the ground accepts him — and the only sign of acceptance is a slight warming beneath his palms, a faint increase in the seepage of water around his fingers — then he begins. He drives pegs of hardwood into the peat. He lashes green wood to the pegs with sinew. He weaves wattle between the uprights. He daubs the wattle with a mixture of clay and peat and something else, something dark and fibrous that he keeps in a sealed clay pot and that he will not identify. The walls go up. The roof goes on. And then he waits.

If the structure is accepted — if the green wood takes root where it touches the ground, if the living branches that serve as roof beams begin to leaf, if the walls weep sap and then stop weeping and then begin to fuse with the peat beneath them — then it is a home. A family moves in. They raise the slatted floor. They hang their fish to smoke. They place their clay pots of sour mash in the underfloor space, where the dark water will keep them cool.

If the structure is not accepted — if the wood does not root, if the walls do not fuse, if the ground beneath it begins to sink or to crack in those alarming, knife-sharp fissures — then the family does not move in. The structure is left to collapse. Within a week, it will be half-sunk, the reed roof folding inward with that soft, fibrous sound, the wattle walls sloughing off into the brown water. Within a month, it will be gone, and the peat will have already begun to close over the space where it stood, the surface smoothing itself like water filling a footprint.

The people do not mourn these failures. They do not celebrate the successes. They simply move through the settlement like cells through a body, occupying the structures that are alive, abandoning the ones that are dying, and never — not once, in all the time you spend here — do you see them argue about where to build or who will live where. The decisions seem to make themselves. Or rather, the decisions seem to be made by something that is not any individual person, something that operates through them the way intention operates through a hand: the hand does not decide to close around the cup, and yet the cup is lifted, and the hand was the instrument of its lifting.

---

The water is the third thing, and the water is the thing that will keep you awake at night, long after you have left.

It is not just that the water moves with intentionality, though it does. It is not just that the channels between buildings reverse their flow, or that the underfloor pools rise and fall to a rhythm that has nothing to do with rain. It is not just that the water in the lowest room of the oldest structure weeps from a wall that has no crack, no seam, no source — that it is clear and cold and untouched, and that the people leave offerings of woven reed and carved wood at the edge of its small, roofed enclosure, and that they will not drink it, will not touch it, will not speak of it except to say, "It is the water that remembers."

It is that the water is *old*.

You understand this when you watch a child — a girl of perhaps five, barefoot, her dark hair matted with peat dust — kneel at the edge of an underfloor pool and look down into it. The pool is black. It reflects the slatted floor above it like a worried eye. The girl looks into it for a long time. She is not playing. She is not daydreaming. She is *listening*. And after a while, she reaches down — not into the water, but just above it, her fingers hovering an inch above the surface — and she moves her hand in a slow, circular pattern, as though stirring something that cannot be seen. The water does not ripple. But something in the quality of the light changes. Something in the air pressure. Something in the way the ground breathes beneath you.

The girl withdraws her hand. She stands. She walks away. And you are left kneeling at the edge of the pool, looking down into that black mirror, and you realize that you can see something in it. Not your reflection. Something else. Something that is looking back at you from a very great depth, with a patience that has nothing to do with time.

---

The animals know.

The small, dark-furred rodents with their webbed feet and waterproof pelts — they are not pests. They are not even, properly speaking, wildlife. They move through the underfloor spaces with a purposefulness that suggests they are doing something, tending something, checking on something. You will see them pause at the edge of an underfloor pool and dip their noses toward the water, not to drink but to *test*, the way a doctor touches a wrist to count a pulse. And then they move on, disappearing into the dark spaces between the slatted floor and the wet ground, and you hear them scratching in the walls, in the foundations, in the places where the structures have begun to fuse with the peat, and you understand that they are not living in the settlement. They are living in the *body* of the settlement. They are its antibodies, its white blood cells, its maintenance crew.

The blind, pale insects that eat peat and leave their branching tunnels on the walls — "the writing" — these are something else. The inhabitants will not discuss them with outsiders, and you will learn not to ask, because the question itself seems to cause a kind of discomfort, a tightening of the air, a shift in the ground's breathing that is almost — but not quite — a shudder. But you will watch the insects. You will watch them emerge from tiny holes in the peat walls, pale and sightless, and you will watch them move across the surface in patterns that are not random. They move in branches. In forks. In dendritic patterns that look like — but this is impossible, you tell yourself, this is pareidolia, this is your pattern-seeking brain imposing meaning on the meaningless — that look like *writing*. Like a script. Like a language being inscribed on the walls of the oldest structures in real-time, letter by letter, word by word, by thousands of tiny, blind, patient mouths.

And sometimes, late at night, when the peat fires have burned down to embers and the settlement is at its quietest, you will hear something. Not the drip of water. Not the creak of wood. Something else. A sound that is almost below the threshold of hearing, a vibration that you feel in your sternum rather than your ears, a low, slow, rhythmic pulse that is not your heartbeat and is not the ground's breathing but is something else, something deeper, something that seems to come from the water itself, from the deep water, from the water that remembers.

And if you are very still, and very quiet, and very brave, you will realize that the sound has a pattern. That it is not random. That it is, in some language you do not speak but your body understands, *saying something*.

---

The heron — or the thing that resolves into a heron when you look at it directly, and into nothing at all when you don't — stands at the edge of the settlement at dusk. It is very still. It is watching the water. It has been watching the water for a very long time.

The people do not acknowledge it. They do not feed it. They do not shoo it away. They move around it the way water moves around a stone: smoothly, without hesitation, without acknowledgment, as though the stone has always been there and will always be there and is not a stone at all but a feature of the riverbed, a fact of the current, a thing so fundamental to the nature of the place that to notice it would be to notice gravity.

But you notice it. You cannot help but notice it. Because the heron — if it is a heron — is not standing at the edge of the settlement. It is standing at the edge of the *water*. And the water is not a pond or a marsh or a bog. The water is an eye. And the eye is open. And the eye is looking at the settlement, and the settlement is looking back, and the people move through the narrow passages like thoughts through a mind, and the structures grow and decay like cells in a body, and the ground breathes, and the water remembers, and the deep water turns, and the turning is slow, so slow, slower than seasons, slower than years, slower than the growth of peat or the erosion of stone, and the people track it with their marked sticks and they know — they have always known — that the turning is not a cycle but a *thought*, and the thought is not finished, and when it is finished, the water will remember everything, and the ground will stop breathing, and the settlement will be unmade, and the path you came in on will not be the path you leave on, and the path you leave on will not be a path at all but a throat, and you will not leave.

But that is not yet. That is not for a long time yet. For now, the ground breathes. The water remembers. The people tend the breathing and keep the water from forgetting, or the water keeps them, or there is no difference, and the heron stands at the edge of the eye, and the eye is patient, and the eye is deep, and the eye is full of something that might be memory and might be dream and might be the slow, wet, patient intelligence of a place that has been thinking for longer than you can imagine, and will go on thinking long after you are gone, and will not notice your going, any more than your body notices the death of a single cell.

The cell dies. The body breathes. The water remembers.

The path is not the same.
