# The Clicking

---

They are the first thing you notice, though they are not the most important.

They come up the wall in a wave — a sheet of them, a living carpet, moving with a purpose that is not individual but collective, each one responding to the ones immediately around it, the whole mass flowing like a fluid over the weeping stone. They are small. The largest is the size of your thumb. The smallest are barely visible, specks of chitin and jointed leg that catch the diffuse light and throw it back in tiny, wet glints.

They have shells. Not shells like a crab's — not a single carapace — but segmented plates, overlapping, flexible, that shift and slide over each other as they move. The plates are dark, wet, warm to the touch, and they are covered in a fine, dense fuzz — not hair, not cilia, but something between, something that senses vibration, that tastes the air, that reads the chemical gradient of the water film on the stone. They are exquisitely sensitive. They can feel your footsteps from a hundred meters away. They can taste the mineral composition of the wall beneath them. They can detect the faint electrical field of a biofilm in active growth, and they move toward it with a speed that seems impossible for something so small.

They have legs. Too many legs. Six, eight, ten — it varies, and the number seems to change as they grow, as segments are added or lost, as the body adapts to the surface it finds itself on. The legs end in hooks, in pads, in something that is both — a microscopic structure that grips the wet stone by molecular adhesion, by van der Waals force, by the simple mechanical interlocking of hook and pore. They can climb anything. They can hang upside down from a ceiling of weeping stone for days, for weeks, waiting.

They have mouths. The mouths are complex — not a simple mandible but a cluster of fine, interlocking plates that rotate, that grind, that scrape biofilm from stone with a precision that is almost mechanical. They eat the mineral blooms. They eat the biofilm. They eat each other — the dead, the slow, the injured. They are not sentimental. They are not cruel. They are hungry, always, and the city provides, always, and the two facts are the same fact.

They are blind. They have no eyes. They do not need eyes. The light here is directionless, diffuse, useless for navigation. Instead, they have the fuzz, and the vibration sense, and the chemical taste, and something else — something deeper, something that reads the hum of the city itself, the deep structural resonance that is the place's voice. They know, in some way that is not thought but is not not-thought, when a wall is about to crumble. They know when the water is about to shift. They know when the warmth is about to spike. They know because they are part of the city's nervous system, because they have been here so long and are so numerous and so sensitive that they have become, collectively, a sensing organ for the place itself.

And they are everywhere. Billions of them. Tens of billions. They fill every crack, every channel, every pore in the stone. They are the dominant fauna. They are the city's primary consumers, its recyclers, its builders — because they build, in their way. Their detritus — their shed plates, their waste, their dead — accumulates in the channels, mixes with the dissolved stone and the biofilm, and becomes the substrate from which new mineral blooms grow, from which new stone accretes, from which new walls rise. They are part of the cycle. They are the living component of the city's metabolism.

They communicate. Not with sound — though they produce sound, the dry chitinous chatter that is the third layer of the city's voice — but with chemistry. They leave trails. Pheromone trails, laid down on the wet stone, that say *food here*, *danger here*, *mate here*, *wall crumbling here*, *water rising here*. The trails are invisible but omnipresent, a chemical map of the city that is constantly being written and rewritten, a living document that every individual reads and contributes to. The swarm intelligence that emerges from this — the way the mass moves, the way it flows around obstacles, the way it pools and surges and retreats — is not directed by any individual. It is an emergent property. It is what happens when enough sensitive, hungry, communicating bodies occupy a complex environment. It is, in a sense, the city thinking.

But they are not the only thing.

# The Deep Ones

---

In the channels — the deep channels, the ones that have been worn smooth by millennia of warm, acidic water — something else moves.

It is large. It is slow. It is old.

You will not see it at first. You will feel it — a change in the hum, a deepening, a subsonic pulse that you feel in your chest more than you hear with your ears. The clickers know. The clickers flee. They pour up the walls in a panicked, rippling wave, clearing the channels, and in the sudden absence of their chatter, the city's voice changes — the hiss and the hum become louder, more present, and beneath them, the pulse.

It comes up from below. From the warm ground. From the deep water.

It is — there is no good word for it. It is not a worm, though it is long and sinuous. It is not a mollusk, though it is soft and wet and leaves a trail of mucus that glows faintly in the dark. It is not a fish, though it moves through the water with a fluid, undulating grace. It is something else. Something that has been here longer than the clickers. Something that may have been here before the city.

Its body is segmented, like the clickers', but the segments are vast — each one the size of your torso, a great, soft, pulsing ring of muscle and membrane and something that might be cartilage or might be mineral deposit. The segments are translucent. You can see through them, vaguely, to the organs within — not organs as you know them, but structures, chambers, tubes, through which fluids move in slow, peristaltic waves. It has no eyes. It has no legs. It has a mouth at the front — a vast, circular mouth, lined with rows of fine, crystalline teeth that are not teeth but mineral growths, accreted from the water, constantly growing and constantly being worn down by the act of feeding. It feeds on the biofilm. It feeds on the mineral blooms. It feeds on the clickers, when it can catch them — and it can catch them, despite its slowness, because it is patient, because it can wait for days in the deep channels with its mouth open, a living trap, and the clickers, for all their swarm intelligence, are not perfect, and some always fall.

It is not aggressive. It is not defensive. It is simply *there*, moving through the city's circulatory system like a blood cell through a vein, performing some function that is not clear, that may not be a function at all, that may simply be what it does, what it has always done, what it will always do.

There are not many of them. A dozen, perhaps. Perhaps fewer. They are long-lived — centuries, millennia — and they reproduce rarely, and their young are small and vulnerable and are eaten by the clickers, mostly, before they can grow. But the ones that survive — the ones that find the deep channels, the warm water, the places where the biofilm grows thickest — they grow. They grow and they grow and they grow, and they become part of the city's infrastructure, part of its metabolism, part of its voice.

Because they sing.

Not with voices — they have no voices. They sing with their bodies. The peristaltic pulse of their movement, the slow contraction and expansion of their segments, produces a sound — a deep, subsonic thrum that travels through the water, through the stone, through the very foundations of the city. It is the hum. The deep, structural hum that you felt when you first entered the place. It is not the city itself that hums. It is them. The deep ones. The old ones. The slow, vast, patient ones who move through the channels like thoughts through a mind, whose pulse is the city's pulse, whose song is the city's song.

They are not intelligent. Not in the way you understand intelligence. They do not think. They do not plan. They do not build. But they are *aware*, in some deep, slow, chemical way. They respond to the city's changes. They move toward warmth. They move toward food. They move toward each other, sometimes, in the deep channels, and when they meet, they intertwine, and the pulse of their combined bodies produces a harmonic — a chord, a resonance — that travels through the entire city, that the clickers feel in their fuzz, that the mineral blooms respond to with a sudden, accelerated growth, that the water itself seems to thicken around.

They are the city's heartbeat. They are its oldest inhabitants. They were here before the walls, before the builders, before the name. They were here when it was just warm water and wet stone and time. And they will be here when the walls have crumbled and been rebuilt a thousand times more, when the clickers have evolved into something unrecognizable, when the city has become something else entirely.

They are the constant. They are the deep, slow, patient pulse that underlies everything.

# The Builders

---

And then there are the others.

You will not see them. Not at first. Not for a long time. They are rare, and they are careful, and they are not what they were.

They were here once — the builders, the ones who raised the first walls, who carved the first channels, who shaped the stone and set it in place. They were intelligent. They were purposeful. They built the city — or began it, or continued it, or inherited it from someone else, someone older, someone whose name has been lost under the weight of subsequent construction. They built, and the city grew, and the water came, and the warmth rose, and the clickers came, and the deep ones sang, and the city became what it is — a process, a system, a self-sustaining equilibrium of growth and decay.

And the builders changed.

Not quickly. Not dramatically. Slowly, over millennia, as the city changed around them, as the water and the warmth and the clickers and the deep ones became the dominant facts of existence, as the original purpose of the city — whatever it was — became irrelevant, buried under the sheer weight of the place's own momentum. They adapted. They had to. The city was not what they had built. It was something else now, something alive in its own way, something that did not need them but tolerated them, that incorporated them, that made room for them in its ecology as it made room for everything.

They became small. Not physically — they are still roughly your size, roughly your shape — but in presence, in impact, in the scale of their interaction with the place. They do not build anymore. They *tend*. They move through the city in small groups — twos, threes, rarely more — following the clickers, following the deep ones, following the water. They carry tools, sometimes — implements of worn stone and shaped chitin and the crystallized secretions of the mineral blooms — but the tools are not for building. They are for *adjusting*. For redirecting a channel here. For scraping a biofilm there. For clearing a blockage in a deep channel where a deep one is passing. For removing a dead clicker mass from a water intake. For smoothing a surface that has grown too rough, too fast, that is disrupting the flow.

They are not maintaining the city. The city maintains itself. They are *participating* in the city. They are one more component of the system, one more species in the ecology, one more voice in the chorus. They have no name for themselves. They have no language, not anymore — or if they have language, it is not a language of words but of gesture, of chemical signal, of the subtle modulation of the city's hum. They communicate with the clickers, in a way — the clickers' pheromone trails are not lost on them, and they can read the chemical map as well as any clicker, and they add to it, sometimes, leaving their own marks, their own signals, that the clickers respond to. They communicate with the deep ones, too — or rather, they respond to the deep ones, moving out of the way when the pulse deepens, moving toward it when it fades, as if they are drawn to the song, as if they need it, as if the hum is as necessary to them as the water and the warmth.

They are not unhappy. They are not diminished. They are *adapted*. They have found their niche. They are the city's gardeners, its custodians, its gentle, quiet, persistent presence — the ones who move through the endless, weeping, glowing, clicking, humming city and make small adjustments, small corrections, small acts of care that the city does not need but that they need, that give them purpose, that give them place, that make them part of the thing they once thought they were building.

They sleep in the walls. In small chambers, carved or grown or found, lined with dried biofilm and the shed plates of clickers, warm and wet and close. They eat the biofilm, and the mineral blooms, and sometimes the clickers — carefully, selectively, taking only the slow and the dead, leaving the swarm intact. They drink the warm water. They breathe the thick air. They live for a long time — longer than you would expect, longer than seems possible in this acidic, mineral-saturated, biologically hyperactive environment — because they have adapted, because their bodies have learned to process the water and the air and the stone, because they are, in some sense, becoming mineral themselves, their bones denser, their skin thicker, their blood more viscous, more copper-colored, more like the water that seeps through the walls.

They are not the city's masters. They are not its servants. They are its *companions*. Its quiet, patient, enduring companions, who have been here as long as anyone can remember, who will be here after everyone else is gone, who move through the city like memories through a mind — faint, persistent, inseparable from the thing they inhabit.

# The City Itself

---

And beneath all of this — beneath the clickers and the deep ones and the builders, beneath the hiss and the hum and the chatter, beneath the warm water and the weeping stone and the mineral blooms that grow and crumble and regrow — there is something else.

Not a creature. Not an intelligence. Not a god.

A *tendency*.

A direction. A vector. A slow, vast, patient inclination toward complexity, toward density, toward the packing of more and more into less and less, the filling of every cubic meter, the occupation of every surface, the conversion of water and warmth and time into structure, into system, into *place*.

It is not conscious. It is not alive. It is simpler than that. It is a chemical tendency, a physical law, a thermodynamic inevitability — the tendency of warm, wet, mineral-rich systems to grow, to accrete, to build, to become more complex over time, to find equilibrium not in stillness but in motion, not in simplicity but in density, not in death but in the constant, violent, beautiful churn of creation and destruction that looks, from sufficient distance, like life.

The city is not alive. But it is not dead. It is *active*. It is *dynamic*. It is a process that has been running for so long that it has become a place, and the place has become a system, and the system has become an ecology, and the ecology has become a home — for the clickers, for the deep ones, for the builders, for the small quick things that skitter and fly and eat and die and are eaten and dissolved and regrown.

It is home to all of them. It is made of all of them. It is the sum of all of them — their bodies, their waste, their dead, their living, their constant, seething, hungry, patient activity — plus the water, plus the warmth, plus the stone, plus the time.

It is the Damp Republic.

It is wet. It is warm. It is loud beyond endurance.

It is home.

It has been here forever.

It is still being built.
