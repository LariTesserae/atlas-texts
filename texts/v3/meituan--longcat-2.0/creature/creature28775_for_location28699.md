To ask who lives in the Arrangement is to ask who lives inside a lung, or who lives inside a symphony. The inhabitants are not separate from the city; they are its respiration, its rhythm, its intent made manifest. But if you are looking for the tenders—the builders, the arrangers, the consciousness that directs this young, screaming, beautiful place—you must abandon your expectations of what a creature looks like, or where a creature ends and its environment begins.

The smallest inhabitants are the Phasing Swarm. At first, you might mistake them for insects—a rippling, skittering carpet of chitin and too many legs that moves through the fast-lichen like a wind through wheat. But they are not insects. They exist at a slight remove from the space you occupy. Their bodies phase in and out of baseline reality, flickering like a signal struggling to hold its frequency. This is not a trick of the light; it is a survival mechanism. The Arrangement is a place of intense acoustic and sub-sonic pressure. By phasing slightly out of sync with the immediate present, the Swarm avoids being crushed by the sheer physical weight of the city’s voice. They graze on the lichens, and as they eat, they vibrate. Their collective vibration is the baseline hum of the city, the first layer of the total sound. They are the city’s microbiome, breaking down the mineral crusts, their phased bodies acting as living buffers between the raw geology and the fragile ecology.

Then there are the Processors. These are the cattle-sized shapes you see moving through the mist, the ones that make your eyes water when you look at them directly. They are vast, slow, and profoundly out of phase with three-dimensional space. When a Processor feeds on the tall, hollow grasses, it does not simply bite and chew. It unfolds. Its mouth—if you can call it that—opens in directions that do not exist in Euclidean geometry, and the grass is drawn into a spatial fold, compressed, and broken down across multiple axes simultaneously. The Processors are the city’s digestive system. Their waste is not mere excrement; it is a highly refined, enzymatically active slurry that they deposit into the cracks of the young limestone. This waste is what accelerates the rock’s decay into soil, what feeds the roots of the broad-leafed plants. The Processors walk on ground that is not entirely your ground; they step between the tectonic plates, using the shifting stone as a treadmill, their massive weight distributed across dimensions so that the limestone does not crack under them. They are the gardeners of the deep rock, tending the island’s very foundation.

But the true inhabitants—the Arrangers—are something else entirely. 

You will not see them easily. You will see their effects: the sudden brightening of a building’s frame, the shift in the sub-sonic frequency, the way the mist suddenly coalesces into a corridor between two structures. The Arrangers are the consciousness of the Arrangement, and they are as non-Euclidean as the sky above. 

They are tall. They must be, to tend the hollow frames that rise hundreds of meters into the amber-grey air. But they are not solid. An Arranger is a column of folded space, a localized gravity well wrapped in a membrane of condensed mist and mineral sweat. They do not have skin; they have event horizons. If you were to touch one, your hand would not meet resistance; it would simply cease to exist as flesh, its matter redistributed into the bright folds of the sky. They move through the city by falling sideways through dimensions, stepping from the base of a building to its apex without traversing the space in between. 

They do not experience shelter, because they do not experience exposure. To an Arranger, the concept of a "wall" is a primitive, almost offensive limitation. They exist within the frames, their bodies interpenetrating the pale, bone-like lattices. They are the amber pulse that travels upward through the structures. When a building brightens, it is because an Arranger is moving through it, their mass resonating with the mineral veins in the stone, drawing up the brine and the heat from the water table below. 

The Arrangers communicate, but not through sound. The total roar of the city is their voice, but it is a voice they speak with their whole bodies. They modulate the waste-sound of the sky-folds, shaping the acoustic force into complex, three-dimensional standing waves that ripple through the limestone. To you, it is a deafening, directionless roar. To an Arranger, it is a symphony of data—a constant, flowing conversation about the island’s growth, the mineral content of the brine, the phase-state of the Processors, the structural integrity of the frames. They speak in vibrations that travel through the stone at frequencies below your hearing, and in pulses of amber light that travel along the building frames like signals through a nervous system. 

They are tending the island. That is their purpose, their obsession, their entire existence. The land is still rising; the tectonic invitation is still open, and the Arrangers are the hosts of this geological party. They direct the Processors where to defecate their mineral-rich waste. They guide the Phasing Swarm to the lichens that threaten to clog the building frames. They adjust the resonance of the hollow towers to ensure the sound remains a tool rather than a weapon. 

And they are waiting. 

In the twelve seconds of silence—when the sound stops and the island holds its breath—the Arrangers are perfectly still. In that fragile, ringing absence, they listen to the water moving through the young limestone. They are waiting for the island to finish rising. They are waiting for the rock to finally settle, for the soil to deepen, for the geometry of the sky to stabilize into its final, incomprehensible shape. 

They are the shepherds of a world that is still becoming. They are the mind of the city, the voice of the stone, the keepers of the Arrangement. And they are as much a part of this place as the wet lime, the hot copper, and the light that comes from the wrong direction.
