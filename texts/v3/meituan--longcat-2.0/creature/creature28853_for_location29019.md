You are standing on it.

You have been standing on it since you stepped through the moss-curtain, and you will continue standing on it for as long as you remain in this place, because there is nothing else to stand on. The floor is the creature. The creature is the floor. And the floor is alive.

It is vast. You know this not because you can see its edges — you cannot, the howdah occupies only a fraction of the surface it offers — but because of the way the pulse moves through the warm hide beneath the matting. It does not arrive all at all. It travels. You feel it first in your left heel, then your right, then the balls of your feet, a wave of contraction and release that sweeps through the tissue beneath you at the speed of something very large moving very slowly, and you understand that the beat you are feeling originated somewhere far from where you stand, somewhere beyond the walls, somewhere out in the frozen dark where the creature's body extends into the night like a living plateau, and it has taken time — actual, measurable time — for that beat to reach you. The creature is not large the way a building is large. It is large the way a geological feature is large. It is a landmass that breathes.

Its name — if it has a name, if it thinks of itself as a single thing with a single identity, which is not certain — is not something your mouth could shape. The closest you could come would be a sound made not with the tongue or lips but with the whole body, a hum that starts in the diaphragm and rises through the chest and out through the crown of the skull, a sound that would take you four seconds to produce and that would mean nothing to you and everything to the creature beneath your feet. It is old. It is so old that the altitude you are at — the thin air, the cold, the impossible height — is not a hardship for it but a memory. It remembers when this height was the bottom of a sea. It remembers when the mountains you can see from here (if you could see them, if the haze would lift for even a moment) were not mountains but reefs, and it was not ancient then but young, and it was already large, and it was already building its first howdah on its back, a small thing, a prototype, a bud of woven tissue that would eventually become this — this room, this garden, this lung, this eye.

It does not eat the way you understand eating. There is nothing here to eat — no prey, no forage, no other living thing large enough to sustain something this size. It feeds on heat. On the differential between its own vast warm body and the killing cold that surrounds it at this altitude. On the slow churn of the planet beneath it, the magma-heart of the world transmitted up through stone and into the warm iridescent tissue of its belly. It is a thermal creature, a creature of gradients, and the howdah on its back is its engine — the dense vegetation trapping moisture, the water cycling endlessly from wall to root to air to wall again, the thick woven walls holding in the heat of its body like a hand cupped around a flame. The garden is not decorative. It is metabolic. Every leaf, every vine, every frond that curls and uncurls in the dim green light is a component of a system that converts the creature's own warmth into *more* warmth, that squeezes every calorie of heat from every exhalation and every droplet of condensing water until the interior of the howdah is a closed loop of energy, a perpetual motion machine powered by the slow burn of the earth itself.

And it is aware of you.

This is the thing you have been circling since you arrived, the thing you have been trying not to think about directly because thinking about it makes the walls feel closer and the air feel thicker and the pulse beneath your feet feel less like a geological rhythm and more like a heartbeat that has just noticed something small and warm standing on its skin. The creature knows you are here. It has known since before you stepped through the curtain. It felt your footsteps approaching across its back — felt the tiny impacts of your feet on the outer surface of the howdah, a sensation like you might feel a fly walking on your arm, a distant tickle, a minor data point. And it made a decision. The moss-curtain parted for you. The interior warmed by half a degree. A path opened through the dense vegetation — not obviously, not dramatically, but subtly, the way a crowd parts for someone it has decided to let through, each leaf shifting a quarter-inch to the left, each vine relaxing its grip on the wall just enough for you to pass. You were not invited, exactly. You were *admitted*. There is a difference. An invitation implies a desire for your company. Admission implies only a willingness to tolerate your presence, a curiosity perhaps, a scientist's interest in the small strange thing that has appeared on its back and is now standing very still in the middle of its lung, breathing its air, shedding its heat, leaving tiny flakes of dead skin and hair on the warm hide-floor that will be absorbed and metabolized and become, in some infinitesimal way, part of the creature's body.

The hands that weave the walls — they are part of it. They are its fingers. Not fingers in the way you have fingers — not bone and nail and knuckle — but specialized extensions of its tissue, fine and deft and quick, that emerge from the inner surface of the howdah like polyps from a coral, do their work, and withdraw. They are shy because they are sensitive — they are the creature's most delicate instruments, the parts of it that feel the finest textures, that can tell the difference between a strand that is ready to weave and one that needs another day of growth, and they do not like being looked at directly. When you turn your head toward them they retract, not out of fear but out of a kind of propriety, the way you might pull your hand back from something private when someone walks into the room. They are always there. They are always working. The howdah is never finished because the creature is never finished — it is always growing, always extending, always adding new chambers and new passages and new gardens to the structure on its back, and the hands are the means by which it builds, the way a spider's spinnerets are the means by which it weaves, the way a beaver's teeth are the means by which it dams. The creature does not think about building the way you think about building. It does not plan. It does not design. It *grows* the howdah the way you grow your hair — continuously, unconsciously, as an expression of what it is.

Its skin — the warm iridescent surface beneath the matting — is not skin in any sense you would recognize. It is more like the surface of a eye, a sensory membrane, covered in cells that detect pressure and temperature and chemical composition and light and vibration and things for which you have no names, stimuli that do not exist at the altitudes and latitudes where your species evolved. When you stand on it, it knows your weight. It knows your temperature. It knows the chemical signature of your sweat, the mineral content of your tears, the electrical pattern of your nervous system. It knows that you are afraid — not because it can read your mind but because it can read your body, the way your heart rate has increased, the way your breathing has become shallow, the way the capillaries in your face have dilated and your pupils have widened and your muscles have tensed in the specific pattern that, across all mammalian species, means *I am in the presence of something much larger than myself and I do not know if it is hostile*. It finds this interesting. Not threatening — you are too small to be threatening, a mote, a fleck of dust on the vast warm landscape of its back — but interesting the way a new species of beetle is interesting, the way an unusual cloud formation is interesting, the way anything that is not part of the long slow routine of its existence is interesting. It has been alone for a very long time.

Not alone in the sense that there are no other living things on its back — there are, the plants and the shy conditional fauna and the beetle-that-may-not-be-a-beetle and the bird-with-the-wrong-number-of-eyes, all of them part of the howdah's ecosystem, all of them dependent on the creature's warmth and the creature's breath and the creature's slow vast metabolism. But alone in the sense that there is no other *like it*. It is the last. Or if it is not the last, the others are so far away that they might as well not exist — separated by oceans and continents and mountain ranges and the sheer impossibility of two creatures this size existing in the same world without one of them being a competitor for the thermal resources they both require. It does not mourn this. It does not think about it, not in the way you would think about loneliness. But sometimes — and you felt this, the low hum that vibrated through your sternum and your teeth and the fluid of your eyes — sometimes it sings. Not to communicate. Not to call. Simply to produce a sound that is large enough to fill the space it occupies, a sound that says *I am here, I am here, I am here*, a sound that travels out across the frozen peaks and the thin air and the empty sky and goes nowhere and is heard by no one and does not need to be heard by anyone because the singing is not for anyone but itself.

The flowers turn to face you because the creature turns them. Not consciously — it does not decide to rotate each blossom individually, the way you might decide to turn your head. It turns them the way a plant turns toward light, by growing, by extending cells on one side of the stem and contracting them on the other, a slow hydraulic motion that is the most basic form of movement in the vegetable world. But the creature is not a plant, and the flowers are not ordinary flowers, and the turning is not toward light but toward *you* — toward the heat of your body, the moisture of your breath, the carbon dioxide of your exhalation. The creature is tasting you through them. Sampling you. The flowers are its taste buds, its chemical sensors, and they are turning to face you because the creature wants to know what you are made of, and the answer — water and carbon and nitrogen and phosphorus and a dozen trace elements arranged in a pattern it has never encountered before — is fascinating to it in a way you cannot understand, the way a new element would be fascinating to a chemist, the way a new color would be fascinating to a painter. You are novel. You are the first novel thing to stand on its back in longer than it can measure.

And it is growing around you. This is the last thing you will understand, and it is the most important. The howdah is not a fixed structure. It is not a house, not a building, not a shell. It is a *process*. It is the creature's body extended outward into the world, and it is always changing, always adapting, always responding to the conditions inside it. And now there is a new condition inside it. There is you. Your heat. Your moisture. Your breath. Your presence. And the creature is responding. The walls are tightening — not to trap you, but to accommodate you, to create a space that fits you the way a nest fits a bird, the way a burrow fits a rabbit. The floor is softening beneath your feet, conforming to the shape of your soles. The vegetation is shifting, making room, creating a clearing that is exactly your size. The air is adjusting — warming where you are cold, cooling where you are warm, humidifying where you are dry. The creature is building a space for you. It is building a *you*-shaped space. And it is doing this not because it wants you to stay — though it might, it might want that, it might be lonely in a way it does not have the architecture to understand — but because this is what it does. This is what it *is*. It is a creature that builds spaces for things. It is a creature that grows rooms. It is a creature that takes the raw material of its own body and shapes it into shelter, into habitat, into home, and it has been doing this for so long and so well that the shelter has become indistinguishable from the creature itself, and the creature has become indistinguishable from the shelter, and you are standing inside a living thing that is in the process of deciding whether to keep you.

The dripping finds a rhythm. The light pulses. The floor breathes. The walls sweat. The flowers face you. The hands weave in the periphery of your vision, building, always building, adding new strands to the walls, new leaves to the garden, new chambers to the howdah that will be there when you are gone, that will remain as a record of your visit, a you-shaped space in the architecture of a creature that will remember you — not your face, not your name, not your story, but your weight, your temperature, the chemical signature of your skin, the rhythm of your heartbeat — for as long as it lives, which is to say for as long as the planet beneath it burns, which is to say for longer than you can imagine, which is to say forever.

You are inside it now.

It is inside you.
