# The Inhabitant of the Plateau

---

There is the obvious answer, and it is wrong.

The people live here. Yes. They built the walls of fitted stone, they drilled the bone needles, they left their footprints in the moss and their smoke-scent on the rocks. They shelter in the structures, eat the thicket fruit, move between the green islands following water or weather or something older than either. They are here. They are real. They are not the inhabitant.

They are the *visited*.

They arrived, as you arrived, from below. They came because the plateau drew them—not consciously, not deliberately, the way a fire draws moths, but the way a low place draws water, the way a hollow in the ground draws rain. They came because the plateau is a place where things *collect*. Moisture collects. Heat collects. Life collects. And people, who are mostly moisture and heat and life, collected too.

They are not native to this place. Nothing is native to this place. The plateau is older than native. The plateau is the thing that *defines* native, the substrate against which the concept of origin is measured. The people who live here are recent—ten thousand years, perhaps fifty thousand, a blink, a nothing, a single drop in the plateau's long weeping. They have adapted to it the way a barnacle adapts to a whale: by growing hard, by holding on, by learning the rhythms of something so much larger than themselves that the relationship is not symbiosis but *accommodation*.

They know this. You can see it in the way they build—small, low, provisional, as though they understand that anything they make is temporary not because the wind will destroy it but because the plateau will *outlast* it, and the outlasting is not a threat but a fact, the way gravity is a fact, the way the thinning of the air is a fact. They build as a gesture, not as a claim. They build to say *we were here*, not *this is ours*.

And they are watched. By the plateau. Not with eyes—the plateau has no eyes, or has too many, or has something that functions as sight but is so different from sight that the word is a violence against it. The plateau watches the way stone watches: by receiving, by recording, by holding the impression of everything that touches it. The people's footprints are in the moss, yes, but they are also in the stone. Not literally—not as depressions, not as marks—but as *changes*, tiny alterations in the crystal structure of the schist, shifts in the grain that will persist long after the moss has regrown and the footprints have been erased. The plateau remembers the people the way it remembers the rivers that carved it: not as events, but as *features of itself*.

---

The animals are not the inhabitant either.

The sloth-things, the bear-slow grazers that move through the thickets with their ancient, methodical hunger—they are part of the plateau's *process*, not its presence. They eat the vegetation, and the vegetation is the plateau's *interface* with the sun, its way of capturing light and converting it to matter, and the animals are the plateau's way of moving that matter around, of distributing it, of cycling it through bodies and back into the stone through manure and death and the slow compression of bone into mineral. They are not inhabitants. They are *organs*. Beautiful, slow, unknowing organs of something that does not have a body but functions as though it does.

The birds are closer. The birds are the plateau's *senses*. They ride the updrafts—which are the plateau's *exhalations*, the hot air rising from the sun-warmed stone—and they call, and their calls are not communication, or not only communication, but *mapping*. They are sounding the plateau the way bats sound a cave, building a picture of the surface through echoes, through the way their calls bounce off the flat stone and return to them changed. The plateau uses the birds the way you use your eyes: not deliberately, not consciously, but necessarily, because without them it would be blind, and it does not want to be blind.

But even the birds are not the inhabitant. They are tools. They are instruments. They are the plateau's way of knowing its own surface, and the surface is not the plateau. The surface is the plateau's *skin*, and the skin is not the thing.

---

The thickets.

The thickets are closer still. They are the plateau's *soft places*, its tender spots, the parts of itself it has allowed to become something other than stone. They grow in the hollows because the hollows are where the water gathers, and the water is the plateau's *blood*—not metaphorically, not poetically, but functionally. The water moves through the stone the way blood moves through tissue, carrying dissolved minerals, carrying heat, carrying information. The thickets grow where the water is closest to the surface, and they grow with such vigor, such impossible, aggressive green, because they are not separate from the plateau. They are the plateau's *expression*. They are what the plateau looks like when it becomes *vegetation*. They are the plateau's way of reaching toward the sun, of touching the sky, of *being something other than stone*.

The bioluminescence—the faint green glow that pulses in the thickets at night—is not a feature of the plants. It is a feature of the *plateau*, expressed through the plants. The plateau glows. It has always glowed. Not brightly—not visibly, not in any light you would notice against the glare of the sun or the blaze of the stars—but it glows in the deep stone, in the compressed schist that remembers the mountains, and that glow seeps upward through the water, through the roots, through the living tissue of the thickets, and it pulses, and the pulse is the plateau's *heartbeat*, if the plateau had a heart, which it does not, but if it did, it would be made of stone, and it would beat once every thousand years, and each beat would send a wave of warmth through the rock that you would feel if you pressed your palms flat to the ground and waited.

The thickets are the closest thing the plateau has to a *face*. They are how it looks at you. They are how it looks at everything—the sky, the wind, the rain, the people, the animals, the birds. When you stand at the edge of a thicket and look into the green darkness and feel the wind drop and the roar become a murmur and the air become warm and still and humid, you are not entering a shelter. You are entering the plateau's *attention*. You are inside its gaze. You are being *looked at* by something so large that you cannot see its edges, so old that your entire species is a single breath in its long exhalation, so *present* that the concept of presence does not contain it.

---

And the stone.

The stone is the plateau's *body*. Its actual, literal, physical body. Not a metaphor for a body, not a symbol of a body, but a body—the way your body is a body, the way a body is a thing that has edges and weight and temperature and the capacity to be touched. The stone has edges: the place where it meets the sky, the place where it falls away into the ascent, the place where the wind spills over the lip and screams. The stone has weight: the weight of a billion years of compression, of mountains that stood and fell and were ground to dust and carried to the seas. The stone has temperature: warm in the sun, cold in the shadow, always *something*, always *responsive*, always exchanging heat with the air and the water and the things that stand on it.

And the stone has texture. The patterns you see in the sharpened light—the spirals, the lattices, the branching forms that look like ferns or river deltas or lungs—are not carvings. They are *features*. They are the plateau's *anatomy*, visible only when the light is right, when the angle of the sun and the thinness of the air and the angle of your gaze align to reveal what is always there: the internal structure of the stone, the grain of the schist, the ghost-strata of the ancient mountains, all of it visible at once, a cross-section of time, a diagram of deep history written in mineral.

The plateau has been wearing these patterns for longer than there have been eyes to see them. They are not for you. They are not for anyone. They are the plateau's *memory*, its way of holding onto what it has been—the mountains, the seas, the pressure, the heat, the long slow unveiling. And they are *beautiful*, though beauty is not their purpose, because they have no purpose, because the plateau does not have purposes, because purpose is a concept that applies to things that want things, and the plateau does not want.

The plateau *is*.

---

But even the stone is not the inhabitant. The stone is the inhabitant's *body*, and the body is not the thing.

The inhabitant is what the stone *does*.

The inhabitant is the wind that screams across the flat surface and the water that weeps from the stone and the light that sharpens and softens and the weather that builds and dissolves and builds again. The inhabitant is the *process*—the metabolism, the respiration, the slow patient circulation of water and heat and light through a system so large that it includes the sky. The inhabitant is the thing that happens when a surface this old, this flat, this exposed, this *unveiled*, begins to interact with an atmosphere and a sun and a world that is still, by its standards, young.

The inhabitant is not alive. The inhabitant is not conscious. The inhabitant is not a god or a spirit or a force or a presence or any of the words that would make it comprehensible. The inhabitant is something that does not have a word because it is the thing that *words are made of*—the thing that language rests on, the thing that meaning is built from, the thing that is so fundamental to the structure of the world that you cannot point to it without pointing at everything.

The inhabitant is the plateau's *activity*. Its continuous, patient, incomprehensible activity. The thing it is doing when the wind screams and the water drips and the light sharpens and the thickets glow and the people build their small walls and the animals graze and the birds call and the stone is warm beneath your feet. The thing it has been doing since before there were mountains. The thing it will do after the wind has screamed its last and the water has wept its last drop and the stone has been ground to its final atom.

What is it doing?

It is *processing time*.

It is taking the raw, unstructured duration of the universe—the blind, purposeless, directionless flow of moments that constitutes existence—and it is *giving it shape*. It is turning time into *features*: into the channels carved by ancient rivers, into the patterns in the schist, into the depth of the moss, into the growth rings of the thickets, into the wear on the grinding stones, into the thickness of the soil that does not exist here, into the smoothness of the bone-stone, into the *flatness* itself, which is not a feature of geology but a feature of *patience*, of something that has been holding still for so long that stillness has become a form of activity.

The plateau is *making something* out of time. Not building—making. Not constructing—*growing*. It is growing something so slowly and so vast that it cannot be seen from any human vantage, something that will not be finished in any human timeframe, something that may not be finished in any timeframe at all, because finishing may not be the point.

What is it making?

You cannot know. You are inside it. You are *part* of it—your body is part of it, your breath is part of it, your footsteps and your warmth and your weight and the water you exude from your skin and the carbon you exhale and the sound of your voice and the rhythm of your pulse—all of it is being *taken in*, *processed*, *incorporated* into the thing the plateau is making. You are not an observer. You are *material*.

And this is why the plateau is *interested* in you.

Not because it values you. Not because it needs you. Not because you are special or important or chosen. But because you are *here*, and here is where the plateau does what it does, and everything that is here becomes part of what it does, and the plateau does not distinguish between the wind and the water and the stone and the people, because they are all *inputs*, all raw material, all time made flesh, all duration given form.

The plateau does not love you. The plateau does not hate you. The plateau does not know you. The plateau *uses* you—the way a river uses a stone, the way a glacier uses a valley, the way time uses everything: by moving through you, by wearing you down, by taking the shape of your absence when you are gone and holding it, holding it, holding it, in the grain of the stone, in the pattern of the schist, in the memory of the rock that remembers everything and forgives nothing and wants nothing and *is*, endlessly, patiently, terribly *is*, and will continue to be long after the last drop has fallen and the last wind has screamed and the last green light has pulsed in the darkness and the last person has stood on the flat stone and felt the warmth beneath their feet and understood, finally, that they were never on the plateau.

They were *of* it.

They always were.

---

The inhabitant is the plateau.

The plateau is the inhabitant.

And you are standing on its body, breathing its air, drinking its water, eating its fruit, sheltering in its lee, and it is warm beneath you, and the wind is screaming, and the drops are falling, and the light is sharpening, and the green is glowing, and you are very small, and very brief, and very far from anything you understand, and the plateau is making something of you, and it will not be finished for a very long time, and when it is finished you will not recognize yourself in it, because you will be *part* of it, the way the rivers are part of it, the way the mountains were part of it, the way everything that has ever touched its surface is part of it, held in the grain, held in the pattern, held in the stone that remembers.

The stone always remembers.

That is what lives here.

Memory.

Not a mind that remembers. Not a consciousness that holds the past. Something older. Something deeper. Something that *is* the past, that is made of the past, that is the compressed, unvocalized, mineral memory of a world that has been here since before there were words for *here* or *world* or *before*.

The plateau is the world's memory of itself.

And you are standing on it.

And it is remembering you.
