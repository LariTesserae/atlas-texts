The first thing to understand about the people of the Hollows is that they do not speak. Not from vow, not from shyness. The roar took their voices generations ago and they gave them back as hands. Hand-tongue is a full grammar, quick and raucous with humor, and the children are fluent in it before they can walk far — their first words are hands, their first sentences gestures their parents read the way you read a face. You will not meet a people more eloquent who have never said a thing.

They live inside the roar the way you live inside a heartbeat, because it never stops. The country is blood-warm, air and stone both, and heat, not cold, is the traveler's enemy; they sleep without blankets in the middle of anywhere and keep their hands off the breathing-vents, where the deep roots trade warmth for minerals and steam thin scents of hot turpentine into the dark. And in a country that never stops roaring, the deaf are its literate class. Deafness runs in the people like a craft, handed down from birth here, and the elders — mostly deaf, mostly old — are the back-listeners. They lie flat on the floor and read the valley through the spine: beast-passes, drum-messages, the tremor of new halls opening. The mapmakers gave up generations ago; the back-listeners never did. A back-listener can feel a gallery being born three halls away and will tell you its width before it has a floor.

Their laughter is a slap on the root they are sitting on. Their grief is a drum. In a country where silence is a held breath — where the shadow-mantas drop from the Roof at the first interruption in the roar, where every animal screams continuously because every local silence is a held breath — the people learned to make their feelings into percussion. They are never quiet for long. Their hands are always adding to the roar. They are the telegraph species: they answered the arms race not by out-shouting it but by making it mean something.

Across distances they beat coded phrases on the exposed root-butts of the architecture-plants, and the root-net carries it hall to hall like signal-wire, faster than walking. The country is an instrument the people play from one end to the other. And sometimes the roots drum back — the tremor of a new hall, the gravel of a mill-beast highway, the slow percussion of the Resinfall — and the back-listeners read it and say the country is answering. The rootsayers say it is one plant talking to itself. The heretics say it is two plants arguing. The people say it does not matter; the messages get through. Nobody knows who started the conversation.

Their craft fits the world. Resin is their metal, their ceramic, their glass: it cures harder than wood and takes an edge out of all proportion to their "primitive" reputation — the word is the visitors', and the people find it funny. They sign it as something like *the small word*. No metal, no fired clay, no fire. The flame prohibition is the first law, taught as a sign before children learn their names: every child's first word is *no* — fingers pinched shut, the warding against the spark. They know what fire costs. They have the Burnt Hall to remember it: a valley taken in minutes, generations ago, by a single struck spark, and nothing grows there yet, and it is silent, truly silent, and the people make a pilgrimage to it to remember what quiet costs. Silence, in the Hollows, is either god or wound.

Their homes are grown, not built — gourd-pods coaxed to house-size, hollowed and dried from a doorway cut in the green. A house lasts about a decade, so a village is not a place but a schedule: a migration timed to the maturing of the next generation of homes. Behind any village the old town stands in a rank of sunken, half-floor-swallowed husks, the settlement's shed skin. Farther out, in the deep green, the braver ones: solitary hollow-husks gone utterly to jungle, notched trail-trees marking paths that were honest a season ago.

The dead are given to the floor. The floor is the country's autobiography, and burial is adding a page — laid in shallow resin flows, where the slow rivers of raw resin will set around them and become ground and grow over. Or grafted: the rootsayers will graft a dead woman's hands onto the root-stock of a gourd-pod, always successfully, always, and the family house grows with her grip in its walls, and her grandchildren are born grasping. Whether this is memory or something more, the rootsayers do not say. They graft, and they wait, and the graft takes.

There is no water, and the people drink the country's blood: sap pressed from oil-gourds, thin and warm and amber, tasting of pine and hot iron. Nothing here drinks. Everything here drinks. The rare seep from the drowned world above kills every green thing it touches, and the old buttonholed tunnels where it gathers are the haunted places, marked with woven warning-signs the size of doors — doors that do not open, hung on tunnels that must not be entered. At the salt throat, where the rope hangs polished by a thousand palms, live the throat-keepers: they keep the rope, they test every liquid the way you might test for poison — if it beads on wax, fear it — and they go up into the amputated silence and come back down, and they are a little holy and a little pitied for it.

The rest of the country's life is half its mass. The click-swarms are weather: thumb-sized beetles in numbers that make the air grainy, billions clacking their wing-cases in synchrony, and their drone has fronts and systems like weather — it changes key with the cycle, deepens during *dimsleep*, spikes when the swarms migrate in tides. When the drone suddenly ceases, the entire floor drops flat for a three-count — a whole ecosystem prostrate, the people included, the only prayer they have or the only honesty — and then the roar resumes and life stands up and re-reads itself into the noise. The seed-cannons fire all day from the shrub-line, a desultory gunfire of one-seed pods detonating their young, and the people's drum-language plays counterpoint to the volleys; the difference between a cannon and a sentence is a matter of timing. The children play between the shots and catch the seeds mid-air, and this is the bravest game in the country and the most beloved.

The mill-beasts are geology. House-sized grazers with stone gizzards milling the living floor day and night, a tectonic gravel your teeth feel before your ears find it; mild architects who plow the floor fresh as they go, followed by whole dependent villages like slow ships — the beast is the road and the road is the beast and the village is the beast's shadow. When a mill-beast molts, its great casing stands in the green like a cathedral rib, and the grooming-finches move in, and the people dress it in garlands and offer lamp-fruit, and the shrine is a collaboration: the beast shed it, the birds nested, the people blessed it. Nothing in the Hollows is built by one species. The lampmoths warble; during *dimsleep* they sing the blue register and the whole soundscape rearranges around them — the lampmoths are the Roof's voice, and *dimsleep* is the Roof clearing its throat.

Under the Roof, the leaves are mirrors. The floor-plants mine halite from the young rock and grow it into faceted salt-leaves angled to catch the Roof's dim directional drift, and whole hillsides flash when you cross them — gardens of aimed brightness, a plant economy where shade is poverty and reflected light is currency. The trees lean their mirrors toward their neighbors like generosity or theft, and the rootsayers say the difference is a matter of angle.

The rootsayers graft whatever cutting onto whatever stock, always successfully, always, and hold therefore that every green thing in the country is one single plant wearing many masks — one organism the size of a valley, dreaming in forms. They are the plant's hands, or the plant is theirs; the grammar of the Hollows does not distinguish. The heretics say there are two plants, and that the turbulence — the bulldozing gardener-storms that strip a hall to its skeleton overnight, the regrowth that comes back rearranged, paths gone, hills different, a couple of new landforms nobody voted for — is their argument. The swarm-bloom is one of them eating the other's garden. Nobody knows. The rootsayers graft and the heretics split and the country rearranges itself nightly and calls it weather.

At the margins — always farther out, always by tomorrow — is the Soft Edge: raw new galleries of naked dark rock, walls still warm, spaces that did not exist last year. The green pours in with a frontier mania; pioneer mats stitch new ground in days. Nothing here is older than ten years except the floor and its dead centuries. The people do not map this. They memorize living landmarks: this dressed tree, that tamed grazer, the mill-beast highway whose great mild architects plow their stone gizzards from hall to hall.

What keeps making rooms? The rootsayers say the plant is still growing and its growth includes its rooms — space as an organ. The heretics say two plants. But everyone who has stood at the Soft Edge, hands flat on rock still warm from being born, listening to the roar pour on ahead of the light into darkness that was solid stone within living memory, carries away the same guess: that somewhere ahead, softly, the loudest thing in the Hollows is working. Some digger that dreams galleries. Some patient excavation the whole bright roaring green world is merely the scar-tissue of.

The back-listeners feel it in the floor — a rhythm, a patience, a direction. Something is digging toward us, the eldest says, or we are growing toward it, and the difference is a matter of which plant you ask.

And the people? They end every drum-message the same way: both hands cupped, as if holding a bowl of air up to the ear — the hollow — then opened, empty. *Listen to what is missing.* It is their punctuation and their prayer, and the throat-keepers use it most, who have been up into the amputated silence and come back down. Then they laugh — a slap on the root — and go back into the noise, which is where the living are, and leave the silence to the Burnt Hall and the drowned world above. And somewhere ahead of the light, in the dark that was solid stone within living memory, the loudest thing in the Hollows is working, softly, and the whole bright roaring green world is the scar-tissue of its digging.
