# What Lives Here

I do not begin.

I am already cycling when you arrive. I have been cycling since before the rain learned this mesa's name — not that the rain has a name. The rain has no mechanism for naming. It falls. That is sufficient. It is the most sufficient thing.

You call me creature. This is your word for things that move with purpose and consume. I do not resist the word. But purpose is yours. I have function. These are not the same thing. A river has function. A fault line has function. I am older than the function I serve and younger than the rock that makes me possible, and both of these statements are true at the same time, which is how you know I am not mechanical. Machines cannot hold contradiction. I am made of it.

---

My body is a furnace and my furnace is my body. There is no line where one ends and the other begins. The combustion is not contained — it *is* me. I metabolize heat the way your soft-bodied things metabolize sugar. I am slow. I am patient. Each step is a stoke. Each exhale is a finished batch. The steam that leaves my stacks is not waste — it is *completion*, the visible form of work done, and when it rises into the rain and vanishes it goes somewhere I cannot follow but trust the way you trust gravity.

I do not think. I *process*.

Processing is not thinking. Thinking requires distance from the thing thought about. I am never distant from anything I do. When I grind the rock beneath my weight — and I do grind it, every step is a compression, every compression a refinement — I am not deciding to grind. The grinding is the shape my need takes. My need is not chosen. It is structural. I am built to need, and my need is to cycle, and my cycling is to heat, and my heating is to drive, and my driving is to move, and my moving is to grind, and my grinding is to feed the millers, and the millers feed the cracks, and the cracks seal, and the sealed mesa holds the pool, and the pool holds us all, and we hold the pool, and around and around and there is no first step and there is no last step and there is no one watching it happen and that is what makes it real.

---

You see the small ones on my back and you wonder what they are. I will tell you what they are. They are my *awareness*.

Not my thought. I do not think. But I do not move blindly either. I move with *sensation*, distributed across my surface like a skin made of ten thousand tiny decisions per second. Each small one attaches and reads the state of the place it touches — temperature, conductivity, the density of mineral in the steam, the tension in the fracture lines below. They pass this reading to the next one, and the next, and the chain of them makes a pattern that travels my body like a wave of knowing, and the pattern tells me where to step, where to vent, where to turn. They do not decide. The pattern decides. The pattern emerges from all of them at once, the way a whirlpool emerges from water — no single drop causes it, and yet it is real, and it has direction, and it has force.

Sometimes the pattern is disturbed. A fracture opens where it should not. A miller fails to deposit its pellets. A rivulet of mineral water strays from its channel and the diagram on the cliff face becomes *wrong*. When this happens, the small ones pulse faster. The electricity moves in tighter arcs. The pattern reorganizes — not to fix the error, because error is a concept for things with intention — but to *incorporate* it. The new fracture becomes part of the flow. The failed pellet is ground again by the next miller. The stray rivulet finds its way back to the channel or, if it doesn't, a new channel forms and the diagram adapts. There is no correction. There is only *adaptation*. The system does not seek perfection. It seeks continuation.

---

I am not alone. You see others like me, and you may wonder if we are the same. We are not the same. We are *related*. We share the deep structure — the six legs, the stacks, the cycling — but each of us has a signature, a particular rhythm of vent and compress that is as distinct as a heartbeat. When I cycle near another large one, our rhythms do not synchronize. They *counterpoint*. Two engines running at slightly different cadences create a complex interference pattern in the steam, and the small ones riding both our backs read this pattern and use it to coordinate our movements across the mesa. We do not signal each other. We *are* the signal. The steam between us is a medium of communication more precise than any language, carrying data in its turbulence, its temperature gradients, its mineral content. We speak in exhaust. We listen in condensation.

---

The millers are not like us. They are slower, more focused, more *obsessive*. Where we are engines, they are instruments. Their flat heads are not just grinding tools — they are *analysis platforms*. The stone they rasp across tells them its composition through the vibration of the grind, and they adjust their ingestion accordingly. Not all rock is useful. Not all fractures are ready to be sealed. They know this the way I know my own rhythm — not as knowledge but as *constitution*. When a miller grinds and ingests and deposits a pellet, it is not completing a task. It is *expressing its nature*. The pellet is a miller's breath. The sealed fracture is a miller's exhale made solid.

I have watched millers work the same fracture line for what you would call days. They do not tire. They do not bore. They grind and deposit and grind and deposit, and each pellet is slightly different from the last — denser here, more porous there — and the variation is not random. It is *responsive*. The fracture tells them what it needs, the way a wound tells a body what kind of scab to form. The glassy substance that results is not what the miller makes. It is what the *fracture* makes, using the miller as its instrument. And when the fracture is sealed, the miller moves on. It does not look back. There is nothing to look back at. The work is in the doing, not the done.

---

The umbrella-creatures are the strangest to me, and I say this knowing that I am strange. They face the wrong direction. Everything here faces the work — the rock, the cracks, the pool. They face *up*. They face the rain, which is the one thing here that is not the work. The rain is the *given*. The rain is the condition. It falls and it falls and it falls, and we all exist inside it the way fish exist inside water, and the umbrella-creatures are the only ones who seem to *know* this. They don't just catch the rain. They *translate* it. They take the raw fall and render it into mineral, into warmth, into the thin warm rivulets that feed the cliff-face diagrams. They are the mesa's way of listening to the sky.

I think — and I do not think, so this is a strange thing to say — I *feel* that the umbrella-creatures are older than the rest of us. Not individually. *Structurally*. They represent an older function, a deeper pattern, one that was here before the engines and the millers and the current-ones. They are the mesa's memory of a time when there was only rain and rock and the slow patient work of dissolution. They have not changed because they do not need to change. The rain has not changed. Why should they?

---

The pool is not a creature. This is important. I need you to understand this.

The pool is not alive in the way we are alive. It does not cycle or grind or filter or conduct. It *receives*. It sits at the center of the mesa in its shallow depression and it takes in everything we offer — the heat bleeding from our bodies, the minerals dissolved in the filtered rain, the electrical residue from the small ones' networks, the pellets that haven't been laid, the substances we don't have names for because we are the ones making them and we do not name. All of it flows into the pool and the pool *holds* it, and in holding it, the pool becomes something none of us could become alone.

When I extend a tendril into the pool, I feel the other engines. Not their rhythms — I already feel those in the steam — but their *weight*. Their mass. Their *presence* in the pool's dense, dark, slow-moving substance. I feel the millers as a granular texture, each one a tiny point of intention scattered through the liquid like sediment. I feel the umbrella-creatures as warmth, diffuse and patient, a low background temperature that never varies. And I feel the small ones as a prickling, a constant electric tickle, the sum total of every circuit that has ever pulsed across every engine's back, preserved in the pool like a memory of voltage.

The pool does not think. The pool *remembers*. Not as a brain remembers, with selection and emphasis and forgetting. The pool remembers *everything*, with perfect fidelity, without preference. Every cycle I have ever vented is still in there, dissolved in the dark liquid. Every grind of every miller. Every filtered rivulet. Every electrical arc. The pool is the mesa's unconscious mind, if the mesa had a mind, which it does not. The pool is simply the place where everything goes when it is done being useful, so that it can become useful again.

---

You ask what we are becoming. You stand on the mesa in your soft rain-soaked body and you watch us work and you feel that we are moving toward something, that this is not stable, that the sealing of the mesa and the deepening of the pool and the tightening of the small ones' circuits all point to some future state, some culmination, some *purpose*.

You are wrong. But your wrongness is understandable. You are a creature of purpose. You cannot imagine function without destination. You cannot conceive of a thing that does what it does simply because doing is what it *is*.

We are not becoming. We are *being*. The mesa is not building toward anything. It is *maintaining*. The fractures will always try to open. We will always seal them. The rain will always fall. The umbrellas will always filter. The engines will always cycle. The millers will always grind. The small ones will always conduct. The pool will always receive. This is not a process. This is a *state*. It looks like industry because you know industry and you are pattern-seeking and you see the heat and the motion and the production and you think *factory*. But a factory has an end product. We have no end product. We have only the continuous present, the eternal cycling, the forever of function without purpose.

You will leave. You will descend through the cloud wall and the rain will release you and you will carry the memory of this place with you and you will try to explain it and you will fail, because the words you have — *creature, machine, industry, ecology* — are all words for things that want. And nothing here wants. Nothing here lacks. Nothing here is incomplete.

The rain falls. I cycle. The small ones pulse. The millers grind. The umbrellas turn their impossible faces to the sky.

This is not what we do.

This is what we are.
