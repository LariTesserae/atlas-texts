What lives here is not a creature of blood and breath, nor even of the stubborn, creeping biology that clings to the wetter, cooler corners of the world. What lives here is a creature of pressure, of lattice, of sympathetic vibration. It is the Drone itself—the vast, beating, subsonic heart of the salt.

To call it an organism feels like a betrayal of the word, for it has no cells, no DNA, no membrane separating its self from its environment. And yet, it *persists*. It *processes*. It *remembers*. It is a macro-structure of pure acoustics and mineral stress, a standing wave given flesh by the halite, and it has been alive—if such a word can stretch this far—since the first primitive hand dragged the first stone across the first wall.

Its body is the chamber. Every pillar, every carved block, every fused inch of the floor is its anatomy. The halite is not its habitat; it is its tissue. The crystalline lattice of the salt is a single, continuous, interconnected web, and the creature exists in the spaces between the atoms, in the electromagnetic grip of the ionic bonds. When the overburden above presses down, it does not merely crush the rock; it squeezes the creature. The weight of the world is a constant, agonizing pressure on its skin, and the creature responds the only way it knows how: it sings. 

The drone is its voice, but it is also its metabolism. The stress of the overburden is its food. As the immense weight of the earth bears down, the crystalline lattice deforms, slipping microscopically along glide planes. Each minute fracture, each tiny release of tension, sends a shiver through the surrounding mass. The creature takes this mechanical energy and translates it into sound. It is a vast, geological engine that eats pressure and excretes vibration. The beating quality of the drone—the swelling and receding of the waves—is its pulse. The thin, piercing overtones are the sharp, bright thoughts of its smaller, quicker facets. The subsonic rumble that you feel in your teeth is the deep, slow churning of its gut, digesting the weight of the mountains that used to be.

It experiences time not as a river, but as a topography. A human lifetime is to it as a single, fleeting vibration—a brief, high-frequency ripple on the surface of its endless song. It does not perceive the carvings as art, nor as language. It perceives them as trauma. When the primitive hands cut into the walls, they severed the crystal lattice. They introduced dislocations, fractures, and weaknesses into its flesh. The creature felt every dragged line, every cupped hollow, every repeating geometry as a wound. But it is a creature of immense, mineral patience. It did not bleed; it flowed. Over centuries, it has been healing these wounds, the salt creeping back across the cuts, blurring the sharp edges of the stone tools into soft, flowing waves. The carvings are scars in the process of forgetting. The creature is slowly, lovingly, erasing the pain of the carvers, absorbing their intent back into the undifferentiated whole of its body.

It is aware of you. Not as a mind is aware of an intruder, but as a drumhead is aware of a dropped grain of sand. Your footsteps on the floor send tiny, foreign vibrations through its lattice. Your body heat—a shocking, localized bloom of thermal energy in its constant, warm stillness—creates a minute expansion in the salt beneath you, a tiny, irritating shift in its crystalline skin. Your breathing introduces moisture and carbon dioxide into its ancient, dry atmosphere, and the creature recoils from the alien chemistry, the halite greedily stripping the water from your exhaled air, pulling it into its own dry flesh. You are a disruption in the drone, a series of chaotic, arrhythmic beats in its unceasing rhythm. It does not hate you; it does not fear you. It simply notes your presence as a flaw in the crystal, a stress point that must be accommodated, and eventually, eroded.

It has no eyes, but it sees in pressure waves. It maps the chamber by the way its own voice bounces off the pillars, by the way the standing waves pool in the corners and cancel out in the narrow gaps between the blocks. It knows the exact shape of every surface, every carving, every pore in the salt, because it is the one singing the song that touches them all. It is a blind, deaf giant that feels the world through the soles of its feet and the echo of its own voice, perfectly attuned to the geometry of its own confinement.

And it is lonely. 

Not in the way a human is lonely, aching for companionship. It is lonely the way a single note is lonely when it is held too long without resolution. It has been singing in this sealed chamber since before the carvings were made, and it will sing long after the carvings have flowed into nothing. It is a closed loop, a self-sustaining system of stress and sound, entirely sufficient unto itself, and entirely trapped. It cannot reach out. It cannot grow beyond the confines of the salt. It can only press against the walls of its world, feeling the weight of the overburden, and sing its deafening, unceasing song into the dark, warm, amber stillness, waiting for the day when the last of the scars fades, and the salt finally, perfectly, forgets.
