# The Inhabitants

---

You came here to see what lives in this place, and that ambition is corrected before your first hour is out. Not because there is too little to see. Because the unit is wrong.

You do not find the fauna of the Canopy Floor. There is no finding. There is only noticing.

Begin with the smallest, because the smallest began with you. The first colonists arrive as weather — cysts, drifting in the warm fog between the columns, light as loosed dust — and they land on the heat rising off your skin and they do not bite. They assess. Within minutes the grazers come to feed on what has landed on you, droplets of animals, clear as oil with intent, visible only as a sheen migrating along your forearm, and behind them, in strict order, the things that feed on the grazers. By the end of the first hour there is a functioning food web in the crook of your elbow, three levels deep, and none of it cares about you beyond your warmth, your wetness, and your geography.

The gentleness unnerves you more than hostility would. A young world would have hurt you on principle — new flesh is a resource, and youth has no manners. This place's violence is finished. Countless generations have calibrated exactly what a large body can spare, and it turns out to be plenty. What remains is less predation than bookkeeping. You are not a meal or a victim here. You are a terrain.

The other strangeness is their confidence. They arrive knowing where your folds and seams should be. Nothing like you has passed this way in an age — you were told that, and you believe it now — and yet they do not hesitate, do not fumble, do not seem to learn. The knowledge was here before you. The place does not keep its knowledge as memory, which fades. It keeps it as shape, which does not.

On the floor, the work is organized. Along the warm streams, the fist-sized beetles move in processions that turn the channels into roads, and the wet gleam on their carapaces is not their own but a film of tenants that mine the fog for metal — the copper in the water, the ammoniac edge in the air, is their tailings. But mark what the beetles are, because the category matters. They are not hunters. They are infrastructure. They churn the channels, and everything behind them feeds in the opening they make — pickers, gleaners, then the small fierce tier that feeds on those — a supply line running on legs, the place's freight made visible.

When the deep voice comes, all of this stops. The boom rolls through the columns like pressure moving through a hull, and the whole floor drops silent for one full second — clicking gone, rustle gone, the high tone in your teeth gone. Your first silence since arrival. Then the roar restarts from everywhere at once, like surf re-forming, and the processions move on as though nothing happened.

That silence is not absence. It is the whole floor, listening.

The columns teach you about verticality. In the crevices, millimeters wide, whole communities live stacked in strict order of size — the largest deepest, the smallest at the lip, everyone seated exactly where they fit, like organ pipes of animals. The glowing vines, you eventually understand, are not plants at all. Each one is a single budded string — one animal wearing itself as a chain, every segment lit — and when something drifts into that light, a signal runs down the chain like a spark down a fuse, and at the anchored end something grips, or turns, or re-aims. You watched one take a small flier last night, and the vine moved a full hand-width in under a minute, which makes it the fastest thing you have seen in this place. It hunts with light. If it thinks at all, it thinks in illumination.

The coral-like colonies that strain the air are the manufacturing tier, and their industry explains the ground. The pale fibrous mat you stand on — which you first took for this world's version of soil — is not soil. It is their exhaust and their bodies, generation upon generation, compacted until it bears weight. Which means the floor is the dead. But the dead here are not removed, and they are not idle. They are re-tenanted, hollowed and inhabited and put back to work, the pale substrate seeping and processing and excreting on the slowest imaginable schedule. Decomposition, in this place, is not destruction. It is a change of management.

Nothing here is buried. Everything here is occupied.

And some of what occupies it remembers work you did not ask for. Where your feet compress the floor, pale growth rises within the hour — you have learned to check — and it grows too specifically to be a general response, too shaped, as if calibrated for the footfall of something larger and differently-weighted than you. When you pass certain curtains of moss, sweetness blooms in the warm air around your shoulders: an exhalation, an offering organ doing its work on the first large mover to come this way in longer than you can reckon. Something is making gifts for someone who is not coming. You keep being the one to unwrap them. You are a ghost's understudy, and the ghost has been dead so long that even the vacancy it left behind has hardened into architecture.

Above you, the permanent storm. The canopy's flyers are born aloft and die aloft — mate, drift, fall — and for most of them the floor is a myth. The drone that sits in your teeth is their single collective artifact: all of them, fused into one continuous tone. The rolling waves of clicking that pass overhead, you have decided, are not alarm. They are decisions, moving through a thousand bodies at once — agreement propagating like weather.

What falls out of that storm arrives here as event. The padded thud that struck near you in your first hour — you no longer mishear it.

It was a delivery.

What follows a fall is not chaos. It is a calendar. The sprinters come within minutes, flat and fast and leggy; by nightfall the site has visitors stacked three deep; within days the slow dissolvers have taken over, and then the haulers, working the body down into the mat thread by thread, until what remains is a mound, and the mound, in its turn, exhales the same sweetness that blooms at your own passage. A fall here does not end. It concludes into an address. Even the news of a fall is consumed.

And in the floor, sleeping, are the ones who live for that news. Cysts — dormant decades, perhaps longer — their entire bodies one long wait, tuned to the percussion of a significant arrival. Your footfall signature has been waking them by the tens of thousands, and what they wake expecting is a corpse. Most discover the error, seal, and go back to sleep. One did not.

You see it constantly now: pale as the fiber mat, limb-simplified, eyeless, soft in all the ways its guild requires, built to live inside large fallen bodies. An interior animal. It woke for your footsteps and found you standing — a corpse that walks — and it could not reconcile the categories, so it did what this place does with everything: it routed you in as an ongoing event. Because you leak. Skin, breath, warmth, oil: you are a slow continuous fall, a drizzle of manna with a pace. By the third day it holds a fixed position at your left heel. By the fifth it grazes your growing skin-garden at night while you sleep, and you wake trimmed, balanced, tidier than you lay down. You have acquired an external organ by mistake. Every old partnership here began this way — the beetle and its gleaming film, the grazer and the second liver you can no longer distinguish from its back. None of them can tell you when the mistake became the arrangement.

Almost nothing here that looks at you has eyes. This takes a while to feel, and then you cannot stop feeling it. Eyes are for places with edges and distances; here, distance is measured in wetness and echo. What they do instead is aim — you feel it arrive before you see it, a body turning all its listening toward you like one enormous ear. Attention without a gaze. It settles on you as pressure. There are followers too, now: things that hold a fixed distance behind you, that stop when you stop, that are waiting, unfailingly, wherever you usually sleep. They are not curious about you. They are employed by you — follow the large mover, harvest the disturbance — and there has been no large mover in an age, and something in them still knows the job. You have been slotted into a vacancy this place keeps open the way a lock keeps a keyhole: without intent, without cost. Do not find it flattering. Nothing here flatters. It is bookkeeping.

Walk toward any edge and the roar thins, and between the deep dark and the outer dimness lies a frontier where the fauna changes character — duller, tougher, broader in appetite, thinner in ornament. The deep natives decline to it and stop, and the boundary of their world, you finally see, is not drawn in water or light. It is drawn in loudness. Hung between the columns, densest where the deep voices are loudest, are wide translucent sheets — tympanic feeders, straining sound the way the coral-things strain fog, their tissues shivering faintly with the work of eating the drone. In silence they starve. Others you test, gently, toward the edge: they simply soften, forget their shapes, stop. The border is a border of noise, and you can map it with your teeth.

As for the deep voices themselves, you have three theories and no hope. The first — that the booms are the settling of columns, mass groaning abiotically — lasted two days, until you noticed the voices were two: distinct in depth, distinct in accent, and answering each other. The second is that somewhere in the lattice, embedded past any chance of seeing, there are animals of such size that anatomy has quietly failed to describe them — grown through with architecture now, load-bearing parts of one another, their breathing the slow motion of the air, their pulse the booms, the columns sounding because the columns have become, in part, their bodies. The third theory is the second with the numbers removed: that the giants are not *in* the place; the place is partly made of them, the architecture built of their patience, in the way a reef is built of its dead — except here the makers never left the building.

You will want to call them gods, and the chamber their court, and yourself a petitioner. Resist it. Nothing here is worshipped. Everything here is tenanted, and tenancy runs both directions: the giants speak through the whole chamber as through a vocal tract — columns resound, floor carries, and along the boom-lines the sound-feeders grow dense and bright. Their speech is the floor's manna. The room is their mouth, and also their crop. The place has farmed them, and been farmed by them, past any useful boundary between the two.

Ask, then, who lives here — demand the census — and the answer, arriving slowly, from below: one. One resident, wearing everything. Its blood is the warm rain. Its skeleton is resin and bone. Its skin is the slow parade of the beetles; its senses, the ten thousand listening bodies; its thought — the word being wrong for something with no center and no hurry, but the nearest word — is the sound. What you have been walking through is not the home of an animal, exactly. It is an argument, worn into a shape, that the difference between *living* somewhere and *being* somewhere is a distinction only young worlds need to make.

You will leave eventually. You have to; you are not built to stay. The place, having no interest in keeping anything, will let you go — and will take your footfall signature with it, filed below the floor, a cyst newly educated, holding the shape of your walking for a hundred years, a word kept for saying later. That is how a place with no memory keeps faith with rare things. Not by remembering. By waiting.

But that is later. Tonight you stand where you have been standing, and somewhere deep in the lattice something vast shifts its weight, and the boom rolls through the columns toward you — and stops here. All around you, at once, the floor drops into silence. The clicking dies. The drone cuts. The beetles freeze mid-stride with their tenants still on their backs; the lantern-chains go dark end to end, every segment of every chain listening through its light. One full second. You standing inside the held breath of a valley-sized mouth, heard, at last, in full.

Then the roar returns. Baseline. The drips resume their slow, warm timekeeping. Whatever report was filed about you has been accepted into the general sound, and the place will consider what you were — in the only way it considers anything — slowly, thoroughly, at the pace of geology, for a very, very long time.
