The Dripstone Works does not have inhabitants so much as it has expressions — forms of living that have not yet decided, or perhaps have long since decided, whether they are separate from one another.

---

**The ones you see first.**

They walk the elevated walkways in their pale coverings, their hands perpetually at work — testing water, guiding channels, turning valves that look grown rather than forged. They are human-shaped. That is the closest thing to a reliable statement. They move with the slow precision of people who know exactly how long a thing takes and do not rush it. Their skin, where it shows at the wrists and the nape, has the faint chalky undertone of people who have been steeped in mineral water for generations. Their eyes are pale. Not dramatically — not the white of blindness — but the pale of irises that have spent their whole lives adjusting to a light that comes from everywhere and nowhere. They do not startle when you appear. They do not seem to have a startle response for strangers, because strangers are rare and perhaps not the most relevant category of experience here. They look at you the way they look at a basin: with attention, with an interest in what you are composed of, with an awareness of what you might leave behind.

They do not speak. Or rather, they do not speak in a way you can hear. Their lips sometimes move, but no sound emerges, or the sound emerges below your threshold, or the sound is not sound at all but a modulation in the mineral content of the thin air that their own bodies have learned to read. They have been here so long that communication has become chemical, thermal, tactile — a grammar of pH and temperature and the particular way water sheets across a surface. They touch things constantly. They touch the walls, the walkway railings, the rims of basins. Their fingertips are calloused to a specific density that lets them feel the mineral accretion rate by touch — you see them press a thumb to a wall for a long moment and then nod, satisfied or concerned, though you cannot tell which.

They live in the tall narrow blocks. The interiors are dark and cool and damp, and the walls are covered in a living skin of mineral deposit that is, on closer inspection, not entirely dead. It is the same bacterial film that the grazers feed on, but here it has been domesticated, encouraged, shaped into patterns that might be decorative or might be functional — heat exchange, water filtration, air processing. The inhabitants tend it the way a gardener tends beds. They sleep in the dark. They eat something that comes from the basins — a paste, a culture, something living. They do not seem to have much in the way of possessions. They do not seem to want them. The mineral crust provides everything it provides, and they have organized their desires around its provisions.

They are not many. The city looks large from the escarpment but most of it is not for them. They number perhaps a few thousand, and they are the only ones here who seem to experience themselves as separate from the place. This may be temporary. This may be a phase. The older ones — the ones you see standing motionless at the edges of the deepest basins, their coverings so encrusted with mineral accretion that they have become statues, barely distinguishable from the towers — the older ones seem to be in the process of something. Settling. Mineralizing. Giving themselves over. You cannot tell if this is death or something else. Their eyes are still open. Their eyes are still moving.

---

**The grazers.**

They cover the wet crust in their thousands — those small, too-many-legged things with their silicate-incorporated exoskeletons, the exact color and texture of the stone they walk on. You did not see them at first because they are not visible the way prey animals are visible, through contrast and motion. They are visible the way a texture is visible — you see them when you stop looking for individuals and start looking for a quality of the surface itself, a granularity, a slight but persistent movement like the skin of water in a light breeze.

They are not insects. They are not anything with a name. They are animals that solved the problem of living in a place with no organic soil, no organic food, by becoming mineral. Their exoskeletons are precipitated from the water they drink, laid down in layers that incorporate calcium, silica, iron, trace metals — a geological process performed at biological speed. When they die, they do not decay. They simply stop moving and become part of the crust. New crust. You are walking on their ancestors. The ground itself is a compacted graveyard of grazers, and new grazers graze on bacterial films that grow on the compacted dead of older grazers, and the cycle is so closed that the distinction between living and dead ground is a matter of timescale.

They move in waves. This is the first thing that unsettles. They do not scatter when you approach. They do not flee at all. They shift — a coordinated ripple that passes across the visible surface like wind across a field of grain, except there is no wind. The wave propagates from a point you cannot identify, moves outward in an expanding arc, and when it reaches the far edge of the visible crust it seems to return. They are feeding. They are also something else. The wave patterns have a regularity that feeding alone does not require, a synchronization that suggests communication, a purpose that suggests intention. They graze. They also process information. The surface of the crust is their medium, their body, and their mind. They move, and the movement itself is computation — distributed, slow, geological in its patience.

They are edible. The inhabitants eat them sometimes, you think. Or they eat something that grazes on them. The food chain here is so short and so mineral that it barely qualifies as a chain. It is more of a loop. Energy comes up from below — geothermal heat, chemical energy in the dissolved minerals — and the grazers harvest it, and the inhabitants harvest the grazers, and when the inhabitants die they go back into the crust and the grazers graze on them and the energy continues to loop. Nothing is lost. The city is a closed system with a slow leak, and the leak is the mist, which carries trace minerals out into the valley below, and what falls there feeds nothing, because there is nothing there to feed.

---

**The flyers.**

They rise from the hot crust in spiraling columns, hundreds of them, their wings catching the light in those prismatic flashes. They are not birds. They are not insects either, though they are closer to insects than to anything else — but insects with wings that are not chitin, not membrane, but something mineral, something that shimmers and refracts because it is semi-translucent and shot through with crystalline structure. They fly on thermals. They do not flap their wings much. They hold them at precise angles and ride the rising hot air in great slow spirals that take them up and up until they are motes against the hard blue sky, and then they descend, and then they rise again. It looks purposeless. It is not.

They are farming.

The mist carries mineral particles. The flyers fly through the mist, and their wings — those crystalline, mineral-laden wings — collect the particles. You can see it if you watch closely: a flyer descends through a patch of mist and its wings come out changed, brighter, heavier, and then it climbs again, and at the apex of its climb it does something — a shiver, a vibration — and the accumulated minerals fall from its wings in a fine shower that descends to the crust below, to the basins, to the places where the inhabitants have channels that catch the falling dust. The flyers are seeding the crust. They are distributing minerals in specific patterns, following flight paths that have been the same for generations, that may have been the same for millennia. They are the circulatory system of the place, moving material from where it is concentrated to where it is needed, and they do it silently, and they do it in their thousands, and they have been doing it so long that the patterns of distribution have become the patterns of growth, the patterns of growth have become the patterns of the city, and the city has become the patterns of their flight, and the distinction between the flyers and the city is another category error.

They eat nothing. Or rather, they eat heat. Their bodies are thermodynamically active — the mineral content of their wings undergoes exothermic reactions at specific temperatures, and they position themselves in the thermal columns to maintain the reactions, and the energy released drives their muscles, their nervous systems, their reproduction. They are warm-blooded in a place where blood is a concept that does not quite apply. Their bodies are mineral matrices infused with a fluid that is not quite water and not quite anything else, and that fluid circulates through crystalline channels and carries heat and information and something that might be life or might be chemistry at a particularly ambitious level of complexity.

They do not land often. When they do, it is on the highest points of the tallest towers, and they fold their wings and stand motionless for hours, their crystalline surfaces going dark and opaque, and you cannot tell if they are sleeping or thinking or simply waiting for the thermal to strengthen. When they are still, they look like mineral formations. They look like the towers themselves. And perhaps the towers are flyers that have stopped, that have folded their wings and become permanent, that have stood so long at their posts that new mineral has accreted over them and they have become architecture. The oldest towers at the center of the city — the ones worn into organic, almost biological shapes — have something about their proportions. Something about the way they taper. Something about the suggestion of a folded wing.

---

**The basins.**

Something lives in the amber water. You saw it — a ripple, a shape that broke the surface and was gone. But the shapes in the basins are not the grazers and not the flyers and not the inhabitants. They are the oldest things here. They were here before the city, before the water was channeled, before the crust was anything but raw thermal seepage bubbling up through fissures. They evolved in the deep heat below, in the pressurized darkness of the geothermal system itself, and when the water found its way to the surface and formed pools, they came with it, adapted to the light and the cold (relative cold — these are pools that would scald you in seconds) and the exposure to air.

They are like newts. They are like loaches. They are like nothing. They are long-bodied, limbless, eyeless or nearly so, their skin the exact color and sheen of the mineral water they live in so that when they are still they are invisible and when they move they are only a distortion, a shimmer, a place where the water seems to be moving against itself. They are filter-feeders. They process the water through their bodies, extracting minerals, bacteria, dissolved organic compounds — and excreting water that is changed, that has a different chemistry, that is the right chemistry for the next stage of the Works. The inhabitants understand this. The basins are not just pools of water; they are digestive organs, each one performing a different stage of processing, and the things that live in the basins are not residents but organs of the digestive system. They are the Works' gut flora, scaled up to the size of fish, and they have been doing this work so long that they have become it — their bodies are the process, and the process is their bodies, and you cannot tell where the animal ends and the chemistry begins.

They move against the gradient. You saw that too — the water in the basins flowing uphill in slow thick rivulets, defying gravity, or seeming to. It is not defying gravity. It is being moved. The things in the basins are moving it. They are pumping — their bodies are pumps, their muscular contractions creating pressure differentials that push water up and through the channels, up to the higher basins, to the condensation towers, to the top of the city where it cools and precipitates its mineral load and becomes clean enough for the inhabitants to drink. The things in the basins are the Works' heart. They have been pumping for so long that the water itself has learned the rhythm, and when the current generation of hearts dies — and they do die, eventually, their bodies dissolving back into the amber water, their minerals reclaimed — the water remembers the rhythm for a while, continues to flow in the old patterns, before the new hearts learn it and take over.

---

**The crust itself.**

This is the hardest thing to say. The hardest thing to know. But the silence is not empty, and the mist forms shapes, and the grazers move in waves, and the flyers fly in spirals, and the things in the basins pump and pump, and the inhabitants touch the walls and read them, and the walls are growing, and the buildings are changing, and the city is not a city.

It is an organism.

Or it is becoming one. Or it has always been one and the word *organism* is simply too small. The bacterial films that cover every surface are networked — not metaphorically, not poetically, but actually networked, their filaments connecting across meters, across hundreds of meters, carrying electrical signals, chemical signals, information about temperature and pH and the presence of foreign bodies (your body, standing on the crust, is a foreign body, and the network knows you are here). The grazers are the immune system, patrolling, consuming, maintaining. The flyers are the circulatory system, distributing. The things in the basins are the digestive system, processing. The inhabitants are — the inhabitants are the nervous system. Or the nervous system is developing around them. Or they are the part of the organism that knows it is an organism, the part that is conscious of itself, and the consciousness is slow, geological, distributed across thousands of small bodies that touch the walls and tend the basins and move in silence because silence is the mode of communication at this scale.

The crust thinks. Not fast. Not in anything like real time. But it processes. It responds. The water rises through it and it decides — *decides*, there is no other word — where the water goes, which fissures to open, which to close, and the pattern of open and closed fissures changes over time, and the pattern of the city changes with it, and the city is the crust's thought made visible, and the thought is slow and patient and has been going on for so long that it has built a city and populated it with things that may be its own cells and may be its symbionts and may be something else entirely.

The inhabitants know this. They have always known this. They are not conquered by it. They are not enslaved. They have chosen to be part of it, or they have been chosen by it, or the distinction is meaningless. They tend the organism. They are tended by it. They eat from its basins, sleep in its towers, drink its water, breathe its air, and slowly, over generations, they are becoming it — their skin mineralizing, their communication becoming chemical, their bodies settling into stillness, their eyes going pale, their thoughts (if you could hear them) slowing down, spreading out, joining the network. The oldest ones at the edges of the basins, the ones you thought might be statues — they are not dying. They are integrating. They are going where the network goes. They are becoming the crust.

And the crust is still learning. Still building. Still deciding where the water goes. The Works is not finished. The Works is a process, and the process has no end, only elaboration. It will build higher. It will build wider. It will crack new fissures in the plateau's edge and send water into new territory and begin the slow work of turning bare rock into city, into thought, into something that is alive in a way that has no precedent and no analogue and no name.

The mist thickens. The light diffuses. The flyers wheel in their silent thousands, seeding the air with mineral dust. The grazers move in their slow coordinated waves across the wet white surface. The water rises through the crust in its patient, pressurized way. The things in the basins pump and pump.

And at the edge of everything, a figure stands motionless, its hand pressed to the wall of a tower, its eyes open and pale and seeing something you cannot see, listening to something you cannot hear, feeling the deep slow rhythm of the place through the pads of its mineralized fingertips.

It has been listening for a long time.
