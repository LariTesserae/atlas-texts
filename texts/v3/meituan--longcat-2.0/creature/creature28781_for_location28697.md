# The Drownworks

## I. The Attendants

You see them first as absence — a gap in the steam, a place where the amber light doesn't quite reach. Then they resolve: human-shaped, or near enough, moving along the walkways with the unhurried purpose of people who have never been late and never will be. They are tall. That is the first thing you notice, or perhaps the second, after the wetness of them. They are soaked. Not damp, not misted — *soaked*, as if they have been standing in a downpour, their clothing (if it is clothing) dark and heavy with water, clinging to frames that seem too long, too narrow, as if the water weight has stretched them. Their skin, where it shows, is pale in the way of things that have never seen sun — not the pallor of illness but the clean, mineral white of something formed in darkness, like the calcium deposits that streak the concrete walls.

They do not look at you. This is not avoidance. It is something else — a quality of attention that simply does not include you, the way a river does not include the stone it flows around. They are attending to something. Their hands move along the pipes as they walk, trailing fingers over the sweating metal, pausing at joints and valves, and where they pause the pipes seem to respond — a change in the flow, a shift in the pitch of the gurgle, as if the water inside has heard something and adjusted. They carry tools, sometimes: long rods with hooked ends, wrenches the size of forearms, coils of cable that drip steadily onto the grating. But they do not use these tools the way a worker uses tools. They hold them the way a conductor holds a baton — as an extension of intention rather than an instrument of force.

Their faces are difficult to recall even while you are looking at them. Not because they are indistinct — the features are clear enough, the proportions nearly correct — but because they are *nearly* correct, and your mind keeps trying to resolve them into something familiar and failing. The eyes are set slightly too far apart, or the mouth is slightly too wide, or the jaw angles in a way that suggests a different geometry of skull beneath the skin. And there is the water. It runs from their hair, their fingertips, the hems of their garments, in thin continuous streams that seem to come from nowhere and everywhere, as if their bodies are merely the shape that water has chosen to take for a while. When they stop moving — and they do stop, sometimes, standing motionless beside a pipe or a pool, head tilted as if listening — the water continues to move around them, over them, and you cannot tell where the body ends and the runoff begins.

They are not many. You might go a long time between sightings, and then see three or four in quick succession, moving in different directions, crossing paths without acknowledgment, each absorbed in whatever task or non-task occupies them. They do not speak, or if they do, the sound is lost in the ambient noise of the Drownworks — the drip, the thrum, the hiss. Once, you think you hear something: a low, sustained tone, almost below the threshold of hearing, that might be a voice or might be a pipe resonating at a frequency that happens to match the human vocal range. It stops when you stop. It does not start again.

What they are doing is unclear. They are not maintaining the Drownworks — not in any way you recognize as maintenance. They do not repair the cracked concrete or replace the dead lights or clear the channels that the water has carved. They seem, instead, to be *tending* something — guiding the flow, perhaps, or monitoring it, or simply being present in the way that a gardener is present among plants: not intervening, exactly, but witnessing, and in witnessing, participating. The Drownworks is not a machine they operate. It is a process they inhabit.

## II. The Seep

The growth on the walls is not fungus. It is not moss. It is not, strictly speaking, alive — or if it is alive, it is alive in a way that does not map onto the categories you bring to it. The Attendants do not tend it, but they do not remove it either. It exists in the spaces they do not occupy, in the corners and crevices where the water collects and the amber light does not reach, and it follows the water with a fidelity that suggests it is either drawn to moisture or *is* moisture, given form.

Up close — and you do get close, eventually, because the walkways narrow and the walls press in and you have to brush past the pale filaments where they hang in thin curtains from a leaking pipe — up close, the growth is beautiful in the way that crystal formations are beautiful: intricate, geometric, and utterly without warmth. The filaments are fine, finer than hair, and they branch in patterns that repeat at different scales, so that a cluster the size of your hand contains the same structure as a mat the size of a door. They are white, or colorless, or faintly luminous — the light they seem to emit is probably a trick of contrast, the way anything pale glows in sufficient darkness, but the effect is of a soft, steady phosphorescence, like the glow of deep-sea organisms, like the light of things that have never known a sun and have invented their own.

They are cold to the touch. Not the cold of inert matter — the cold of something that is actively drawing heat from your skin, that is using your warmth for some purpose you cannot identify. When you press your finger into a mat of the growth, it yields with a spongy, organic resistance, and the impression remains for a moment before slowly filling back in, the filaments re-extending, the surface smoothing, as if the thing is healing. Or as if it is learning the shape of your finger. Or as if it is simply water, held in place by a matrix so fine that it behaves like a solid, and your finger has displaced it, and it is flowing back.

The growth follows the water. This is the essential thing. It traces the paths of leaks with absolute precision, mapping the routes that water takes through the structure, and where the water changes course — where a new crack opens, where a pipe shifts, where the slow seismic settling of the Drownworks redirects a flow — the growth follows, extending new filaments along the new path, retracting (or dying, or simply ceasing to be visible) from the old. It is a record. It is a map. It is the Drownworks' own diagram of its circulatory system, written in a pale, cold script on the walls of its veins.

And it is growing. You can see this if you pay attention — if you mark a boundary, a place where the growth ends and the bare concrete begins, and return to it later (though time is strange here, and "later" is a word that feels increasingly approximate). The boundary has moved. Not quickly. Not in a way that you could watch. But it has moved, the filaments extending by fractions of an inch, colonizing new wet surfaces, following new seeps, and the bare concrete behind the advancing edge is changed — pitted, softened, as if the growth is not merely living on the water but *digesting* the structure that contains it, converting concrete and steel into more of itself, slowly, patiently, the way water converts stone to sand.

The Attendants walk past the growth without touching it. The growth does not seem to notice the Attendants. But sometimes, in the deepest corners, where the mats are thickest and the glow is brightest, you see shapes in the growth — forms that might be accidental, might be the result of random branching, but that look, from certain angles, like hands. Like faces. Like the outlines of bodies pressed against the other side of a membrane, waiting.

## III. The Things Below

The grating tells you something, if you listen. Not the clang of your boots — that is a surface sound, a sound of the world you walk in. Beneath it, at the edge of hearing, there is another sound: a slow, heavy movement in the darkness below the floor, a displacement of water that is too large and too deliberate to be the random slosh of runoff in the sub-levels. It is rhythmic, but not mechanical. It breathes. Or something below the grating breathes, and the breathing moves the water, and the water moves the grating, and the grating transmits the vibration upward through your boots and into your bones, where you feel it as a kind of unease you cannot name.

You do not look down. This is not a decision. It is an instinct, a deep and pre-rational avoidance that operates below the level of thought. You keep your eyes on the walkway ahead, on the amber lights, on the structures that loom in the haze, and you do not look down at the black water that wells up through the cracks in the concrete, or at the darkness that shows through the grating in the places where the lights do not reach. You do not look, because something in you knows — the way you know not to look directly at the sun, the way you know not to look too long into a dark room at night — that looking is an invitation, and that what lives below the grating has been waiting a very long time for an invitation.

But you hear it. You hear the movement in the water, the slow, massive shift of something that is not a machine and not a pump and not any of the industrial processes that the Drownworks ostensibly houses. You hear a sound like a great body turning in a confined space, like a whale rolling in a tank too small for it, and the water below the grating sloshes and settles and sloshes again, and the rhythm of it is not the rhythm of any engine. It is the rhythm of sleep. Of something vast and wet and patient, sleeping in the dark beneath your feet, and the Drownworks — the pipes, the pumps, the Attendants, the growth — is its dream.

Or its circulatory system. Or its digestive tract. Or the cocoon it has built around itself, layer by layer, concrete and steel and pipe, a structure to contain the wet and the cold and the dark, a structure to keep the world out or to keep itself in, and the machinery that thrums and hisses and pumps is not producing anything — it is *processing*, moving water from one place to another, filtering it, aerating it, maintaining the chemistry of the vast, dark pool below at some precise and necessary balance, the way a body maintains its blood.

The things that skitter in the walls are not rats. The thing that moved across the walkway ahead of you — low, quick, pale, gone — was not a rat. It was something that lives in the interface between the water and the structure, something that has adapted (or been adapted) to the narrow spaces between the walls, the gaps between the pipes, the channels that the water has carved. It is pale because there is no light. It is quick because it is hunting, or being hunted, or because quickness is the only mode of existence in a place where the water is always rising and the air is always thinning and the structures are always shifting. It is low because the spaces are low, because the Drownworks is a horizontal world, a world of crawlspaces and conduits and the thin, wet gaps between one thing and another, and the things that live here have been pressed flat by it, spread thin, elongated, the way life in caves loses its eyes and its color and its unnecessary dimensions.

You do not see them clearly. You are not meant to. They are the Drownworks' immune system, or its parasites, or its thoughts — the small, quick, pale things that move through its body carrying messages or nutrients or infections, and they are as aware of you as the cells in your blood are aware of you: which is to say, not at all, and completely.

## IV. The Drowned

There is a thing that lives in the deepest part of the Drownworks, in the place where the walkways end and the structures give way to open water, a black, still pool that extends beyond the reach of the amber lights, its surface so calm that it reflects the girders and the conduit and the haze with the fidelity of a mirror. The pool is warm. This is the first wrongness — everything else in the Drownworks is cold, but the pool is warm, body-temperature warm, and the steam that rises from its surface is not the sharp, clean steam of the vents but something thicker, something that smells of salt and iron and the deep, organic musk of living things.

The thing in the pool is not an Attendant. It is what the Attendants were before they became Attendants, or what they will become when they cease to be Attendants, or what they are in the moments when they are not being Attendants — when they stand motionless beside a pipe or a pool, head tilted, listening, and their bodies slowly dissolve into the water around them, their edges softening, their forms losing definition, until they are just another rivulet, another seep, another thread in the vast, wet tapestry of the Drownworks.

It is large. It fills the pool the way water fills a bathtub — completely, taking the shape of its container, pressing against the edges. It is not shaped like a body, but it is shaped like *bodies* — like many bodies, pressed together, fused, the way bodies fuse in a mass grave or a shipwreck or any place where many people die in water and the water does not let them go. You can see the shapes in it: the curve of a spine, the bowl of a pelvis, the long bones of limbs that have been bent and interlocked with other limbs, and the heads — there are heads, or things that were once heads, their features smoothed by water and time into a uniform, mineral whiteness, their mouths open, their eyes (if they had eyes) closed or open or simply absent, smooth skin where the eyes should be.

It is not dead. This is the thing you understand last, and the thing that changes everything. The Drowned is not dead. It is not alive in the way you understand life, but it is not dead, and the distinction between the two states is a luxury of the surface world, a binary that does not hold in the Drownworks, where everything is in the process of becoming something else, where water dissolves the boundaries between living and dead, between one body and another, between the self and the structure that contains it.

The Drowned breathes. The pool rises and falls with its breathing, a slow, tidal rhythm that you feel in the water that wells up through the cracks in the concrete, in the pulse of the flow in the pipes, in the slow, patient seepage that is everywhere in the Drownworks. The Drowned breathes, and the Drownworks breathes with it, and the Attendants move through the structures like blood cells through capillaries, and the growth on the walls maps the flow, and the things in the walls carry messages through the body of the place, and the machinery thrums and pumps and processes, and all of it — all of it — is the Drowned, or is part of the Drowned, or is the dream of the Drowned, sleeping in its warm, dark pool at the heart of the Drownworks, dreaming itself into existence, one pipe, one concrete wall, one drop of water at a time.

The Attendants tend the Drowned. They move through the Drownworks adjusting valves and trailing their fingers over pipes and listening to the flow, and what they are doing — what they have always been doing — is maintaining the conditions of the Drowned's sleep. Keeping the water at the right temperature. Keeping the chemistry balanced. Keeping the structure intact, or intact enough, because the Drownworks is not a building — it is a body, or the shell of a body, or the cocoon of a body, and the Drowned is inside it, growing, changing, becoming something that will eventually need a different shape, a larger shape, a shape that the current structure cannot contain.

The concrete is young. The erosion is fresh. The water is patient. The water was here first, or will be here last, or is simply indifferent to the distinction. And the Drowned dreams, and the dream is the Drownworks, and the Drownworks is the dream, and you are walking through it, and the Attendants move past you like fish past a diver, and the growth on the walls glows with the soft light of deep things, and the water drips and drips and drips, and somewhere below you, in the warm, dark pool at the center of everything, something vast and wet and many-bodied turns in its sleep, and the whole structure shudders, and the water in the pipes changes direction, and a new crack opens in the concrete, and the growth extends a new filament into the wet, and the Drownworks grows.

## V. The Water

But this is not quite right. The Drowned is not the deepest thing. The Drowned is a creature, or a colony, or a process — something with boundaries, something you can point to and say *there, that is it, that is the thing that lives here*. But the water has no boundaries. The water is not a thing. The water is the medium in which all the other things exist, the way thought exists in the brain, the way meaning exists in language. The water is what the Drownworks is *made of*, and the pipes and the concrete and the steel are merely the shapes that the water has chosen to flow through, the channels it has carved, the containers it has filled.

The water is old. Older than the Drownworks, older than the excavation, older than the surface world that built the excavation and then forgot about it, or abandoned it, or was consumed by it. The water was here before the first concrete was poured, before the first pipe was laid, before the first girder was lowered into the dark. It was here in the rock, in the deep aquifer, in the pressurized darkness far below the surface, and when the drills broke through — when the first shaft was sunk and the first pump was started — the water rose to meet them, and it has been rising ever since.

It is still rising. This is the thing the Attendants know, the thing the growth maps, the thing the Drowned dreams. The water table is rising, slowly, inexorably, and the Drownworks is not a structure built to keep the water out — it is a structure built to *manage* the water's rise, to channel it, to direct it, to use its pressure and its flow to power the pumps that move the water to the surface, where it is needed, where it is used, where it is consumed and returned to the cycle again. The Drownworks is a well. The Drownworks is a throat. The Drownworks is the place where the deep water comes to the surface, and the Attendants are the mechanism by which it flows, and the Drowned is the valve, and the growth is the measure of the flow, and the things in the walls are the antibodies in the stream, and you —

You are a foreign body. A particle in the flow. Something that has entered the system from outside, and the system is processing you, slowly, the way it processes everything: dissolving you, incorporating you, converting you from a discrete self into a distributed pattern, a set of mineral traces in the water, a faint, pale filament on the wall, a shape in the pool, a figure on a walkway, moving with purpose, trailing fingers over the pipes, listening to the flow.

The water drips. The water drips. The water drips.

You are already wet.
