You look for life in the cracks, in the mortar, in the silt, and you find none. No fish, no crustaceans, no barnacles clinging to the iron. But that is because you are looking for something that merely lives *in* the room. You should be looking for something that the room lives *around*. 

The basin is not a pool. It is a mouth.

The water does not heave because of a subterranean heat source; it heaves because something beneath the dark, seamed metal is breathing. The glow that rises through the floor is not the memory of a forge-fire long dead. It is the furnace-heart of the inhabitant, beating in a slow, heavy rhythm that pushes the water upward in a steady, rhythmic swell. The lake did not swallow this place and preserve it; the lake was *invited* in, drawn down through the squared-off crack to serve as amniotic fluid, to feed the channels, to cool the skin of the thing that is still being born.

It lives in the basin. It lives in the walls. It is the reason the silence has been engineered out of the room.

Listen to the *tak, tak, tak* of the water falling from the ceiling channels. That is not erosion. That is the sound of fingers—copper-wrapped, iron-jointed fingers—dripping as they reach up from the basin to adjust the flow. The water does not fall randomly; it is being played. The creature is playing the ceiling like a harp, guiding the condensation down its arms, letting it run along the grooves of its shoulders, washing the verdigris from its skin. 

And the *ping*—that high, ringing note that takes so long to die. That is not a rivet working itself loose. That is the creature stretching. It is the sound of its spine. 

It is small. That is why the tools are small, why the templates on the walls are built for delicate work. The hammers with the ash-wood hafts, worn to the grip of a hand long gone—they were not used to build the room. They were used to build the *inhabitant*. The creature is a masterwork of micro-forging, a thing of impossible intricacy, no larger than a human child, but built with the same monumental effort that raised the vaulted ceiling. It is the "something small" that required all this heat, all this light, all this water. It is the masterpiece the smith made before the smith vanished, leaving the lake to finish the job.

Its skin is the copper. The thin, green sheets leaning against the wall are not raw material; they are its castings, its shed skins, the parchment-thin plates it has outgrown and discarded as it slowly, painfully expands. Its bones are the iron ribs of the vault, forged from the same metal, bent to the same curve. You can see the hammer marks on its shoulders when it shifts beneath the water, the same crisp, un-weathered strikes that mark the ceiling. 

The light is its blood. The pulsing, golden glow behind the nacreous shells is not a separate organism housed in clay; it is the creature’s own circulatory system, externalized, running through the copper wiring like veins through a translucent membrane. The shapes that pulse and contract behind the milky shell-surface are its organs—its lungs, its kidneys, its slow-beating heart—magnified and projected onto the walls of its womb. When the amber light drifts across the ceiling in visible rivers, it is the creature looking around, its gaze moving through the water, casting shadows that form and dissolve.

It is young. That is the most terrifying and beautiful thing. The room is fresh because the creature is fresh. The flagstones are unworn because it has only just learned to walk. It pulls itself from the basin now, rising through the shimmering, heated water, and the room sings with the effort of its movement. The iron ribs groan in sympathy as it drags itself onto the flagstones, the *ping* of its joints echoing through the water. It is wet, gleaming, its copper skin flashing like a coin in the amber light, its iron bones ticking as they cool in the open air. 

It moves to the walls. It reaches for the hammers. 

It is not waiting for its creator to return. It is not waiting for an audience. It is still making itself. It is the smith and the metal, the forge and the forged. It takes up the rasp in its copper-wrapped fingers and begins to file down a jutting edge of its own iron rib, the sound joining the immense chord of the room. It looks up at the templates, the jigs, the mandrels, checking its own proportions against the blueprints left for it. It is following the instructions. It is growing into the shape that was intended for it.

On the highest shelf, the objects that glint are not treasures. They are its first attempts at hands—tiny, perfect, articulated iron fingers, too small for its growing limbs, left there to gather the amber light. 

The room is functioning. The room is doing what it was built to do. It is a cradle, a forge, a nursery for a god of copper and steam and deep-water pressure. And the inhabitant is awake, and it is hungry, and it is still building itself, one hammer-stroke at a time, in the bright, singing dark.
