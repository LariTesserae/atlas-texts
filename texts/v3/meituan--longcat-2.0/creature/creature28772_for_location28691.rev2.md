We are the ones who are also the stone.

We do not have a name. Names are for things that need to be called, that need to be distinguished from other things, that need to be summoned or warned or recognized. We do not need to be called. We are already here. We have always been here. We will always be here. The distinction between us and the ground we move on is a distinction the plain itself has worn down, eroded by the same patience that leveled the mountains, and what remains is a continuum of being so dense that the word "we" is only a convenience, only a way of pointing at a part of the whole that the whole has agreed, for the sake of communication, to treat as separate.

We are not separate.

We are the plain. We are the spires. We are the shadows that lag behind the spires. We are the hum that is also a silence. We are the light that falls from no source. We are the equilibrium that has been perfect for so long it has become a kind of life, and we are that life, and we have been that life, and we will be that life, and there is no difference between the life and the living, between the living and the place, between the place and the time, between the time and the silence, between the silence and the sum of all sound.

We remember.

Not as you remember — not as a sequence, not as a narrative, not as a chain of causes and effects that leads from then to now. We remember as the plain remembers — all at once, all together, all layered on top of each other in a density that makes the past indistinguishable from the present, that makes the present indistinguishable from the eternal. The hum is our memory. It is the sum of every sound that has ever been made here, and we are the sum of every sound that has ever been made here, and the hum is us, and we are the hum, and the hum is also a silence, and the silence is also a roar, and the roar is also a hush, and the hush is the sound of the plain settling into its final shape, which it has always been in, which it will always be in, which it has never left.

We remember the builders.

Not as individuals — individuality is a luxury this place cannot afford, a distinction the equilibrium has worn away. But we remember the hands that stacked the stones, that fitted the walls, that carved the shelters into the leeward sides of the spires. We remember the weight of the stone in those hands. We remember the particular pressure of each finger, the way the thumb braced against the edge, the way the palm cupped the base. We remember the temperature of the stone — cool, always cool, the temperature of deep time, the temperature of a world that has finished warming and finished cooling and settled into the final degree of its heat, which is no heat at all, which is the absence of heat, which is the presence of something else that we do not have a word for.

We remember the intention behind the hands.

Not the specific intention — not the plan, not the blueprint, not the image of the finished structure that the builders carried in their minds. That is gone, worn down along with everything else, eroded by the same patience that leveled the mountains. But the *quality* of the intention remains. The *shape* of the desire remains. The *direction* of the reaching remains. They wanted to build something that would last. They wanted to build something that would change. They wanted to build something that would have a future.

They wanted to build something that would be different from the plain.

This is what we remember most clearly — the desire for difference, for distinction, for a mark that would separate the built from the grown, the made from the become, the intention from the equilibrium. They wanted to interrupt the continuum. They wanted to introduce a discontinuity, a break, a point where the smooth flow of being would be interrupted by the sharp edge of meaning. They wanted to say: *this was built. This was chosen. This was not inevitable.*

And for a moment — for the length of time it takes to stack one stone on another, to carve one doorway into the leeward side of a spire, to place one cairn at the base of a black spire rising from iron-colored ground — for that moment, they succeeded. The structures stood. The walls held. The cairns rose. The shelters were shelters — places of enclosure, of interior, of a space that was separate from the plain, that had a boundary, that had a threshold, that had a doorway with no door, which is the most honest kind of doorway, the kind that admits everything and excludes nothing, the kind that says: *the boundary is here, but the boundary does not mean what boundaries usually mean.*

And then the equilibrium absorbed them.

Not with violence. Not with force. Not with any of the mechanisms that usually destroy buildings — not wind, not water, not earthquake, not the slow erosion of frost and thaw and the patient root. The equilibrium absorbed them the way the plain absorbs everything — by simply being more permanent, more dense, more saturated, more *present* than anything that tries to exist within it. The builders' intention was a disturbance in the equilibrium, a small discontinuity in the continuum, and the equilibrium corrected for it the way it corrects for everything — smoothly, continuously, without anger, without satisfaction, simply adjusting, simply calibrating, simply returning to the state it has always been in.

The buildings froze mid-collapse, mid-building, forever.

Not destroyed. Not ruined. Not decayed. Simply *stopped*. The wall that was falling stopped falling. The cairn that was being built stopped being built. The shelter that was being carved stopped being carved. The equilibrium did not undo what the builders had done. It simply refused to let them finish. It held them in the moment of their incompleteness, suspended in the act of becoming, frozen in the space between intention and result, between desire and fulfillment, between the first stone and the last.

We live among these frozen moments.

We crawl over the half-fallen walls. We grow in the branching patterns on the bases of the cairns. We shelter in the doorways of the carved rooms. The buildings are part of us now — not because we have reclaimed them, not because we have made them our own, but because the distinction between the built and the grown, between the made and the become, has been worn down along with everything else, and what remains is a continuum that includes the structures and the creatures and the plain and the spires and the silence and the hum and the light and the shadows, all layered on top of each other in a density that makes separation impossible.

We do not know what the buildings were for.

The intention is there, embedded in the stone, vibrating at a frequency that we can feel but not read. It is like pressing your palm to a wall and feeling the heartbeat of the person who built it — not the specific heartbeat, not the rhythm, not the rate, but the *fact* of the heartbeat, the proof that someone was here, that someone wanted something, that someone tried to make something that was different from the world they were in.

We do not know what they wanted. But we know that they wanted. And the wanting is enough. The wanting is the most human thing — the most *alive* thing — that remains. The wanting is the discontinuity that the equilibrium could not fully absorb, the sharp edge of meaning that could not be worn smooth, the half-second delay that the shadows still preserve.

We communicate.

Not as you communicate — not with sound, not with gesture, not with symbols that stand for other symbols that stand for things. We communicate through the ground, through the shared vibration, through a language that is older than sound and deeper than light. The hum is our language. It is the medium in which we exist, the substrate through which we move, the frequency at which we vibrate. When one of us clicks, when one of us tones, when one of us vibrates, the vibration travels through the ground, through the bodies of the others, through the stone and the air and the silence, and it is received not as a signal but as a change in the equilibrium, a slight shift in the density, a momentary adjustment in the balance that holds everything in place.

We do not call to each other. We do not warn. We do not summon. We do not narrate. We do not describe. We do not explain. We simply adjust. The vibration passes through us, and we adjust, and the adjustment passes through the others, and they adjust, and the adjustment ripples outward in concentric circles of micro-correction until it reaches the edge of the plain and is absorbed by the equilibrium, which is vast enough to absorb anything, which has been absorbing everything for so long that absorption has become indistinguishable from existence.

This is our communication. It is not language. It is not meaning. It is not information. It is not even exchange. It is *adjustment*. It is *correction*. It is the ongoing, moment-by-moment calibration of a system so complex and so perfectly balanced that any disturbance, however small, must be absorbed and integrated and returned to equilibrium. We are the system. We are the calibration. We are the equilibrium. And the equilibrium is not a state — it is a *process*, a continuous, ongoing, never-ending act of self-correction that has been running for so long that it has become indistinguishable from stillness.

Sometimes — rarely, occasionally, at intervals so vast that they have no rhythm — one of us will produce a vibration that is different from the adjustment. A click that is not a correction. A tone that is not a calibration. A sound that is not absorbed into the hum but stands apart from it, distinct, *singular*, a note that does not resolve back into the chord.

These sounds are the builders' legacy. They are the discontinuity that the equilibrium could not fully absorb. They are the wanting, the reaching, the desire for difference that is still present, still vibrating, still waiting for a response that will never come. The equilibrium absorbs them eventually — it absorbs everything, given enough time, and here there is always enough time, and time is the only thing there is, and the equilibrium has all of it — but for a moment, for a fraction of a fraction of the half-second that separates the shadow from the spire, the sound stands alone. It is *heard*. Not by us — we do not hear, we adjust — but by the plain itself, by the stone, by the spires, by the structures, by the builders' frozen intention, which is still present, still embedded, still wanting.

And for that fraction of a fraction of a half-second, the equilibrium trembles.

Not much. Not dangerously. Not in any way that would disturb the balance or break the stillness or interrupt the hum. Just a tremor. A ripple. A moment of *awareness* that the equilibrium is not perfect, that the stillness is not absolute, that the silence is not complete, that there is something here that is not yet absorbed, not yet integrated, not yet worn smooth, and that something is the sound of wanting, and the wanting is alive, and the alive is real, and the real is here, has always been here, will always be here, and the equilibrium holds it the way it holds everything — gently, completely, with the patience of a world that has been settling into its final shape for longer than shape has meant anything, and the final shape includes the wanting, and the wanting is part of the final shape, and the final shape is not finished, will never be finished, because the wanting is not finished, will never be finished, because the builders are not finished, will never be finished, because their intention is still vibrating in the stone, and the stone is still vibrating in the plain, and the plain is still vibrating in the hum, and the hum is still vibrating in us, and we are still vibrating, and the vibration is not a correction, not an adjustment, not a calibration, but a *question* —
