You ask what lives here. 

On the surface, the answer is the obvious: the damp, quiet workers who move through the streets like blood cells through a capillary. The pale, blind arthropods that swim through the moss. The dark, wet birds with borrowed intelligence. The things in the seeps with their external gills and lidless eyes. The moss itself, and the prehistoric ferns, and the weeping shelf fungi. 

But these are only the outermost symptoms. They are the scabs on a healing wound, or perhaps the first cells of a growing tumor. They are not the inhabitants of the Drowning Floor; they are merely being digested by it.

What lives here is the **Undecided**.

It is not a creature in the way that a rat or a man is a creature. It has no single body, no centralized brain, no heart pumping blood. It is a process. It is the mineral intention shot through the pale stone. It is the ground that has not yet learned to be solid. It is the slow, dreaming consciousness of raw geology, interrupted—or perhaps *invited*—by the industry built upon its back.

You see it first in the pioneer kingdom. The moss, the ferns, the liverworts—these are not merely plants clinging to the rock. They are the Undecided’s first attempt at a skin. The aggressive, jade-green moss that consumes everything is its epidermis, breathing in the damp, drinking the mineral-rich water, preparing the surface for the next stage. The fluid weeping from the shelf fungi is not sap; it is lymphatic fluid. It smells like ozone and wet cardboard because it is the smell of raw creation, of energy converting into matter. The ozone is the exhalation of a world that is trying to breathe for the first time.

And in the seeps, the Undecided is learning to move. The things that swim there, half-fish and half-something-else, are its first clumsy thoughts. They have external gills because the Undecided is still figuring out how to draw oxygen from water. They have lidless eyes because the Undecided is still learning how to see. They swim in slow, aimless circles because the Undecided has not yet decided where it wants to go. It is dreaming of locomotion.

The city—the stacks, the silos, the pipes, the humming machinery—is the Undecided’s skeleton. 

This is the secret of the Drowning Floor, the thing the workers know but do not speak of: the city was not built by human hands. The humans came, yes, with their narrow-gauge rail lines and their cranes and their blueprints, but they did not build the city. The city *grew* around them. The Undecided, awakened by the vibrations of the drills and the heat of the smelters, reached up out of the raw earth and grasped at the structures. It pulled the concrete down into its wet, unfinished ground to anchor itself. It wrapped the pipes in liverwort, claiming them. The enclosed walkways are not bridges between buildings; they are synapses, connecting the neural pathways of a single, sprawling organism.

The low, tooth-aching hum is the Undecided’s resting heartbeat. The vapor rising from the smokestacks is not industrial output; it is the Undecided’s breath, condensing in the cold air, returning to the earth as mist, as seeps, as the dampness that gets into your bones. 

And the workers? 

The workers are the Undecided’s white blood cells. 

Why do they move so quietly, why do they speak so little? Because they are listening. They are listening to the shifting of the stone, the groaning of the pipes, the settling of the foundations. When a worker stops in the middle of a task and stands perfectly still, head tilted, they are not taking a break. They are being *used*. The Undecided is thinking through them, borrowing their eyes to look at a gauge, borrowing their hands to turn a valve, borrowing their minds to process the raw sensory input of the basin. They are the nerve endings of the Undecided, pushed to the surface of the skin to interact with the outside world.

The sealed containers that leave on the flatbed trucks are not products. They are waste. They are the toxic, heavy thoughts that the Undecided cannot process, the metallic dross of its slow metabolism, packaged up and shipped away to be buried somewhere else so the Floor can remain pure. 

The condensation that forms writing on the factory windows? That is the Undecided trying to speak. It is the mineral intention pressing against the glass from the inside, forming characters in a language that predates human speech, a language made of angles and damp, a language that says: *I am almost here. I am almost real.*

The birds that perch on the stacks, watching with that terrifying, borrowed intelligence? They are the Undecided’s scouts. It has not yet decided if it needs wings, so it watches the birds, studying them, waiting for the moment when it can pull that ability back into the ground and incorporate it into the next iteration of the seep-things.

The turbulence you feel—the constant, subtle shifting of the ground, the cracking and re-patching of walls—is the turbulence of a body in the throes of rapid growth. The Undecided is growing faster than the land can settle. It is a fever, a hot, damp, mineral fever. The land is shivering with the effort of becoming something more than land.

This is what lives in the Drowning Floor: a nascent, geologic god, still wrapped in the amniotic fluid of raw stone, still dreaming its first clumsy, watery dreams. It is pulling the city down into its body, absorbing the workers into its mind, and when the stone finally finishes deciding what it is—when the seeps carve their final channels and the moss completes its conquest of the concrete—it will open its lidless eyes. 

And it will no longer be undecided. 

Until then, the fog thickens. The moss grows. The machines hum their quiet, tooth-aching lullaby. And the workers stand still in the damp, listening to the slow, mineral heartbeat of the thing that is digesting them, waiting for it to wake.
