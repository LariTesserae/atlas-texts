# The Dwellers in the Throat

They are pale. That is the first thing. Not white — there is no white here, everything is stained by tannin and resin and the perpetual wet — but a pallor that stands out against the deep green-black of the mat: a grayish, faintly luminous pallor, like the underside of a root, like something that has never needed pigment because light has never asked anything of it.

They are small. Perhaps two feet long, perhaps less — it is difficult to judge because they are rarely still, and because they tend to move in groups that overlap and separate and overlap again, and because the eye refuses to settle on any one of them as the primary subject. They are segmented. They have many legs — not an insect's legs, not the scuttle-and-pivot of something exoskeletal, but a slow, distributed locomotion, a wave of tiny points of contact across the mat, as if the animal's entire underside is one long foot, rippling. They are flat. They press themselves into the surface of the mat and they move along it the way a slug moves along a wall, except faster, and with purpose, and with an awareness of where they are going that slugs do not have.

They have heads. Or rather, they have a differentiated anterior end that serves as a head: a cluster of sensory structures — not eyes, or not eyes as you would recognize them, but a patch of dark, smooth tissue that is photosensitive in some way, that orients toward warmth and away from the open air — and a mouth, or mouth-analogue, a vertical slit that opens and closes with a rhythmic, muscular motion, revealing inside a pale pink-red interior that looks wet and living and not at all mechanical, though the motion of it is regular enough to seem mechanical, the way a heartbeat seems mechanical, the way the peristaltic squeeze of the gut-valleys seems mechanical.

They eat the mat. Or they eat *in* the mat. Or they eat something *in* the mat — the fungal networks, perhaps, or the bacterial films that coat every root surface, or the slow-decaying lignin itself, processed through a gut that is long and coiled and, based on the sounds one hears when one presses an ear to the ground near a feeding cluster, *busy* — a wet, churning sound, a sound of breakdown and extraction, the sound of digestion amplified across a thousand small, pale bodies working in concert.

---

**There are a lot of them.**

Not a swarm — swarming implies chaos, and there is nothing chaotic about their movement. A network. A distributed population. They are densest near the gut-valleys, where the saturated zones pulse, and they are present in smaller numbers everywhere: in the channels, along the resin columns, beneath the compressed platforms where the trunks have stood for decades. They are the fauna. They are the *only* fauna. They have no predators. They have no competitors. They have this place the way a mitochondrion has a cell: not as inhabitant but as *component*, as something so thoroughly integrated into the system that the system would not function — perhaps would not exist — without them.

Watch them in the channels. This is where they are most visible, where their pale bodies stand out against the dark fluid moving in the troughs. They move along the channels the way maintenance workers move along a factory floor: with a kind of weary attentiveness, stopping at intervals, lowering their anterior ends to the fluid surface, doing something — tasting? testing? adjusting? — with their mouth-slits. Sometimes they excrete a substance into the channel: a small, measured quantity of something darker than the fluid already there, something that disperses quickly. Sometimes they remove something: a clot, a thickened mass of resin or decay, which they carry a short distance away from the channel and deposit on the mat, where it is absorbed within hours. They are tending. They are maintaining. They are keeping the channels clear and the fluids moving and the chemistry within parameters that they seem to understand in a way that you do not and cannot.

You try to pick one up. It resists — not with force but with a sudden, startling increase in surface adhesion, its flat underside bonding to the mat with a tenacity that makes you understand that this animal is, in some fundamental sense, *attached* to this place. When you pull harder, it releases — but the release is accompanied by a spasm of its body, a full-length contraction, and a sudden sharp smell, a chemical burst that is acrid and resinous and that makes your eyes water and your throat close. The others nearby do not flee. They stop. They orient toward you. Their anterior ends lift from the mat and turn in your direction with a synchronized precision that is, more than anything else about this place, the thing that makes you decide to leave.

They are not aggressive. They are *alert*. They are responding to a disturbance in the system. And they are communicating — not with sound, not with visible signal, but with something chemical, something airborne, something that the others detect and respond to within seconds, a ripple of attention spreading outward from the point of disturbance like a wave, like the peristaltic pulse of the gut-valley, like the slow beat of heat from the hollow trunks.

---

**They live inside the trunks.**

Not all of them. Perhaps a third of the population, at any given time, resides within the hollow columnar structures, accessible through openings near the base — not holes, not exactly, but thin spots in the waxy bark where the resin secretion is reduced and the underlying tissue is softer, more yielding. The openings are smooth-rimmed, almost polished, and they are sized — or they have been *worn* — to match the dimensions of the animals precisely. Inside, the trunks are warm. Inside, the air is thick with moisture and the sharp, metallic smell of the resin, and the walls are coated with a pale, fibrous growth that the animals eat, and that grows back, and that is — you begin to understand — *cultivated*.

The interior of a trunk, if you could see it whole, would resemble a hive. Not a bee-hive, with its geometric precision, but something more organic, more *grown*: chambers and passages that follow the grain of the wood, that branch and narrow and open into small, rounded spaces where the animals rest in clusters, pressed together, their pale bodies overlapping, their breathing — if it is breathing — synchronized into a slow, collective rhythm that matches the pulse of heat from the trunk's vent above. They are incubating something. Or they are being incubated. Or the distinction, for them, is meaningless.

The heat is not a byproduct. It is a *product*. It is generated by the metabolic activity of the animals — by the sheer density of their bodies, by the fermentation of the material they consume, by some exothermic process occurring in the pale growth on the walls — and it rises through the trunk and exits at the vent, and the rising heat draws air upward through the base openings, and the drawn air feeds the processes occurring in the gut-valleys below, and the gut-valleys feed the channels, and the channels feed the resin columns, and the resin columns feed the trunks, and the trunks feed the animals, and the animals feed the trunks, and the whole system cycles, and the cycling produces heat, and the heat drives the air, and the air feeds the valleys, and it goes on, and it has gone on — you understand this now — for longer than you have a framework to measure.

---

**They have a social structure.**

Or rather, they have a *distribution*. There is no center, no leader, no individual that commands or directs. The network manages itself the way the mat manages itself: through distributed signals, through chemical gradients, through the slow, patient accumulation of small decisions made by small bodies in response to local conditions. An animal in a channel detects that the fluid is thickening and begins to clear it. An animal in a gut-valley detects that the peristalsis has slowed and begins to eat more, to generate more, to accelerate the process. An animal inside a trunk detects that the temperature is dropping and presses closer to its neighbors, reducing the collective surface area, conserving heat. No animal knows what the whole system needs. Each animal responds to what is in front of it. And the system, impossibly, *works*.

But there is something else. There are animals — a small number, perhaps one in fifty — that do not feed, do not tend, do not maintain. They are larger. Their segmentation is less distinct, their bodies thicker, smoother, their pale flesh taking on a faintly translucent quality through which you can see — if the light is right, if you are close enough, if you dare to be that close — a dark, coiled mass inside them. They move slowly. They move with a deliberation that reads as *importance*, though you resist that reading, you resist the anthropomorphism, but you cannot help it: these animals are going somewhere specific, and when they arrive, the others make room.

They go to the low points. The gut-valleys. The saturated zones. They position themselves on the mat directly above the places where the peristalsis is strongest, and they lower their bodies to the surface, and they open their mouth-slits wide, and they *release*. Not excrement. Something else. Something dark and viscous and warm, something that soaks into the mat and is absorbed immediately, as if the mat has been waiting for it, as if this is the input the system needs, the catalyst, the *feedstock* without which the peristalsis would slow and the channels would thicken and the trunks would cool and the whole vast, slow machine would begin to wind down.

These animals — the larger ones, the carriers — are fed by the others. You see this, if you watch long enough: the tenders approach the carriers and regurgitate into their mouth-slits, a careful, measured transfer of processed material, the product of channel-work and trunk-work and gut-work, concentrated and delivered. The carriers take it and carry it to the low points and the system consumes it and the system continues.

You do not know what to call this. It is not a caste system. It is not a hierarchy. It is a *metabolism*. These animals are not individuals who have chosen roles. They are organs. They are the organs of something larger — something that is not an animal and not a plant and not a machine but that has qualities of all three, something that extends across the entire valley, across the entire plateau, across the miles and miles of mat and channel and trunk and valley, something that *thinks*, perhaps, in the way that a system thinks: slowly, chemically, without a brain, without a center, without anything you would recognize as consciousness, but with a purpose, a direction, a *drive* toward continuation that is, in the end, indistinguishable from will.

---

**They are not the only thing that lives here.**

Below them — below the mat, below the compressed strata of dead and living plant matter, below the fungal networks and the root-threads and the slow geological accumulation of centuries — there is something else. The animals know it. The tenders avoid certain spots, channels route around certain areas, and the carriers — the large, translucent carriers — orient toward these spots with a kind of solemnity, a kind of *reverence*, if a body moving toward a chemical gradient can be said to revere the source of the gradient.

You cannot see what is below. The mat is too thick. But you can feel it: a deeper vibration, lower in frequency than the thrumming of the resin columns, slower than the peristalsis of the gut-valleys, a pulse so deep and so slow that you feel it in your chest before you feel it in your feet, a pulse that does not match your heartbeat, that does not match any rhythm you know, that is too slow for an animal and too regular for geology and too *alive* for anything that is not, in some sense you cannot define, awake.

The animals rest on it. The animals orient to it. The animals — all of them, tenders and carriers alike, the whole pale network of them — stop, sometimes, in their work, and they press their bodies flat against the mat and they go still, and the stillness lasts for thirty seconds, for a minute, for longer, and in that stillness you can feel it too: the deep pulse, the slow beat, the thing below the mat that is not the mat, that the mat grew *around*, that the mat grew *from*, that the mat is.

And then they resume. The tenders return to their channels. The carriers continue toward the low points. The heat pulses from the hollow trunks. The resin pools in its basins. The peristalsis of the gut-valleys continues. And the system — the vast, slow, breathing system — does what it has always done.

It grows.
