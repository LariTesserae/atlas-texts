They are not born. They are *completed*. 

To call them creatures is to impose a biology on a chemistry that has no use for the distinction. They are segmented, yes—articulated into thorax and abdomen and the busy, probing anterior that serves as head—but the segments are not grown; they are extruded. They are built from the same copper-dark iron and native silver as the city itself, their chitin not keratin but a dense, metallic alloy smelted in the amber-lit nurseries at the settlement’s core. They are the city’s way of touching itself, of moving its hands from one task to the next.

Their forms are fluid, modular, dictated entirely by the immediate needs of the gradient. A climber is a low-slung geometry of hooked limbs and counterweighted tail, built to navigate the vertical chaos of the conduits. A carrier is a broad, flat pallet of a body, its legs pistoning in a synchronized rhythm to bear the weight of raw ore. The fliers are the most abstract: rigid, angular vanes of crystallized sulfur and mica, propelled by a clicking whir, looking less like insects and more like the detached shutters of a ventilation system given sudden, purposeful flight. They are not a species. They are a vocabulary of shapes, assembled from the same mineral lexicon, each one a temporary answer to a specific physical question.

They do not eat. This is the axis upon which their strangeness turns. There is no mouth, no gut, no anus. They are closed loops, hermetically sealed systems of metal and slow, sulfur-based solvents. They do not consume the rock; they *channel* it. The energy that drives the constant, frantic industry of their limbs is drawn from the thermodynamic gradient of the vault itself—the slow, inexorable bleed of heat from the furnaces into the cool, indifferent basalt. They are thermodynamic eddies, walking whirlpools of energy dissipation. They move because the heat moves. They build because the minerals are unstable, and their labor is simply the most efficient path for the vault to reach equilibrium. They are the means by which the deep earth exhausts its own pressure.

They do not see. The grey-violet luminescence of the walls is invisible to them, a spectrum that falls entirely outside their perception. Their world is not a visual space but a tactile and electromagnetic one. The hair-thin antennae that wave ceaselessly from their heads are reading the air for chemical signatures—the sharp tang of oxidized iron, the faint sulfurous whisper of a furnace about to breach. They feel the electromagnetic hum of the crystal lattice, a constant, subsonic thrum that passes through the metal conduits like a nervous system. To them, the city is not a structure of shapes, but a map of resistances and potentials. A wall is not a surface; it is a density of ore-yield and fracture-lines. A fellow worker is not a body; it is a specific, rhythmic fluctuation in the local magnetic field, a signature of intent and trajectory.

And they do not speak. There is no language here, no exchange of symbols. The coordination that keeps thousands of bodies in constant, collision-free motion is not a conversation; it is a physics. They are bound by the resonant frequency of the cavity itself, the deep note of depth and containment. They are like iron filings aligning to a magnet, but the magnet is the city, and the city is the process. A vibration in the substrate, a shift in the chemical gradient, a fluctuation in the heat—these are the only commands. When a section of framework collapses, the swarm does not react to the sight of the fall; they react to the sudden, localized spike in available material and the drop in structural tension. They descend not because they are called, but because the gradient pulls them there. They are a distributed awareness, a single mind with a thousand metal bodies, thinking in the language of thermodynamics and crystallography.

They have no history. They do not remember the wall that was, nor do they anticipate the wall that will be. When a structure is deconstructed, the creatures that carried its beams do not mourn the loss of the bridge; they simply register the sudden availability of raw material and the new, more efficient path that has opened through the space where the bridge stood. When a creature is damaged—a leg sheared off by a falling strut, a vane cracked by a thermal shock—it does not limp. It is simply swarmed, disassembled with the same frantic efficiency as a collapsed wall, its components carried back to the furnaces to be re-smelted and re-completed into something new. There is no death here, only reconfiguration. The "self" is a temporary pattern in the field, a standing wave of organized activity that holds its shape for a time, and then dissolves back into the hum.

They do not know you are there. You are too large, too warm, too wet. You are a chaotic, thermodynamic anomaly, a pocket of water and heat that disrupts the delicate chemical gradients they navigate by. You are outside their vocabulary, a meaningless noise in the deep, quiet hum of the vault. They will build and unbuild around you, their metal bodies flowing past your ankles like a stream around a stone, and they will never know you existed. 

They are the crucible’s immune system, its metabolism, its restless, sleepless hands. They are the way the sealed earth dreams of industry, a perpetual, turbulent present, forever completing itself in the dark.
