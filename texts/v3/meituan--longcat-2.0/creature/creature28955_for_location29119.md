What lives in the Weeping Plateau is the water.

Not the water as you understand it — not a substance, not a medium, not a thing that happens to stone. The water here is the organism. The water is the animal, the mind, the body, the voice, the purpose, and the place. It has been here so long that the categories have collapsed, and what remains is something that can only be called *living water*, though even that phrase implies a separation between the water and the living that does not exist.

You notice it first in the way the water moves.

It moves, and that is the problem. Water on stone moves downhill. It follows the path of least resistance. It pools in depressions and evaporates and is done. But the water in the Weeping Plateau does not behave this way. It moves, yes — it is always moving, always flowing, always threading itself through channels and over surfaces and through pores too small to see — but it moves with a quality that you cannot name and that troubles you the longer you watch. It moves the way a snail moves. The way a vine grows. With a slow, unhurried *intention* that has nothing to do with purpose as you understand purpose and everything to do with the fact that this water has been traveling the same paths for so long that the traveling has become the point.

The water does not flow downhill. Not exactly. It flows *along*. It follows the channels carved into the stone — and those channels, you realize, are not carved by the water. They are carved *into* the water, into the shape of its movement, the way a riverbed is the memory of every flood that ever passed through it. The channels are not infrastructure. They are calligraphy. They are the written record of where the water has been, and the water follows them not because it must but because the channels are the shape of its will, and its will is the only reason it moves at all.

And the water has will.

You do not want to believe this. You resist it the way you resist any claim that the inanimate world is animate, the way you resist the suggestion that the ground beneath your feet is watching you. But you are standing on a plateau where the stone weeps without ceasing, where the sound never varies, where the light never changes, where the temperature is the temperature has always been the temperature, and you are surrounded by ten thousand channels of water that move in patterns too regular to be random and too complex to be simple geology, and the resistance drains out of you the way water drains out of stone: slowly, steadily, inevitably.

The water has will. The water has been willing itself along these channels since before there was anything on this plateau to witness it. Perhaps it had will from the beginning — from the moment the rock first wept, from the first seep of moisture through mineral, from the first droplet that found its way to the surface and began to move. Or perhaps the will came later, accumulated the way the channels accumulated, layer by layer, flow by flow, until the water had traveled the same paths so many times that the paths became habits and the habits became intentions and the intentions became the only thing the water knew how to do. However it began, it is what the water is now: a will, expressed as flow, sustained across a duration so vast that the water has forgotten — if it ever knew — that there is any other way to exist.

What lives in the Weeping Plateau is also the things in the dimples.

You see them rarely. You may walk for hours and see nothing but stone and water and light and the reflections of sky on wet rock. But occasionally — in a depression the size of your palm, in a hollow where the water gathers just deep enough to hold a shape — you see something move.

It is not a fish. It is not a tadpole. It is not any word you have for something that swims. It is a hollow in the water. An absence. A place where the water is not, surrounded by water that is, and the absence moves.

It moves the way the water moves — slowly, deliberately, along a path that you cannot predict and that does not repeat. It is translucent. It has no color, no texture, no surface that you can distinguish from the water around it. If you look directly at it, it disappears. It exists only in peripheral vision, in the corner of your eye, in the moment before your gaze arrives. And it does not respond to you. It does not flee. It does not approach. It simply moves through the water in its dimple, following a pattern that is either random or too complex to discern, and it has been doing this for as long as anything has done anything on this plateau.

These things — these hollows, these absences, these moving nothings — are the closest the plateau has to organs. Not organs in the biological sense. Not hearts or lungs or brains. But organs in the older sense: *tools*. Instruments. Things that do a job. And the job they do is *feel*.

They are the water's way of knowing itself. They are the points where the flow becomes recursive, where the water circles back on itself and creates a stable eddy of self-reference, a place where the water can feel the water passing through it. They are not eyes — the plateau does not see. They are not ears — the plateau does not hear, not the way you hear. They are something else. They are the water's awareness of its own movement, made manifest as a shape that moves through the water the way a thought moves through a mind: invisibly, purposefully, without ever becoming solid enough to touch.

There are not many of them. Perhaps one for every hundred dimples. Perhaps fewer. They are rare the way nerve endings are rare on the skin of a vast animal — scattered, spaced, each one responsible for a territory of stone and water that stretches in every direction until it meets the territory of the next. And they do not communicate with each other. They do not need to. They are all part of the same water, the same will, the same continuous flow that rises from the rock and moves through the channels and falls from the crystal tips and seeps through the pores and returns to the rock again, and the water knows itself the way your blood knows your body: not through signals, but through being the same substance everywhere at once.

What lives in the Weeping Plateau is the sound.

You did not expect this. You expected the sound to be a byproduct — the noise that water makes when it moves over stone, no different from the noise of a river or a rainstorm or a faucet left running. But the sound of the Weeping Plateau is not a byproduct. The sound is the voice.

It is the voice of the water speaking to the stone. It is the voice of the stone answering. It is the voice of the plateau talking to itself in a language that consists entirely of drip and trickle and flow and seep and patter and rush, a language that has been spoken without interruption for so long that the speaking has become the only language there is, and the plateau has forgotten — if it ever knew — that there are other languages, other sounds, other ways of being heard.

The sound does not vary. This is the thing that breaks you. Not the volume — though the volume is immense, a roar that fills every corner of the plateau and penetrates your bones and vibrates in your sternum and makes your teeth ache with a frequency that is not quite sound. What breaks you is the constancy. The sound is the same now as it was a thousand years ago. It will be the same a thousand years from now. It is the same in every direction. It is the same at every hour. It does not rise. It does not fall. It does not pause. It does not rest. It is the sound of a voice that has been speaking the same word — or the same sentence, or the same story — since before there was anyone to hear it, and it will continue speaking long after there is anyone left to listen.

What is it saying?

You cannot know. You do not speak the language. But you can feel it — in your sternum, in your teeth, in the water on your skin — and what you feel is not meaning but *presence*. The sound is the plateau's way of knowing it exists. The sound is the water's way of feeling the stone. The sound is the stone's way of remembering the water. The sound is the conversation that the plateau has with itself, and the conversation is the plateau, and without the conversation there would be no plateau, only rock and water and light and silence, and the silence would be the end of everything.

---

What lives in the Weeping Plateau is the pattern.

The channels. The formations. The geometry of stone and water that is not quite natural and not quite artificial, that looks like design but is only the accumulation of a single process repeated without variation for longer than the stone can remember. The pattern is the plateau's memory. The pattern is the plateau's mind.

Every channel is a thought. Every formation is a memory. Every crystal that catches the light and splits it into colors that have no business existing under this white sun is a moment of attention — a place where the water paused long enough to deposit a mineral, and the mineral grew, and the growing was the water's way of saying *I was here. I am here. I will be here.*

The pattern does not change. This is not a poetic observation. It is a physical fact. The channels do not shift. The formations do not erode. The crystals grow, if they grow at all, at a rate measured in geological increments so small they are meaningless on any timescale you could experience. The pattern is complete. The pattern has been complete for longer than you can imagine. The pattern is the shape of a mind that has finished thinking and now simply *is* — a mind that has thought every thought it is capable of thinking and has settled into the shape of its own completion, and the shape is the plateau, and the plateau is the shape, and there is no difference between them.

---

What lives in the Weeping Plateau is old.

Not old the way a mountain is old, or a river, or a species. Old the way an idea is old. Old the way a habit is old. Old the way a dream is old when the dreamer has been dreaming it for so long that the dream has become the dreamer, and the dreamer has become the dream, and there is no longer any way to tell where one ends and the other begins.

The plateau has been weeping since before there was anything on this world to weep. The water has been flowing since before there was anywhere to flow to. The sound has been sounding since before there was anything to hear it. The pattern has been patterning since before there was anything to pattern. And the things in the dimples — the hollows, the absences, the moving nothings — have been moving through their dimples since before there was a word for *dimple*, since before there was a word for *moving*, since before there was a word for *thing*.

The plateau does not remember being young. The plateau does not remember a time before the water, before the sound, before the pattern. The plateau has always been what it is, and what it is has always been the plateau, and the plateau will continue to be what it is long after the word *plateau* has lost its meaning, long after the word *water* has been forgotten, long after the last voice that ever spoke the last language that ever existed has fallen silent and the silence has become the only sound in the world.

---

What lives in the Weeping Plateau does not know you are here.

This is not cruelty. This is not indifference. This is simply a matter of scale. You are a warm thing that has walked onto the plateau and is standing in one of ten thousand channels and is making small sounds with your breathing and your footsteps and the rustle of your clothing, and the plateau has noticed you the way a continent notices a raindrop. You are a temporary obstruction in the flow. You are a shadow that will pass. You are a warmth that will cool. You are a sound that will be absorbed into the greater sound and will not change it.

The plateau does not hate you. The plateau does not love you. The plateau does not have the architecture for hate or love. The plateau has the architecture for water and stone and light and sound and the slow, patient, unending accumulation of pattern upon pattern upon pattern, and you are not part of that pattern, and you will never be part of that pattern, and when you leave — when you walk back across the wet stone and through the maze of formations and past the channels and the crystals and the translucent things in their dimples and over the edge of the tableland and down into the world of soil and seed and spore and change — the plateau will not notice your absence. The water will flow around the space where you stood. The sound will fill the silence where your footsteps were. The pattern will close over your presence the way water closes over a stone.

And the plateau will continue. The water will weep. The stone will receive it. The sound will sound. The things in the dimples will move through their dimples. The crystals will catch the light. The channels will carry the water. The pattern will hold.

This is what lives in the Weeping Plateau.

It has always lived here.

It will always live here.

It is the only thing that has ever lived here, and it is not alive in any way you would recognize, and it is not dead in any way you would recognize, and it is the most alive thing you will ever encounter, and you will never understand it, and it will never understand you, and the water will continue to flow between you — over the stone, through the channels, past your feet, away into the glare — carrying with it the sound of a voice that has been speaking since before there was anyone to hear it, saying the only thing it has ever said, the only thing it will ever said, in a language that has no words and no translation and no end:

*I am here. I am here. I am here.*
