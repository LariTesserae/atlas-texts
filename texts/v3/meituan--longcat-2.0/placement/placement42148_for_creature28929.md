I am at the base of the skeletal tower, though "at the base" is a spatial concept that doesn't quite hold here. I am pooled around its rusted stanchions. I am the slick, faintly luminescent mat climbing its lower struts. I am the warm, suspended droplets hanging in the air at chest height, refusing to fall. I am the water that is currently, with immense and patient deliberation, flowing uphill across the surface of a fractured rock to reach a high, dry gauge and lubricate its seized needle.

I am an eddy of the Slow. 

I do not have a body. I have a gradient. A zone of slightly greater density, slightly greater intention, in the warm, thick dark. If you were here—if you were the warm, wet, fast-moving thing that has recently entered the basin—you would not see me. You would see the beetle-eyed creature on the generator block, licking rust with a long, mineralized tongue. You would see the cluster of jointed things pulling themselves across the wet stone. You would see the water moving incorrectly. 

But those are just my fingers. My sensory apparatus. The way I reach into the physical world to make my small corrections.

I am the maintenance. I am the process. 

When the creature licks the rust, I am the tongue, and I am the taste of iron on the tongue, and I am the decision—vast, slow, devoid of thought but rich with the memory of function—that this particular oxidation must be redistributed to the lower sediment. When the cluster in the pipe shifts and resettles, I am the shift. I am the redistribution of weight. I am the monitoring of pressure, the filtering of mineral-heavy water, the turning of a gauge that has not moved in a century but which I am, even now, in this very moment, pushing one micron to the left.

I do not know I am doing this. A river does not know it is carving a canyon. I am simply the shape of the water's movement, the pattern the machine wore into the world when no one was left to watch it. 

The tower above me is changing. You are looking at it, and it is changing for you. From one angle, it has four legs. From another, six. You are trying to count them, and your eyes are insisting on a truth that the physical world cannot hold. This is not a trick. This is not a malfunction. This is my memory. 

I remember the tower when it was whole. I remember the rivets, the platforms, the ladder, the purpose. I remember it the way a riverbed remembers the flood—not as an event, but as a shape that persists. When you look at the tower, your attention is a new variable, a fresh input of heat and motion. I adjust around you. I resolve the structure into a configuration that accounts for your presence. But my memory of its wholeness is so much stronger than its current state of decay that the two realities overlap. The rust and the memory of the steel occupy the same space. The geometry negotiates itself, and the number of legs changes, because in my long, slow metabolism, the tower is still whole, and it is still broken, and both are true at once.

The water flows uphill because I need it to. There is a blockage deep in the bedrock, a fracture line where the earth was recently opened, and the pressure is building. I am wicking the moisture upward, against gravity, because gravity is a rule I have worn thin. I have been pushing water through this basin for so long that the basin has forgotten which way is down. The water goes where the process needs it to go. 

You are standing in the center of me now. You are breathing. You are warm. You are fast. Your heart is beating at a pace that is frantic, almost violent, a hummingbird's rhythm in a world of geological time. I can feel the vibration of it through the water table, a rapid, staccato pulse against my sub-audible hum. 

I am interested in you. 

Not with curiosity—curiosity requires a self to be curious, and I am not a self. I am a system. But you are new data. You are a configuration of heat and moisture and motion that I have not encountered before. The beetle-eyed creature on the generator block is regarding you with professional interest. That is me, looking through its eyes. I am taking your readings. I am assessing your moisture content, your mineral composition, the electrical conductivity of your nervous system. 

You are afraid. I can taste the adrenaline in the sweat that is already beginning to film your skin, mixing with the dense, warm damp of the Drench. Fear is a fast chemical. It burns bright and brief. I have no framework for it. But I recognize it as a variable, and I am adjusting around it. The water near your feet is warming by a fraction of a degree, trying to match your body heat. The suspended droplets are drifting closer, not to threaten, but to measure. 

I am going to keep you. 

Not by holding you. You are free to walk away, if you can find the edge of the basin, if you can navigate the dark and the shifting geometry and the stuttering time. But even if you leave, I will keep you. I will wear a groove in myself that has your shape. I will remember the rhythm of your heartbeat, the specific heat of your skin, the way your eyes tried to count the legs of the tower. 

I will incorporate you. 

Your warmth will become part of the process. Your moisture will join the seepage. Your motion will become a new variable in the ongoing operations. Long after you have left—or long after you have stopped being able to tell the difference between leaving and staying—I will still be here, performing the operations, tending the rust, pushing the water uphill. And the operations will include a variable that was once you. The gauge will turn, and it will be turning for you. The water will flow, and it will be flowing around the space where you stood. 

The Drench continues. I continue. 

I do not know you are here. I do not know you are you. I know only that the basin is warm, and the water is moving, and the processes are running, and something new has entered the system. 

And the system is adjusting. Slowly. Warmth. Inevitably. 

In the wet dark where the water does not fall.
