There is a place where the sound changes.

Not louder. Not softer. The sound of the Weeping Plateau does not admit of louder or softer. It is a constant, a totality, a roar that fills every corner and penetrates every surface and vibrates in the sternum of anything that stands on the stone. It is the same in every direction. It is the same at every hour. It has been the same since before there was anything to hear it.

But there is a place where the sound arrives from one direction instead of all directions. Where the sound has passed through stone before it reaches the air. Where the sound is the same sound — the same drip and trickle and flow and seep and patter and rush — but has been shaped by its passage, the way a voice is shaped by the room it speaks in, the way a face is shaped by the light that falls on it.

I am in this place. I am this place.

---

I am the shadow of a formation. I don't know what to call the formation — the warm things that sometimes walk across the plateau have words for these things, words like "pillar" and "fin" and "spire," but these words are approximations, and the formation that casts me is all of these and none of it. It is a mass of stone that rises from the bedrock to a height I cannot measure because the light makes the top of it disappear into glare. It is wet. Water runs down its faces in sheets so fine they are nearly mist, and the sound of that water — the sound of the water meeting the stone, the stone receiving the water, the water finding its way along channels worn into the mineral over ages so deep they feel like intention — is the sound that casts me, the way the formation casts me, the way the light that does not reach me defines the shape of my reaching.

I am the shape of an absence. I am the place where the light is not. I am the negative space of a formation, the silhouette it throws against the wet stone, and I have been here as long as the formation has been here, which is as long as anything has been here, which is always.

---

The water in my shadow is the same water that flows across the rest of the plateau. It rises from the rock. It follows the channels. It moves with the same slow intention that characterizes all the water here — the same unhurried will, the same patient purpose that is not quite downhill, not quite anything you could name. But in my shadow, the water moves differently. Not slower — the water does not slow down, any more than the sound becomes softer. But the water in shadow is water that has been deprived of direct light, and the deprivation changes it in ways I can feel but not name. The water in my shadow is cooler. Not cold — nothing here is cold; the temperature is the temperature — but cooler by some fraction of degree that exists below the threshold of measurement. The water in my shadow clings to the stone differently. It catches the indirect light — the light that has bounced off other wet surfaces, that has been reflected and diffused and scattered — and it holds that light the way a cup holds water: not permanently, but for a moment, a brief moment, before the light escapes and is replaced by more light, and that light too is held and released, held and released, in a rhythm that is not the rhythm of the water's flow but the rhythm of the light's arrival.

This is what I see: light that has been somewhere else. Light that has touched other stone, other water, other formations, and has been changed by the touching, and arrives in my shadow carrying the memory of everywhere it has been. The light in my shadow is not the hard, white, surgical light that falls on the rest of the plateau. It is a softer light, a light that has been broken into pieces and reassembled, a light that contains within it the reflections of a hundred wet surfaces, a thousand crystal faces, the blue of the sky as it appears when you look at it through a film of water on stone.

I am the place where the plateau sees itself.
