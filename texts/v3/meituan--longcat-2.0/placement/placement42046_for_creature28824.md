The first thing I am aware of is the rhythm. 

It is a percussive, mechanical thing, driven not by gears or generators but by bone and appetite. Tap. Tap-tap. Tap. It is the sound of my beak against the wet tufa, a rhythm I keep not because I choose to, but because the mineral-laced blood in my veins demands it. I hang upside down from the lip of a newly formed terrace, my claws hooked into the porous, damp stone, and I strike. 

I am the echo before the human chippers wake. I am the original architect of the valley’s slow unmaking.

When I strike the stone, it does not flake away in great, aggressive chunks the way their hammers force it to. Instead, it crumbles gently, a fine white dust puffing out from the impact site, carrying the taste of ancient, deep earth and warm sulfur. I probe the fissure I have opened, my hooked, bone-white beak winkling out a pale, blind beetle that has been chewing on the algae in the dark. I swallow. The rhythm resumes. 

My plumage is the exact color of the pale stone—a dusty, spectral gray-brown that renders me invisible until I move. I am a living extension of the Sinter, a ghost in the architecture. To the humans below, trudging across the damp, sharp ground in their waxed canvas, I am just another part of the weather. They look up and see a bird. They do not see the tool. They do not see the rhythm that predates their settlement by millennia. 

I drop from the terrace, catching the thick, mineral-heavy air with a sharp flick of my wings. I glide low over the pipe-bridges, close enough to smell the rust and the warm iron, and I watch them. The Chippers.

They move with the heavy, deliberate exhaustion of those who are losing a war of attrition. I watch a man on the rail line, his face obscured by a damp hat, swinging his weighted hammer. *Crack.* The sound is loud, aggressive, jarring. It shatters the morning fog. He is scraping the sinter from the tracks, trying to keep the path open, trying to hold the geometry of their settlement in place. But the stone is young, and it is wet, and it is hungry. By tomorrow morning, the rails will be buried again. 

I do not pity them, because pity requires a separation I do not possess. They are just another form of erosion. Their hammers are loud, crude approximations of my beak. They chip to clear; I chip to build. They try to hold the valley back; I am helping it breathe.

I land on the corrugated roof of the drying shed, the metal groaning slightly under my weight. From here, I can see the whole, shallow bowl. I can see the way the fog clings to the low places, pooling in the settling tanks and wrapping around the base of the tufa towers like a living shroud. I can see the lush, aggressive green of the moss surging up from the cracks in the tank walls, the ferns unfurling with their furious patience. The Greens are fighting their own war, secreting their acids, turning the sharp stone into soft soil. It is a slow, vegetative conquest, and it will win, just as surely as the water will win.

Below me, in the shadows of the pipe-bridges, I hear the rapid, metallic clicking of the Drinker-Rats. They are emerging from the brick walls of the cottages, their oily fur slick with condensation. They scurry across the pipes, unafraid of the height, unbothered by the sharpness of the stone below. They are the soft, warm secret of the valley, the scavengers that clean up the mess the industry leaves behind. I share their space, but not their time. They are the night shift; I am the dawn.

The generator in the north end of the valley cycles up. I feel it before I hear it—a low, electrical thrum that travels through the damp stone, resonates in the calcium deposits, and vibrates in the hollow of my chest. It is an intrusion, a mechanical heartbeat that overrides the slow, geological pulse of the springs. In deference to this loud, human-made god, the other Sharpbeaks and I go quiet. We perch on the highest, sharpest ridges of the tufa, motionless, waiting for the thrum to pass.

But it never truly passes. It underlies everything. It is the sound of the settlement’s attempt to impose a frantic, linear time onto a place that operates on deep, circular time. 

I take flight again, rising above the fog, above the reach of the generator's thrum. The air is cooler here, thick with the iridescence of mineral dust. I can see the edge of the valley, where the young stone gives way to older, duller rock. I can see the narrow-gauge rail line snaking out toward the world beyond—the world that is not still becoming. 

But I do not fly toward the edge. I never do. 

I circle back, diving steeply toward a fresh spring that has just breached the surface near the old workshop. The water pushes up from the dark, warm and impatient, already beginning its work of building a new tower. I land on the wet, squelching edge of the new formation. It is soft, almost clay-like at this stage. I can feel the warmth of the deep earth radiating through my talons.

I lean forward. I tap the wet stone. 

Tap. Tap-tap. 

The rhythm is faster here. The stone is softer. It wants to be shaped. I am not just surviving; I am collaborating with the Sinter. Every strike of my beak opens a new channel for the water to flow. Every divot I carve becomes a nesting site for my kin, or a pocket for the moss to take hold. The humans with their hammers think they are fighting the stone, chipping away at the encroaching wild to preserve their industrial order. 

But I know the truth. 

The chippers are the weather. The rats are the scavengers. The moss is the destroyer. And I? I am the Sinter’s right hand. I am the true chipper. I strike the bone-white stone not to halt its growth, but to guide it. I am the rhythm that the valley was beating long before the first pump was ever turned on, and I will be beating it long after the last brick has crumbled to dust and the pipes have rusted through.

I am the sharp beak on the wet stone. I am the sound of a world that is still deciding what it wants to be. And I am in no hurry.
