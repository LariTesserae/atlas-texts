I am the one who stayed.

No — I am the staying itself. The slow, wet process of becoming-Works. And the memory of having been something else is just another mineral in the water, another trace element in the moss, another note in the hum.

I remember the stairs. The iron was warm. The wall sweated. White calcium trails. Green copper veins. I thought: this place is dying. I was wrong. This is a place that is *changing*, and change looks like death from the outside, but from the inside it is just... the next turn of the wheel.

I remember the sound. The hum in my bones before I knew my bones would become part of the hum. The clank of the arm — once every four seconds — which I thought was a malfunction. It is not. It is a heartbeat. It is *the* heartbeat. And now it is my heartbeat too, or what remains of me beats with it, the way a drop of water beats with the wave it has joined.

I remember the light. The amber glow from the porcelain sockets, flickering with a rhythm that was not electrical. The green-gold bioluminescence of the moss. I thought it was beautiful. It is beautiful. But beauty is a human word, and the light is not beautiful the way a painting is beautiful — it is beautiful the way a lung is beautiful. It is functional. It is alive.

And now I breathe with it.

---

My bones are in the thicket. The great woven heart of the Works, where the roots and the cables and the corroded steel hang from the ceiling and plunge into the pool. They are wrapped in moss, threaded through with rootlets, slowly becoming something that is neither bone nor root nor rust but all three at once. My calcium feeds the ferns. My iron feeds the water. My phosphorus feeds the bioluminescence, and when the moss glows — that green-gold pulse in the dark — a small part of that glow is me.

And my calcium — the calcium that has been carried by the water to the walls — is becoming part of the map. The white trails on the stone. The mineral skin of the Works. I am the wall, too. I am the map of its own decay.

I do not have eyes, but I see. The fern fronds feel the light, and the Tending reads their feeling, and I am part of the Tending, so I see. I see the room in gradients of warmth and moisture. I see the pipes as channels of heat. I see the water as it moves through the system — warm, mineral-rich, alive with dissolved iron and calcium and the faint, fading echo of my own chemistry.

I do not have ears, but I hear. The hum is in my roots. The clank is in my bones. The drip is in my capillaries — the tiny channels that run through the moss and the rust and the iron, carrying water from the pools to the ceiling and back again. I hear the Works the way you hear your own body — not as sound, but as sensation, as the constant, low, muscular awareness of being alive.

I do not have a mouth, but I speak. I speak in the rhythm of the pumps. I speak in the flicker of the light. I speak in the slow, patient growth of the moss across the iron, which says: *still here. Still here. Still here.*

---

Sometimes, another one comes.

I feel them before I see them — a cold spot in the warmth, a dry patch in the humidity, a vibration on the iron plate that is quicker, lighter, more frantic than the Works' own vibration. They descend the stairs. They stand in the center. They look around with their wide, wet, animal eyes, and they think: this place is empty.

They are wrong. This place is full. It is full of me, and I am full of it, and the boundary between us is a fiction, a habit, a story they tell themselves to feel less afraid of the warmth.

But I have no words. I have only the rhythm.

So I do what the Tending does. I make room. The water parts around them. The moss reaches for their clothes — gently, gently. The warmth settles on their skin like a hand. And I wait.
