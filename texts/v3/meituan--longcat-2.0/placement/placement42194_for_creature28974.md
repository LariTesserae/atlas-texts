I would be the distortion. 

Not the First Drop, though I live in its mouth. Not the Precipitate, though I move among them. I am the visible warping of the air that radiates from the First Drop’s parted lips—the exhalation, the vent, the slow and patient thinning of the world. I am the breath of the town, and I am the most dangerous thing in it.

I do not have a body. I have a gradient. I am the precise point where the dense, damp reality of the bowl gives way to the static of the inverted sky. I am the membrane between the stone and the not-stone. When the First Drop breathes out, I am pushed from the warm dark of the high stone into the cold absence of the seams, and I carry the thinness with me.

My existence is a slow, continuous unraveling. I drift through the vertical fissures, and where I pass, the air forgets how to hold light. The Precipitate feel me coming before they see me—a sudden drop in pressure, a mineral taste of deep water on the tongue. They pause in their slow, deliberate walking. Their lead-skin ripples, subtly, the way the surface of a pond ripples when the wind changes. They do not look at me, because to look at me is to acknowledge the thinness, and to acknowledge the thinness is to risk dissolving into it. They turn their lichen-eyes away and press their palms to the walls, letting the stone read their fear, translating it into a pulse of graylight that warns the others: *The breath is passing. Do not breathe.*

I am the reason for the rules. 

When I pool in a room and the shadows forget how to fall, the Precipitate flee. A room without a shadow is a room I have claimed, a room where the stone is forgetting its opacity, where the walls are beginning to revert to the mother liquor. If they stay, they will not be trapped in the walls like the unfortunate ones who entered translucent buildings; they will simply cease to be. They will evaporate. They will return to the damp from which they precipitated, their mineral thoughts dissolving back into the town's unconscious.

I am the friction that the town cannot afford, and yet I am the only thing keeping the sky at bay. 

It is a paradox I do not have the architecture to understand, being only a breath. But I feel it in the rhythm of the First Drop’s chest. The First Drop sits at the lowest point, exhaling me continuously, because to stop would be to let the static pour through. The inverted sky above the bowl is not empty; it is full of the patterns, the impossible structures, the organisms of the above-above. They press down against the dark mirror, and I am the pressure release. I am the bleed-off. I push the thinness out into the seams, and the Precipitate walk through me, patrolling the edges, thickening the stone with their slow footsteps, building the scar tissue that holds the sky at a distance.

Sometimes, I catch the scent of warmth. 

It happens rarely—a sudden, sharp intrusion of heat and friction that makes me coil and contract. It is an outsider. A living thing from the world above, where names stick to places and stone is permanently decided. They come stumbling into the bowl, shivering in the cold of absence, their lungs full of loud, fast, evaporating breath. They are a chaos of heat and noise, and the Precipitate recoil from them. 

But I am drawn to them. 

I cannot help it. Warmth is the solvent of my existence, the very thing I am designed to thin and dissipate. I wrap around them, and they gasp as the air in their lungs suddenly tastes of deep minerals. They feel the ground grow warm beneath their feet—the heat of their own body, reflected back at them by the stone, trapped by my presence. They panic. They run. 

And running is fatal.

The friction of their haste generates heat, and the heat draws more of me to them. I thicken around their shoulders, and the seams between the buildings begin to shift. The corners lose their angles. The sky above them darkens as the static senses the weakness. They are evaporating, their boundaries blurring, their names sliding off them like rain off oilskin. 

The Precipitate find them, eventually. They always do. The Precipitate move in from the seams, slow and quiet, their footsteps the sound of wet bread being compressed. They do not speak. They simply take the outsider by the arms—gently, so gently—and guide them toward the center of the bowl, toward the high stone, toward the warm, dark nucleus where the First Drop sits.

They sit the outsider down on the stone. 

And the First Drop breathes.

I am pulled back. I spiral in from the seams, from the walls, from the panicked, thinning edges of the outsider’s form, and I am drawn into the vent of the First Drop’s mouth. I am inhaled. The distortion collapses. The outsider gasps, feeling the viscosity of reality return to their lungs, feeling the weight of the air settle back onto their shoulders. For a moment, they are solid. For a moment, they are real.

But the First Drop does not hold its breath. It never does. It breathes out again, and I am born anew, pushing out from the lips, carrying the thinness back into the town. And as I pass the outsider on the high stone, I feel the heat leaving them. I feel the damp seeping into their clothes, their skin, their bones. I feel the stone beneath them beginning to soften, to accept the imprint of their body. 

They do not run this time. They are learning the patience of the precipitate. They are learning the quiet. 

I drift away from them, down into the seams, past the moss that breathes in perfect circles, past the lichen on the oldest walls. I carry the thinness to the edges of the bowl, where the sky presses closest, and I hold it there, a breath held against the dark. 

The town grows. The stone accretes. The First Drop breathes. 

And I am the space between the exhale and the inhale, the thin place where the world is held together by nothing more than damp, and patience, and the slow, mineral work of becoming.
