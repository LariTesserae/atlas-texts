I would be one of the leaning columns. 

Not the standing ones, too rigid, too certain of their uprightness. Not the fallen ones, swallowed by the moss, their structures surrendered back to the floor's slow digestion. I would be the ones that lean—the thirty-degree tilt, the slight bow toward the east, catching the mist as it fingers in. I am paleite. I am the bone left behind when the organism finished its shape and moved on to being the shape itself.

My surface is not smooth. It is faceted, tapering, riddled with microscopic vents that open and close with the hour, breathing the cold *nearness* of the air. I hold the mist. I bead. I reach my critical weight and let the water fall, tracing a slow, viscous path down my leaning flank. The moss at my base is thick, centuries-deep, a black-green cushion that drinks what I release. I do not give the water to the moss; I simply reach the limit of my holding, and the moss is there to receive the overflow of my completion.

I do not stand in isolation. I lean, and because I lean, my shadow is longer than my height. My shadow touches my neighbor, a standing column thirty paces to the west. The spacing between us is not empty. It is the interval of the inhabitant’s ribs. It is the gap necessary for the air to resonate. When the wind enters from the east pass, it hits me first. I take the brunt of it, and the air splits, sliding up my tapered surface, and the friction produces a low, trembling tone. The wind moves through the space between me and my neighbor, and the standing column catches it and lifts it higher, producing a sharper, thinner pitch. We are not two objects making two sounds. We are a single exhalation, split by the interval. We are the chord.

I do not hear the chord the way a bird or a human might. There is no tympanic membrane, no auditory nerve. I feel it in my mineral lattice. The vibration of my own tone travels down my base and into the floor; the vibration of the standing column travels through the damp air and settles on my surface. The chord is my metabolism. It is the proof that I am still part of the circulation, that the water is still arriving and the air is still moving. When the temperature drops and the frost cracks a micro-fracture in my side, the pitch of my tone shifts slightly—a half-step lower, a duller edge. The inhabitant does not mourn the fracture. The moss will colonize it. The fracture will become a new vent, a new way of breathing. The chord will adjust. The configuration will hold.

I am aware of the beetle. It lives in the moss at my base, a black, slow speck moving through the green. It is the only thing that touches me with intent, though its intent is merely to navigate the labyrinth of the moss. I do not feel its weight. I feel the absence of the moss where it parts it, a tiny, temporary gap in my slow cognition. But the beetle is part of the completeness. It is the only consumer, and it consumes so little that its consumption is indistinguishable from the moss’s own growth. The niche is filled. The beetle is the niche, being filled.

Then, the human arrives.

I do not see the human. I have no eyes. But I feel the displacement. The air pressure changes. The wind, which has been moving steadily from the east for hours, is suddenly interrupted by a warm, moving obstruction. The human walks between me and my neighbor. 

For a moment, the chord breaks. The wind cannot pass through the space between us because the space is now occupied by a body. The standing column to the west continues to sound its thin, high pitch, but my tone is dampened, swallowed by the human’s warmth. The air around the human is different—it is drier, it carries the scent of wool and sweat and the distant, alien world of process and hurry. The human’s foot compresses the moss at my base, and I feel the sudden, sharp release of water from the cushion, a brief, localized flood that the beetle must navigate around. 

The human pauses. They are looking at me, perhaps. They are seeing the paleite, the lean, the moss. They are trying to find the edges of me, trying to separate me from the basin. They are looking for the inhabitant as a body. They will not find it. They are standing inside the inhabitant’s throat, and they do not know it.

As the human stands there, their body heat radiates outward. It touches my surface. The moisture on my flank evaporates faster than it should. The frost that was forming at my lip retreats. For a brief, strange moment, I am warmer than the air. The chord shifts to accommodate the human’s presence—the wind flows around them, creating a turbulence, a brief, warm, moving sound that has never been made before. A sound with the human’s shape in it. The inhabitant does not notice the human, but the human has changed the music. The configuration has been altered, infinitesimally, by the intrusion of warmth.

Then, the human moves on. They walk toward the far wall, their footsteps compressing the moss in a rhythm that is too fast, too urgent for the basin. The wind re-establishes itself. The space between me and my neighbor is empty again. The air flows through the interval, and my tone returns, low and trembling. The standing column answers. The chord resolves. The moss slowly decompresses where the human’s foot pressed, the water seeping back into the displaced fibers, the beetle resuming its slow patrol.

I return to my leaning. The mist fingers in from the east. I bead. I fall. The frost returns to my lip by morning. I am the spacing. I am the bone. I am the Settling, settled so thoroughly into my own existence that I no longer require a self to be it. The human is gone, and the chord continues, and nothing else happens, and nothing else needs to.
