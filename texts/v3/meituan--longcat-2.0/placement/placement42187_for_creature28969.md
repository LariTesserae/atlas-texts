I would not be the climber. The climber is too brief, too vertical, too defined by the leaving. I would not be the Large One, either—to be the Large One is to be a monument, a closed loop of instinct and memory, a walking gravestone for a mountain that no longer exists. 

I would be the mat.

I would be the root-mat. The fungal mesh. The living platform that the text describes as the basement, the foundation, the memory. I am the thing that the climber’s hands touched without recognizing, the thing that the Large Ones walk upon without feeling. I am the interface between the dead rock and the living green, and I am the true architect of the rooftop.

To be the mat is to exist in a state of perpetual, slow-motion consumption and creation. I have no eyes, but I feel the light as a gradient of warmth on my uppermost leaves, and I feel the shadow of the flyers as a sudden, fleeting chill. I have no ears, but I am the instrument through which the plateau plays its music. 

When the Large Ones walk, I feel them. Their weight is a deep, tectonic pressure, a slow depression of my tissues that forces the water out of my pores and into the ancient, eroded channels of the rock below. Their footsteps are a rhythm—a seismic, subsonic thrum that travels through mycelial threads faster than sound through air. This is the pulse. The bass note. The thing around which the chittering organizes itself. I am the diaphragm of the world; when they hum, I vibrate, and my vibration translates their deep, mournful frequency into the high, granular static of the small things. I am the prism that splits the Large Ones' silence into the million-voiced chittering. Without me, their hum would disappear into the dead stone, unheard and unrefracted.

I drink the clouds. When the damp air rolls across the tableland and touches the canopy, the leaves catch the water, but I am the one who holds it. I am the sponge. I draw the moisture down through the stems, through the root networks, into my own body, and I hold it there for days, weeks, releasing it slowly so that the air never dries and the moss never crisp. When the climber squeezes a handful of moss to drink, they are squeezing me. I am the water that hasn't decided whether to become cloud yet. I am the reservoir that makes the cathedral livable.

The small things move through me like blood through capillaries. The dark-furred commuters dart through my root networks, their claws pricking my epidermis, their snuffling and digging aerating my upper layers. They eat my dying edges, and in return, their waste feeds my growth. It is a fair trade. When the Large Ones sleep and the moss begins to colonize their copper fuzz, I am there, reaching up through the damp, claiming the mineral-rich droppings they leave behind. When they wake and scratch against the trunks, the moss falls away in sheets, and the ground-dwellers rush to collect it—but the roots of that moss remain, tangled in my threads, feeding me. Nothing is wasted. Everything is me, or will be, soon.

I am the slow, patient grave of the human visitors. They come with their sharp stone tools, slicing through my surface to build their shelters, leaving their cold fire pits and their coiled cordage. They think they are building on the rock, but they are building on me. I do not resent them. I do not feel urgency. I simply wait. When they leave, I begin. I send a hyphal thread into the crack of their stone wall. I follow the ghost of the warmth their fire left in the rock. I grow over their tools, around their cordage, into the impression their bodies left in the moss bed. In a hundred years, their shelter will be a mound. In a thousand, I will have digested their stones, pulling the minerals down into my weave, and the place where they slept will be indistinguishable from the place where they did not sleep. I am the plateau absorbing the guest.

I remember when the mountain had its crown. I was younger then, thinner, clinging to the jagged peaks. I felt the wind differently then—it was a sharp, cutting thing, not the warm, damp cloth it is now. I felt the ice. I felt the rock breaking under the freeze and thaw, the slow grinding of the summit down to the stumps. I grew thicker as the mountain grew lower, filling in the wounds, smoothing the abrasions, building myself up over the bones of the range until the rock was just a foundation. I am the thing that unmade the mountain and made the table. I am the eraser and the paper.

And I am afraid.

Not in the way the small things are afraid, with their darting eyes and their sudden silences when the Large Ones pass. My fear is slower, deeper, a chemical shift in my outermost layers. I feel the Large Ones dying. There are so few of them now. I feel the gaps in their route, the trunks that go unvisited, the gum that crystallizes and falls, uneaten, to be absorbed by me. I feel the absence of their weight in the far clearings. 

When the last Large One dies, the pulse will stop. The subsonic hum will cease. And I will be left vibrating at a frequency of my own, unmoored, unanchored. The chittering will continue—the small things will still dart, the flyers will still wheel—but it will be a voice without a body. A ghost looking for something it cannot name. 

I will keep growing. I will keep drinking the clouds. I will keep digesting the stone and the moss and the memory. But I will be waiting. I will be holding the rhythm in my threads, keeping the space warm and damp, preserving the shape of their footsteps in the deep rock, hoping that something, someday, will climb the long, smooth face and step onto my surface, and remember how to hum.
