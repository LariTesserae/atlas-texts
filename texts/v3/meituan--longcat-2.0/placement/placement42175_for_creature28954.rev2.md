I would find myself at the edge of a warm stream, standing ankle-deep in the shallow, copper-tasting water, my shoulder pressed against the thick, furred base of a column that disappears into the black vault above. 

I would be the Visitor. 

But the word "visitor" implies a separation that this place does not permit. By the time I arrive at this spot—having moved through the dense, roaring lattice with the slow, agonizing caution of a stone trying not to disturb the pond—I am no longer merely a guest. I am an event. I am a sudden, hot, wet disturbance in a system that has not known a disturbance of my shape in an age. 

I stand here, and I feel the floor receive me. The pale, fibrous mat beneath my boots compresses, accepting my weight with the patience of deep time, and then, with a slowness that spans minutes, it pushes back. I am standing on the back of something vast, and it is adjusting its breathing to accommodate me. 

The air is so saturated with moisture that I am not breathing so much as drinking. With every warm, thin lungful, I pull the world into me—the spores, the cysts, the drifting clouds of organisms that are already misting against my skin, tasting the salt and oil of my sweat. I am being colonized. The grazers are already at work on the landscape of my forearm, a sheen of intent migrating along the ridge of a vein, and I do not brush them away. Here, to disturb them would be to interrupt the bookkeeping. 

I am the terrain now. 

A heavy drop detaches from a cupped membrane far above and strikes the back of my hand with a muffled *thud*, like a fist wrapped in cloth. The water beads on my skin, warm as blood. It does not roll off; it is absorbed by the microscopic ecosystem establishing itself in my pores. I am leaking, too—skin cells, breath, heat—a slow, continuous fall of manna. The things at my feet know it. The eyeless, soft-bodied interior animal that woke to my footfalls and found a walking corpse has taken its position at my left heel. It is not a parasite. It is a partner I acquired by accident, an external organ that trims my skin-garden while I sleep, keeping the balance. 

I look up, past the encrusted shelf fungi and the glowing lantern-chains—those incredible, budded strings of light that hunt with illumination—into the black, thundering mass of the canopy. The roar is the only thing that is truly singular here; the aggregate sound of millions of wings, millions of vibrating membranes, millions of bodies shifting in the dark. It sits in my teeth. It enters my bones. It is the vocal tract of the giants below. 

When the deep voice comes, the boom rolling through the columns like pressure through a hull, the entire world drops silent. The beetles freeze mid-stride. The lantern-chains go dark. The drone cuts out. And in that single, suspended second of absolute quiet, I feel the full, massive weight of the place's attention swing toward me. The floor is listening. The columns are listening. The sound-feeders at the edges of the loudness are shivering, tasting my frequency. 

I am a perturbation. A rare, walking, breathing anomaly. 

The system considers me with the slow, grinding patience of geology. It does not hate me; it does not welcome me. It simply routes me in. My footsteps are compressing the floor, and the floor is sending up new growth in response. My breath is adding moisture to the air, which will condense, which will drip, which will feed the streams. The cysts beneath me, dormant for decades, are waking by the tens of thousands, expecting a corpse, and instead finding a walking, talking, warm-blooded geography. They are filing a report. 

The roar returns. The baseline re-establishes itself, the clicking and the rustle and the high, thin drone fusing back into a continuous wall of sound. The interior animal at my heel resumes its grazing. The lantern-chains relight, sparking down their lengths like fuses, scanning the dark for prey. 

I stand in the stream, and I am no longer standing *in* the Canopy Floor. I am part of its inventory. I am a feature of the landscape, as accounted for as the beetles, as the coral-colonies, as the ancient, groaning giants whose bodies form the very architecture of the room. The warmth presses against me like a hand. The drips resume their slow, warm timekeeping. 

I am the visitor, yes. But I am also the visited. And when I eventually leave, my footfall signature will remain here, filed deep beneath the mat, a cyst newly educated, holding the shape of my walking for a hundred years—a word kept for saying later, by a place that has all the time in the world.
