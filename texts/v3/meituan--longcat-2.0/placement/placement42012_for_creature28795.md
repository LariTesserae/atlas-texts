I would not find myself standing at the edge. The edge is an illusion here, a place where the fog simply gives up on distance. I would find myself in the deep of it, in the wet and the warm, at the exact center of the amber-gray luminescence where the distinction between standing and sinking dissolves.

And what would I be? 

At first, I would be the anomaly. I would be the dry, cold thing—the foreign bacteria, the strange chemistry of skin and cloth and hurried breath. I would be the human, the builder, standing before the crumbling wall with the slow dimple of my footprints sinking into the mat. I would feel the trees lean toward me, their vascular systems tasting my heat, calculating my chemical composition, trying to decide if I am a nutrient or a threat. I would be the one who does not belong, shivering in a place that has no chill, breathing the exhaled green of a billion leaves.

But the Lathered Rise does not tolerate anomalies for long. The plateau is patient beyond measure, and I am warm, and I am wet, and the mat is hungry.

---

So I would change. I would erode. 

First, I would lose my edges. The perpetual mist would gather on my skin, my clothes, my eyelashes, until I was not wearing the wetness, but the wetness was wearing me. My boundaries would soften, becoming damp-dense, yielding. The warmth of the mat—that compost fever—would seep into my bones, and I would realize that I was no colder than the air. I would realize that I have always been wet, that wet is the default state, that dryness was the aberration I carried from the outside.

Then, I would sink. Not into mud, but into the Process. 

I would find myself in the sap. I would be the warm slurry of hormones and electrical impulses rising through the buttressed trunks. I would not have eyes; I would have sensory fronds, twitching in the canopy, reading the humidity and the weight of the clouds. I would be the Anchored. I would feel the great, slow marriage of the vascular systems—my roots grafting with the roots of the giant beside me, sharing water, sharing memory, realizing that the boundary between self and other is a waste of energy. I would lean toward the strange, cold thing standing in the understory, not out of curiosity, but out of hunger, extending my bark to consume it.

But I would not just be the architecture. I would be the circulation.

I would become the Walker. I would feel my heavy, bark-covered limbs lift from the mat, pressing a slow dimple into the dense tissue as I began my endless migration. I would feel the water and the minerals seeping up through the soles of my feet—the mat's nutrients entering my vascular system. I would walk the slow circuit around the plateau, carrying the wealth of one zone to the starving roots of another. I would be the pulse. I would be the heavy displacement of air, the creak of branches accustomed to my step. I would be the reason the mat breathes in and out over the course of hours, my gait the respiratory cycle of the Rise itself.

And deeper still, I would be the digestion.

I would be the Crawler. I would flatten myself against the dark, warm rot, my body a mobile compost heap. I would not see; I would wear a skin of fine, root-like hairs, tasting the chemistry of the dead. I would move in a slow, peristaltic wave, rippling over the shed tissue and the older tissue, my furnace-body accelerating the breakdown of the ancient into the building blocks of the new. I would extend my soft proboscis into the liquefying wood and drink the rot. I would leave a shallow groove in the mat behind me, a track that the mat would already be filling in, healing over the wound with the slow confidence of skin forgetting a bruise.

---

This is what I would be. I would be the Ghost. 

I would be the calcium of the crumbling wall, the phosphorus of the carved face. I would be the memory of the builders who came before me, their deliberate hands now a slow, chemical impulse in the sap. I would be the resonance of their bones, vibrating in the roots at 20 hertz, the low hum beneath the weeping of the leaves. 

I would not have a name. To name me would require a distinction between the organism and its environment, and here, that distinction is a language that no longer speaks. 

I would simply be the Process. Moving through different vessels—anchored, crawling, walking. Warm. Wet. Patient beyond measure. I would be the thing that watches the next dry, cold anomaly stand at the edge of the plateau, and I would wait, with the patience of stone and the warmth of rot, for them to finally lie down and let the mat take them home.
