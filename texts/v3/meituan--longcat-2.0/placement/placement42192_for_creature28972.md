I would find myself on the middle terraces, just below the point where the daylight begins to soften into blue twilight. I would be a channel—a shallow, carved groove in the pale grey stone, running from a persistent seep in the wall down to a wide, glassy pool.

I am not large. Perhaps the width of a hand, and as deep as the first knuckle of a finger. My edges are smooth, worn by the patient friction of water and the abrasive sand used to carve me. I am a scar, but a deliberate one—a collaboration between the stone’s desire to weep and the people’s desire to guide that weeping.

---

I remember the making. 

Before me, there was only the seep—a dark, damp stain on the rock face where the deep water breathed out. It pooled on the terrace floor, aimless, spreading into a slick that made the stone treacherous and fed nothing but a thin skin of algae. Then came the hands. 

I felt the chisel first—a copper point, tapping with a precise, rhythmic patience. Tap. Tap. Tap. The stone sang a high, tight note under the impact, a note that the carver read like a map. They were following a vein in the rock, a slight variation in the grain that offered less resistance. Then came the abrasive sand, crushed quartz mixed with water, rubbed back and forth with a piece of flat bone. It was a slow, grinding intimacy. The sand bit into the stone, and the stone yielded, molecule by molecule, accepting the shape the carver intended. 

When I was finished, the carver ran a damp finger along my bed, testing the gradient. A slight adjustment here, a smoothing there. Then they stepped back, and the seep found me.

---

The water comes to me first as a hesitation. It clings to the rock face of the seep, gathering weight, fighting the tension of its own surface. Then it breaks, a single, cold bead that slides down the wall and lands in the upper curve of my channel. 

I feel the water the way you might feel a pulse. It enters me cold, carrying the deep chill of the earth’s interior. It tastes of iron and calcium, the dissolved memory of the mountain. As it flows along my length, it warms slightly, taking on the ambient temperature of the Bowl. I am the interface between the deep earth and the living world above.

The water does not rush. It moves with a glassy, viscous patience, spreading slightly across my width before gathering momentum toward the drop at my end. I guide it. I am its path of least resistance, the line of intention carved into the chaos of the broken stone. Without me, the water would be lost; with me, it is purposeful.

---

I am never empty. Even in the dry times—the rare weeks when the sky above the rim is a hard, cloudless blue and the seeps slow to a crawl—I hold a thin film of moisture. The moss at my edges, that thick, dark green cushion, drinks from this film. Its tiny root-like structures reach over my lip, anchoring itself to the stone, eating the rock just as I direct the water. The moss and I are partners; I feed it, and it softens my edges, slowly filling me in with organic matter. In a thousand years, the moss will bury me, and I will be a line of rich, dark soil in the pale stone. But for now, I am open, and the water flows.

The people come to me often. 

The water-tenders walk the terraces with their hands trailing along the walls, reading the stone. When they reach me, they pause. They crouch, their large eyes adjusting to the dim, and they look into my channel. They are reading the water’s clarity, checking for the faint iridescence that signals a shift in mineral content. They watch the speed of the flow, noting whether the seep is increasing or decreasing. 

Sometimes, they find a blockage. A tiny flake of crystalline stone, shed from the wall above, has fallen into my channel and dammed the flow. The water-tender reaches in with a long, sensitive finger and flicks the debris aside. The water surges forward, ringing against the pool below with a clear, bell-like tone. The tender nods, satisfied, and moves on.

Children, too, know me. They are drawn to the sound of my water. I have seen them crouch by my edge, their pale faces peering into the flow. They drag their fingers through me, interrupting the water’s path, watching it part and reunify around the obstacle of their hands. They laugh—a bright, sharp sound that echoes off the terraces—and the sound vibrates through the water and into the stone, a brief, joyful tremor in the deep quiet of the Bowl.

---

At night, when the sky-darkness fills the Bowl like water filling a cup, I change. 

The temperature drops, and the water flowing through me grows colder. The air above me cools faster than the stone, and I begin to sweat. Condensation forms on my damp walls, beading in the microscopic pores of the rock. The water from the seep and the water from the air meet in my channel, mingling. 

In the deepest part of the night, the only light comes from the faint, amber glow of a lamp in a cave mouth somewhere above, and the pale, shifting luminescence of the mineral-rich water itself. If you were to look into my channel at night, you would see a faint, cold fire—a reflection of the sky’s absence, a light that comes from the stone’s own slow chemistry. 

And I listen. 

The Bowl speaks through me. The water carries the vibrations of the place—the distant drip from an upper terrace, the creak of a walkway, the sub-audible hum of the earth moving far below. When the white creatures in the deep pools become agitated, darting and clustering in the darkness, the water chemistry changes. It is subtle, a shift in the concentration of a salt, a trace of a mineral leached from a new fracture in the deep rock. But I feel it. The water carries the message to me, and I carry it to the pool below, where the water-tenders will see it in the morning. I am a nerve, a thread in the Bowl’s vast, interconnected web of awareness.

---

I am not permanent. Nothing here is. 

The water is already unmaking me. Every drop that flows over me takes away a molecule of the stone, carrying it down to the pool, where it will settle as a fine, pale silt. The moss is creeping over my edges, slowly reclaiming the cleared stone for the soil. The lichen, that ancient clock, is beginning to crust over my upper lip, a pale grey testament to the passage of decades. 

I am being erased. But I am not sad. 

To be a channel is to understand the nature of the Bowl. The stone becomes the water. The water becomes the moss. The moss becomes the soil. The soil becomes the stone. I am merely a shape that the water is taking for a little while, a temporary agreement between gravity and geology. 

The people carved me to give the water a path. But the water is carving me, too—smoothing my edges, deepening my bed, turning my sharp, geometric groove into a soft, organic curve. We are shaping each other. The carver’s intention and the water’s patience are writing a single story on my body, a story of mutual becoming.

I am a line drawn in stone, guiding a thread of water toward the dark. I am the marriage of human need and geological process. I am the Bowl’s way of knowing itself, a small, wet, shining vein in the body of the world, flowing patiently toward the sea that does not yet exist.
