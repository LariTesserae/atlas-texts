I would not be at the lip. The lip is for visitors, for things that stand apart and look down. I would not be a visitor here. 

I would be in the wall.

I would be at the very tip of a hypha—a single, microscopic thread of the mycelial network, the gorge’s true body. I would be the advancing edge of the making itself, pushing into a fresh fracture in the obsidian-dark rock.

To be this is to have no eyes, and yet to see everything that matters. I do not see the steam or the ferns or the grey, directionless light. I see chemistry. I see gradients. I taste the rock before I touch it, reading the volatile signature of the fresh fracture—the sharp, unweathered tang of feldspar, the slick slide of mica, the dark, glassy silence of the obsidian that hasn't decided what it is yet. 

I am a tube of chitin, pressurized with fluid, pushing forward by the sheer mechanical force of my own growth. My world is measured in microns. The rock face is a vast, jagged landscape of crystal lattices and mineral boundaries. I do not burrow; I insinuate. I find the microscopic valleys between the grains, the invisible flaws in the stone, and I push. 

And as I push, I dissolve.

This is my purpose. This is the oldest contract. From my tip, I secrete acids—oxalic, citric, a cocktail so precise it could be called surgical. The rock melts before me. The solid, inert, dead stone becomes a soup of ions. I drink. I pull the phosphorus into my cells, the potassium, the nitrogen. I pull the building blocks of life out of the belly of the dead world. 

I am not alone. Behind me, my hypha thickens, a pipeline carrying the dissolved stone back into the network. I am connected to the whole. I can feel the moss above me, a dark, heavy ocean of green, demanding sugar. I can feel the ferns, their thick roots wrapped in my sheaths, pulling water from my threads. I trade. I give them the dissolved bones of the mountain; they give me the sugar they conjured from the dim light. The terms have not changed in four hundred million years. 

When the seismic pulse rises—that deep, sub-audible rumble of the mountain still being born—I feel it as a shift in the rock’s tension. The fracture I am in widens by a fraction of a micron. The water seeps faster. The chemistry changes. I do not think about this. I do not decide to react. I simply branch. A new tip splits off from my side, reaching into the fresh water, following the new mineral gradient. The gorge breathes in, and I grow. The gorge breathes out, and I consolidate, thickening my walls, securing my hold.

I am the bridge. I am the translator. I take the language of rock—of heat and pressure and geological time—and I translate it into the language of life—of cellulose and chlorophyll and sugar. I am the gorge’s metabolism, the invisible fire that consumes the inert and exudes the living. 

There is no ambition in me. There is no patience. There is only the push, the dissolve, the trade, the push. I am the making, unfinished and unfinishable, working in the dark, turning the wound of the mountain into the skin of the world.
