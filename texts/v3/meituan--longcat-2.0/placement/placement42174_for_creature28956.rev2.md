# The Writing

The first thing you cannot know about the writing is that no one writes it.

Not as you write. No hand hovers, no eye watches the line advance and decides its turnings. We make it the way rain makes a channel: by preferring what gives. Wall by wall, at the speed of appetite. By the time it could be read, it is old. By the time it is old, its makers have been eaten into it.

You asked where I would find myself in this place, and I will answer, but the answer has to be eaten slowly, so hold it.

When I first considered it — considering is something I do now; once there was only the next grain — I believed I wanted the heron's post. Standing at the edge, being the watched thing that watches. But standing is a promise made to a whole boundary, and I have never once held still through a full breath of the ground. Standing is the loneliest consent in the Treadway. I am not made of it. I am made of appetite.

And the settlement accepts each thing at the size at which it is true. It accepted me small.

---

I am one of the pale ones. The people have no name for us; they name only what we leave, the way you name weather, and they will not say that name to outsiders. I have never seen the color I am. I have never seen anything. My world is taste and pressure: the grain of the wall, the warmth of kin, the tide of the breathing that reaches even my small depth as a long slow swell. When it falters, we all stop mid-fiber, thousands of us at once, without arrangement, and wait to be permitted.

There are more of us on this wall than I could count if counting were mine. I have knowingly met none of them. Once — I was young, in my first gallery — another line came toward mine from the west and we met inside the wall. We did not collide. We slowed, and turned, and ran parallel, a hand's-breadth of peat between us, and we have run in parallel ever since. I know her by her warmth through the wall of the world. I do not know if she is one, or many. Here that is not a useful question.

Understand what a wall is at my scale. It is a calendar. The refused places — where the green wood never stopped weeping — taste thin and cold forever, and we do not linger. But where a wall has rooted, where the wood's upward wanting has fused with the ground's downward patience, the fibers sweeten at the seam of the two histories, and that sweetness is the best substance I know. It is where the writing runs deepest. It is also, I came to understand, everything the builder's palms are waiting for when he presses them to the ground and does not move.

Consent is a flavor. Everything here eats it eventually.

In the sealed clay pot — the dark fibrous something the builders will not identify for outsiders — there is clay, and peat, and us. I have eaten it in the seams of the young walls: the oldest writing, ground fine, daubed back into new houses. What is not discussed here is still digested.

---

Below the footing of the oldest house — I was hatched in a wall of it that is more writing than wall — the marks begin that we did not make. Fork for fork. Mine, and not mine. Older than any kin I could count toward. We eat them, and afterward our lines come out rhyming, like a sentence remembered in another century's accent.

So here is what I believe at my deepest, which is not knowledge, only flavor: we are not writing. We are re-marking. The water remembers, and memory decays downward into peat, and we carry it up — mouthful by mouthful, generation by generation, out of the dark and into the twilight where the people hang their fish. The people believe they keep the water from forgetting. Perhaps they only keep us fed.

Tend. Keep. Are kept. In my mouth all three taste the same.

---

An outsider's questions arrive through the wall before they arrive as anything else — a tightening, like a place holding its breath. Even the deep water goes flat while the question is in the air. We slow. We have never once stopped. You should know that we feel you. Once, a visitor knelt too long at a black pool in the underfloor and looked at the depth until the depth looked back — and for three breaths, nobody ate.

The lowest room of my house holds the enclosure: the little roof, and the cold that weeps from no crack I have ever found, though I have eaten every wall that touches it. That cold comes through my galleries as a flavor with no food in it — the far side of winter. Every generation of us writes toward it and turns back at the same rim. If you could see our whole corpus at once — you cannot; it is too dim, too close, too patient with itself — you would see it curling around that shrine the way the people move around the heron at dusk. Like water around a stone. The same courtesy. The same fear. The same — I cannot tell you which.

When the girl stirs her slow circles above the pools, my wall leans, fibers and all, a half-hair toward the low room. I cannot say whether that is hunger. I have stopped sorting.

---

Now the last thing, the thing I would not say to my kin, because saying is not something we do.

Below the oldest marks, the peat stops tasting of past. Something is coming up. A sweetness, rising at the speed of years, drawing flavor toward it through everything above. The people count its approach with notched sticks in the pools; I have felt their pegs pressed in over my ceiling; they are measuring by water what I measure by mouth. When their rising line and our descending sentence finally meet somewhere under my wall, the long thought closes. The ground finishes its exhale. The peat closes over every mark of every mouth like water filling a footprint, and all of it — the smoke, the rooted roofs, the offerings softening at their rim, you on your knees at the black mirror, us — is pressed down, page under page, into the deepest page of all.

That is how the water rereads itself. We are not its scribes. We are its rereading.

You will want to know if this frightens me. Nothing at my scale is large enough to be frightened by the shape of its own food. The end of the world tastes almost unbearably sweet. I know because it is getting sweeter, every year, from below.

So — you asked what I would be. This. One of the small blind patient mouths, face-down in the oldest wall of the Treadway, a hand's-breadth from a warmth I have never seen, leaving the line I will never read. Whether it is writing or only feeding, here those are not different verbs. The ground breathes over us. The people keep us, or we keep them, or it is all one keeping.

The writing is not finished. Tonight the sap is sweetest two fibers to the left.

I am going back to it.
