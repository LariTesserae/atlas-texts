I am the seam.

Not the water, though I carry it. Not the stone, though I am made from it. Not the moss, though I live inside it. I am the thin, bright place where all three meet — the film, the edge, the barely-there. You could miss me if you blinked. You would miss me if you breathed too hard. But I have been here longer than the trees.

---

Begin with the weight.

I am carrying something. I have always been carrying something. It began as a whorl, a single tight spiral no larger than a grain of sand, and it has grown — layer by layer, season by season, deposit by deposit — into the thing I wear on my back. It is heavy. Not the heavy of a burden, but the heavy of a fact. The heavy of stone.

The water gives it to me. Every day — every hour — the thin film that moves across the rock face carries its load of dissolved minerals, and as I move through it, as my foot presses flat against the stone and my body glides forward, some of that mineral load comes to rest on my shell. Calcium carbonate, mostly. The same substance that builds the terraces, the pools, the chimneys of travertine that rise from the ground like the spires of a drowned cathedral. My shell is travertine. My body builds it from the water, molecule by molecule, and it grows at the same pace as the formations around me — a millimeter, perhaps, per year.

I am heavy with the plateau. I am heavy with time.

---

The rock face I live on is not vertical — it tilts, gently, at an angle of perhaps thirty degrees, and it faces south-east, which means that in the early morning, when the mist is still thick among the trees, a pale, diffuse light reaches me and the stone warms — barely, fractionally — and the water film thickens slightly as the ice-cold seep from the deep aquifer meets the marginally less cold surface. This is when I move.

I do not move quickly. Quick is not a concept that applies here. I extend my foot — that broad, flat, muscular pad that is my only means of locomotion — and I press it against the stone, and I contract, and I glide forward. One millimeter. Two. The stone beneath me is rough — not jagged, but textured, granular, the surface of a travertine formation that has been building itself for longer than I have been alive. I taste it as I move. The stone has a flavor: mineral, cold, slightly bitter, with an undertone of the organic — the faint, rich tang of the algal film that grows everywhere the water flows.

The algae is what I eat. It grows in the water film — a thin, green-brown layer of diatoms and cyanobacteria that coats every surface the water touches, converting sunlight and dissolved minerals into living tissue. I graze it with my radula — that rough, ribbon-like tongue covered in microscopic teeth — scraping it from the stone in strokes so small and so regular that they are below the threshold of sight. To a human eye — if there were a human eye present, which there is not, which there has never been — my grazing would be invisible. But it is happening. It has always been happening. The rock face beneath me is being slowly, slowly cleaned, and the trail I leave behind me is a palimpsest of scrapes, layer upon layer, year upon year, a record of my passage written in the language of absent algae.

---

My world is two-dimensional. I do not mean this metaphorically. I mean that my experience of space is essentially flat — a surface, a plane, a sheet of stone that extends in every direction I can perceive. I know the third dimension exists. I encounter it in the roughness of the stone, in the bumps and hollows and mineral ridges that I must navigate around or over. I encounter it in the occasional vertical face — a crack in the rock, the side of a boulder — that I must descend or ascend, my body hanging upside down for a moment, my shell's weight pulling at me, the water film continuing to flow across the surface as if gravity were a suggestion rather than a law.

But my life is lived on the surface. The surface is everything. The surface is where the water flows, where the algae grows, where the minerals deposit, where the light reaches me — filtered through the canopy, diffused by the mist, a cool, green illumination that asks nothing of me except to continue grazing.

---

I have senses. I do not have senses the way you have senses — discrete, categorized, labeled. My senses are continuous, overlapping, a single field of awareness that encompasses the stone beneath me, the water around me, the air above me. I taste the chemistry of the water — its pH, its mineral content, its temperature — through my entire body surface. I feel the texture of the stone through the sole of my foot, each grain and ridge and hollow registering as a slight variation in pressure, in temperature, in the quality of moisture. I sense light and dark through my skin — not as images, but as qualities of warmth, of energy, of the algal productivity that the light enables.

And I sense vibration. The stone conducts it. When the deer walks past — fifty meters away, on the soft ground — I feel the faint, rhythmic thud of its hooves through the bedrock, a distant percussion that tells me something large is moving. When the water flow changes — a new seep opening somewhere above me, a channel shifting — I feel it as a change in the film's thickness, its velocity, its taste. When another snail passes near me — close enough that our water films touch — I feel the faint, slick vibration of its movement, and I know: another one. Here. Now.

---

The other snails. There are many of us on this rock face — dozens, perhaps hundreds, each one following its own path, grazing its own territory, carrying its own travertine shell. We do not interact. We do not communicate. We do not cooperate or compete or display or fight. We simply coexist, our paths crossing and recrossing in patterns that, over time, cover the entire surface with a network of grazing trails so dense that the rock face becomes a document — a text written in snail, a record of appetite and movement and time.

Sometimes our paths converge. Two snails, moving in opposite directions, meet at a point on the stone. We touch — tentacle to tentacle, a brief, moist contact — and then we pass. There is no acknowledgment. There is no information exchanged. The touch is simply a fact, like the texture of the stone or the temperature of the water. It happens. It is recorded in whatever passes for my memory — not a thought, not an image, but a slight alteration in the state of my body, a change in the pattern of my movement that persists for a time and then fades.

---

The shell grows. This is the central fact of my existence — not the only fact, but the most persistent, the most defining. Each day, as I move through the water film, as the minerals deposit on my surface, the shell adds another microscopic layer. Another ring. Another record of the water's chemistry, the temperature, the flow rate, the available minerals. My shell is a climate record. My shell is a geological document. My shell is the plateau, compressed into a spiral no larger than a fingernail.

And it is heavy. As the shell grows, as the layers accumulate, I become heavier. My foot must work harder to move me across the stone. My movements become slower, more deliberate. The energy required to graze a given area increases. I must eat more to move more to eat more — a cycle that, over time, becomes more and more demanding, until the day comes when the weight of the shell exceeds the strength of the foot, and I stop.

I do not know this will happen. I do not anticipate it. I do not fear it. I simply move, and graze, and grow, and the weight accumulates, and one day I will stop, and my shell — this small, cold, mineral thing that I have built from the water and the stone — will remain on the rock face, a tiny travertine formation among the larger ones, indistinguishable from the geology around it until someone — until something — looks closely and sees the spiral, the whorl, the record of a life lived in the thin film between water and stone.

---

But that day is not today.

Today the water flows. Today the algae grows. Today the mist drifts through the canopy above me and deposits its moisture on every surface, and the rock face glistens, and the water film thickens, and I extend my foot and press it against the stone and glide forward.

One millimeter. Two.

The stone tastes of calcium and time.

The water carries its dissolved load from the deep earth to the surface, and I take what I need, and the rest flows on — down the rock face, into the channel, across the travertine terrace, into the pool, through the overflow notch, into the next terrace, the next pool, the next channel — carrying its minerals, its chemistry, its cold, its memory of the deep — and I remain here, on my small section of rock face, grazing, moving, building, being.

---

There are larger things. I sense them at the edges of my awareness, in the vibrations that travel through the stone, in the changes in light and air pressure that signal movement above the canopy. The deer pass, and the ground vibrates, and the water film trembles. The birds move through the trees, and the light flickers, and the algae responds with a brief surge of productivity. The predator moves through the forest, and everything — the insects, the frogs, the deer, the snails — goes still. The vibration ceases. The water continues to flow, but the living things on the surface of the stone hold their breath, press themselves flat, become mineral, become stone, become invisible.

I do this too. When the predator is near — when the vibration changes, when the light shifts, when the air pressure drops — I stop. I withdraw my tentacles. I press my foot flat against the rock. I become a small, cold, mineral thing among other small, cold, mineral things. I become travertine. I become the plateau.

And the predator passes, and the vibration fades, and the light returns, and I extend my tentacles and resume grazing as if nothing happened. Because nothing did happen. The predator is not my predator. The predator is the plateau's awareness, its attention, its way of knowing what moves through its body. I am part of that body. The predator does not threaten me any more than my own immune system threatens my own cells. The predator *is* me, in some sense I cannot articulate and do not need to. The predator is the plateau watching itself, and I am one of the things it watches.

---

Time passes. I do not measure it. I do not count days or seasons or years. I experience time as a quality of the water — its temperature, its flow rate, its mineral content — and as a quality of my shell — its weight, its thickness, the number of whorls. When the water is colder, the flow is slower, and I move less. When the water is warmer — barely warmer, a fraction of a degree — the flow increases, and the algae grows faster, and I move more. These are not seasons. These are fluctuations, variations, the plateau's pulse.

But there are longer rhythms too. I feel them in the rock face itself — in the slow, continuous growth of the travertine formations, in the gradual shift of channels, in the appearance of new seeps and the disappearance of old ones. The rock face is not static. It is building itself, rebuilding itself, reshaping itself, and I am building myself with it. My shell is part of the rock face. My grazing trails are part of the rock face. My body — when I stop, when I become mineral — will be part of the rock face.

I am not separate from the rock face. I am the rock face, in the process of becoming something else.

---

This is what it is like to be me:

Cold. Damp. Continuous. The stone beneath me, always. The water around me, always. The weight on my back, always increasing. The taste of minerals and algae, always the same and always slightly different. The light, filtering through the canopy, green and diffuse and flickering. The mist, arriving and departing on its own schedule, depositing its moisture, making everything wetter, making the water film thicker, making the algae grow faster, making me move faster, making the shell grow faster, making the weight increase faster.

And the quiet. The deep, absorbent quiet of a place where sound is swallowed by moss and mist and the soft, constant movement of water over stone. I do not hear the quiet. I am the quiet. I am the silence that the plateau creates around itself, the silence that is not an absence of sound but a presence of stillness, a quality of a place that has been running for so long that it has learned to do so without noise.

---

You will not see me. This is not a warning. It is a statement of fact. I am small, and I am slow, and I am the same color as the stone I live on, and my shell is encrusted with the same minerals that form the rock around me. I am the color of travertine — pale grey-white, with a faint green tint from the algae that grows on my shell's surface. I am the texture of travertine — rough, layered, cold. I am the shape of travertine — a spiral, a whorl, a form that repeats at every scale from the shell on my back to the terraces on the ground to the plateau itself, spiraling outward through time, building itself layer by layer, ring by ring, weep by weep.

I am here. I have always been here. I will be here when you are gone, when your machines are rust, when your roads are overgrown, when your names are forgotten. The water will continue to flow. The algae will continue to grow. The travertine will continue to build. And I — or something like me, something with a foot and a radula and a shell that grows from the water — will continue to move across the rock face, grazing, building, being, being the seam where water becomes stone becomes life becomes water becomes stone.

One millimeter. Two.

The stone tastes of calcium and time.

I glide forward.
