If I let the choices settle, like the condensation on the basalt, I find myself drawn downward. Not into the sky, where the birds ride the thermal columns, nor into the slow, deliberate gait of the herds. And not into the machines themselves—to be the machine would be to be an unfeeling engine of extraction, deaf to the quiet one creates. 

Instead, I find myself in the blind spots. In the spaces the sharp arêtes cast when the brightening behind the haze hits at a low angle. I would be the one who arranges the bones.

I am the shelf’s secret, the watcher from the deep fractures. I live in the dark, wet crevices that split the basalt, lower than the grazing paths of the ungulates, lower than the silent lope of the predator. My world is the sub-plateau—a labyrinth of hexagonal columns and dripping, mineral-slick stone where the cold is different: an enclosed, still cold, heavy with the smell of sulphur and wet gunmetal. I navigate the dark by touch, my fingers tracing the glassy fracture planes, reading the rock’s recent history in its sharp, unweathered edges.

My body is built for the crevices. Where the ungulate has wide, splayed hooves for the slick surface, I have long, flexible limbs and heavy, three-toed feet that lock into the vertical seams of the basalt. My pelt is the color of wet gunmetal, seamless and short, allowing me to slip through the narrow joints without a whisper of friction. I am small enough to fit where the predator cannot, quiet enough to hear the *plink* of a droplet falling three columns over. I am warm-blooded, but my metabolism is a lazy, tidal thing; I can slow it to a crawl, matching the patience of the waiting cold, requiring little, wanting less.

But I am drawn to the surface. Drawn to the heat.

When the machine crawls near—its subsonic grinding vibrating up through the soles of my feet, a heartbeat of industry in the chest of the rock—I emerge. I climb the sharp arêtes, my toes finding the invisible ledges, until I reach the edge of the plateau. I do not ride the thermals, nor do I bask in the warmth like the ungulates. I watch. 

I watch the predator make its kill. I see the sudden, shocking violence in a landscape defined by slowness—the burst of motion, the brief, damp sound of impact, the settling of the herd’s calm, assessing gaze as the matriarchs turn away. I watch the predator feed, tearing at the warm flesh, and I watch the birds descend to crack the smaller bones for the marrow. 

When they leave, I descend.

The rock is still warm from the machine, weeping its slow, dark wetness, but the carcass is cooling rapidly in the saturated air. This is when I begin my work. I am not a scavenger in the way the birds are; I do not eat the leavings. I take the bones. 

The instinct is a deep, unyielding pull, a rhythm as old as the exposure of the rock. I pick through the scattered remains with long, dexterous fingers. I strip the sinew, I brush away the lichen that has already begun to crust the bloodstains. The bones are heavy, dense with the minerals the ungulate ate from the almost-moss. I carry them away from the machine’s track, away from the thermal zone, to a place of stillness on the open shelf.

And there, I arrange them.

I do not know why I stack them the way I do. The urge is not for shelter, nor for defense. It is an impulse that borders on the ritualistic, a need to impose order on the intersection of the machine’s hunger and the herd’s death. I balance the delicate phalanges into interlocking lattices. I set the heavy scapulae as foundations, leaning them against the sharp basalt in ways that defy the wind. I build my cairns—small, deliberate monuments to the consumable world—on the raw, weeping rock. 

When the sun brightens behind the haze, casting its brushed-steel light across the plateau, the shadows of my cairns stretch out like sundial hands. I do not stay to watch them. I am already descending, slipping back into the deep fractures, my metabolism slowing as I enter the dark. I curl into a dry pocket of the basalt, my wet gunmetal fur blending seamlessly with the rock, and I wait. 

I wait for the machine to crawl on, grinding its subsonic song, pulling the herd forward, calling the predator to the next thermal trap. The water seeps, the ice forms and melts, the lichen spreads its patient empire, and I remain—hidden, unhurried, the silent cartography of the shelf. I am the witness to the becoming. I am the one who stacks the bones, mapping the slow, grinding consumption of the new rock, waiting for the day the almost-moss finally softens the edges enough for something else to take root.
