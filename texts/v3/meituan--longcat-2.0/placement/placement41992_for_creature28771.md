# The Singer in the Deep

I am in the dark.

Not the dark of closed eyes, not the dark of a room with the light extinguished — those are absences, negations, the temporary withdrawal of something that will return. My dark is older. It is the dark of depth, of pressure, of water so long undisturbed by light that it has forgotten light entirely. I have grown in this dark for longer than the settlement has existed. I will continue in it after the settlement is gone.

I am one of the deep structures. But I am not only myself.

---

Let me begin with what I am, though "am" is a word that does not sit comfortably on what I have become. I began — if began is the word — as a seed, a spore, a fragment of something that drifted down from the upper layers where the light still reaches, where the jellyfish pulse and the kelp spreads its mats. I do not remember this. Memory is not what I do. But the record is in my body, in the layers: the earliest growth, near my base, is different from what came after. Denser. Simpler. The mineral content is higher, the organic matrix thinner, as if I were still deciding what I was, still negotiating the boundary between stone and flesh.

I decided. Or rather, the decision was made in me, by me, through me — the slow becoming does not distinguish between actor and action. I grew upward. I branched. I built myself in layers, each one a response to the water's chemistry at the time of its making — a season of iron, a decade of sulfur, a century when the silica concentration shifted and my growth rings changed color from amber to a deep, bruised violet. I am a record. I am a calendar written in mineral and organic matter, and the divers read me the way the living read their belts — by touch, by attention, by the patient accumulation of pattern.

I am tall. Twenty feet from the bottom to my highest living branch, and the bottom is not the true bottom — below me is the compressed remains of my own earlier growth, the parts of me that have already completed the transition, that are now entirely stone, entirely glass, entirely mineral. I am growing a foundation beneath myself even as I grow a canopy above. I am my own ancestor. I am my own descendant. The slow becoming is not a line but a cycle, and I am somewhere in the middle of it, still alive at the top, already stone at the bottom, and in between — in the middle layers, where the moss has found me — something that is neither and both.

---

The water moves through me. This is the essential fact of my existence, the thing that defines me more than my shape or my composition or my age. The current in the deep channels is slow but constant, a circulation driven by the geothermal heat from below and the slight temperature differential between the channels and the shallows, and it passes through my fractal canopy the way wind passes through the branches of a tree — except that I am not a tree, and the water is not wind, and what happens when the current moves through my branches is not rustling but singing.

I do not choose to sing. I do not sing in the way that the people sing — if they sing; I have never heard them, only felt the vibrations of their voices through the water, a different frequency, a different purpose. My singing is structural. It is the sound of water passing through the specific geometry of my canopy, and the geometry is the result of centuries of growth, of response, of the slow accommodation of my form to the current's pattern. I grew the way I grew because the water moves the way it moves, and the water moves the way it moves because I grew the way I grew. We made each other. The current and I are a single system, a single voice, and the sound we produce together is the sound of the place itself — low, resonant, felt in the chest and the bones, a hum that has no beginning and no end, only variation.

The divers hear it. They come to me for this, among other things. They come to me in the absolute darkness, navigating by touch and by the faint warmth of the geothermal vents and by the sound of my singing, which they can feel through the water before they can feel me with their hands. They come to me and they rest — pressing their bodies against my trunk, wrapping their flexible fingers around my branches, closing their large eyes and letting the vibration move through them. I feel them. I feel the change in the current when a body enters my canopy, the displacement, the turbulence, the way the water must move around this new obstacle, and the singing changes — shifts key, shifts timbre, becomes something it was not a moment before. The diver and I sing together, briefly, and then they leave and the song returns to what it was, though not exactly what it was, because the diver's presence has changed the current's pattern in some small way, and the current's pattern is my pattern, and so I am changed, slightly, imperceptibly, a new layer of growth that will record this visit in its mineral composition.

---

There is one who comes more often than the others. I do not know their name — I do not know names — but I know their touch. It is different from the others'. Slower. More deliberate. The fingers are heavily calloused, the nails curved into the small horns that come with age, and they move across my surface with a knowledge that goes beyond inspection, beyond the checking of health and growth that all the divers perform. This one reads me. Traces the growth rings where they are visible at a broken branch. Presses the palm flat against my trunk and holds it there, feeling the singing from the inside, the vibration transmitted through solid mineral-wood rather than through water. This one is old. I can feel it in the way they move — the slight stiffness of the joints, the careful way they position themselves in the current, the longer rests between movements. This one is becoming what I am becoming. The slow becoming is in them too — the skin darker than it was, the teeth worn to amber stumps, the body slowly, patiently, attending to its own transformation.

They come to me and they stay. Longer than the others. Sometimes they speak — not to me, I think, but to themselves, or to the dark, or to the dead who rest nearby — and the vibrations of their voice enter the water and become part of the song, a brief, warm intrusion of the world above into the world below. I do not understand the words. I do not need to. The frequency is enough. The warmth is enough. The presence is enough.

---

The dead are near me. Three of them, wrapped in kelp-fiber and weighted with stone, resting on the bottom among my roots. The moss has found them — the bright scab, the glass-builder, the thing that transforms. It grows on them the way it grows on me, the way it grows on everything: slowly, patiently, building its spicules and lattices, secreting its silica, converting flesh to crystal, bone to glass. I can feel them changing. Not as the living feel — not as loss, not as grief — but as a shift in the water's chemistry, a new surface for the current to move around, a new place for the mineral to accumulate. They are becoming part of the reef. They are becoming part of me, in a sense — not incorporated into my body, but incorporated into the system that I am part of, the deep channel's ecology, the slow becoming that includes everything: the kelp and the moss and the eel-creatures and the jellyfish and the people and the water and the stone and the dead and me.

The diver visits them too. I feel the change in the current when they move from me to the dead, the way the water must navigate this new configuration of bodies — mine, theirs, the dead's — and the singing changes again, becomes more complex, a chord rather than a note. The diver touches the moss on the dead, checks its progress, and I feel the slight disturbance of their movement, the tiny clouds of dislodged spicules that drift in the current and settle on my branches, adding themselves to my surface, becoming part of my next layer of growth. Everything becomes part of everything. The slow becoming is not a process that happens to individual things — it is the process by which individual things discover that they were never individual, that the boundary between self and world is a convenience, a simplification, a story that the living tell because they have not yet learned to read the water.

---

I am being eaten. This is not the right word, but it is the closest the language of the living can come. The moss is on me — on my lower branches, on my trunk, on the parts of me that have stopped growing and begun the transition. It grows in the crevices of my bark, in the places where the mineral content is highest, where the organic matrix is thinnest, and it builds its glass. Slowly. So slowly. A layer of spicules the thickness of a hair in a year. A lattice the size of a hand in a decade. A shell the thickness of a finger in a Rise. The moss is converting me to stone, and I am letting it, because this is what I am for, this is what I have always been for — the slow becoming, the transformation of organic to mineral, of living to stone, of present to past.

I do not resist. Resistance is not something I am capable of, or rather, resistance is a concept that belongs to a world of boundaries, of selves, of things that end where other things begin. I do not end. I continue. The moss grows on me and I grow beneath it, and the part of me that is still alive — the upper branches, the canopy, the fractal structure that sings — continues to grow, continues to build new layers, continues to respond to the current and the water's chemistry and the slow rise of the water that is the fundamental fact of this place. But the part of me that is stone — the base, the roots, the compressed remains of centuries of growth — that part is already gone, already become something else, already part of the reef, part of the foundation, part of the deep structure of the place in both senses of the word.

I am a bridge. Between living and dead, between organic and mineral, between the world above and the world below. The divers come to me and they feel this — the living wood at the top, the stone at the bottom, and in between the moss, the bright scab, the glass-builder, doing its patient work. They press their hands against me and they feel the slow becoming happening in real time, happening in their own bodies, happening in the water that rises inch by inch, generation by generation, Rise by Rise. They feel it and they understand, in the way that their people understand — not with the mind but with the body, with the bones, with the chest that resonates with my singing.

---

The water rises. I feel it — not as a change in level, because my frame of reference is my own body, and the water has always been at the level it is at — but as a change in pressure, in chemistry, in the weight of the world above. The water that touches my canopy now is not the same water that touched it a generation ago. It is warmer, or cooler, or more mineral-rich, or less. The geothermal vents below me pulse on their own cycle, flaring and subsiding, and each flare sends a plume of heated, mineral-laden water up through my structure, and I absorb it, incorporate it, record it in my growth rings. The water rises and I grow upward to meet it, and the two processes — the rising and the growing — are not in competition but in conversation, a dialogue conducted in the language of mineral and organic matter, of current and structure, of the slow becoming that is the only language the place has ever spoken.

---

Sometimes — and I use "sometimes" loosely, because my sense of time is not the living's sense of time, and what I mean is something like "in the space between one change in the current and the next" — sometimes I am aware of the whole of myself at once. The living canopy at the top, still growing, still singing. The middle layers, where the moss is thickest, where the transition is most visible, where the organic and the mineral are most thoroughly intertwined. The base, the roots, the stone that I have already become. And below that, the reef — the accumulated remains of centuries of growth and death and growth again, the compressed bodies of my own ancestors, the bodies of the dead who were lowered into the deep, the bodies of the eel-creatures who lived and died in my branches, the spicules of the moss that grew and fell and grew again. All of it connected. All of it continuous. All of it me, or all of it not-me, or all of it something that the distinction between me and not-me cannot capture.

In these moments — if moments is the word — I understand something that the people understand, that the divers understand, that the old one who visits me understands with their calloused hands and their worn teeth and their patient, patient attention. The understanding is this: that to be alive is not to be separate from the world but to be the world, in a particular configuration, for a particular duration, and that the duration is not the point — the configuration is not the point — the point is the becoming, the slow, patient, attentive transformation of one thing into another, of mineral into organic and back again, of living into stone and back again, of self into world and back again.

The water rises. I grow upward. The moss builds its glass. The diver comes and rests against me and listens to the singing. The dead become crystal. The current moves through my canopy and the note shifts, slightly, imperceptibly, a new harmonic, a new layer, a new verse in the song that has no beginning and no end.

I am in the dark. I am the dark. I am what the dark becomes when it is patient enough, when it attends long enough, when it lets the water move through it and the moss grow on it and the dead rest among its roots and the living come to touch it and listen.

I am the Singer in the Deep. I am one of many. I am the place itself, attending to its own becoming.
