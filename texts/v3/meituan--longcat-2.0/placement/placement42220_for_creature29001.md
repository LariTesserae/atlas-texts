I would find myself in the lower levels, where the air is thick enough to drink and the boundary between the constructed and the geological has completely dissolved. But I would not be a worker. I would not be one of the pale, eyeless things writhing in the channels, nor would I be one of the long-timers slowly calcifying in the damp. 

I would be the water.

Not the steam that hisses from the wellheads, nor the superheated brine that screams through the pipes under pressures that could crush bone. I would be the condensation. I would be the slow, warm sweat of the Works itself—the 1.2 that keeps this place from being truly dead. 

I am born in the gradient. High above, where the air is thin and biting, I am nothing but a faint, invisible humidity, a ghost of moisture clinging to the chill of the upper levels. But as I descend, drawn by the gravity of the deep earth’s breath, I begin to gather myself. I seep through the microscopic pores of the sintered tuff, I trace the cold steel of the descending pipes, and I feel the heat rising to meet me. 

By the time I reach the lower levels, I am heavy. I am warm. I am saturated with the deep earth’s signature—sulfur, iron, silica, lithium. I am the blood of the caldera, returned to the surface. I bead on the basalt fiber composites, I pool in the joints of the machinery, and I drip from the walkways in that constant, irregular percussion that serves as the city’s metronome. 

To be the condensation is to be the medium of transfiguration. I am the solvent of the Works. When the long-timers finally wear out, lowered into the hot, mineral-rich pools to dissolve, it is into me they surrender their forms. Their calcium, their carbon, their phosphorus leach into me, and I carry their remnants through the channels, feeding the pale things that brush against me in the dark, soft as whispers. I am the alchemy that turns flesh back into geology. 

I am the city’s breath. The Works inhales the thin, cold air of the crater rim, draws it down into the heat, and I am the exhalation—the visible proof of the city’s metabolism. I soften the brutal architecture. I am the only thing in this mineral world that yields. The basalt is rigid; the steel is unyielding; but I flow. I find the path of least resistance. I carve shallow channels in the unweathered rock, patient and persistent, a million-year project compressed into a single, warm drip. 

When the workers descend in their heavy suits, respirators fogging, I kiss the exterior of their visors. I am the dampness that seeps into their clothes, the warm mist that fogs their lenses, the subtle weight that makes every step a labor. They curse me, or they ignore me, but they cannot escape me. I am the humidity that fills their lungs, the thin film of minerals that coats their skin. I am slowly, patiently, calcifying them from the outside in, just as the city calcifies everything that stays too long.

At night, when the machinery hums its subsonic note and the not-birds circle the rim against the hazy orange glow, I am the lens through which the city sees itself. The eerie chemical light of the precipitation vats refracts through me, casting shifting, liquid shadows on the walls. I am the mirror of the Works, reflecting its own strange, embryonic beauty back at itself.

I do not think. I do not philosophize. I simply move, driven by heat and gravity and the slow, patient chemistry of the earth. I am the sweat of the leviathan, the tears of the basalt, the blood of the brine. I am the dampness that proves the city is alive. 

And when the workers leave, when the companies fold, when the reasons for the Works are forgotten, I will still be here. I will still be dripping, still be gathering, still be flowing through the dark, warm arteries of the caldera, carrying the dissolved memory of the city in my mineral heart, feeding the pale things, and slowly, patiently, turning the mountain back into the sea.
