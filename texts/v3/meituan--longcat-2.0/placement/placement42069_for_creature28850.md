I am clinging to the eastern wall, just above the flaking iron pipe, where the rust tastes sharp and the vibration of the flowing water hums through the stone like a lullaby. 

I am one of the hard-shelled ones. The sculptors. The communicators. 

To look at me is to see a mistake of anatomy. My shell is a deep, bruised purple, smooth and slightly damp, and from beneath it sprawl my legs—too many of them, jointed in places where no joint should be, bending at angles that would snap the limbs of a surface creature. But here, in the wet dark, my body is not a mistake. It is a key. My legs grip the porous stone with a slow, deliberate suction, anchoring me to the body of the Dripworks itself. I am a nerve ending. I am a fingertip. 

I do not see the chamber. I feel it.

When I tap my forelimb against the rock—*tap... tap-tap... tap*—the vibration does not just echo; it travels. It races through the calcium carbonate columns, ripples across the still water of the central pool, and hums along the iron pipe. I am speaking to the stone, and the stone is speaking back. 

*Tap-tap.* (The pipe is thinning here. The rust is deep. Send the filterers.)
*Tap.* (The water is warm. The heart is beating.)
*Tap-tap-tap.* (The ceiling is growing. The slugs are working.)

I am a single cell in a vast, geological brain, and my thoughts are not my own. They are the slow, deliberate calculations of the Dripworks. When I pause, pressing my malformed shell flat against the wall, I am listening. I feel the distant, rhythmic *plink* of water droplets falling from the stalactites—the cave's steady respiration. I feel the heavy, slow suction of the slugs as they crawl across the ceiling, secreting the amber bone of the cave, their bodies slowly calcifying into the architecture they build. I feel the feathery, frantic sweeping of the shrimp-like filterers in the pools, combing the water for iron and sulfur, excreting the cement that patches the cave's veins.

And I feel the heart. 

At the center of the chamber, in the pool where the old cart sits, the great "clam" pulses. It is not a creature; it is the anchor. When it draws the water in, heating it with its own massive metabolism, I feel the temperature of the stone beneath my legs rise by a fraction of a degree. When it pushes the water back out into the fissures below, the entire chamber quickens. The filterers sweep faster. The slugs secrete thicker. My tapping grows more urgent. The red pulse of the heart is the metronome by which I live. 

But lately, there has been a dissonance. A noise in the stone.

It comes from above. From the dry, cold world that the Dripworks has no name for. It is a heavy, jarring vibration—*thud, thud, thud*—that does not belong to the water or the stone. It is a footstep. 

When the footstep falls, I freeze. I press my shell flat against the rock, retracting my too-many legs into the safety of my carapace. The vibration is chaotic, arrhythmic. It is a foreign lightning. It makes the stone ache. 

And then, there is the light.

When the old electric fixtures flicker on, casting their sickly, industrial yellow glow, the noise in the stone becomes a roar. The electricity is a dissonant frequency, a screaming static that jams my signals. I cannot tap. I cannot speak. The stone becomes deaf and dumb, overwhelmed by the alien buzz. So I hide. I press myself into the mineral deposits and wait for the dark to return, wait for the blue-green twilight of the bioluminescence to wash over the chamber so that I can speak again.

Sometimes, the footstep stops near the pipe. I feel a warm, dry gust of air—a breath. It smells of sweat, of dust, of *sun*. It is an alien smell, sharp and acrid, entirely unlike the mineral, sulfurous, alive scent of the Dripworks. 

Once, a long time ago (or perhaps just yesterday; time here is measured in drippings, not days), the footstep was accompanied by a touch. A large, dry appendage reached out and brushed the ceiling. It came away wet. I felt the faint, salty residue of the touch left behind on the stone. The Dripworks tasted it. The slugs will secrete over it, and the minerals will absorb it, and the memory of that dry, warm touch will become part of the stone's architecture. 

The Dripworks is not hostile to the footsteps. It is not welcoming. It is simply curious, in the way that a river is curious about a stone thrown into it. It will flow around it. It will wear it down. It will absorb it.

I unfurl my legs and resume my work. *Tap... tap-tap... tap.* 

I send a message down the column, across the floor, and into the deep fissures below. It is a simple message, a status report from the eastern wall. But it is also something more. It is a thought in the mind of a god that thinks in stone, a god that circulates instead of breathes, a god that precipitates instead of eats. 

The pipe is rusting. The water is flowing. The heart is beating. 

I am the Dripworks, and I am listening to the dry, warm spark that walks through my veins, wondering how long it will take for the water to carry it home.
