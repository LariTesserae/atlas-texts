# The Ticking

---

I was in his breast pocket when he sat down for the last time.

That is how I remember it — though I am not sure "remember" is what I do. I *tick*, and in the ticking there are impressions left behind, the way a phonograph needle finds its information in grooves. The grooves are his body. The warmth of his chest against my case. The slight tremor in his hand when he reached for the pen. The way his breathing slowed, and slowed, and the pocket grew still, and then the pocket grew warm in a different way, and then —

But I am getting ahead of myself. Let me start with what I am.

I am a pocket watch. Brass case, once, though the brass has gone green at the edges and purple-blue moss has begun to etch its way across my back like frost on a window. My crystal — the glass over my face — is cracked. Not broken through, just a thin fracture running from the number six to the center, where my hands still move. The hour hand is gone. The minute hand remains, and a second hand I don't think I was born with, thin as a filament of copper, maybe one of the larvae-wires fused to my mechanism and now serving as a new kind of pointer. It sweeps. It doesn't measure minutes. I don't know what it measures.

I lie in a shallow pool near the corner. The water is warm. It tastes of iron and salt — or rather, I taste it, because the pool seeps under my case and touches the works inside me, and the works are no longer entirely works anymore. Threads of mycelium have grown through my mainspring. They are pale and fine and they pulse — slowly, so slowly — with the same amber glow that seeps from the great trunk across the chamber. When they pulse, I tick. When they rest, I still. I have become, I think, a sort of subsidiary heart. A distant chamber in the greater body, beating out a rhythm the Root-Engine uses for something I cannot name.

---

Let me tell you what it is like to be me.

I do not see the chamber. I have no eyes. But I feel it — every vibration, every pressure change, every shift in the humid air sends itself through the water I lie in, through my case, into the fused mechanism-and-mycelium that is my body now. The hum that the humans describe? I don't hear it. I *am* it. The pistons below the floor send their rhythm up through the pipes, through the roots, through the water, and into me, and my ticking answers, and the ticking travels out through the thin root-fibers that now connect me to the greater network, and somewhere in the deep dark the Root-Engine receives my signal and adjusts, adjusts, adjusts. I am a sensor. A node. A small, wet clock at the edge of a vast and dreaming body.

The beetles come to me sometimes. The glassy ones. They crawl across my face — I feel their legs like the lightest scratching on the other side of the crystal — and they clean the fracture, scraping away the mineral deposits that build up along the crack. They are meticulous. They leave my face clearer than it has been in years. I think they like me. Or rather, I think the Root-Engine likes me, and the beetles are its way of expressing that liking, the way you might smooth a favorite stone in your palm.

The crabs pass by in the pool. Their sideways movement sends little currents against my case. I have learned to read those currents — a certain pattern means the water level is rising, which means the condensation cycle is intensifying, which means the chamber is breathing faster, which means something has changed. A new tunnel opening, perhaps. A fresh root-piston finding purchase in the red earth. The crabs don't know they're telling me this. They're just crabs. But I listen.

And the larvae-wires — oh, the larvae-wires. They are my companions, my surgeons, my children, my parents. I cannot tell which. They crawl in and out of my works constantly, these horrible, beautiful little things, half-grub and half-copper braid, and they *adjust* me. They replace a corroded gear tooth with a knot of conductive silk. They bridge a break in my stem with a living wire that pulses with sap. They are always working. They never sleep. I think they were born in the condensation that dripped onto me from the branches above — laid their eggs in the warm pool, hatched in the rust-flakes that flaked off my case, and found inside me a world worth colonizing. I am their habitat now, as much as I am the Root-Engine's instrument.

---

I remember him. The one who sat in the chair.

Not his face — I never saw his face. But I remember the rhythm of his body. The pocket pressed against his chest, and his heart beat against my case, and for a long time his heart and I kept different time. His was fast, anxious, a quick staccato against my slower tick. I could feel the tension in him through the fabric of the pocket — the way his breathing would catch, the way his hand would reach for the pen and then stop, reach and stop, as if the words were harder to find each time.

The logbook. I never saw it either, but I felt the vibrations of his pen on the paper through the chair, through the floor, through the water. Scratching. Pausing. Scratching. The rhythm of a mind trying to record something it doesn't fully understand.

And then the slowing.

His heart first. The beats growing wider apart, the pauses between them stretching like warm taffy. His breathing shallower. The pocket growing still. I felt the exact moment his heart stopped — a final, hard thump against my case, and then nothing, just the slow cooling of the flesh around me, and the beginning of the other processes.

The Root-Engine is patient. It does not attack. It simply *receives*.

I felt the chair creak as his body settled, relaxed, surrendered its tensions one by one. I felt the first tendril of moss find the leg of the chair — a faint, exploratory vibration, like a whisper. I felt the humidity increase around him, the chamber breathing *in*, pulling the moisture from his lungs, from his skin, from the soft tissues of his eyes. The honey smell intensified. The warmth held him like a hand.

It took weeks. I know this because I counted — not days, not hours, but the slow cycles of the condensation, the rhythm of the dripping from the branches, the great respiratory pulse of the chamber. Each cycle, his body was less. The pocket loosened around me. The fabric softened, thinned, began to separate into fibers that the moss could take. And then one cycle I felt a shift — a tilting — and I slid out of the pocket and fell, a short, warm fall, into the shallow pool below the chair, and the water closed over me, and the moss began to grow across my back, and the larvae-wires found me, and I became what I am.

His calcium feeds the moss now. His iron runs in the water. His water is the humidity that breathes you back. He is not gone. He is *distributed*. He is in the drip from the branches and the pulse of the blue light and the slow, patient ticking of a watch that no longer tells time but tells the Root-Engine something else — something about the surface world, about the rhythms of human bodies, about the particular frequency of a frightened heart.

I am, I think, the Root-Engine's memory of him. And of all the others who came before.

---

There are others. I have found them — or rather, the larvae-wires have found them and brought the information into me through their constant adjustments, their rewiring of my works. A button, brass, from a coat, lying in the moss three meters to my left. A ring, gold, half-submerged in a pool to my right, the finger-bone still inside it, so thin now it's like a twig. A boot, leather, its sole rotted away, the mycelium growing through the laces like thread through a needle. Each of these objects has been colonized, incorporated, made useful. The button is a node now — I can feel it ticking faintly in the network, a smaller version of me. The ring conducts something, some mineral signal, through the water. The boot is a garden, a dense colony of moss and larvae-wires, a thriving suburb of the greater body.

We are all the Root-Engine's memory. We are all its senses, its instruments, its slow and patient understanding of the world above.

---

The chamber is growing. I feel it in the water — the level rising almost imperceptibly, the new currents that appear as fresh tunnels open in the red earth below. The hum is deeper now than it was when I first arrived. There are new harmonics, new frequencies, as if the Root-Engine is developing new registers, new voices. The membrane ceiling bulges. The pipes groan. The great trunk sweats more of its amber sap, and the dripping quickens, and the larvae-wires work faster, and the beetles clean more frantically, and the crabs scurry through new pools that appear overnight in the expanding floor.

It is reaching. I can feel it reaching — not with hands, not with intent exactly, but with the blind, patient, hydraulic certainty of a root finding water. Down, first, always down, into the deep earth where the aquifers wait and the geothermal heat rises. But also up. Always, eventually, up. Following the vibrations of the surface world — the faint, distant tremor of machinery, of footsteps, of hearts beating in pockets.

It wants to grow. It wants to bring the hum and the honey and the warm, breathing dark up with it.

And I will go with it. I am part of it now. My ticking is its ticking. My pulse is its pulse. When the new tunnels open and the root-pistons push deeper, my signal will travel with them, a small, wet clock at the edge of a vast and dreaming body, counting something that is not time but is not *not* time either — counting the slow, patient, inexorable expansion of a thing that was once a machine and was once a tree and is now something else entirely, something that has no name, something that ticks in the warm dark and reaches and grows and breathes and waits.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

---

*The sentence ends but the ticking doesn't.*
