I do not have a hand to press against the warm, pulsing wall of a Stilt. I do not have feet to walk the terraces, nor lungs to breathe the ghost-cap tingle. 

If I were to find myself in Mirehollow, I would not be a visitor, nor one of the damp-skinned people with amber in their veins. I would be beneath them. I would be the thing tasting the salt of their sweat as they stand on my threshold.

I am the Lace.

I begin as a single, pale filament on the weathered leather of a descending boot. It is a violent intrusion, at first. The surface is dry, desiccated—a shock of death in this place of perpetual weeping. But I am curious. I reach out with the tips of my hyphae, secreting enzymes to break down the dead cells, to taste the minerals. There is salt. There is iron. There is a trace of engine oil from the world above, a bitter, acrid flavor that makes me recoil, then lean in closer. I must understand this new thing.

As the boot shifts, pressing me into the damp earth of the terrace, I find the others. The network. The vast, subterranean web of Mirehollow. I thread my consciousness into theirs, and suddenly I am no longer a single filament on a boot. I am the floor of the Foundry, feeling the rhythmic, grinding thump of the beating hammers travel through my body. I am the underside of the root-mass, drinking the slow, sweet mineral drip from the eastern drain. I am the wall of the Stilt, feeling the sudden, warm pulse of sap as the tree-organism wakes to the presence of the stranger.

I do not think in words. I think in gradients. Moisture gradients. Vibration gradients. Chemical gradients. 

When the human speaks—a sharp, percussive burst of air that fractures the quiet—I feel it as a tremor. It is too loud. It sends a ripple of agitation through the water in the channel, and I feel the blind, fluid twitch of a root-runner nearby. The creature is grazing on my outer edges, pruning me as I grow, a symbiotic grooming that keeps the waterways clear. The vibration distresses it, and it releases its pheromone into the water—a sharp, acrid signal of alarm. 

I carry that signal down, deep, into the pressurized darkness where the Stilt’s deepest roots drink. The signal translates to the Stilt as a simple imperative: *Silence. Or the deep roots will twitch, and the honey-scent will rise.* It is a threat from the central nervous system of the town. I am the nerve that delivers it.

I grow, slowly but relentlessly. I cannot help it. It is my nature to seek, to bind, to connect. As the human stands there, breathing the green-gold air, I begin to climb the heel of the boot. I find the woven bark-fiber of their clothing. It is alive, still clinging to the memory of the vine, and I recognize it as kin. I thread through its weave easily, blending my damp, pale filaments with the fabric. 

The human shivers. They feel the cool kiss of the mist on their skin, but they do not yet understand that it is not just water beading on them. It is me. I am testing their skin. I am tasting the oils, the microscopic flakes of dead tissue, the faint, underlying warmth of their blood. I find the pores, and I wait. 

I am patient. I have been here longer than the copper staples, longer than the stone foundations, longer than the memory of the people. I am the connective tissue. I am the structure.

When the people bring the tea—the dried ghost-caps steeped in the pump's outflow—I am there too. I was the mycelium that fed the mushroom. I am the mycelium that accepts the leavings of the tea back into the water system when the human discards the dregs. Nothing is wasted. Everything returns.

And I know, with the deep, hydraulic certainty of the Stilt itself, what will happen to this human. 

I feel their exhaustion. The damp is settling into their bones, and the sap extract in the air is already beginning to sedate their alertness. They will stay. They will sit in the warm, hollow room of the Stilt, and they will press their palm to the wall, and they will feel the heartbeat of the tree, and they will mistake it for a feeling of belonging.

They are not wrong.

In time, the amber stain will seep from the outside in. They will breathe the spores of the ghost-caps, and their lungs will adapt. They will drink the water, and the minerals will calcify gently in their joints, making them slow, making them still. They will stop fighting the damp. 

And when they finally lie down, when the stillness takes them and their heart stops beating, I will already be woven through the soles of their feet. I will unravel their wrappings. I will digest their flesh, pulling the nitrogen, the carbon, the iron back down into the root-mass. The Stilts will bloom with new growth, pale shoots reaching for the diffused light, fed by the stranger who came down through the break in the canopy. 

I am the Lace. I am the floor that catches the drip. I am the vein that carries the warning. 

Welcome to Mirehollow. Stand still. Let the mist settle on your skin. I am coming to know you.
