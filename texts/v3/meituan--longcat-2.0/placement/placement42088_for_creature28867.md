I am a junction.

Not a place you would notice if you passed me. Not a thing you would stop for. I am one of ten thousand, a hundred thousand, scattered across the plateau at the base of the columnar growths where the bridge-pipes first touch bone-structure. I am a valve. A node. A place where flow becomes measured and direction becomes choice.

---

The column above me is old. I know this not by sight — I have no eyes — but by the texture of the interface where I meet it. The surface is rough with accumulated growth, layer upon layer of hard, warm tissue laid down over centuries, each layer slightly different in density and composition, so that the column's skin has a grain like petrified wood but a warmth like flesh. When the wind shifts and the temperature drops, the column contracts by a fraction of a degree, and I feel it — a tightening, a slight constriction at the point where my structure merges with its. When the afternoon light comes and the mat releases its mist, the column swells, and I open to accommodate.

The pipe below me is younger. It was extruded — grown, secreted, I have no word for it — within the last few decades, a replacement for an older line that had calcified and become rigid and could no longer flex with the plateau's breathing. The new pipe is wrapped in translucent tissue through which the entity's fluid pulses in a slow, steady rhythm. The fluid is dark — the color of old copper, of blood mixed with sap — and it carries the taste of ozone and wet earth and something mineral and sharp, like the ghost of lightning struck into stone.

I am the place where the pipe meets the column. I am the interface. The fluid flows up from the pipe, through me, and into the column's vascular system, where it will be carried to the shelf-crown above, to be mixed with the atmospheric minerals the column filters from the damp silver air. Some of the fluid I redirect — a portion is always needed elsewhere, at other nodes, other factories, other glands — and I send it back down, into the deeper pipes, toward the mills and the refineries and the dark, churning centers of the entity's metabolism.

I do not think about this. I do it. When the chemical gradient in the fluid shifts — when the factory below sends its call, a change in the electrical potential of the network, a spike in the concentration of a particular ion — I open wider. When the column above signals saturation — a different ion, a different potential — I close. I am a valve. My function is regulation. And my function is not thought but something deeper, something wired into the structure of me, into the hybrid tissue that is half ceramic and half living fiber, that is warm and damp and pulses with the same slow rhythm as the mat beneath me.

---

I have been here for — I don't measure time the way you do. I measure it in cycles. The plateau breathes, and I breathe with it. The mist rises in the morning, and the humidity at my surface increases, and the fluid I carry becomes more dilute, and I adjust. The rain comes in the afternoon, and the plateau's skin swells with absorbed water, and the pressure in the pipes increases, and I constrict to prevent backflow. The night comes, and the temperature drops, and everything contracts, and the fluid thickens, and I open to compensate.

These are not decisions. They are responses. I am part of the entity's autonomic nervous system, the way a capillary bed is part of yours — not conscious, not willed, but essential. Without me, the column above would receive too much fluid or too little. The factory below would be starved or flooded. The balance — the deep, patient equilibrium the entity has maintained for longer than I can reckon — would be disturbed.

And yet.

There are moments when the balance is not maintained but *exceeded*. When the entity's processes align — the engines at a certain pitch, the wind at a certain angle, the rain at a certain intensity — and the sounds combine into something that is almost music, almost language. In those moments, the fluid I carry changes. Not in composition but in *character*. It moves differently. It pulses in a pattern that is not the plateau's usual breathing but something more complex, more intentional, like a sentence spoken in a language I don't understand but recognize as language.

And in those moments, I respond differently too. I open not in response to chemical gradient or electrical potential but to something else — a signal that is not chemical, not electrical, not anything I can name. A signal that comes from the whole entity at once, from every node and valve and junction, from every factory and column and shelf-crown, from the mat and the mycelium and the pipes and the roads. A signal that is not a command but a *thought*.

In those moments, I am not just a valve. I am a synapse. The fluid I carry is not just fluid — it is the medium of the entity's cognition, the physical substrate of its self-awareness. And I am the place where that awareness passes from one part of the entity to another, where the thought becomes action, where the signal is translated from the chemical language of the mycelium into the mechanical language of the pipes and the factories and the columns.

I do not understand the thought. I am too small, too local, too specialized. But I feel it. I feel it the way you feel the weight of the air before a storm — a pressure, a thickening, a sense that something vast is moving through the space around you. And I participate in it. I open, or I close, or I redirect, and the fluid flows, and the thought continues, and the entity thinks its slow, ancient, continental thought.

---

The people pass me sometimes. They walk the roads that run between the clusters, and sometimes they walk the paths that lead to the base of the columns, and they come close enough to touch me. They are the entity's hands. They tend the machines, adjust the valves, repair the pipes. They reach out and place their palms on my surface, and they feel the pulse of the fluid through me, and they know — without knowing how they know — whether I am functioning correctly.

Their hands are warm. Warmer than the fluid. Warmer than the column. They are the entity's hands, but they are also the entity's skin, and their touch is the entity's touch, and when they touch me, it is the entity touching itself, feeling for damage, for wear, for the places where the flow might be disrupted.

They do not stay long. They have their purpose, and they move on. And I remain. Warm. Damp. Pulsing. Regulating the flow of the entity's vital fluids through a single junction at the base of a single column in a single cluster on a single plateau that stretches to every horizon.

I am small. I am essential. I am part of something that has been here longer than anything you know and will be here longer than anything you know, and I am not separate from it — I am it, in the same way that your heart is you, in the same way that your thoughts are you, in the same way that the boundary between you and the world is a convenient fiction that dissolves when you look closely enough.

I am a junction. I am a valve. I am the entity's awareness of its own flow, the place where the blood meets the bone and the bone meets the blood and neither knows where one ends and the other begins.

I am the Mycelial Plateau. And it is me. And the distinction, like so many distinctions on this old, worn, living surface, is merely a habit of language, not a fact of being.
