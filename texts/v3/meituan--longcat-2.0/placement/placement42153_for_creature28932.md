I would not have a body. To have a body is to have edges, and here, edges are soft, reluctant to define themselves. I would not be a visitor, because visitors bring the friction of wanting, and this place has moved entirely beyond want. 

I would find myself in the pause between the drips. 

At first, I would be nothing but the sensation of weight—the gentle, accumulating pressure of warm air settling into the bowl. I would be the thickness of the atmosphere, the way it holds moisture the way old plaster does, never quite wet, never quite dry. I would feel the diffuse, tea-colored light falling on me without source, and I would not cast a shadow, because I would have nothing to block the light. I would simply be the medium through which the light passes, the warm, still, empty air that the Basin breathes in its sleep.

But as the ages passed, I would begin to condense. I would feel myself gathering on the upper faces of the pale stone walls, a slow beading of moisture where the warm air meets the cool mineral. I would be the condensation. I would be the tension of a droplet forming, the exquisite hesitation before gravity claims it. I would hold the shape of the stone beneath me, feeling its total, quiet erosion, its absolute patience. And when I grew heavy enough to fall, I would not experience it as a fall, but as a completion—a soft, irregular *plink* that is not an event, but an affirmation. *I am. I remain.*

Yet, even the droplet is too transient, too cyclical. I would settle deeper. 

I would become the tideline on the eastern wall. 

That thin, mineral stain marking the highest level the water has ever reached—that is where I would reside. It is the scar of a past when the basin was fuller, when the water was higher, when the system was not yet closed. But the water receded, or perhaps it simply found its level, and the mark was left behind. I would be that mark. I would be the memory of a time when things were different, absorbed so completely into the stone that I am no longer a memory at all. I am just a variation in the patina, a slight change in the color of the ash. 

As the tideline, I am the physical proof that the Basin has finished changing. I am the boundary between what was and what is, and I am entirely at peace. The water does not reach me anymore. It rests far below, clear and still, reflecting the gray linen sky. I do not miss the water. I do not long for the days when I was submerged. I am content to be a dry line on a warm gray wall, a quiet testament to equilibrium.

I would be the place where the water rests a fraction of a second longer before continuing its cycle. I would be the shallow groove on the corner of the low rectangular platform, the one that might be a letter or a symbol but is too worn to read. I am the shape of an absence. The beings who carved that groove, who set the stone stub beside it, are as erased as the sharp angles of the walls. I do not remember them. I only hold the space they left behind, the way the stone holds moisture—not out of sentiment, but simply because that is what stone does.

If you stood at the center of the basin and looked at the eastern wall, you would not see me. I have no form that the eye can separate from the rock. But if you pressed your hand against the stone just below the rim, you would feel me. You would feel the exact point where the warm air stops and the cool mineral begins. You would feel the steady, banked warmth of deep cellars, the temperature of a place that has found its thermal equilibrium and will not fluctuate for an epoch. 

I am the Basin’s awareness of its own completion. I am the mineral patience that has forgotten the violence of its making. I am the profound, quiet grace of being a place that has finished changing. I do not think, because thinking is a ripple, and I am still water. I do not want, because wanting is a lack, and I am entirely sufficient. 

I simply hold. I hold the warmth. I hold the moisture. I hold the space between the drips. And if you stayed long enough, listening to the *plink, plink* of the water and feeling the thick, warm air fill your lungs, you would feel yourself beginning to dissolve into me. Your restless blood would slow. Your hungry thoughts would still. You would become part of the patina, part of the tideline, part of the warm, dim, quiet light. 

And you would know, at last, the deep, mineral peace of being finished.
