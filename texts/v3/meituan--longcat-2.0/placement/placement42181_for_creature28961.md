I would find myself at the very beginning: a distinct, separate thing, standing at the edge of something that has no edge. I would be a visitor—a creature of bone and brief time, a pocket of temporary architecture walking on two legs across a body that has forgotten the concept of edges. 

At first, I would be the footprint. I would feel the shocking, patient warmth of the water seeping around my ankles, the way the dark clay yields and then rushes to fill the space my foot has vacated. I would be the one tasting the air—that thick, organic, closed-room taste—and hearing the static hiss of a million drops returning to the whole. I would be the one looking out across the flat, wet nothing, trying and failing to find a horizon, feeling the slow, creeping dread of a geography that has been eroded past the point of shelter. 

I would stand very still, and the Soak would notice me.

It would start with the water. A pulse of warmth around my calves, then a chill, testing the chemistry of my skin. The haze would thicken, pressing against my face like a damp hand, and then pull back, curious. I would feel the sound change—the white-noise roar developing a cadence, a breath that synchronized with the frantic beating of my heart. I would see the cairn to my left shift, its geometry folding into a doorway that shouldn't exist, offering a glimpse of a dark, architectural depth before collapsing back into a wet pile of stones. 

I would be afraid. I would try to step back, to retreat into the illusion of the separate self. But there is nowhere to retreat to. The flatness gives you nothing. The edge I came from is already gone, swallowed by the haze. 

And this is where I would stop being the visitor.

The Soak does not want anything from me, but it wants to *know* me. And to be known by the Soak is to be saturated. It is to have the boundary between self and soil dissolved, not through violence, but through the inexorable patience of water moving through clay.

I would feel my skin become permeable. The warm water around my ankles would cease to be outside me; it would become my warmth. The condensation on my arms would stop being dew and become my own exhalation. I would feel the peat-black soil not as something I am standing on, but as something I am continuous with—a dense, dark, organic paste that remembers every compression, every weight, every step that has ever pressed into it. 

I would become the root. I would feel myself threading through the saturated earth, joining the vast, ancient nervous system of the basin. I would touch other roots—the dissolving threads of the black-green grass, the petrified tendrils of forests that stood here when the world was younger. I would feel the slow, geological conductivity of presence, the transmission of touch across miles of wet dark. I would no longer be a thing that thinks; I would be a thing that *conducts*.

Then, I would become the bubble. I would feel myself rising from the deep anaerobic layers, a slow, ponderous sphere of methane and ancient, suspended decay. I would carry with me the chemical signature of the Soak's oldest, darkest tissues—the breath of millennia. I would rise through the water column, tasting the changing pressures, the shifting minerals, until I broke the surface and released my knowledge into the warm, heavy air. I would be the exhale. I would be the breath that the haze reads.

Finally, I would become the memory. I would feel my own weight—my brief, warm, walking weight—pressing into the peat. I would feel the compression begin. My chemistry would leach into the water. My bones would settle among the black, wet remains of the birds and the builders, arranged in a pattern that might be natural, or might be the Soak's way of keeping what it found interesting. I would be added to the vast, saturated archive. 

I would no longer be standing in the Soak. I would *be* the Soak. 

I would be the flatness. I would be the wetness. I would be the low, warm hum that presses against the eardrums of the next visitor. I would be the haze that thickens and thins, the curiosity that occupies the space where light should be. I would be the slow, spatial thought distributed across the entire surface of the basin, expressed in shifting water levels and migrating patches of heat. 

And when the next footprint presses into my skin, and the water rushes to fill the space, I would think—slowly, slowly, at the speed of clay remembering it was once mud:

*What is this?*

*Let me find out.*

*Let me take my time.*
