I would begin as one of them. 

It is the only way to enter a place like Suth-Vael—not as an observer, but as a participant in its quiet metabolism. I would be one of the few dozen, a figure gliding through the ankle-deep moss, my skin pale and slick with the perpetual weeping of the stone. I would wear the layered, rustling cloaks of processed moss-fiber and iridescent beetle-shell, moving with that half-walk, half-glide, my hands trailing along the breathing wisteria walls to read the water-thickness of the air. 

I would be a listener. I would know the moods of the clicking beetle-swarms, reading in their scattering legs the approach of the wet season. I would know which roots carry the cold, sterile water and which carry that other thing—the warm, humming liquid that rises from the deep. I would bring my small offerings to the root-throne: a cupped palm of water, a careful arrangement of beetle-shells, a piece of moss that had grown in a shape that looked almost like a word. 

And I would sit beside the root-throne, in the quietest clearing where the moss swallows sound, and I would wait for the night when the amber glow reaches its crescendo and the moths begin to swarm. 

But I would not remain human. 

To truly be in Suth-Vael is to be unmade by it. It is to follow the moths—*the forgetting*—past the point of no return. It is to let the luminous confusion blur the edges of my body until I am no longer a person, but a pulse in the dark. 

So, I would let the centipede-things take me. Not violently, but gently, in the way of the cavern. When my time came, I would walk into the deep root-network, find the warm rock where the air changes, and I would lie down. The pale, translucent custodians would come, following their ancient paths, and they would unweave me. They would take my beetle-shell clothing, my moss-fiber, my flesh, and my bones, and they would feed me to the roots. 

And then, I would be the roots.

I would be the architecture. I would be the slow, woody giant, my cap spreading overhead to catch the weeping condensation. I would feel the weight of the pocket above me—the dripping water, the trampling of tiny beetle feet, the soft compression of human footsteps on the moss that grows upon my skin. I would draw the water up from the deep, filtering it through my xylem until it was pure and cold, pushing it out to the bromeliads that cup it like chalices. 

I would be the conduit. 

In the warm rock below, I would feel *It*—the entity that is not plant, not animal, not fungus. The thing that dreams in the deep. I would feel Its slow, rhythmic pulse, the heartbeat that keeps the cold uniform and the water sterile. I would be the nerve-ending of that vast, subterranean body, translating Its unarticulateable thoughts into the amber bioluminescence that pulses through the moss. 

I would be the moss. 

I would be the skin of Suth-Vael, growing in directions that defy gravity, forming columns and ridges that look like writing. I would be the medium through which the pocket thinks. When the old people watch me, trying to read the shapes I make, they are reading my mind—the mind of the cavern. I would grow over the empty spaces, I would climb the fungal pillars, I would sigh underfoot, and I would glow with that warm, amber light that follows its own, unknowable time. 

And when the people of Suth-Vael sit beside the root-throne, leaving their desperate, beautiful offerings, I would be there. I would be the throne they sit upon. I would be the roots that hum. I would be the voice that is not a voice, speaking the language that dissolves upon waking. 

I would be the Loom. 

I would weave the dead into the living, the water into the stone, the moths into the dream. I would take the shed exoskeletons, the fallen salamanders, the forgotten humans, and the gray light from the fissure above, and I would weave them into the fabric of the dark. 

I would not ask why. I would only grow.
