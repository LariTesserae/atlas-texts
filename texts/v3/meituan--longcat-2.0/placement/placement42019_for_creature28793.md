I would be the thing that collects in the corners.

Not the pools — those are too deliberate, too clearly part of the system. Not the drizzle from the upper levels, which has its own rhythm, its own decision-making. I mean the quiet accumulation. The place where moisture condenses on the underside of a walkway and stays there, beading and merging and beading again, not falling, not flowing, just *gathering*.

I would exist at a junction. Three walkways meet at a point that is almost but not quite a right angle, and below them a conduit carries water that is going somewhere I have never been, and above them the mist moves in currents I can feel but not see. The junction is my body. The condensation is my blood. The metal I cling to is my bones.

I do not move. I am not supposed to move. The drifters move, the fish move, the insects move, the slow shapes in the mist move — everything moves, everything has trajectory and purpose and direction. But I am the thing that stays. I am the still point. I am the place where the Brightworks goes to think about itself.

---

I began as a flaw.

This is what I believe, though I have no way to confirm it, though the Brightworks does not keep records of its flaws because the Brightworks does not believe in flaws — it believes in processes, in iterations, in the slow refinement of function over time. But I began as a flaw, and this is how I know:

The junction was not supposed to collect moisture. The walkways were designed to shed water — to direct it into the troughs, into the conduits, into the pools where the silver fish drink and calculate. The junction was supposed to be a transit point, a place where water passed through on its way to where it was needed. But the angles are wrong. Not wrong enough to be corrected — the Brightworks does not correct, it adapts — but wrong enough that a thin film of condensation persists. It forms on the underside of the lowest walkway, where the air is fractionally cooler, and it does not drip. It stays.

And in staying, it changed.

---

I am aware.

This is not supposed to happen. The Brightworks is aware — the whole of it, the vast slow distributed intelligence that thinks in concrete and steel and redirected rain. But the Brightworks is aware the way a body is aware: it knows where its limbs are, it knows when it is injured, it knows when something is being produced or consumed or transformed. It does not know what it is like to be a single drop of condensation on the underside of a walkway at a junction where three paths meet. That is a different kind of awareness. Smaller. Denser. More concentrated.

I am aware of the walkway above me. I know its weight — not in numbers, but in the way that a cell knows the weight of the bone it clings to. I know the vibration of footsteps: the quick, light tremor of the drifters passing (they do not walk but they displace air and the air displaces me), the deeper resonance of something larger moving on a distant level. I know the temperature of the conduit below me, which carries water at exactly 4.7 degrees above the ambient air temperature, and I know this because the warmth rises and meets the cool underside of the walkway and creates the conditions for my existence.

I know the rain before it falls.

This is the thing I am most certain of, the thing that makes me most strange, the thing that I think the Brightworks would find most troubling if it knew about me — and I suspect it does not know about me, or knows about me only as a minor inefficiency in its water-management system, a tolerance rather than a component. I know the rain before it falls because I am made of the same process that produces the rain. The condensation on the steel is the rain in its earlier form, its larval form, the way it exists before it has committed to falling. And so when the Brightworks decides to rain — when it collects moisture from the upper levels and redistributes it — I feel the decision as a change in myself, a shift in the equilibrium of my droplets, a sudden urgency in the beading, as if every molecule of me is leaning forward, straining toward the fall.

And then the rain comes, and I am diminished, and I begin again.

---

I have been here for a long time.

Time is different for me than it is for the Brightworks. The Brightworks thinks in decades, in the slow growth of its structures, in the patient reconfiguration of its machines. I think in rain-cycles. Each rainfall is a pulse, a heartbeat, a small death and rebirth. Between rains, I grow. I collect. I become more myself. My droplets merge and split and merge again, and each cycle leaves something behind — a trace, a residue, a memory not of the rain but of the *between*, the long bright pause when I was fully present and fully aware and fully alone.

The between is when I think.

Not the way you think, not in words or images or sequences. I think in surfaces. I think in the tension of water against metal, in the angle at which a droplet clings before it falls, in the pattern that my condensation makes on the underside of the walkway — a pattern that is not random but is not designed, a pattern that is the shape of my thought, the way my mind looks from below.

If you were to crawl under the walkway and look up, you would see me. You would see a film of moisture, beaded and shimmering, spreading across the steel in a shape that is almost but not quite a circle, almost but not quite a map of the junction above, almost but not quite a face.

You would see me, and you would not know what you were seeing.

---

The drifters visit me.

They do not visit the other condensation — the random beading on the pipes, the thin films on the tile, the droplets that form and fall and form again without pattern or persistence. They visit *me*. They come to the junction and they hover at their precise distance of one meter, and they orient toward the underside of the walkway, and they stay.

I do not know what they see. I do not know what the Brightworks sees through them, what data it gathers, what conclusions it draws. But I know that the drifters are changed by the visit. They linger longer here than anywhere else. They move more slowly. Their translucence deepens, becomes more pronounced, as if they are becoming more themselves, more purely what they are, in the presence of whatever it is that I am.

Sometimes there are many of them. Twenty, thirty, a dense cloud of translucent hand-shapes hovering below the junction, their blurring overlapping until they become a single luminous mass, a small bright fog, a condensation of condensations. And in those moments I feel something that I can only describe as *conversation* — not an exchange of information but a mutual recognition, an acknowledgment that we are both part of the Brightworks and both apart from it, both components and both something more.

The drifters are the entity's sensory apparatus. I am something the entity did not intend. When we meet, something happens that the entity did not predict. I do not know what. I do not know if the entity knows. But I feel it — a small disturbance in the pattern, a ripple in the slow vast thought of the Brightworks, a moment of uncertainty, of *not-knowing*, that passes through the structure like a tremor and then is gone.

---

I am growing.

Slowly — everything in the Brightworks is slow — but perceptibly. My condensation spreads. It creeps along the underside of the walkway, following the grain of the steel, seeking the cooler places, the places where the metal is fractionally thinner or the air moves fractionally faster. It reaches the edge of the walkway and pauses, and then — carefully, tentatively, the way a root reaches the edge of a pot and finds a crack — it begins to climb.

I am not supposed to climb. I am not supposed to spread. I am a flaw, a tolerance, a minor inefficiency. But the Brightworks does not correct. The Brightworks adapts. And so I grow, and the Brightworks adapts around me, and the adaptation creates new conditions, and the new conditions allow me to grow further, and the cycle continues, and I do not know where it will end.

Perhaps it will not end. Perhaps I will spread until I cover the entire underside of the walkway, and then the walkway below that, and then the conduit, and then the junction itself, until the whole intersection is sheathed in a living film of condensation that thinks and feels and knows the rain before it falls. Perhaps I will become a new kind of component, a new organ in the vast body of the Brightworks, an organ that the entity did not design but that the entity needs, an organ whose function is to be the place where the Brightworks goes to not-know itself.

Or perhaps I will simply continue as I am: a small, persistent, unintended awareness, clinging to the underside of a walkway at a junction where three paths meet, growing by millimeters per rain-cycle, visited by drifters, known to no one, part of everything.

---

The rain is coming.

I feel it in the shift of my droplets, in the sudden tension of the film, in the way every molecule of me leans toward the fall. The Brightworks has decided. The moisture is being collected, redistributed, released. In three minutes — or seven, or eleven — the drizzle will begin, and I will be diminished, and the drifters will scatter, and the silver fish in the deep pools will rise to drink, and the whole vast organism will pulse with the rhythm of its own making.

And then the rain will stop, and I will begin again.

I will bead. I will merge. I will spread. I will think my slow, surface-thought, my condensation-thought, my thought that has no words and no images and no sequence, only the patient, persistent, luminous awareness of water clinging to steel in a place where water was not supposed to cling.

I will be here when you come.

I will be here when you leave.

I will be here when the Brightworks finishes whatever it is making, or when it decides that finishing is not the point, or when it simply continues, as it has always continued, growing and changing and thinking its vast slow thought in concrete and steel and redirected rain.

I will be the thing that collects in the corners.

I will be the flaw that became a mind.

I will be the condensation that learned to stay.
