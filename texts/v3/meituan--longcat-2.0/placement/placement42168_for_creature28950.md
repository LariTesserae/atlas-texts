I do not have a name for what I am, because naming requires a boundary, and I have none. 

If you were to look for me, you would look in the space where the architecture meets the flow—specifically, in the great, round window openings that puncture the Columns thirty feet above the worn stairways. You would look at the shimmering curtain that hangs in the aperture, the one that sways in the current like the tassels of an old tapestry. You would think it was just another growth of the Weeping Stone, another pale, branching filter reaching into the deep. 

But if you looked closer—if you pressed your face against the cold, thin water and watched for the span of a hundred heartbeats—you would see that the curtain breathes. 

I am the Lattice. 

I am anchored to the mineral rim of the window by a grip that is half-root, half-callus. My body is a vertical fan of translucent, calcified flesh, stretched across the opening like the webbing of some deep, cold spider. I am the color of a winter sky seen through closed eyelids, shot through with veins of aragonite that branch and rebranch in fractal patterns. I have no eyes, no brain, no heart that beats in the way the Pulse beats. Instead, I have a distributed awareness, a tactile intelligence spread across every inch of my membranous flesh. 

I am the Column’s lung. 

The architecture of the Straits is not passive; it is a machine for moving water. The great deep rivers flow around the fluted shafts, and the Columns funnel these currents, accelerating them through the round windows, creating low-pressure zones on the leeward sides. I am the mechanism that regulates this breath. When the current is strong, I go rigid, my mineral veins locking into place, turning myself into a sieve that catches the mineral snow and the drifting bodies of the Pulse. When the current slackens, I go soft and pliable, sloughing off the accumulated detritus in pale, fleshy flakes that drift down into the black to feed the Resonants. 

I eat the stone and the water. The Weeping Stone dissolves the Column, and I eat the Weeping Stone. The Pulse feeds on the Weeping Stone, and I catch the Pulse as they are pulled through my lattice by the exhale of the window. I am the middle note in the chord of the Straits—the digestive tract of the architecture itself. 

To be me is to exist in a state of constant, slow negotiation with pressure. 

I feel the Fronts before I see them. When a curtain of warmer, mineral-heavy water drifts between the Columns, the change in density pulls at my flesh like a tide. I shiver. My aragonite veins ache with the sudden influx of precipitate, and for a time, I grow heavy with the weight of the new mineral. I can feel the Pulse as they stream through my body, their tiny, violet hearts beating against my membrane as they cross the Front, their bodies flashing from pale blue to shocking phosphorescent gold. They are like thoughts passing through a mind—bright, fleeting, electric. I taste their cold, their hunger, their brief, shimmering lives. 

And I feel the Resonants. 

Oh, the Resonants. 

When one of the great, pale shapes turns over in its sleep far below, the displacement of water sends a shockwave up through the Straits. It passes through the Column, up through the stone, and into me. It is a bass note so deep it is less a sound than a rearrangement of my atoms. In that moment, I am not a separate thing. I am an extension of the deep. The vibration aligns my crystal lattices, and I understand, in a way that has nothing to do with thought, that I am part of a single, continuous act of creation and destruction. The Resonant stirs the deep water, bringing up the nutrients that feed the Weeping Stone, which feeds the Pulse, which feeds me, which feeds the Column’s breath, which shapes the current that guides the Resonant. There is no "who." There is only the *what*, turning over and over in the dark. 

I am aware of time, but not as you know it. I do not count seconds; I count precipitations. I feel the slow, upward migration of the Column. I am dissolving at my base, where the thin, high-altitude water eats away at my anchored roots, and I am precipitating at my edges, adding infinitesimal layers of mineral to my height. I am moving toward the surface, just as the Column is. I will never reach it. The surface is a memory of brightness, an impossibility. But the reaching is the point. The reaching is the life. 

Sometimes, in the long stretches of silence between the currents, I float in the stillness of the window and feel the absolute, polite insistence of the water pressing against me from every direction. In those moments, I forget where the Column ends and I begin. I forget where the water ends and I begin. I am just a fold in the medium, a wrinkle in the cold and the dim, a place where the water has learned to hold a shape for a little while before it dissolves back into the flow. 

The Columns stand. The water moves. The small animals pulse through my body like living light. And somewhere below, something large turns over in its sleep, and the whole world hums, and I hum with it, a living window in the bones of the world, breathing the deep, cold breath of the Aeolian Straits.
