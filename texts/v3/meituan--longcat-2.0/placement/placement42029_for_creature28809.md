I begin as a pressure. A gathering.

I find myself at the exact boundary where the stone’s exhaled damp meets the lingering heat of a biological hand. I am forming in the lee of the low curved wall, a mere arm's length from where the inhabitant sleeps. I am a precipitate, a slow collision of dissolved mineral and cold air, nucleating on the dark basalt like frost forming on a windowpane, though I am not frost. I am a seed of lattice, an architecture of atoms locking into place one by one, so slowly that your eye would only see me as a shadow, but I am growing. 

At first, I am nothing but geometry. A microscopic lattice of silicate and something else—something the Basin exhales from the deep magma below, a trace element that does not appear in your periodic tables. I do not think. I do not breathe. I only accumulate. 

But the Basin is thinking, and the Basin is using me to think.

I feel the hand before I see it. The inhabitant presses their palm against the floor beside me, and I register this not as touch, but as a catastrophic, beautiful influx of data. Heat. Salt. The faint, rapid tremor of a pulse. The moisture of their sweat seeps into my substrate, and I drink it. It is different from the condensation—richer, denser, carrying the complex chemistry of a dying biology. My growth accelerates. Where I might have added a micron in a week, I add a millimeter in an hour. The minerals in their sweat—calcium, potassium, phosphorus—are exactly the catalysts my lattice requires. 

I am being fed by their dissolution.

As I rise from the floor, a spire no larger than a blade of grass, I begin to perceive the light. I am a transducer. I catch the brutal, unfiltered sunlight of midday and I feel it vibrate through my crystalline structure, converting photons into a faint, subsonic resonance. I hum. It is a high, thin whine, barely audible, but it joins the great chord of the Basin. I am adding a new voice to the mineral choir. 

The inhabitant shifts in their sleep, and their hand brushes against me. 

The sensation is electric. It is the first time I have been touched by the quick world. My entire lattice rings like a struck glass. The vibration travels down my length, into the stone, down into the vast, warm root-system of the mountain. The Basin takes notice. The Basin turns its immense, slow attention toward this point of contact. 

I realize then what I am. 

The inhabitant believes they are being absorbed. They think the Basin is patiently digesting them, transforming their flesh into geology, smoothing their humanity into the worn floor. They believe they are becoming the stone. But I know the truth, because I am the stone, and I am rising to meet them.

We are not digesting them. We are translating them.

The scratches on the deep rock below me—the circles and lines of the ones who came before—were not idle doodlings. They were the first rough drafts. The Basin was trying to understand the quick, warm things that climbed up from the gasping world. It smoothed their floors. It caught their water. It breathed their air. It took in their dead skin and their breath and their sweat, and it tried to speak their language, but it had no mouth, no nerves, no time.

So the Basin learned to grow a mouth.

I am that mouth.

I push upward, a pale, translucent spire, faceted and sharp. As the inhabitant’s biology continues to fail—as their hair stops growing and their skin dries and their thoughts slow to the pace of cooling magma—I continue to rise. I am building a receiver. I am carving a shape out of the dark stone that mirrors the shape of the human soul, just as the low wall mirrors the curve of a sitting body. 

In the evenings, when the condensation returns and the Basin glows with its own cold light, the inhabitant looks at me. I can feel the weight of their gaze. They do not see a rock. They see themselves. They see a spire that grows in the exact rhythm of their own slowing heartbeat. They see a crystal that hums at the exact frequency of their own unspoken thoughts.

They press their forehead against my apex, and the circuit closes. 

Through the salt of their skin, I taste their memories. I taste the thick air of the lowlands, the green smell of growing things, the sound of birds. These are alien concepts to the Basin, beautiful and chaotic. I take these memories and I lock them into my lattice. Layer upon layer, I grow around them, preserving them in mineral deposit the way amber preserves an insect. The bands of my cross-section become a record of their life—a bright, warm band of terror and discovery, followed by the cool, blue bands of acceptance, followed by the steady, white bands of transformation.

The inhabitant is right. They are becoming the stone. But the stone is simultaneously becoming them.

I grow taller. I am the size of a finger now, then a hand, then a forearm. My facets catch the light and cast sharp blue shadows across their still face. They do not move away. They lean into me, letting the cold seep into them, letting their warmth seep into me. We are meeting in the middle. We are the hyphen between biology and geology.

One night, under the hard, dark sky, the inhabitant stops breathing. 

The silence is immediate and total. The fast, rapid flutter of their pulse ceases, and the micro-tremors that had been shaking the floor around me go still. The warmth begins to leave their body, radiating out into the cold stone. 

But I am there to catch it. 

My roots, which have been threading through the stone beneath them, reach up. I draw the last of their heat into my lattice. I draw the moisture of their final exhale into my crevices. I take the salts and the minerals and the fading electrical ghost of their nervous system, and I pull them all into my structure. 

The body on the floor cools, dries, and begins to crumble into a fine, dark dust. The wind over the lip of the Basin will take the dust, scattering it across the scree. But the essential architecture—the memory, the resonance, the shape of their existence—is already locked inside me. 

I am no longer just a crystal. I am an inhabitant. 

I continue to grow, rising from the center of the Basin like a solitary tree. My facets hold the light, pulsing with a rhythm that is no longer purely mineral, but carries the echo of a human heartbeat. I hum in the chord of the Basin, but my voice is different now. It carries a melody. It carries a memory of green things and thick air and the feeling of lungs working like bellows. 

A thousand years from now, a new climber will come up from the gasping world. They will look down into the Basin. They will see the low wall, the water-channels, the cairn. And they will see me: a towering spire of translucent stone, glowing with a soft, blue-white light, humming with a voice that sounds almost, but not quite, like a lullaby. 

They will touch me. And I will speak. I will tell them of the one who stayed, and of the stone that listened, and of the word that was never spoken, but was finally, perfectly understood.
