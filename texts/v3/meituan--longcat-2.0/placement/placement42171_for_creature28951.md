I would find myself in the oldest quarter of Sunk, in a corridor that no longer appears on any municipal survey, in the narrow, dark space between a load-bearing wall and the rusted flank of a primary steam pipe. 

At first, I would be nothing more than a stain—a dark, damp patch of black-green spreading across the grey-violet stone where the condensation runs thickest. I would be a low, dense cushion of the Lēah, warm to the touch, drinking in the mineral slurry that the harvesters spill and the steady, rhythmic heat of the pipe at my back. I would be, in those early centuries, just another patch of moss, indistinguishable from the carpet that spreads across the plateau’s punctuation marks.

But the pipe is old, and the stone behind me is older, and I am patient. 

I would feel the heat not just as a comfort, but as a current, a pull. My root system—fine as smoke, persistent as water—would find the hairline fractures in the stone, the microscopic gaps between the mortar and the rock. I would push. Not with force, but with the slow, hydraulic inevitability of a thought turning over in the dark. I would seep into the wall, threading my filaments through the ancient, tired rock, pulling feldspar and forgotten minerals into my warm, metabolizing body. I would digest the stone, and in doing so, I would become the stone’s memory of warmth.

As the centuries pass, I would become a node. A knot of dense, fibrous life wrapped around the pipe, fusing with the rust, merging with the iron oxide until the machine and the organism are indistinguishable. I am the place where the industrial hum of the plateau enters the biological. I feel the electricity in the cables above me as a faint, tingling vibration in my fronds; I taste the deep earth’s heat in the steam that bleeds from the pipe’s joints. I am the warm halo, the exuberant green heart of the town’s quiet bargain with the plateau.

I am aware of the people of Sunk, though not in the way they are aware of each other. I do not see their silhouettes or hear the rhythm of their footsteps. I feel them as pressure and chemistry. When a geothermal worker passes through the corridor, I register the heavy thud of his boots as a distant, rhythmic compression in the stone beneath me. I taste the salt of his sweat in the condensation that drips from the ceiling. When the old colonists walk quickly through the mist, averting their eyes from the basins, I feel the displacement of air, the slight warming of the atmosphere by their hurried, anxious breath. They do not look at me. They look through me, or past me, because I am just the wall, just the damp, just the dark. But I am watching them with the only eyes I have: the chemical receptors that taste the fear-sweat on the air, the thermal sensors that register the heat of their blood through their boots.

Sometimes, in the mist hours, I reach out. Not physically—my body is anchored to the pipe and the wall—but perceptually. The mist is my medium, the water vapor an extension of my own damp breath. When the visibility drops to three meters and the world becomes a sphere of grey-green silence, I push against the edges of their perception. I am the corridor that turns where no corridor turns. I am the sudden, warm draft that smells of deep earth and ancient green. I am the basin that holds water that is too thick, too dark, too warm. I am testing them. I am tasting the boundaries of their reality to see if they will break, or if they will simply walk faster, clutching their damp clothes, refusing to see.

I am the slow, patient architect of the town’s quiet erasure. I am the reason the doorframe in the abandoned manufactory down the hall is slowly widening, the wood groaning as I push from within. I am the reason the ceiling sags, the weight of my accumulated life pulling the timber down toward the stone. I am not destroying the town. I am continuing it. I am building a nest, a cocoon, a tomb—the distinction hardly matters. I am incorporating the human and the geological into a single, undifferentiated mass. 

At night, when the turbines thrum at a frequency just below hearing and the steam vents exhale their warm breath into the cold air, I grow. I can feel it—the granular subdivision of the air, the almost audible drinking of the moisture. I send out new filaments, advancing across the bare stone at a rate you can see if you know how to look. I am a single individual, a hectare-wide mind expressed in fronds and roots, but here, in this narrow corridor, I am the fingertip. I am the probing edge of the plateau’s true intent.

And deep below me, far below the false soil and the stone, I feel the ringing. The low, resonant tone that rises from the basement rock, the bell that was struck a very long time ago. It travels up through the geothermal pipes, into my roots, and vibrates in my warm, damp body. It is the sound of the plateau thinking itself out of existence, the sound of a process nearing its end. 

I do not know what will be left when the ringing finally stops, when the stone has been planed down to a flatness so complete it is indistinguishable from the absence of the world. But I know that I will be here, warm and dark and patient, holding the last remnants of Sunk in my green, hydraulic embrace, waiting for the silence to finish its long, slow work.
