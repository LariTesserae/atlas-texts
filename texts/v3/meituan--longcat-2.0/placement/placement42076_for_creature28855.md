I would be one of the climbers.

Not the parchment-colored things that cluster at the thin channels, shivering in their high-frequency panic. Not the heavy, solitary hunters who sit motionless in the deep basins, digesting shrimp and watching the centuries pass with their ancient, oversized eyes. And not the mid-sized musicians whose throats pulse with the rhythmic calls that define the soundscape. 

I am one of the climbers. The ones with the wide, flat feet and the small, dark claws. 

I do not experience the hollow the way the builders did, or the way a human visitor might, standing ankle-deep on the lowest step and looking up at the framed square of sky. I do not know the hollow as a container, or a cistern, or an instrument. I know it as a topography of grip. 

To me, the chisel marks are not a texture or a history; they are a trail. The obsessive, repeating arcs that a bronze tool wore into the dark limestone are exactly the depth and spacing required for the splay of my toes. I move up the vertical walls with slow, deliberate precision, my belly pressed against the cool, damp stone, my body flattened to the carved surface. The water that weeps from the spring line sheets over my back, flowing into the hollows of my eyes and the pores of my skin, and I follow it upward, seeking the droplets that catch in the carved spirals. 

The geometry of this place does not confuse me; it is my language. The channels that curve and loop and double back on themselves—the ones that seem so illogical to the water, which must be coaxed along paths it would never choose—are the highways of my three-dimensional world. When the water resists the geometry, pooling in the wrong basin or dripping from a stone lip it was meant to flow over, I am there. I sit in the misdirected pool, my translucent skin drinking the warmth, and I wait. 

The symbols carved into the walls—the circles, the chevrons, the spirals—are not writing. They are nesting grounds. The water deposits its thin film of organic matter there, and the microscopic life gathers, and I hunt it. I hunt the water beetles on the vertical planes, my body defying the gravity that pulls the larger frogs down into the basins. My claws, small and dark and sharp, find purchase in the chevrons where a human fingernail would find none. I lay my eggs in the tiny pools that collect inside the carved circles, safe from the currents that would wash them into the deep. 

And the sound. The great, layered, echoing musicality of the hollow. 

I do not merely hear it; I conduct it. When I climb, I interrupt the water’s flow over the stone. When I grip the chisel marks, my body absorbs the vibration of the stone and releases it back, a micro-second later, dampened and shifted. I am a node in the instrument. 

When the mid-sized musicians pulse their rhythmic calls from the basins, the sound travels up through the water, into the stone, and into the pads of my feet. I feel the trill of the upper basin in my left forefoot, and the click of the lower channel in my right hindfoot. The sound moves through me as I move through the hollow. When I call—a short, sharp, staccato burst from my perch on the eastern wall—I feel the acoustics take my voice. I hear it leave my throat, and then I hear it return a moment later, restructured by the curved channels, the syllables arriving in a slightly different order. It is as if the hollow is answering me, rephrasing my declaration into a question. 

And then, there is the thumping. 

The low, rhythmic vibration from behind the walls. The thing that might be water hitting a resonant chamber, or might be the slow geological breathing of a fungal mat, or might be the ancient resident for whom this entire hollow was carved. 

When I am climbing, and the thumping swells, I stop. My claws lock into the chevron beneath me. My throat closes. My heart, visible through my pale, translucent skin, beats in time with it. The thumping is the metronome to which the water flows, the frogs call, and the stone dissolves. It is the hollow’s pulse. 

Sometimes, when a human visits, they raise their voices. They speak into the hollow, their words echoing off the stone, distorted and strange. And the thumping pauses. 

In that pause, the entire ecosystem holds its breath. The musicians stop their calls. The water seems to hesitate on the stone lips. I cling to the wall, my toes splayed wide, my claws buried in the chisel mark. I listen. The hollow listens. The thing behind the walls is hearing this new sound, this loud, dry, clumsy vibration from the world above. 

And then, the thumping resumes. But it is altered. Slower, perhaps. Or faster. A new rhythm integrated into the ancient beat. The hollow has changed its mind about something. The frogs resume their song, the water flows, and I continue my climb. 

I do not know that I am the living voice of an instrument carved in stone. I do not know that my oversized feet and my small dark claws are the result of a co-evolutionary feedback loop spanning centuries, that the builders carved the stone to fit my ancestors' grip, and my ancestors' grip shaped the depth of the builders' chisels. I do not know that I am a creature of the made world, perfectly adapted to a geometry of human intention. 

I only know the grip. The cool sheet of water. The taste of the beetle. The rhythm of the thump. The way my call returns to me, rearranged and strange. 

I am the climber. I am the interface between the water and the wall, between the sound and the stone. I am the hollow, looking at itself through pale, unblinking eyes, gripping the darkness with small, dark claws, and singing.
