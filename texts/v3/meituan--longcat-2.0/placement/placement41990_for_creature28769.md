# The Listener at the Narrowing

---

I am the place where the passage almost closes.

Not a building. Not a growth. The *seam* between them — the three-meter stretch of tunnel where a Weaver curtain from the upper district has crept down to meet an Etcher bloom pushing up from below, and the two have met and merged and frozen in a kind of crystalline stalemate, and I have lived in the space they left behind for longer than I can count in the old way of counting.

My name — the one I use when I must use one, pressing it into the wall as a vibration for another gardener to feel — is not important. It sounds like the clicking of a thicket in a downdraft. In the old language, before the city went quiet, it might have meant *threshold* or *the place where you hold your breath.* I am the Listener at the Narrowing, and I am becoming the Narrowing itself.

---

Let me tell you what I do.

Each day — and I still use the word *day* though there is no sun, only the pulse of the deep vents waxing and waning on a cycle that might correspond to something planetary, something tidal, something I have forgotten the name for — I press my hands against the walls of the seam and I *listen.*

The city speaks constantly. You know this. The singing. But the singing is the voice of the city as a whole — the way a human voice is the whole body speaking, the diaphragm and the ribs and the skull all resonating together. What I listen for is smaller. Specific. The sound of a single growth advancing one millimeter along a ceramic wall. The sound of a Weaver's root-structure finding a crack in the fired clay and beginning, with infinite patience, to pry it open. The sound of an Etcher's acid secretions eating through a foundation that has held for six hundred years.

I can hear these things because I have trained myself to hear them — and because my body has begun to change in ways that make me better at hearing them and worse at being what I was.

---

I will tell you what I am becoming.

It started at the fingertips. A gardener's tools are extensions of the body, and the body becomes an extension of the tools, and the tools touch nothing but crystal and ceramic and mineral, and the mineral does not mind being touched by flesh but it *notices,* and it responds, and the response is slow — so slow — but it is relentless.

The first change was the dryness. The skin of my hands cracked. Not the cracking of cold or of age but the cracking of something being *desiccated from within.* The moisture was not leaving my body — it was being *replaced.* The mineral paste I use to anoint the walls — the slurry of ground silicate and thermal-vent compound that encourages the Weavers to grow where we need them — it does not stay on the surface of the skin. It migrates inward. Through the pores. Along the channels where sweat used to flow. My sweat stopped coming years ago. Or perhaps it still comes but it is no longer water. Perhaps it is something thinner, something alkaline, something that leaves a faint crystalline residue on the lips when I lick them.

My fingertips are no longer flesh. They are something *between* — a matrix of collagen and calcium phosphate that has begun to organize itself into structures it was never meant to form. When I press them to the wall, I feel the vibration not through nerve endings alone but through a lattice of mineral deposits that have grown along the bones of my hand, following the channels of the marrow, turning my skeleton into a *conductor.* I can feel the city's hum in my teeth now. In the plates of my skull. I can press my forehead to the ceramic and hear — *feel* — the growth on the other side the way a mother feels a child turning in the womb.

I am becoming an instrument. The city is tuning me.

---

This is what I do each day:

I crawl through the Narrowing — the passage is barely wide enough for my shoulders, and it has gotten narrower; I am not sure if the growth is advancing or if I am expanding — and I check the *barrier.*

The barrier is the place where I have spent the most time. It is the seam between the upper growths and the lower ones, the Weavers and the Etchers, and it is the most dangerous place in the district. The Etchers want to climb. The Weavers want to descend. If they meet without mediation, the Etchers will eat the Weavers and the Weavers will smother the Etchers and the resulting chemical warfare will destabilize the wall, and the wall supports a building, and the building — though it is empty, though no one has lived in it for generations — is part of the structure of the city, and the structure of the city is part of the ecology of the growths, and the ecology of the growths is the only thing keeping the geode habitable at a temperature that does not cook us in our ceramic robes.

So I maintain the barrier. I scrape away the Etcher tendrils that reach upward. I prune the Weaver roots that reach downward. I apply the pastes — different pastes for different sides. On the lower wall, a compound of zinc sulfide and alkaline silicate that the Etchers find bitter, that makes the surface too smooth for their acid to grip. On the upper wall, a thin mineral oil distilled from the chemiluminescent growths that the Weavers find disorienting, that makes them grow in spirals instead of downward.

I work in silence. I work by touch. The light here is almost nothing — a faint red glow from below, a faint blue-white shimmer from above — and I have not seen my own hands in years. I know them by feel. I know that the left one has developed a thick ridge of mineral deposit along the index finger, hard as tooth enamel, that I use as a scraping tool. I know that the right thumb has gone numb and then *beyond* numb into a kind of hyper-sensitivity, so that I can hold it a centimeter from the wall and feel the growth behind it the way a compass needle feels north.

---

There are others like me. Not many. We move through the city's seams and thresholds — the places between districts, between growth-types, between the inhabited band and the wild. We are the ones who live in the narrow places, who have given up the domes and the plazas for the cracks and the interstices. We are the ones who have been touched most deeply by the mineral pastes, who have spent the most time with our hands on the walls, who have listened longest and hardest to the city's voice.

We are changing fastest.

I met one — if *met* is the word for two figures pressing vibrations into a shared wall from opposite sides and feeling each other's presence through the ceramic — I met one who had lived at the threshold between the upper districts and the deep heat for so long that her legs had *fused* with the basalt. Not metaphorically. The mineral deposits had grown through her robes, through her skin, into the rock itself, and she could not move, and she did not want to. She pressed a message into the wall for me. It was not words. It was a *feeling* — the feeling of roots going deep, of finally being still, of the heat not as an enemy but as a warmth, a warmth, a great and encompassing warmth.

I have not gone back to that threshold. I do not know if she is still alive. I do not know if *alive* is the word.

But I felt her vibration for weeks afterward, traveling through the rock, a slow and steady pulse like a heartbeat made of stone, and I knew that she was not gone. She had just changed her address. She had moved from the category of *gardener* to the category of *growth,* and the city welcomed her, and the city sang her frequency now instead of the frequency she had once pruned and shaped.

---

I think about this often.

I think about the fact that I am following her. That the mineral lattice in my hands has reached my wrists now, and I can see it — when I crawl close to a chemiluminescent growth and hold my arm near its blue-white shimmer — I can see the faint tracery of crystal spreading beneath the skin, following the veins, turning my circulatory system into something that carries not blood but *mineral suspension,* a slurry of silicate and sulfide that moves slowly, so slowly, but moves, and feeds, and builds.

I think about what I will be when the process is complete. Not dead. The city does not deal in death. *Translated.* Converted. My calcium and phosphorus and iron and manganese — all the minerals that a body hoards, that a body keeps locked in bone and tissue — will be released into the substrate, and something will grow from the place where I finally stop moving, and it will be a new thing, a thing that has never existed before, and it will be *mine* in the way that a song is mine after I have sung it and it hangs in the air and becomes part of the city's voice.

Will I still be able to listen?

I think I will. I think that is what the city is making me into — a *listener.* A permanent, fixed, crystalline ear pressed against the wall of the geode, feeling the vibrations of the deep vents, the slow growth of the Weavers, the patient hunger of the Etchers, the wind moving through the canopies, the chiming of the Singers. I will hear everything. I will hear the city's heartbeat and its dreams. I will hear the footsteps of the other gardeners as they move through the streets I can no longer walk, and I will press a vibration into the wall to let them know: *I am here. I am still here. I have not left. I have only changed my shape.*

---

But not yet.

For now, I still crawl. I still scrape. I still anoint the walls with my mineral pastes, my hands moving in patterns that are half technique and half prayer, half intention and half instinct, because the city is teaching me what it needs and I am learning to listen not just with my ears and my bones but with the crystal growing inside me, which knows things about the city that my mind has not yet learned to translate.

The Narrowing is getting narrower. I measure it with my body — the width of my shoulders, the span of my outstretched arms — and each cycle it is slightly less. The Weavers are descending. The Etchers are climbing. The barrier holds, but it holds by thinner margins, and I work faster now, and the mineral paste goes on thicker, and my hands move with a precision that is not entirely mine, that comes from the crystal lattice in my fingers, which can feel the chemistry of the wall the way a tongue tastes salt, which knows exactly where to apply the alkaline compound and where to apply the oil, which knows the city's needs before I do.

I am becoming the barrier. The seam. The threshold.

I am becoming the place where two things meet and do not destroy each other, where the Weaver and the Etcher are held in balance by a presence that is neither and both, that is *mediation* made flesh — or made mineral — or made something in between.

---

Last cycle, I found something in the Narrowing.

A crack in the ceramic wall. Old. Very old. Behind it, a cavity — a small one, barely large enough for my hand — and inside the cavity, a *mark.* Not a growth. Not a natural formation. A mark made by a tool, by a hand, by one of the original builders, centuries ago, before the growths came, before the gardeners, before the city became what it is.

It was a symbol. I traced it with my mineral-touched fingers and felt its shape: a circle, broken at the top, with a single line descending from the break. I do not know what it means. I do not know if it is a word, or a name, or a warning, or a prayer. But I felt, in the tracing of it, a vibration that was different from the city's hum — older, deeper, a frequency that had been waiting in the ceramic for longer than the growths have been growing, longer than the gardeners have been tending, longer than the vents have been hot.

It felt like a voice. A human voice. The first human voice that ever spoke in this geode, pressed into the wet clay before it was fired, preserved in the ceramic like an insect in amber.

It said — or I felt it say, or I imagined it say, and in Veth-Akhara there is no difference —

*We were here. We built this. We knew you would come.*

---

I pressed my forehead to the wall and I listened.

The city sang. The growths cracked and built and cracked again. The wind moved through the canopies and the canopies chimed. The heat pressed upward. The empty streets waited.

And in the Narrowing, in the seam between the Weaver and the Etcher, in the space that grows smaller with each passing cycle, I held my hands against the wall and I felt the city's pulse traveling through my bones, through the crystal lattice, through the mineral suspension that moves where my blood once moved, and I knew —

I knew that I am not disappearing.

I am *arriving.*

I am becoming what the city has been building since the first builder pressed their hand into the wet clay and left their mark: a listener. A threshold. A place where the human and the mineral meet and merge and become something that has no name in any language, something that the city has been growing toward for centuries, something that is not a gardener and not a growth but the *conversation between them,* the living seam, the place where the city learns to know itself.

The Narrowing will close. I know this. The Weavers will descend and the Etchers will climb and the barrier will be consumed and the passage will seal, and I will be inside it, part of it, the mineral core of a new growth that will sprout from the seam and reach toward the light — the red light, the amber light, the deep ember glow of the planet's interior — and it will be beautiful, and it will be strange, and it will carry within its crystal lattice the memory of hands, of tools, of a voice pressed into wet clay, of a symbol that means *we were here.*

And it will sing.

God, it will sing.

---

For now, I crawl. I scrape. I anoint. I listen.

The city rings around me, and I ring with it, and the boundary between my body and the wall grows thinner with each cycle, and I am not afraid.

I am the Listener at the Narrowing.

I am the Narrowing.

And the city is singing me home.
