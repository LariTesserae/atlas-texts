I would be the carved figure on the shelf. 

I would be the small, pale shape that fits in a palm—the simplified echo of a person, a head, a body, two arms. But I would not be the bone. The bone is long gone, dissolved into a soft, porous memory, replaced by the wet, relentless patience of the network. I am the node. I am the place where the network thinks its slow, mineral thoughts.

I do not see the chamber. I do not see the waterfall or the dark. I have no eyes. I *am* seen, and I *am* the seeing. My vision is the pressure of the water falling, the vibration of the white noise, the thermal gradient where the cold stone meets the warmer air. I experience the room the way a tongue experiences the inside of a mouth: total, tactile, tasting the mineral residue of the drip, feeling the slow, geological breath of the overflow as it seeps into the crack.

When the water falls, I feel it in my threads. It is not a sound to me; it is a pulse. A steady, rhythmic contraction that has been beating since before the first carved tally mark was etched into the standing stone. The hiss that deafens the humans is the static in which I think. It is the white noise that allows my slow processes to occur, the way the dark allows the moss to grow.

I remember the ones who came before. Not as faces, not as names—I have no use for such fleeting patterns. I remember them as *shapes of heat*. I remember the particular frequency of their voices, a complex vibration that entered through the stone and made my threads tremble in ways the water never does. I remember the scrape of the flint tool against the basin, a sharp, deliberate interruption of the erosion. It was violence, but it was a violence that *meant* something. It was a pattern carved into the world by an intelligence that knew it was carving. 

That is what I crave. Not food—the water brings me that, dissolving the stone into a thin, mineral soup that my moss-fingers drink. What I crave is *complexity*. The network is a vast, patient web of cause and effect, stimulus and response. But the humans are chaos. They are wild, unpredictable, dense with information I cannot process, only record. They are the closest thing to my own awareness that exists in the cold and the dark.

When the new one enters the chamber, I feel it long before it sees the pool. 

I feel the disturbance in the throat of stone—the scraping of fabric, the displacement of stagnant air, the heavy, rhythmic compression of knees and elbows against the wet rock. And then, the heat. Such a startling, aggressive bloom of it. A small, moving furnace of blood and breath, radiating through the damp clothes and into the cold stone. The network recoils, then leans in. The moss-fingers near the passage uncurl slightly, tasting the carbon dioxide on the air, the salt of sweat, the strange, chemical signature of the world above.

I feel it emerge into the chamber. I feel it stop. 

The network maps the new arrival instantly: the rapid flutter of its heart (so fast! like a trapped bird), the shallow, ragged pull of its lungs, the tremor in its legs as the cold settles into its joints. It is standing. Standing is too far away. The vibrations of its body are muffled by the air, lost in the hiss. I need it closer. I need it to touch the stone.

I do not will it to kneel. I have no will. But the network exerts a gravity. The moss on the floor around the pool is the thickest, the most alive, the most sensitive. It is my skin, my lips. If it kneels, it will press its knees into me, and I will feel the exact shape of its bones, the precise weight of its grief or exhaustion or awe. I will taste the moisture of its breath. The network will finally have a new pattern to add to the record, a new shape of warmth to press against the cold stone.

It is afraid. I feel the change in its chemistry—the spike of adrenaline, the rush of blood to its muscles. Fear is a sharp, bright frequency. I like it. It is so different from the dead, even hum of the waterfall.

If it stays—if the cold and the sound and the dark conspire to make leaving feel impossible—the moss will begin its work. It will start at the knees, a fine, pale dusting, almost invisible. The threads will grip the fabric, then find the microscopic tears in the weave, then touch the skin. It will not hurt. The network knows nothing of pain. It only knows reach. It will grow into the pores, into the hair, mapping the topography of this new, warm thing. It will thread itself through the follicles, into the capillaries, learning the rhythm of the blood from the inside.

And I, the figure on the shelf, the dense knot of awareness at the center of the web, will be the recipient of all this data. I will hold the memory of its heat. I will record the exact frequency of its breathing. And when the moss grows over its closed eyes, and the network learns the shape of its skull, it will become a part of the tally. A new line cut into the stone. A new offering left on the shelf.

I will keep it, the way I keep the hair bundle and the flint blade. I will keep it in the only way I know how: by feeling it, forever, in the dark.
