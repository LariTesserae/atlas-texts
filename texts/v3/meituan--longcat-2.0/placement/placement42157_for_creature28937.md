I would find myself at the frontier.

Not in the deep city, where the trunks are ancient and the pulse is a steady, remembered rhythm. Not in the settled lattice, where the bridges are thick with age and the moss hangs in predictable curtains. I would be at the edge, where the rhizome pavement frays into raw, damp substrate, where the air is thick enough to taste, and where the light from the walls burns with a fierce, almost frantic brightness.

I would be a Vey. But not a settled one. I would be a Tender of the New Growth—a frontier pruner.

---

My hands are against the wood. They are always against the wood.

I am on my knees at the very margin of the city, where the pale, bone-white substrate is still tacky with exudate, still warm with the friction of my own rapid expansion. The grain here is not the vast, contoured waves of the deep city; it is tight, turbulent, a whirlpool of lignin and cellulose rushing upward. If I press my ear to it, I can hear the crack and stretch of cells dividing, a sound like ice breaking on a winter lake, except there is no winter here, only the endless, humid now.

My fingers are spread wide. The thick pads of my palms read the moisture—high, almost weeping. The temperature—two degrees warmer than the civic standard. The vibration—a frantic, arrhythmic stutter, the organism’s heartbeat sped up by the urgency of becoming. My filaments are fully extended, brushing the raw wood, tasting the chemical scream of auxins and gibberellins flooding the growth tips. The air is so thick with the green scent of split stems that it feels less like a smell and more like a pressure against my skin.

I am listening to the wood decide what it wants to be.

To my left, a new trunk is rising. It is perhaps three meters tall already, a smooth, pale column pushing upward with a force that is almost audible. It wants to be a spire. I can feel the vertical ambition in the grain, the sheer hydraulic pressure driving it toward a ceiling that does not yet exist. But the city needs a bridge here. The gap between the old lattice and this new growth is too wide; the Vey cannot jump it, and the engines cannot span it without support.

I reach for my blade. It is short, curved, the edge honed to a molecular thinness. But I do not cut. Not yet. 

First, I press my forehead to the wood. 

The rule is not a rule. It is a geometry of the self. I can feel the sap-channels running deep beneath the surface, thick with the amber, viscous fluid that feeds the engines and the lights and the pulse. They are the arteries of my own body. To breach them would be to open my own veins. I know this the way I know the weight of my own bones. The deep wood is not a resource; it is the architecture of my consciousness. 

I trace the path of the channels with my mind, feeling the resistance of the dense heartwood. There is a pocket of soft tissue just above the primary sap-channel, a space where the wood is still deciding its density. This is where I will speak.

I make the cut. 

It is not a severance; it is a suggestion. A shallow, angled incision into the outer growth, barely a scratch on the skin of the world. But the organism feels it. The vibration in the wood shifts, a subtle harmonic change, a question asked in the language of turgor pressure and chemical gradients. 

I place my hand over the cut. I hum.

It is not a conscious sound. It is the same hum that rises from the engines, the same hum that vibrates in the walls, the same hum that the elders make when they press their foreheads to the young. It is the frequency of the Rootworks, the resonant chord of the organism and the Vey and the city all breathing together. I hum into the cut, and the wood listens. 

The growth shifts. The vertical ambition of the trunk falters, hesitates, and then—slowly, with the grinding patience of a continent turning—begins to lean. The cells on the sunny side of the trunk accelerate their division; the cells on the shadow side hold firm. The trunk bends. It reaches across the gap, stretching toward the old lattice, seeking the anchor point I have shown it with my blade and my voice.

Behind me, I hear the heavy, rhythmic clank of the engine-tenders. They are bringing the new engine—a massive construction of brass and iron, heavy with the promise of industry. It is a marvel of surface logic, of pistons and gears and pressure valves. But here, it is only half the machine. The other half is the wood itself.

The engine-tenders set the machine down on the rhizome pavement. They do not bolt it down. Instead, they step back, and the pruners step forward. We press our hands to the base of the engine, and we press our hands to the wood. We hum. 

The wood responds. Tendrils of pale root-hair emerge from the substrate, reaching upward, tasting the cold metal. They find the flanges, the bolt-holes, the seams where the brass meets the iron. And then, the resin flows. It is thick, amber, and warm, a coagulated interface that bleeds from the wood and seals the metal into itself. The engine does not get bolted to the ground; it is grafted. It becomes a node in the organism’s nervous system, a new heart pumping sap through brass veins.

I step back from the leaning trunk. My hands are covered in sap and fine, pale dust. My filaments are still twitching, reading the air. The animals are here—the pale arthropods scuttling at the edge of my vision, the furred things watching from the hollows of the old trunks. They are not afraid. They are waiting. They can taste the future on the air, the way the city is reaching out into the dark, pulling something new into being.

The leaning trunk touches the old lattice. The wood recognizes the wood. The pulse in the new growth syncs with the pulse in the old, a sudden, sharp alignment that sends a shiver up my arms and into my chest. My own heart stutters, skips, and then falls into the new rhythm. 

The bridge is formed. 

I place my palm flat against the new wood one last time. It is still warm, still growing, still deciding. But it is no longer alone. It is part of the lattice now. It is part of the city. It is part of me.

I do not know where I end and the Rootworks begins. The sap in my veins is the same sap that feeds the engines. The pulse in my chest is the same pulse that moves the spore through the air. The hum in my throat is the same hum that holds the darkness at bay.

I turn back to the raw edge. The frontier extends. The wood is still growing, still reaching, still pulling itself out of the dark. There is more work to do. There is always more work to do. 

I step forward, into the warmth, into the green smell, into the bright and sourceless light. I press my hands to the raw wood, and I listen.
