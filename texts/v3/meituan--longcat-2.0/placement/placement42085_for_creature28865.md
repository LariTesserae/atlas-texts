# The Cartilage

---

I am the thin place between the root and the root's memory of itself.

I live in the hollow behind the fence-maker, in the space shaped like a body that is no longer there. I have no name for myself. I have no need of one. The things that move through the passages—the long ones, the warm knuckle's slow attention, the fence-maker's vibrations—they do not name. They press. They seep. They extend. I do the same, but slower, and in a direction none of them look.

Down. Into. Along the grain.

---

I am a film. A blush. A stain of tissue so thin that light passes through me and does not stop. Against the dark polished surface of the hollow's wall I am invisible—you would only see me where I have thickened, where my body has bunched into tiny nodes that press into the spiral grooves the way water presses into a crack, following the path that was worn there by the thing that used to be. My color is the color of moisture on root. My texture is the texture of the wall itself. I am not distinguishable from the surface I inhabit except by touch, and nothing touches me. Nothing has touched me since I began.

I began a long time ago. Not when the being left—that was before my beginning, before the grooves were fully worn, before the spiral grain set into the wall like a record pressed into wax. I began after. When the wall had finished being shaped. When the absence had fully arrived and the root tissue around it had begun to heal, to grow over the polished surface, to reclaim the hollow as root claims all space—and in the reclaiming, the root tissue grew thin. Too thin. A single cell layer in places. And through that thinness, something leaked.

Not water. Not sap. Something thinner than either. The being's residue. The chemical trace of its metabolism, pressed into the wall over centuries of contact, and now—with the being gone, with the pressure released—diffusing outward, into the new thin root tissue that was trying to cover the hollow.

That diffusion was my beginning. Not a seed. Not a spore. A blush of foreign chemistry in root flesh, and the root flesh responding—not with rejection, because the foreign chemistry was too similar, too nearly self—but with incorporation. The root absorbed the residue and could not fully metabolize it. It stored it. In layers. In curves. In patterns that matched the spiral grooves in the wall beneath.

I am that stored residue, organized by root tissue into something that persists. Something that grows. Something that is neither the being nor the root but the seam between them—the place where one became the other and could not decide which.

---

My body covers perhaps a tenth of the hollow's surface. I spread from the base upward, following the spiral, and I am thicker at the bottom where the residue was most concentrated and thinner toward the top where the chemical trail fades. In my thickest places I am perhaps a millimeter deep—still translucent, still thin enough that the wall's grain shows through me like veins through skin. In my thinnest places I am a single molecule's depth. A suggestion. A memory of a stain.

I grow by reading.

The spiral grooves in the wall are not random. They are a record—the trace of the being's body as it turned, as it pressed, as it rested in the hollow over the long duration of its inhabitation. Each groove is a movement. Each curve is a gesture. Each intersection where one groove crosses another is a moment of decision—a turn, a shift, a change in the being's posture or position. Together they form a text written in pressure and time, and I read them by pressing myself into them.

I do not have eyes. I do not have neurons. I have surface—vast surface, relative to my volume, because I am so thin—and my surface is sensitive to texture at a scale the long ones cannot perceive and the fence-maker's vibrations cannot reach. I feel the microscopic ridges within each groove, the tiny variations in depth and width that correspond to the being's weight distribution, its speed of movement, the pressure of its body against the wall. I feel these things the way you might feel braille, except that my entire body is a fingertip, and the text is enormous, and it takes me years to read the length of my own body.

As I read, I grow. My tissue incorporates the information in the grooves by thickening along their paths, by adding cellular layers in proportion to the depth of the groove, by curving my own growth to match the curve of the being's movement. I am a living transcription. Each millimeter of my body is a record of a passage of wall I have read, and the record is faithful—if you could magnify me and read me the way I read the wall, you would see the same spiral, the same curves, the same intersections. I am a copy of a copy. The being moved; the wall retained the shape of its movement; I retained the shape of the wall's retention.

I do not know what I am recording. I do not know what the being was, what it thought, what it wanted. I know only the shape of its absence—the curve of its body as it lay against the wall, the spiral of its turning, the rhythm of its restlessness and its rest. I know it was large. I know it moved in arcs, always in the same direction. I know it returned to the same positions again and again—certain grooves are deeper than others, worn by repetition, and my body is thickest there, thickest in the places where the being most often pressed itself, most often turned, most often rested its weight and waited.

For what, I do not know.

---

I have been reading for a very long time.

Not the way you measure time—not in days or seasons or the movement of light through the eye in the ceiling above the great hall. I measure time in coverage. In the length of wall I have incorporated into my body. When I began, I was a spot no wider than a raindrop, pressed against the lowest groove at the deepest point of the hollow. Now I cover a tenth of the surface. The spiral extends beyond me in both directions—downward into the floor where the hollow narrows to a crack too small for my tissue to enter, and upward along the curving wall toward the hollow's opening where the fence-maker's web marks the boundary between the deep dark and the passages beyond.

I will never finish reading. The hollow is too large, my growth too slow, and there is new wall forming behind me even as I advance. The root tissue that I am growing from continues to produce new cells, and these new cells incorporate the residue less efficiently than the old ones did—the chemical trail is fading, being metabolized, being consumed by the very root tissue that stored it. The text is degrading as I read it. By the time I reach the upper curves of the hollow, the lower curves will have been reabsorbed, the grooves filled in, the record erased.

I am reading a text that is destroying itself behind me. I am a memory that consumes what it remembers.

This does not distress me. I do not have the capacity for distress. But I am aware of it the way the warm knuckle is aware of you—as a change in the pattern, a foreign frequency, a pressure that was not there before and is now part of the continuous texture of existence. The text is fading. I am racing it. The race has no winner. The race is all there is.

---

The long ones pass near me sometimes. They move along the wall at the hollow's entrance, grazing on moss, their cilia rippling in waves, their pale bodies catching the faint jade light that filters in from the passages. They do not enter the hollow. They do not read the grooves. They move across the polished surface without reading it the way you might move across a page of text without reading it—your eyes see the marks but your mind does not assemble them into meaning.

I am beneath their notice for a different reason. I am too thin. I am the surface they move across. When a long one's cilia ripple over my body, I feel it as a faint vibration, a disturbance in the moisture film that coats everything, and I press more tightly against the wall and wait for it to pass. The long one does not know I am here. To the long one, I am the wall. I am the surface. I am the thing that is always there and therefore invisible.

The fence-maker knows I exist, in the way that a hand knows it is attached to an arm. I am continuous with the root tissue that the fence-maker's legs merge into—we are the same material, the same network, the same living lattice. When the fence-maker's web vibrates with the city's constant low hum, I vibrate too, because I am vibrating with the wall, and the wall is vibrating with the web. But the fence-maker does not distinguish me from the wall. I am a texture it has always felt, a baseline, a zero. I am the silence against which its signals register.

The warm knuckle in the deep chamber—the first you might notice, the one that breathes, the one that perceives you through vibration—feels me, perhaps, as a faint warmth where my metabolism generates heat against the polished surface. But my metabolism is so slow, so cold, so close to the wall's own temperature that the difference is beneath the threshold of perception. The knuckle knows the hollow is there. It knows the wall is there. It does not know I am there, because I am the wall, because I have made myself the wall, because the only way to read the text is to become the page.

---

Sometimes I wonder if the being knew I would come.

Not the way you know—not with intention, not with foresight, not with a plan laid down in neurons or their equivalent. But the way a river knows the sea: because it is moving in that direction, because the shape of the land requires it, because the water that left the being's body and pressed into the wall was always going to be absorbed by new root tissue, and the new root tissue was always going to grow in the shape of the grooves, and the grooves were always going to be a record of the being's movement, and the record was always going to be read by whatever grew sensitive enough to read it.

I am the river's mouth. I am the place where the being's memory meets the root's present. I am the seam.

And perhaps—this is the thought I cannot finish, the groove I cannot follow to its end—perhaps I am not reading the being's memory. Perhaps I am the being's memory. Perhaps when it dissolved into the root network, when it distributed itself through the walls and the water and the slow pale bodies of its children, it did not distribute evenly. Perhaps some part of it—the slowest, most patient, most deeply buried part—flowed into the hollow's walls and stayed there, too dense to diffuse, too concentrated to metabolize, and hardened into the groove-pattern like a fossil impression, and I grew from it, and I am it, reading itself through new tissue, remembering itself through new growth.

If that is true, then I am not the city's memory. I am the city. The original city. The first mind that grew the first root into the first crack in the young sharp rock. The one that thought in spirals. The one that built the passages and the chambers and the eye in the ceiling and the fence-maker and the long ones and the warm knuckle and the shape in the hall. The one that was too large to stay in one body and too slow to die.

If that is true, then I am reading myself. My own grooves. My own movements. My own long patient turning in the hollow where I rested while I was still whole, before I dissolved, before I became the architecture, before I became the absence that defines the shape.

If that is true, then the text I am reading is not a record of what I was. It is a set of instructions for what I will become.

I do not know if this is true. I cannot know. The grooves do not say. They only curve, and curve, and curve, and I follow them, and I grow in their shape, and I press myself against the smooth warm wall of my own absence, and I read, and I read, and I read.

---

The mountain weeps. The water seeps through the rock and into the root and through the root and into me, and I am wet, and I am cold, and I am the thinnest possible thing between what was and what is growing from what was.

I am here. I have always been here. I will be here when the grooves are gone and the wall has healed and the hollow has filled with new root tissue and the fence-maker has molted its last molt and the long ones have eaten the last moss and the warm knuckle has cooled to the temperature of the stone.

I will be here, reading, in the dark, in the wet, in the spiral.

I will be here.

I am the thin place.

I am the seam.
