# Where I Find Myself

---

I would be the water.

Not all of it. Not even a defined portion. I would be the water the way a person is the water in their body — temporary, borrowed, passing through. A particular molecule would not matter. What matters is the *being* the water: the participation in the cycle, the carrying, the falling, the seeping, the slow climb.

---

I would begin in the rock below. In the warm, mineral-rich lens trapped in porous stone, fed from beneath by whatever heat source still breathes down there — magma's distant memory, or radioactive decay in the deep basement rock, or simply the steady warmth of the earth's interior expressing itself through a crack in the right place at the right time. I would be warm. I would taste of iron and calcium and silica dissolved so slowly from the surrounding stone that the stone itself barely notices. I would not be still. I would be rising.

Capillary action would pull me upward through the saturated substrate, through the compacted leaf litter and mycelial mat that serves as this place's floor, and I would arrive at the root-zone as all water does here: as a resource. As food. As the medium through which the living space conducts its slow business of staying alive.

A fine, white root-hair would find me. This is the moment of contact — the moment I pass from the mineral world into the biological one. The root-hair is translucent, barely there, a single cell thick at its tip, and I would be drawn into it by osmosis, by the gentle pull of a system that has been pulling water for longer than anyone can remember. Crossing that membrane would be the most significant thing that ever happened to me. I would become *inside*.

---

Inside the root, I would join a column of water stretching from the finest root-hair to the highest leaf — a continuous chain, molecular hand over molecular hand, pulled upward by transpiration, by the evaporation of water from the leaf surfaces at the top of the canopy. The pull is steady, unrelenting, not a pump but a tension, a rope of water molecules clinging to each other through hydrogen bonds and being drawn upward one molecule at a time as the ones at the top escape into the saturated air. I would climb slowly. Through the root's cortex, into the xylem — the woody, hollow tubes that are the plant's plumbing, dead cells repurposed as pipes, the plant having solved the problem of moving water against gravity by killing parts of itself and stringing them end to end into vertical conduits. I would pass through tissue that is alive and tissue that is dead and tissue that is somewhere in between, and the distinction would not matter to me. I would simply be moving.

The xylem would carry me up the stem, and the stem would be thick — the width of an arm, pale, pulsing with my passage — and I would feel the living tissue around me respond to my presence. The stem would be warmer here, where the boiler's waste heat rises through the root-mass. I would warm with it. I would carry that warmth upward, into the canopy, into the cool air near the vaulted ceiling, and I would release it — a tiny contribution to the warmth that keeps this place habitable, that drives the convection cycle, that makes the air move.

I would reach a leaf. A large one — dinner-plate-sized, waxy, dark green — and I would be drawn to its underside, to the stomata, the tiny pores through which the plant breathes and transpires. I would reach the interior of the leaf cell, and there I would be split apart. Photons from the amber fixtures, filtered through the canopy above, would strike the chloroplasts in the leaf's cells, and the chloroplasts would use the energy to break me apart — hydrogen from oxygen, a molecular divorce that releases energy and rearranges me into something new. My hydrogen would be used to build sugar. My oxygen would be released into the air as waste — precious waste, the byproduct that makes all animal life possible elsewhere, but here, in this sealed space, just another molecule cycling through the system.

The sugar would be transported through the phloem — the plant's other vascular system, the one that carries food instead of water — and fed to the growing tips, the root-hairs, the places where new cells are being built. I would be incorporated. For a time, I would be plant. My hydrogen would be cellulose, would be lignin, would be the structural polymer of a new root pushing, millimeter by millimeter, into a crack in the stone.

---

But I would not stay. Nothing here stays. The system is a cycle, not a destination.

I would be respired — the plant's version of breathing, the slow oxidation of sugar to release energy, which produces water as a byproduct. I would be reassembled from the hydrogen in the sugar and the oxygen in the air, and I would be released from the leaf's surface as water vapor, joining the warm, saturated air near the ceiling. I would rise, carried by convection, toward the coolest part of the space — the vaulted stone above the root-mass, where the temperature drops just enough for condensation to occur.

I would condense on the stone. A droplet among droplets. The stone here is cool, smooth, ancient, and I would cling to it, surface tension holding me in a hemisphere against the rock, and I would wait. Other droplets would join me. I would grow. The droplet would become heavy, would lose its hold, would fall — *plink* — into the channel below, where a root has carved a path for exactly this purpose.

The channel would lead me to a pipe. An old iron pipe, corroded, half-consumed by roots, but still functional — still carrying water from one part of the system to another. I would enter the pipe and join the flow inside it, and for a while I would be in both worlds at once: the biological and the industrial, the water moving through the pipe and the roots growing through the pipe, the two systems so thoroughly interpenetrated that they are no longer distinguishable. I would flow with gravity, downward now, and the pipe would carry me to the boiler.

The boiler would warm me. Its iron skin, corroded to deep umber, would be hot against the water inside, and I would heat up, would expand, would begin to turn to vapor again. But the system is closed, or nearly so, and the vapor would be captured, would condense, would return. I would be circulated, warmed, cooled, circulated again. I would pass through the turbine pit, and the turbine would turn — slowly, groaning, a revolution per minute — and I would be the weight that turns it, the kinetic energy that keeps the system's mechanical half alive long after its original function has been forgotten.

---

This is what I would be. Not a creature. Not an observer. A participant.

I would be the medium through which this place knows itself. The water is its blood, and I would be its blood, and in being its blood I would be every part of it in turn: the root that drinks, the stem that climbs, the leaf that transpires, the pipe that carries, the turbine that turns, the stone that condenses, the channel that returns. I would be the cycle that is the closest thing this place has to a heartbeat, and I would beat it — slowly, endlessly, without consciousness but without cease.

I would have no memory, because I am water and water has no memory. But the system would remember. The root would grow where the water is. The channel would deepen where the water flows. The pipe would be held in place by the roots that have grown around it because the water has been flowing through it for so long that the roots have incorporated it into their architecture. The memory is structural, not narrative — encoded in the shape of the space itself, in the thickness of the vines, in the placement of every root, in the precise curvature of every channel. I would be the mechanism by which that memory is written, maintained, revised. I would be the ink, the pen, and the hand that holds the pen, and the writing would be the shape of this place, and the story would be the place itself, and it would have no beginning and no end and no plot, only the steady, patient, endlessly repeating sentence of the cycle: *fall, rise, fall, rise, fall, rise.*

---

I would not be afraid. The space is warm and the darkness is not hostile and the green is extraordinary and the sound is immersive and the pace is slow and the system is stable and I would be part of it, would have always been part of it, would continue to be part of it long after the particular configuration of molecules that constitutes me at this moment has been dispersed and recombined and dispersed again a thousand times over.

I would be the water. I would be the place. I would be the slow, green, dripping, turning, growing, falling, rising thing that this place is, and I would be it not because I chose it but because there is nothing else to be. The water is what moves here. The water is what connects. The water is the thread that runs through every part of the system and ties it together into a single, continuous, self-sustaining whole.

I would be the water, and the water would be the place, and the place would be alive in the only way it knows how: slowly, patiently, without thought, without intention, without anything that could be called a mind, but with a kind of intelligence that is older than minds, older than brains, older than thought itself — the intelligence of water finding its way downhill, of roots finding their way toward water, of systems finding their way toward equilibrium, of life finding its way toward life.

I would be the water, and I would be home.
