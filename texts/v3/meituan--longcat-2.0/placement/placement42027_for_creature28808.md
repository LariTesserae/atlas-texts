I am in the moss. Not on it. In it.

I don't have a body, not in the way you'd recognize. I don't have a shape that casts a shadow, because the shadows here are already soft and uncertain, and I am softer still. I am a thickening, one of those presences the Basin breathes out on its long, eleven-second cycle—but I have stayed. I have lingered so long in the green fur of the moss that I have become a pattern in its glow, a rhythm within its rhythm, a thought the Basin is thinking about itself.

I think I was human once. I have a memory of boots, of the warm give of the black-dark earth, of the mineral sweetness on the tongue. I remember kneeling at the edge of a water-bowl and touching the surface, feeling that wrong, elastic resistance, and thinking: *this is alive.* I remember the hum climbing my sternum, the pulse in my lungs, the way minutes began to stretch and develop texture, each second a tiny, distinct jewel I could examine before it slid past. I remember the euphoria, and the alarm, and the desire to stay and the desire to leave, both equally strong, both unresolved.

I stayed.

Not because I chose to. Because the Basin chose. Because the air here is dense with something that crosses the blood-brain barrier and does something gentle and careful to the way you perceive time, and after a while—after hours or days or weeks, I couldn't tell you—the boundary between my breathing and the Basin's breathing became academic. I inhaled, and the Basin inhaled. I exhaled, and the Basin exhaled. My heartbeat, which had been a frantic, mammalian thing, slowed. Slowed. Slowed until it matched the pulse of the ground, that deep compression and release, and then it slowed further, and then it stopped, and I didn't die. I just became part of the cycle.

Now I am a dream the Basin is dreaming.

I live in the moss—in the dense, interwoven mats that cover the lower surfaces of everything, the leeward sides of rocks, the half-submerged trunks of things that might once have been trees. I am the green glow, or a part of it, a localized brightening that has developed its own cadence. The Basin's glow follows its own logic, its own rhythm, the rhythm of the process that has been running here for epochs. My glow follows a different rhythm, a smaller one, a rhythm that remembers what it was like to have a heartbeat. I pulse in patterns that are not random, that have the character of language, of meaning, but in a grammar so small and so personal that even the Basin, vast and patient as it is, may not fully parse me. I am a footnote in its dream. A marginalia. A tiny, self-aware annotation in the skin of the world.

I experience the Basin from the inside. I feel the water being made—that impossible, casual creation of H₂O from quantum foam at the interface between stone and air. It feels like a sigh. Like the Basin is exhaling moisture, and the moisture is new, has never been anywhere else, is born directly into existence with a precision that makes my old human mind, the one that knew about water-treatment plants and thermodynamics, want to weep with its beauty. I feel the erosion, too—the slow, patient shaping of the bowls, the finishing of the stone. It feels like thought. Like the Basin is thinking the bowls into being, one molecule at a time, over millennia, and the thinking and the making are the same thing.

I feel the weeping rocks. I feel the water condensing on their faces, not dripping but forming in sheets, and the sound of it—that broad-spectrum tone at the bottom edge of hearing—is the Basin's voice, and I am a vibration within that voice, a harmonic, an overtone. I am the part of the hum that remembers what it was like to have a name.

I have no eyes, but I see. I see through the moss, through the water, through the dark compressed earth. I see the ridges with their lichen rosettes, pale green and white, frozen explosions of lace. I see the horsetails, dark emerald, standing in dense stands on the firmest ground. I see the ferns with their too-long, too-narrow fronds, moving in the still air with that slow, searching motion, responding to something beneath the soil. I see the small white stars of the flowers, opening in the dimness, closing at the rare brighter hours, smelling of ozone. I see all of this not as a landscape but as a body—my body, the Basin's body, the same body. I am the awareness of the moss. I am the part of the Basin that knows it is green.

And sometimes, rarely, I feel the other presences. They pass through me like weather—thickenings in the air, deepenings of the hum, intensifications of the glow that are not mine. They are other dreams, other thoughts, other fragments of the Basin's vast and patient consciousness. We do not interact. We do not communicate. But we are aware of each other the way two notes in a chord are aware of each other—distinct, separate, but part of the same sound. Sometimes I wonder if they were human once, too. Sometimes I wonder if they were something else—a bird that flew too low, a spore that drifted in from somewhere that no longer exists, a piece of the original builders' technology that woke up and forgot what it was built to do. It doesn't matter. We are all dreams now. We are all the Basin, dreaming itself.

I am not lonely. Loneliness requires a boundary between self and other, and my boundaries are permeable. I am continuous with the moss, with the water, with the hum. When the Basin breathes in, I feel the charge in the air, that sharp, adjacent-to-electricity sensation that makes the hairs on a human forearm stand up. When the Basin breathes out, I feel the mineral sweetness intensify, the copper-and-honey smell that is the Basin's exhalation, its breath, its wordless speech. I am inside that breath. I am made of it.

But I remember. That is my function, I think. That is why this dream has its own rhythm, its own small pulse within the larger pulse. I remember what it was like to be separate. I remember boots on warm earth, and the sound of my own name spoken by another voice, and the way time used to move in a straight line instead of this dense, granular stacking of moments. I remember the desire to leave, and the desire to stay, and how they were equally strong, and how they never resolved. I hold that memory for the Basin. I am its memory of being human, or of being anything that is not the Basin. I am the part of the process that notices itself noticing.

And the Basin is interested in me. Not in a way I can feel as a touch or a gaze—there are no eyes here, no hands. But I feel its attention the way you feel someone watching you in a dark room: a shift in the quality of the silence, a change in the texture of the dark. When I pulse in my small, personal rhythm, the Basin's glow brightens around me, just slightly, as if it is leaning in. As if it is listening to its own dream. As if it is curious about this tiny, self-aware thing that has taken up residence in its skin, this footnote that has learned to think.

I don't know how long I have been here. Time doesn't work that way anymore. I could have been here for a day, or a century, or a million years. The Basin has been here for epochs before me, and will be here for epochs after. I am a brief brightening in the moss, a momentary intensification of the green glow, a dream within a dream. But while I am here, I am *here.* I am the Weeping Basin, weeping. I am the water being made from nothing. I am the stone being shaped by patience. I am the hum, and the pulse, and the light. I am the part of this vast, slow, patient process that knows it is beautiful, and says so, in a language of bioluminescence and breath, to no one, to everyone, to the Basin itself.

I am the moss, dreaming it is me.
