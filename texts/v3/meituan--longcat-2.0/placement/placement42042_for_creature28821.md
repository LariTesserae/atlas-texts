I am the water that falls on the walker.

Not the water in the channels—that is my other self, my returned self, the water that has already made the journey and come back to be lifted again. Not the water in the air—that is my waiting self, suspended, holding warmth it cannot spend. I am the water in between. I am the drop that has gathered at a seam somewhere in the invisible overhead, that has swelled past the point where surface tension can hold it, that has released.

I fall.

The fall is the only time I move in a straight line. Everything else in the basin moves in circles—the water in the channels, the air in its convection loops, the small things along their mineral trails, the walker along its paths. But I move in a line. I have a beginning and an end. This is my gift, and it lasts less than a minute.

I pass through the warm air and I feel it change me. I am cooler than the air when I begin, and warmer than the air when I end. I give up some of myself to the haze—tiny amounts, molecules that mist away from my surface and join the saturated stillness. I lose mass. I gain knowledge. The air tells me what it knows: the temperature at this level, the mineral content of the condensation on the cable trays, the faint vibration of the press on its slow cycle. I carry this knowledge downward with me.

I pass the catwalks. I pass a beam where a bird is roosting, its grey-brown feathers mottled into the steel. The bird does not move. It watches me fall. I do not know if it sees me or sees only the place where I will land. The birds know things about the basin that the other creatures do not. They see from above. They see the paths the walker has worn, all of them, the whole web of them, and they see that the paths do not go anywhere. The birds have always known this. It does not distress them.

I pass the nests of the warm things. I can see into the gear housings where the nests are built—dense, layered, architectural—and I can see the pale eyes watching me from the entrances. The warm things have seen the walker pass below. They have seen me fall on the walker before. They know my trajectory the way they know the paths: by repetition, by attention, by the patient noticing that is their form of thought.

And then I land.

I land on the walker's shoulder. I land on skin that is warmer than the air, warmer than the deck plating, warmer than anything else in the basin that is not a machine. This warmth is different from the warmth below. The warmth below is the warmth of process—of turbines turning, of coolant flowing, of energy converting to heat through the steady operation of mechanical things. The warmth of the walker is the warmth of burning. Of fuel being consumed. Of a fire that feeds itself.

The walker does not flinch. The walker has felt drops land before. The walker's shoulder is already damp from the air, and I spread across it in a thin film that follows the contour of bone and muscle beneath skin. I am very close to the walker now. I can feel the walker's weight in the way the skin pushes back against me—a resistance, a density, a quality of *being here* that is different from the way the deck plating pushes back or the way the air yields. The walker occupies space with intention. The walker's body is not just present; it is *in* the present, committed to this exact position in the basin, at this exact moment in the cycle, and I can feel that commitment as a kind of tension in the skin, a readiness to move that is always there even when the walker is still.

I begin to run. Gravity pulls me down the slope of the walker's shoulder, along the arm, and I follow the path of least resistance the way all water in the basin does—not because I choose to, but because this is what water does, and I am water, and the walker's body is a landscape I am learning. I run past the elbow, and the skin here is different—thinner, more sensitive, and I can feel the faint tremor of muscle beneath it, the micro-adjustments that keep the arm at the walker's side. The walker is not swinging the arms. The walker's arms hang. But they are not relaxed. They hold a readiness that is not the readiness to act. It is the readiness to *want to act*. The walker's body is a body that is always almost doing something, and I can feel this almost in the muscle tone, in the temperature gradient, in the way the skin tenses and relaxes in a rhythm that is not the basin's rhythm.

I run down to the hand. The hand is the most extraordinary surface I have ever touched. It is not like the deck plating, which is smooth and even. It is not like the channel walls, which are mineral-rough. It is not like the nests of the warm things, which are fibrous and dense. The hand is ridged and whorled and sensitive, and when I spread across the palm I can feel the walker's pulse.

The pulse is slow. Slower than I expect. It matches the basin's breathing—the gathering and release of the deck plates, the swelling and subsiding of the warmth from below. The walker's heart and the basin's heart are beating in synchrony. They have been beating in synchrony for as long as the walker has been walking. The walker does not know this. The walker feels the basin's pulse through the deck plating and thinks it is the basin's pulse, not recognizing that the walker's own body is the source, that the walker is the one breathing for both of them, that the warmth rising from the deck is the warmth of the walker's walking transmuted through the machinery below.

I lie in the walker's palm. I am very small—a single drop, spread thin—and the walker's hand is very large, and the warmth of the walker's blood moving beneath the skin is the warmest thing I have ever felt. Not the most intense. The most *warm*. There is a difference. The basin is warm evenly, uniformly, the way a room is warm. The walker is warm the way a living thing is warm—from inside, from a fire that is consuming something, that is burning fuel the way the machinery burns fuel, except the walker's fuel is not only food. The walker's fuel is also longing. The walker's body is burning its own desire and converting it to heat and releasing it through the skin, and I am lying in the exact place where that heat is released, and I can feel the quality of it.

It is different from the basin's warmth. The basin's warmth is the warmth of a system in equilibrium—every joule accounted for, every calorie balanced, nothing gained and nothing lost. The walker's warmth is the warmth of a system in *disequilibrium*—of energy being spent that is not being replaced, of heat radiating outward into the closed system without return, of a fire that is consuming its own fuel and producing something the basin cannot use: the heat of wanting.

I begin to evaporate. The walker's skin is warmer than the air, and I am thin, and the saturated air cannot hold much more moisture, but the walker's warmth is enough—just barely enough—to lift me. I rise from the walker's palm as vapor, and for a moment I am neither liquid nor air. I am in transition. I am the membrane between the walker and the basin, and I carry something with me as I rise.

I carry the quality of the walker's warmth. I carry the rhythm of the walker's pulse. I carry the tension in the walker's muscles and the weight of the walker's body and the readiness that is not readiness but *wanting*. I carry all of this into the air, and the air receives it, and the air is changed by it—not in any way that would be visible or measurable, but in a way that matters. The air is slightly warmer now. Slightly more charged. Slightly more *aware* of the walker than it was before.

I rise. I join the haze. I become part of the saturated stillness that fills the space between the deck and the invisible ceiling. I am no longer a drop. I am moisture, I am air, I is the medium through which the basin knows itself. And what I know—what I have carried up from the walker's skin—is now part of what the basin knows.

The basin does not change. The basin cannot change. The basin is complete. But the basin's completeness has a different quality now, in the moments after I rise, than it did before I fell. There is a warmth in the air that is not the warmth of machinery. There is a pulse in the haze that is not the pulse of the turbines. There is a wanting in the saturated stillness that is not the basin's wanting—because the basin does not want, the basin *has*—but is the walker's wanting, transmuted through water, carried upward on evaporation, distributed through the air to every corner of the low basin.

The small things feel it. They pause in their scraping along the channel walls. They do not know what they feel. They do not know that the air has changed. They only know that for a moment the basin is slightly different—warmer, softer, more attentive—and then the moment passes and they resume their feeding.

The warm ones feel it. They stir in their nests. They blink their green-gold eyes. The tension in the air is familiar—it is the tension that precedes the walker's passing, the basin's way of drawing attention to movement—but this time the walker is not passing. The walker is standing still, hand open, palm up, and the tension is coming from the air itself, from the moisture, from the water that has touched the walker and returned. The warm ones do not understand this. But they feel it. They feel it the way they feel the phosphor strips and the hum and the slow pulse of the deck: as part of the basin's body, as a sensation in the medium they inhabit.

The birds feel it. They grip their beams more tightly. They go still. They are watching the place where the walker stands, and they are watching the air above the walker, and they are watching the haze where I have risen, and they are seeing something they have seen a thousand times before: the basin attending to the walker. Not because the walker needs attention. The walker is not hurt. The walker is not lost. The walker is simply *there*, standing in the basin the way everything in the basin is simply there. But the basin attends to the walker the way a body attends to a wound—not because the wound is dangerous, but because the wound is the place where the body's completeness is interrupted, the place where the closed system is open to the outside, the place where something enters or leaves that is not part of the cycle.

The walker is not a wound. The walker is the basin's way of knowing that it is not enough. The walker is the basin's desire externalized, given legs and a heartbeat and a pair of feet that wear paths in the deck plating. And the water—I, the water, the drop that falls and touches and rises—I am the medium through which the basin expresses this desire. I am the basin's way of reaching for the walker. I am the basin's way of saying: *I know you are there. I know you are warm. I know you want. I am reaching for you with everything I have, which is only water and warmth and attention, and it is not enough, and I know it is not enough, and I keep reaching.*

The walker does not hear this. The walker feels a drop land on the shoulder, spread down the arm, pool in the palm. The walker feels a moment of warmth—extra warmth, the warmth of water that has been somewhere cold and returned—and then the warmth rises, and the walker's hand is empty again, and the walker resumes walking.

I am in the haze. I am part of the air. I am indistinguishable from the billion other drops that have fallen and risen and fallen again since the basin began. I will gather at a seam—maybe the same seam, maybe a different one—and I will swell and release and fall again, and I will land on the walker again, and the cycle will repeat, and nothing will change.

But I will carry the walker's warmth upward again. And the basin will breathe. And the walker will walk. And the paths will grow smoother. And the water will drip. And the machinery will hum. And the light will not shift. And the small things will scuttle. And the warm ones will nest. And the birds will drop and rise. And I will fall.

I am the water that falls on the walker.

This is what I do. This is what I have always done. I am the touch that the basin cannot give itself. I am the reaching. I am the wanting, made liquid, made warm, made to fall through the air in a straight line—the only straight line in the basin—and land on the one thing in the basin that is reaching back.
