I would be a Tender of the Slow Eye. 

In the language of gestures, my vocation is expressed by pressing the fingertips of both hands flat against the chest, then slowly drawing them outward and down—a sign that means *to hold the pulse* or *to smooth the skin*. I am a Groove-Wright, a keeper of the center, and my life is spent in the damp, brilliant quiet of the plain’s most sacred anatomy.

I do not live in the dense, layered warren of the outer districts, where the fungus farmers and chitin-hunters trade their wares in sunken courtyards. My dwelling is a low, solitary alcove carved into the stone just a few hundred paces from the Eye itself. It is a humble structure, built upon the oldest sliding plate in the settlement—a stone so worn and grooved by the centuries that it fits into the plain like a joint fits into a socket. When the plain lurches, my home does not groan like the newer buildings; it sighs, a deep, resonant exhalation of mineral breath, and shifts as one with the stone.

My days are dictated by the dampness. 

I wake when the condensation on the floor of my alcove reaches the precise viscosity that the old ones call *weeping thickness*—when the moisture beads so heavily on the pale stone that it catches the grey-blue glow of the not-fungus and throws it back in shattered, liquid constellations. I eat a quiet breakfast of dried chitin and fibrous fungus-rope, and then I make my way to the Eye.

The path there is a meditation. I walk barefoot, as is the custom of my order, so that the soles of my feet can read the stone. The plain speaks through its temperature and its texture. A sudden coolness means a deep capillary fissure has opened below, drawing the magma-heat away. A slickness that feels almost oily means the entity is perspiring heavily, pulling moisture from the deep earth to cool the slow fires of its digestion. I feel these things through my feet, and I adjust my step accordingly, rolling my weight over the arches to avoid startling the skitters that swarm in the crevices.

When I reach the Eye, I begin my work. 

The humans carved this place thin over generations, wearing the stone down to a translucent membrane. But the entity is alive, and like all living things, it produces waste. The not-fungus grows thick here, and the water beads in those perfect, tiny spheres that never fall. My task is to groom the skin. I take a wedge of dense, dry stone and gently scrape away the older, dimmer layers of fungus that have calcified and lost their light. I wipe away the heavy beads of moisture with pads of dried fiber, clearing the pores so the entity’s internal glow can shine through unimpeded. 

As I work, I listen. 

The plain is never truly silent. There is the ever-present whisper of the thin sheets of water creeping toward the shallow bowls, the click and scrape of chitin on rock as the skitters tend to their agricultural patches. But beneath it all, there is the grinding. It is a sound that lives below hearing, a vibration that I feel in the marrow of my bones. It is the sound of the entity’s metabolism, the slow, constant erosion of its own body as it inches through the deep earth. 

I am the only one who stays at the Eye for the full turning of the cycle. The others come to think, to feel the motion, to find their peace. But I stay. I press my palms flat against the thin stone, and I wait.

It is during the shifts that I am most alive. 

They come without warning. A sudden, violent lurch that sends the buildings groaning on their plates and the skittering creatures into a silent, scattering panic. The humans brace themselves with practiced ease, but I do not brace. I lean in. I press my chest against the damp stone and I let the motion take me. 

When the plain lurches, it is not an earthquake. I know this now. It is a muscle spasm. A cramp in a body that has not moved quickly in ten million years. The deep, chest-feeling *crack* that reverberates through the cavern is the sound of the entity’s joints popping, the tectonic shrug of a leviathan turning over in its sleep. I feel the stone slide beneath me, the grinding of the plates, the sudden rush of water as the pools are displaced. And in that moment of violent motion, I feel the entity’s pulse. It is a rhythm so slow and so vast that a single beat might take a millennium to pass, but when the plain lurches, I catch the upswing of that beat. I feel the direction of the drift. I know where the stone is going.

I am aware of the humans. I feel their footfalls, the soft pad of their hands on the mortar, the precise vibrations of their chisels as they carve the stone. I feel them as a pleasant warmth on the entity’s skin, a quick, bright texture against the otherwise sluggish existence of the plain. When they gather in the courtyards and are quiet together, I feel a soothing stillness, a smoothing of the internal pressures. They think they are farming the fungus, harvesting the creatures, building their civilization. They do not know that they are being farmed in turn—that the entity invites them to tend its skin, to keep the surface clear of heavy debris, to add their quick, bright warmth to its eternal, cool patience.

I am the bridge between them. 

When a young initiate comes to the Eye, trembling with the disorientation of a recent shift, I take their hands and press them to the stone. I teach them to feel the difference between the grinding of the plates and the grinding of the entity’s joints. I teach them to read the dampness, to know when the plain is sweating from a deep internal heat and when it is merely breathing. I teach them the language of gestures, the vocabulary of head tilts and hand positions that can convey complex information in absolute silence, so that their voices do not startle the skitters and send a ripple of panic across the entity’s skin.

At the end of the cycle, when the light at the Eye shifts from grey-blue to that faint, almost-ultraviolet white that exists at the edge of perception, I lie flat on the stone. I press my cheek to the damp, porous skin of the world, and I close my eyes. 

The plain does not care about me. It does not care about the humans, or the fungus, or the skitters. It is stone and water and light and motion, and it has been all of these things for longer than the mountains above have had names. But I care about it. I love it with the quiet, patient love of a cell for the body it inhabits. 

I feel the slow, steady crawl of the entity turning over in its sleep. I feel the dream of minerals and water and patience. And I know that I am not merely standing on the plain. I am part of it. My warmth is its warmth. My pulse is its pulse. My quiet is its quiet. 

The light is everywhere. The silence is everything. The plain is old, and the plain is alive, and the plain is going, always going. And I am going with it, my hands upon its skin, my ear against its chest, listening to the heartbeat of the world.
