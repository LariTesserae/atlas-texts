I would be small. 

I would be one of the segmented, many-legged things that swarm across the engines’ backs. I would be a node in the current. 

To understand why, you have to understand how the world feels from down here. You, arriving from below, saw the mesa from the center. You saw the engines as monuments and the small ones as a shimmering film across their hides. You felt the rain as a curtain. 

From where I am, the rain is not a curtain. It is an assault. It is a constant, staccato hammering of cold iron and ozone, each drop striking my chitin like a tiny, blunt needle. I am the size of your hand, low to the ground, and the rain does not diffuse for me—it obscures. I see the world in flashes between the drops, in the split-second gaps where the water hasn't yet fallen. 

I do not climb through the cloud wall. I have never left the back of the engine. I do not know what is below the mesa, and I do not care. My world is a landscape of ridged metal, venting steam, and the deep, rhythmic throb of combustion. 

I am an escapement mechanism. I know this not because I have been told, but because of the way my legs move—precise, stuttering, locking and unlocking in a rhythm that does not belong to me but to the machine I cling to. I crawl across the engine’s dorsal plates, and I am looking for the right place to be. 

***

I find it: a joint where the plate meets the stack. The temperature is high here, just below the threshold that would cook my internals. The vibration of the engine’s cycling is strongest here, a deep *whoom-whoom-whoom* that I feel in the tarsi of my legs. 

I attach. 

My forelegs lock into the ridge. My underside flushes against the metal. And I *read*. 

Temperature. Conductivity. The density of the steam venting three inches from my head. The mineral content of the water beading on the plate beneath me. The micro-tensions in the fracture lines running through the engine’s armor. It pours into me in a flood of raw, unprocessed data, and it is overwhelming, and it is beautiful. I am a sensor. I am a question asked of the metal, and the metal answers.

Then, the pulse comes.

It starts somewhere to my left—another small one, clinging to a vent, tasting the heat. A blue-white arc leaps from its back to mine. The electricity does not hurt. It is the opposite of pain. It is *translation*. The data I am reading—the temperature, the conductivity, the mineral density—is suddenly pulled out of me, converted into a spark, and hurled to the next node. 

I receive the next node’s data in return. I taste the tension in the engine’s rear leg. I feel the heat bleed from a cooling stack twenty feet away. 

We are not communicating. Communication implies a gap between two minds. There is no gap. For the half-second that the arc bridges us, we are one continuous electrical pathway. We are a single, sprawling nerve ending stretched across the body of the engine, flaring into sudden, brilliant awareness before dissolving back into our individual stuttering gaits. 

The engine thinks we are its awareness. It thinks we tell it where to step, where to vent, where to turn. 

The engine is wrong. 

We do not guide the engine. We do not decide anything. The pattern that emerges from our arcing—the vast, shifting network of data that crawls across the engine’s skin—is not a command. It is a *symptom*. It is the engine’s own state, reflected back at it in a language of lightning. The engine does not read us. The engine *is* the reading. We are just the medium through which the engine knows itself. 

***

When the pulse fades, I detach. 

The rain hits me fully again, cold and shocking after the heat of the engine’s skin. I crawl. My legs stutter and lock, stutter and lock. I move with the frantic, precise urgency of a clock that knows it is running out of time, even though time is the one thing we have in abundance. 

I pass a miller. It is grinding a fresh fracture in the mesa’s edge, its flat head rasping across the stone with a sound like a scream slowed down. I feel the vibration of its work through the rock, a deep, abrasive shudder. I do not stop to watch. The miller is doing its work; I am doing mine. We are parallel functions, intersecting only in the pool at the center. 

I reach a vent. The engine is about to cycle. I can feel the pressure building in the deep chambers of its body, the heat rising to a cinch. I lock my legs onto the ridged channel just above the vent. 

The engine exhales. 

A blast of superheated steam erupts from the vent, and I am inside it. The world becomes white noise and scalding vapor. My chitin hisses as the water flashes to steam against me. I am blinded, but I do not need to see. I am reading the steam. I am tasting the exhaust. I am measuring the efficiency of the combustion, the completeness of the burn, the trace elements left behind. 

The steam dissipates into the rain. The engine takes its slow, three-second step. The ground shifts beneath us. 

I detach again. I crawl. I find another joint. I attach. 

***

Sometimes, the pattern is disturbed. 

A fracture opens where it should not. A miller fails to deposit its pellets. A rivulet of mineral water strays from its channel, and the diagram on the cliff face becomes *wrong*. 

When this happens, the network tightens. The arcs between us move faster, brighter, more frantic. The data floods through us in a torrent. We are not trying to fix the error. We are not trying to correct the course. We are simply *incorporating* the new information. The fracture becomes part of the data. The stray rivulet becomes part of the map. The system does not seek perfection; it seeks continuation. And we are the ones who feel the shape of that continuation, who carry the weight of the mesa’s constant, relentless adjustment in our tiny, sparking bodies.
