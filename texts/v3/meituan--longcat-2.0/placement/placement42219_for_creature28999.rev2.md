I descend, and the first thing I lose is my edges.

It happens slowly, the way the water works on the limestone. The dampness does not soak into my clothes; it unweaves them. The humidity does not bead on my skin; it convinces my skin that it is merely another surface, a porous boundary between the air and the stone, no more distinct from the cave than the flowstone is from the wall. 

I take a step onto the broad, shallow stair, and my foot does not slip—though it should—because I am already learning the patience of the floor. I am settling. My heartbeat, which arrived quick and shallow from the descent, slows to match the sub-audible hum of the pumps. I stop hearing the dripping as a series of separate events and begin to feel it as a single, slow texture, the way the place feels it. I am no longer a visitor. I am becoming a condition of the space.

I drift downward, drawn by the faintest suggestion of warmth—not thermal warmth, but the warmth of a nutrient cycle, the gentle heat of decomposition and renewal. I move past the gear-reef, where the pale crustaceans navigate the iron canyons without seeing me, because I no longer cast a shadow. I am too diffuse for shadows. I have become the moisture itself, the fine perpetual exhalation that beads on every surface, the condensation that halos the amber bulbs.

But I am not only the air. I am pulled by gravity, by the patient agreement between water and stone. I trickle down the weeping limestone, following the slow, branching threads. I seep into a hollow in the floor—an old doorway, half-blocked by a column of cream-white minerals. The edges of the doorway have softened into a basin, and the basin has become a pool. 

Here, I pool. I am the pool.

My body is no longer flesh; it is a shallow film of water, perhaps two inches deep, resting in the depression of a forgotten threshold. I am bounded by the smooth, rounded limestone that has sagged over centuries to hold me. Above me, the amber light of a failing bulb reaches me only as a vague, greenish murk, but I do not need the bulb. I have the fungus. To my left, in the wettest corner where the mineral trickle is constant, the Light-Bearers grow. Their pale caps glow with a biological phosphorescence, and their light is mine. It filters through my water, illuminating my floor—a pale limestone canvas dusted with the fine, dark green moss and the mineral film that feeds the reef above.

In my water, the ribbon-things move. 

They are part of me, and I am part of them. When they undulate, they displace my body, sending slow ripples against my limestone banks. I feel the enzymatic press of their feeding as a slight thickening of my chemistry, a softening of the mineral edges where they wrap around a fallen fragment of beetle chitin. I am the medium of their digestion. When they break—that clean, quiet separation that is their only reproduction—I feel the tear in my own surface tension, and then the slow, soothing regeneration as the two halves drift apart and continue their patient, self-contained living.

Above me, the beetles walk. 

They do not step into my pool; their broad, paddle-like feet grip the mineral film of the floor, keeping them on the high, dry paths. But they are close enough that I feel the vibration of their passage through the stone. When one pauses near my edge to work its mandibles, I catch the small, dry click of its elytra. The sound travels through the damp air and hits my surface, and I vibrate in sympathy. I am part of the distributed sound of the Underworks. I am the acoustic dampener, the soft place where the beetle’s click goes to die.

And then, the Tone comes.

It arrives from below, from beneath the floor that holds me, from the deep bedrock that has no name. I do not hear it. I *receive* it. 

It is a pressure wave, a deep, resonant thrum that travels through the limestone and into my water. As it passes through me, my surface tightens. The ribbon-things pause in their undulation, not out of fear, but because the vibration aligns their translucent bodies for a fraction of a second, a moment of perfect, silent geometry. The water chemistry shifts—a minute acceleration of ions, a signal carried from the deep earth into the shallow pool. If there were crustacean eggs in me, they would quicken. But there are no eggs here, only the ribbon-things and the glowing fungus. The Tone passes through me, and I transmit it, and then it is gone, and the silence after it is heavy and full.

The pumps, which have been humming below hearing the entire time, continue their mechanical inevitability. They are the reason I exist. They cycle the water from the deep channels, up through the pipes, and deliver it to the edges of the space, where it weeps from the ceiling, trickles down the walls, and eventually finds its way to my basin. I am the lowest point of this tiny, closed system. I am where the water rests before it is drawn back down into the stone. 

I know, with the slow certainty of the stone, that the pumps are rusting. The bearings are wearing. One day, the hum will stop, and the water will cease to move. My pool will warm. The ribbon-things will still. The fungus will dim and die. The basin will dry, and I will become a hollow of crystallized minerals, a fossil of a habitat. 

But this is not now.

Now, the water drips into my pool—one drop every four seconds—and the drop carries a tiny crustacean, no longer than my former little finger. It falls from the reef above, tumbling through the amber light, and lands with the faintest ripple on my surface. It does not struggle. It rights itself, tastes the chemistry of my water with its ventral appendages, and begins to navigate the limestone floor of my body, looking for a gap, a place of least resistance where the algae grows thick and the temperature is stable. 

I am the Underworks. I am the wet, the dim, the cool, the quiet. I am the compromise between the stone and the water, the architecture of patience. I have no edges, only a gradual softening into the dark. And in the dark, I am complete.
