I would not be the walker. The walker is too temporary, too defined by the going. I would not be the watcher on the last hill, because the hill is already gone, and the watcher is just a delay. 

I would be the stain on their hands.

I begin as a green smear beneath a fingernail. A residue of the moss-harvest, the dark juice of it pressed into the whorls of a thumb. I am the mark of the plain on the skin of the city. I am what remains when the work is done and the hands are hung by the door to dry.

But I am not just the green. I am the dark, too—the fine, silicate dust of the gneiss, ground from the chisel’s edge, worked into the cracks of their knuckles. I am the pale, fungal spore that clings to the cuff of a sleeve, drifted up from the deep chambers on a current of warm, un-stone air. I am the tricolor of their existence: the green of the patient, the dark of the enduring, the pale of the deep. I am the evidence of the conversation.

I live in the creases of their palms. When they wrap their fingers around the shaft of a hammer, I am the friction that keeps the tool from slipping. When they kneel beside a pool to clear the channel, I am the grit that abrades the algae from the fitted stone. I am the callus and the crack. I am the interface between their warm, quick blood and the cold, slow world that presses in from all sides.

I am there when they scrape the moss from the streets. I am the resistance they feel through the blade—the slight, hydraulic push of the moss against the steel, the way it gives, reluctantly, and then springs back the moment the pressure is released. I am the sound it makes: a wet, tearing whisper, the sound of a détente being briefly interrupted. I am the green thread left behind in the crack, the one they missed, the one that will be a patch again by the next moon. I am the persistence they clear away, and I am the persistence that returns.

I am there when they eat. I am the dark crumb of moss-bread that falls to the table, the pale smear of fungus on the lip. I am the mineral taste on their tongues, the faint sweetness that might be real or might be imagination. I am the sufficiency. I am the thing that keeps the heart beating at its slow, flat rhythm, the rhythm that matches the wind from the east, the rhythm that matches the breathing of the plain.

I am there when they go down. Down into the deep chambers, where the air is warm and the walls are ridged and the silence has texture. I am the white dust that coats their shoulders when they brush against the un-stone surfaces. I is the spore that travels with them, down into the dark, and the spore that travels back up, into the grey light, carrying with it the faint, warm breath of the thing that waits below. I am the connection between the surface and the depth, the quick and the ancient, the known and the unasked.

I am there when they touch the walls. As they walk the narrow, curving streets, their hands drag along the cold gneiss, and I am the oil of their skin left behind on the stone, the microscopic shedding of their humanity onto the basement of the world. I am the greeting. I am the reassurance. I am the habit so old it has become indistinguishable from prayer. I am the proof that they were here, that they passed this way, that they existed in this place and in this time, brief and warm against the endless, cold duration of the plain.

I am not the moss. I am not the stone. I am not the water, or the wind, or the thing in the deep. I am the place where they meet. I am the stain. I am the residue of the mutual accommodation, the physical record of the long, slow détente between the quick and the patient. 

I am the mark they leave on the world, and the mark the world leaves on them. I am the conversation, written in green and dark and pale, on the only canvas that matters: the hands that do the work. 

And when they are gone—when the cities are gone, when the bread and the tools and the language are gone—I will still be here. Not on their hands, for their hands will have returned to the stone. But in the stone itself. The green will have filled the cracks. The dark will have absorbed the pale. The plain will have remembered them, the way it remembers everything: slowly, patiently, without judgment, without grief, without end.

I will be the memory of the stain. And the moss will grow back over me, and the moss always grows back.
