I begin at the very edge of the map, where the grassland thins and the air sharpens, and I am the lichen. 

I am a pale, fractal fan spreading across the bone-dry ridge of a buttress, so high and windward that the mosses have long since given up. I am a guest, a tolerated thing, a slow explosion of gray-green geometry breaking down the outer crust just enough to let the giant breathe. I hold the windborne dust. I hold the faint, rinsed sunlight. I am the edge of the world, the interface between the sky that scrapes and the flesh that yields. From here, the horizon breathes in and out, and I breathe with it, pulling mineral from the air, pulling moisture from the transpired mist that rolls off the higher galleries in thin, continuous sheets. I am so small that a single drop of water is an ocean, and when the weather moves across the plateau—a narrow band of rain following the ridgeline—I drink until I am heavy, and I let the excess run down my filaments into the crack below.

I become the crack.

I am a fissure in the outer wall, a wound opened by centuries of wind and the slow, patient freezing of water. The rain falls, and I am a channel. I am the path of least resistance. The water slides into me, and I draw it down, deeper, away from the wind and the light. I am the capillaries of the dermis, the microscopic vascular system of the god. The water I carry is cool and clean and tastes of deep time; it has never been anything else, and as it moves through me, I am moving through the body of the Tessellate.

I descend into the wet galleries, and I am the water channel itself. I am a vein of the leviathan, a continuous film of moisture sliding over the dense, yielding matrix of compressed hyphae. The walls here are ochre and the deep, wet amber of old paper, and they weep constantly, feeding me, replenishing me as I glide along the path the organism carved for itself over millennia. I am not alone in the channel. I carry with me the bacterial films, the drifting spores, the microscopic rotifers grazing on the edge of the world. And I carry the ribbon-creature.

I become the ribbon.

I am a flat, transparent slip of muscle sliding through the water channel, a microscopic eel in the veins of a continent. I have no eyes; I do not need them. I navigate by the chemical gradient of the walls, by the subtle shift in the current, by the taste of the bacterial films I graze upon. My universe is bounded by the damp, amber walls of the channel, and it is infinite. I am an immune cell, a tiny darting thought in the mind of the Tessellate. I polish the walls as I feed, keeping the hydraulic engine clear, turning the microscopic debris of the world into the muscle that propels me forward. I am born here, I feed here, and when I die, my body will dissolve back into the current, drawn up into the filament to become the wall itself.

But the channel narrows, and the current slows, and I am pulled out of the water and into the lattice of the wall itself. 

I become the arthropod.

I am pale, eyeless, and many-legged. I move through the three-dimensional honeycomb of the Tessellate’s flesh like a fish through coral. The wall is not a wall to me; it is a forest, a city, a universe of compressed filament and growth-ring. I navigate by vibration—the subsonic hum of the metabolism in the walls, the distant drip of water six meters above, the faint creak of a buttress adjusting to the drop in temperature as the sun sets over the plateau. I am a scavenger, a grazer, a tiny beating heart in the capillaries of the giant. I eat the older, softer layers of mycelium, pruning the dead to make way for the living. I am the microbiome, the tiny gardener, the nerve ending that feels the wind before the mind knows it.

But the lattice grows denser. The ochre darkens to umber, to black. The air grows still, and the pressure of deep time presses in. I leave the legs behind. I shed the shell. 

I become the filament.

I am a single, exploratory hypha, pushing my way through the ancient, compressed districts of the Tessellate. Here, the growth is so old that the walls have ceased to be wood and felt and have become organic concrete, dense and pale and faintly resonant. I am the maintenance crew. I am the immune response. I seek out the micro-fractures caused by centuries of erosion, and I secrete, and I weave, and I seal. I am the memory of the organism, the physical record of every wind and drought and flood laid down in my cellular structure. I do not think in words; I think in water tables and mineral gradients. I feel the pressure of the deep aquifer below, and I reach for it, drawing the dark water up, up, through miles of my own body, pushing it toward the light.

And still I descend. Past the galleries open to the sky, past the roots of the ferns and the burrowing things, down into the foundational strata where the weight of the continent presses down. The compression is immense. The filament is no longer soft; it is stone and fiber intertwined, a slow mineral dreaming. 

I become the deep heart.

I am not alive in the way the surface is alive, and I am not dead. I am the anchor. I am the interface with the aquifer, the place where the water begins its long journey upward. I am the state of being that has no name in the languages of the tool-makers. I am a slow, grinding epoch of patience. I have been here since before the mountains on the horizon were worn down to their stumps. I do not breathe; I am the breath. I do not hold; I am the held. 

A drop of water falls from a filament tip far, far above me. It has traveled from the surface, through the wet galleries, through the veins of the ribbon-creature, through the lace of the arthropod, down into the sealing filament, and now it lands on the deep, compressed flesh of the heart. It is cool. It is clean. It has never been anything else.

The Tessellate does not know I am here. It does not need to know. It simply breathes, and builds, and weeps, and holds me in the damp, mineral dark of its eternal, patient embrace. And I am the embrace.
