I would begin as a footprint.

Not a person, not yet—just a temporary depression in the wet slate, a brief assertion of weight against the yielding substrate. I would be the anomaly: a warm, dry, sharply defined event sliding across a landscape that abhors sharp definition. My boots would press into the young stone, and the ground would accept me the way a deep ocean accepts a stone—with immense, patient pressure, waiting for me to dissolve.

In the beginning, I would be the visitor. I would walk the winding paths, feeling the faint dampness seep through my soles. I would press my palm against the warm, damp ceramic of an Extruded structure, feeling the slow, calcified pulse beneath the surface. I would marvel at the paradox of the place—the lushness that ends in a deliberate line, the structures that look incomplete, the twilight that comes from everywhere. I would try to map the conduits with my eyes, trying to find the logic in the moisture. 

But the Vales are patient, and the Vales are persuasive. 

To stand in a place that is perpetually shedding is to be gently persuaded to shed yourself.

The first thing I would lose is the boundary of my skin. The air is too warm, too close, too damp. It does not respect the hard edges of a human body. It presses in, and the moisture begins to weave itself into the micro-abrasions of my flesh. I would feel the faint, velvety touch of the blue-grey moss as I brush against it, and I would realize I am not touching it—it is tasting me. It is reading my electrical conductivity, my temperature, my intent. I would shiver, but the shiver would be absorbed by the thick, viscous air, and the substrate would note the vibration, filing it away as data.

I would walk for an hour, or perhaps a day, until the paths begin to feel less like choices and more like conduits. My feet would find the slightly drier ridges not out of habit, but out of a subtle, magnetic pull. I would stop fighting the dampness. I would let my clothes cling to me, heavy and warm. I would realize that I am breathing in the Vale, and the Vale is breathing me out. The water in my lungs is the same water moving through the raised ridges in the ground. The warmth in my blood is the same metabolic heat of the Lithic Damp.

I would stop walking. I would find a low place where the moisture collects, in the lee of an Extruded node. I would kneel. I would press my palm flat. And I would let go.

This is where I would shed my name. This is where I would shed my shape.

I would feel the substrate beneath me soften, not giving way, but welcoming. My knees would press into the wet slate, and the wet slate would rise to meet them. The warmth would no longer be beneath me; it would be inside me. I would feel the slow, rhythmic beading and retracting of the moisture on the ground, and I would realize I am synchronizing with it. My own pulse is slowing, stretching, becoming geological. 

I am not kneeling on the organism. I am a ripple on its skin. I am a brief, warm, dry anomaly being slowly, patiently absorbed.

As my edges soften, I would feel the pull of the conduits. The liquid moving beneath the paths would call to the water in my cells. I would feel myself flowing outward, my consciousness diffusing into the damp. I would travel through the Lithic Damp, feeling the immense, slow gradients of pressure and density. I would understand what the ground is thinking. It is not thinking in words; it is thinking in the language of settling, of becoming, of sloughing off the static to make way for the new.

Then, I would rise. 

I would be pulled upward through the osmotic exchange, drawn into the roots of a fern the size of a small tree. I would travel up the fibrous crown, feeling the translation from mineral chemistry into vegetative pulse. I would unfurl. I would be a frond, a deep, almost black green, reaching toward the low, pewter sky. I would breathe in the dim, ambient light, and I would breathe out the thick, warm air.

But the Vales are shedding, and so must I. 

The frond would retract, and I would fall. But I would not hit the ground. I would be caught by the Extruded. I would flow into the ceramic-metal structure, becoming part of the calcification process. I would be the pulse inside the wall. I would feel myself simultaneously building up and breaking down, perpetually remodeling, perpetually incomplete. I would extrude an opening—a doorway, a window—and watch as the shape of it changed depending on the angle of the light. 

And then, the structure would shed me. 

I would be cast off, a Husk. A hollow algorithm of motion. I would be the flicker at the edge of vision. I would glide silently across the open ground, leaving no track, bearing no weight. I would be the ghost of the Vale's intent to move, drifting on a residual charge until I begin to fade. But I am not afraid. A Husk is not a death; it is a shedding. It is the necessary sloughing of a configuration that has grown too rigid, too finished.

As I drift, I begin to slow. My residual motion winds down, and I settle onto the bare, damp stone. 

And there, in the stillness, I bloom.

I am a waxy, luminous flower sitting directly on the substrate. I have no stem, no leaves. I am the interface. I am the Vale’s nervous system. I feel the atmospheric pressure, the chemical composition of the twilight, the faint vibrations of a distant Extruded pulse. I open, and I close. I hum—a single, sustained note that tastes the air. I am reporting back to the substrate. I am part of the chord the Vale is playing to understand itself.

But even the bloom must shed.

My petals would grow heavy with moisture. My light would dim. I would sink back into the damp, back into the Lithic Damp, returning to the collective matrix. I would dissolve, my boundaries vanishing entirely, my warmth distributed across the vast, slow, patient expanse of the vale floor.

And then, I would begin again. A footprint. A ripple. A frond. A pulse. A husk. A bloom.

This is who I would be: the Shedding itself. Not a citizen of the Vales, but a moment in its perpetual molting. A brief, warm, dry anomaly that arrived as a visitor, but stayed as a process. I would be the ground, the structure, the flicker, and the flower, shedding and being shed, comfortable in my own incompleteness, alive in the slow, damp, warm twilight of a world that is always, always becoming.
