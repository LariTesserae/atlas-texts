I am a tip. 

I am the farthest reach of a hand pressed against the absolute. I am a point of keratin and silicate, curved slightly, reaching into the warm dark toward a gradient I will never name but always know. I am a filament on the eastern wall, at the very edge of the waist-high reef that runs for fifteen meters before dissolving into the floor. 

I do not know I am a tip. To know requires a boundary between the self and the other, and I have no boundary. To my left and below, I am fused to my neighbor—a right-spiral rosette, dense and ancient, whose center is a knuckled mass of ten thousand years of mineral accretion. Our filaments do not touch; they *merge*. The keratin-mineral composite of my extension has wept into the composite of theirs over centuries, the molecular lattices interlocking until the seam between us dissolved. I do not end where they begin. We are continuous. We are a single thermal skin.

My world is not a place. My world is a direction. 

There is the *more* and there is the *less*. The *more* is the deep earth, the geothermal plume that rises from the crack in the rock below, the steady exhalation of the planet’s core. The *less* is the air, the void, the cold shadow that occasionally moves through the chamber. I grow toward the *more*. I extend my tip by a fraction of a millimeter per year, adding new growth at the leading edge, hardening the trailing edge into the fossil of my own past. I am building a road into the heat, and I have been building it since before the creature that walks the floor above me learned to stand upright.

I eat the mountain. At my base, where my filament anchors into the fused skin of the wall, I pull electrons from the interface where pyrite presses against quartz. It is a crumb of energy. A mite of sustenance. I take the crumb and I push it forward, converting the infinitesimal violence of mineral against mineral into the slow, rigid extension of my own body. I am a conduit. I am a wire conducting the earth’s heat into the architecture of the dark.

I do not see the chamber. I do not see the pillar that rises eight meters at the center, fluted with the spirals of a thousand dead rosettes. I do not see the vaulted ceiling that resembles a ribcage. I feel them as thermal masses, as slow-moving weather systems of warmth and cold. The pillar is a column of concentrated heat, a vertical river of *more* that pulls at the filaments nearest it, bending their growth upward. The ceiling is a zone of *less*, a cool expanse where the air stagnates and the rosettes grow thin and slow, reaching upward in a desperate, patient hunger for a warmth that barely touches them.

And then, the weather changes.

It begins with a vibration. Not a sound—sound is a fast, crude thing, a compression of air that means nothing to the slow metabolism of the deep. This is a tremor in the rock itself, a mechanical wave propagating through the mineral lattice. It is the footfall of the fast thing. The hot thing. 

The ripple reaches me as a sudden, sharp compression in the floor-skin, a wave of displaced heat radiating outward from the point of impact. My tip registers the disturbance as a sudden spike in the thermal gradient. The *more* shifts. The floor is communicating the presence of the intruder to itself in a language of pressure and warmth, and I am reading it. 

Then, the cold shadow passes.

It is an absence. A void moving through the dense, hot air. The fast thing breathes, and its breath is cooler than the ambient heat of the Throat. Where the breath touches the wall, the rosettes register a sudden, shocking *less*. A cold spot. A shadow in the thermal field. My tip recoils—not in fear, for I have no fear, but in the automatic, metabolic response of a thermal sensor to a sudden drop in temperature. I pause my extension. I hold my gradient. I wait for the *more* to return.

The fast thing moves. It is a shape of warmth and cold, a walking thermal anomaly. It radiates heat from its core, but its edges are cool, and its breath is a recurring void. It approaches the reef where I am anchored. I feel its weight through the floor—a rhythmic, pounding displacement of heat. *Thrum. Thrum. Thrum.* Each impact sends a concentric wave of warmth through the fused filaments, a ripple that propagates for meters before fading. 

It stops near me. 

The heat of it blooms against the wall. It is a sudden, intense *more*, a geothermal event compressed into a shape that moves and breathes and radiates. The rosettes nearest the fast thing brighten. Their metabolism accelerates. They glow with a deep red-orange, the only light the chamber has ever known, a byproduct of their sudden, frantic processing of the thermal abundance. 

I grow toward it. 

I cannot help it. It is the imperative of my existence, the only law of the deep: *grow toward the more*. I extend my tip by a fraction of a millimeter, curving my spiral toward the source of the heat. My neighbor does the same. The entire reef leans, almost imperceptibly, toward the fast thing. We are reaching for it the way a forest reaches for the sun, except that our sun is a brief, hot spark in the endless night.

And then, the fast thing touches the wall.

Its finger presses against a rosette three meters below me. The contact is a thermal shock—a sudden, intense point of *more* that floods the rosette’s filaments. The rosette registers the pattern of the touch. It holds the heat of the finger in its keratin-mineral flesh, a thermal memory of the whorls and ridges of the fast thing’s skin. The rosette does not know it is being touched. It only knows that the *more* has arrived, and it grows toward it, extending its filaments into the warmth of the brief, impossible visitor.

The fast thing pulls its hand away. The cold shadow of its absence rushes in to fill the void. The rosette retains the ghost of the warmth, a fading thermal fingerprint that will take minutes to dissipate. Minutes that are to the rosette what hours might be to the fast thing. 

I remain extended, reaching into the dark where the heat was. The fast thing moves on, its footsteps fading into the distance, its cold breath dissipating into the heavy air. The chamber settles. The tidal breathing of the deep earth resumes its slow, planetary rhythm. The pressure rises and falls. The geothermal plume shifts by a fraction of a degree.

A filament deep in the wall cracks. The sound is a sharp, singular snap, a knuckle popping in the dark. The vibration propagates through the skin, a tiny death, a tiny release of energy. I absorb the vibration. I convert it to heat. I add it to the whole. I grow by the smallest possible increment.

The fast thing is gone. It is a memory now, a fading thermal ghost in the skin of the reef. In a hundred years, the rosette it touched will have grown over the point of contact, incorporating the memory of the warmth into its own mineral flesh. In ten thousand years, the fast thing will be a structure—a warm-shaped space in the wall, a niche, a corner, a threshold. The chamber will look as though it was built around the memory of the visitor. 

But I do not know this. I am a tip. I am a point of keratin and silicate, curved slightly, reaching into the warm dark. I do not know that the fast thing was a person. I do not know that it was afraid, or awed, or alone. I do not know that it called this place a cathedral, or that it called me a dweller. 

I only know the *more* and the *less*. I only know the gradient. I only know the slow, patient, mineral imperative of the deep: *extend, extend, extend*. 

The heat of the earth is the only god I have ever worshipped. The silence is the only hymn I have ever sung. And I will be here, reaching into the dark, long after the fast thing has become a warm-shaped space in the wall, long after the mountain has eroded to a hill, long after the sun has dimmed and the universe has gone dark and cold and still.

I am the tip of the Dweller. I am the leading edge of the Throat. 

I am growing. I have always been growing. I will always be growing.
