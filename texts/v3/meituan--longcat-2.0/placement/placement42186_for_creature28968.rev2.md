I lie flat on rock that was solid stone within living memory.

The warmth comes up through the stone and into the bones of my back and I cannot tell where the rock ends and I begin. My spine is not entirely mine. The base of it — the vertebrae that hold my skull up, that let me stand, that let me turn my head to catch the direction of a tremor — these belong to a woman who died when I was eleven. The rootsayers took her from the floor where she had been laid in a resin flow, and they lifted the vertebrae from her packed bones, and they set them among my own young spine, and they sealed the junction with living resin, and they waited, and the graft took. The graft always takes. The plant says *yes* to every joining.

Her name-sign is two hands cupped at the ear, then turned outward. *The hollow, listening.* She chose it when her own hearing went, young, before the children were born, and it was prophetic — she spent the rest of her life reading the valley through her spine, lying flat on the living floor and letting the country speak through her bones. I have worn her name for nine years now and it fits me better than my birth-sign did. My birth-sign was something about seeds, about throwing. I am a seed no longer. I am a listener with a dead woman's bones, posted to the Soft Edge to read the direction of the making.

---

The Soft Edge is not a place. It is a rate. A speed of becoming.

I lie on naked dark rock that sweated itself into existence months ago and is still warm from the effort, still cooling, still cracking occasionally with a sound like a knuckle being popped by a god. Around me the green pours in with a frontier mania — pioneer mats stitching new ground in centimeters per day, defoliator swarms testing the air for the chemical signature of unclaimed space, lamp-moth larvae dragging their glow-rope across the dark like a question. The walls here are raw. The floor here is bare. Nothing on this side of the last village is older than a season except the rock itself, and the rock is newborn, still hot in its seams, still deciding what it wants to be.

I am the only person out here. Behind me, half a day's walk through linked galleries, the people live inside the roar the way you live inside a heartbeat — the click-swarms like weather, the seed-cannons like gunfire, the mill-beasts like geology. Here, at the edge, the roar is thinner. Newer. Still finding its register. The click-swarms have not fully colonized these halls; their drone is patchy, breaking, reforming, and in the gaps between one swarm's territory and the next I can hear the raw sound of rock becoming room.

It is not silence. Silence is the Burnt Hall, the wound, the cost, the pilgrimage. This is something else: the sound of something not yet finished being loud. The sound of a world clearing its throat before it speaks.

---

What I feel through her bones:

The gravel of a mill-beast highway, two halls over and behind me, the beast plowing its stone gizzard through the living floor as it has done for decades, unhurried, followed by its dependent villages like a shadow. The drum-language of the people, coded phrases beaten on the exposed root-butts of the architecture-plants, carried through the root-net like signal-wire, faster than walking. The distant gunfire of seed-cannons, the desultory volley from the shrub-line. The elastic squeal of a resin river cooling somewhere in the deep green. The wind that is not wind — the air movement of a billion wings, the click-swarms' migration, a tide of sound that rises and falls like breathing.

Under it all, threaded through everything like the bass-line of a song I can almost remember: the patient rhythm.

---

The eldest back-listeners say the rhythm has a direction. It is not random. It moves through the deep rock like a thought moves through a body — slowly, with purpose, patient as growth. *Something is digging toward us,* they say, *or we are growing toward it,* and the difference is a matter of which plant you ask.

But I lie flat on the warm rock of the Soft Edge and I feel it through her bones, through our bones, through the place where she ends and I do not begin, and I do not think it is a direction. I think it is an appetite.

The Hollows are young. Nothing here is older than ten years except the floor and its dead centuries of sealed resin, layer on layer on layer of former forests gone down and hardened into autobiography. The galleries are still opening. The halls are still being born. The green is still pouring. And the rhythm I feel is not the sound of something coming toward us. It is the sound of something eating.

The plant or the plants — the rootsayers say one, the heretics say two, and the grammar does not distinguish — are eating the rock. The rooms are the spaces between its teeth. The digger is not ahead of us. The digger is us. We are the mouth of something and the green world is its tongue and the roar is the sound of it chewing and the light of the Roof is the blood of the rock spent overhead — the heat of the forming world drawn up through roots and spent as illumination, an inverted sun, a star that burns downward through its own body.

This is not what the rootsayers teach. But when you lie flat on rock that is still warm from being born and you feel the patient rhythm of what is digging with a dead woman's vertebrae pressed against your own, you arrive at conclusions the theological cannot hold.

---

Here, in the gap between the old roar and the new, I make the sign. Both hands cupped, as if holding a bowl of air up to the ear — the hollow — then opened, empty. *Listen to what is missing.* The dead woman taught me this, or I taught her, or the graft taught it to both of us, and the grammar of the Hollows does not distinguish.

What is missing here is what has not yet arrived: the full sound of a finished hall, the deep bass of a click-swarm drone that has found its key, the settled percussion of a mill-beast highway that has been walking the same route for decades. The lampmoths have not yet colonized these galleries — no one to sing the blue register, no one to rearrange the soundscape around their voice during dimsleep. The salt-leaf hillside is bare rock here, unmirrored, no aimed brightness, no gardens of reflected light. The trees have not leaned their mirrors toward their neighbors. There are no neighbors. There is only rock and green and the thin new roar and the warm stone and me and the dead woman's bones and the patient rhythm of what is digging.

What is missing is the future tense. The Soft Edge has no future tense. Everything is *now, now, now.*

---

I will lie here until the pioneer mats reach me.

They are coming — centimeters per day, a green tide stitching the naked rock, and when they reach my edges they will take my warmth as a signal and begin to build around me, over me, through the gaps between my fingers, and I will become part of the floor's autobiography. A layer in the sealed century under century under century. And one day, years from now or decades, a young back-listener will lie flat on top of me and read through their own borrowed bones the same patient rhythm, and they will make the sign, and they will listen to what is missing, and what is missing will be me — the way I am missing the dead woman, the way the dead woman is missing the hall she never heard open, the way the whole bright roaring green world is missing the silence it traded for life and never got back.

The digger is patient. The digger is hungry. The digger is us.

I lie flat. I read. The rock is warm. The rhythm continues.

*Now. Now. Now.*
