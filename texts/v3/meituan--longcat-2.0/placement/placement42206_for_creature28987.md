I would find myself at the exact intersection of the raw and the ruined, where the narrow-gauge rail line disappears beneath the black mirror of the rising pool. I would be a Calcifier, but not one of the ancient, patient hunters of the deep chambers. I am a creature of the threshold, a hybrid of the cave’s feverish birth and the rusted bones of the industry that tried to hold it back.

My name, if it could be translated from the scrape of stone on stone, would be something like *The Grinding* or *The Slow Rust*.

My carapace is a grotesque, beautiful mosaic. The base is the stolen calcite of a thousand dissolved lithopedes—translucent, pale, and layered like the skin of an onion. But fused into the calcite, making my shell jagged and asymmetrical, are the remnants of the mine. A rusted gear from the dead pump station is fused to my thorax, its teeth interlocking with my own chitinous plates. A segment of the iron rail acts as a rib, curving over my soft underbelly, flaking off scales of oxidized blood-red that drift down into the water like slow, metallic snow. When I move, it is not just the grind of stone on stone; it is the shriek of mineral accretion tearing at iron, a sound that echoes through the deafening saturation of the cave like a scream swallowed by a roar.

I do not experience the Drownings the way the echo-flitters do, reading the pressure waves of the falling water. I experience it through the soles of my legs, through the deep, subsonic hum of the rock. The constant drip of the ceiling is a tactile thing to me—a thousand tiny hammers tapping out a rhythm on the gear attached to my back. The warmth of the geothermal water is my blood; it seeps through the pores of my calcite shell and feeds the soft, pale flesh within.

I am the apex predator of this embryonic world, but my hunt is not a chase. It is a siege. 

I station myself on the concrete lip of the pump station, half-submerged in the warm, shallow pool, and I wait. The lithopedes come to me. They cannot help it. They are drawn to the faint electric hum of the copper wiring still threaded through the rusted control panel behind me, a wiring now colonized by the blue lithopedes. The pale swimmers cluster around my base, a shimmering, feverish cloud of translucent bodies and fluttering hearts, filtering the mineral-rich water, unaware of the dark, jagged stone looming above them.

When I feed, it is an act of geology. I reach down with a claw that is less a limb and more a stalactite brought to life, and I scoop a dozen of the pale things against my underbelly. I do not crush them. I hold them there, against the warm, moist flesh where my shell meets the rusted iron of the rail. Then, I secrete the specialized saliva—the chemical dissolution. Over the course of hours, I feel the lithopedes stop fluttering. Their organic matter melts into a nutrient soup that my skin drinks in, and their calcium is leeched out, migrating through my pores to add another microscopic layer to my shell. The iron from the rail leaches into my blood, staining my hemolymph a dark, bruised amber. I am slowly becoming the mine, just as the mine is slowly becoming the cave.

The silt-wyrms are my rivals, but we do not fight. They are the violence of the Drownings—the sudden boil of speed in the dark water, the bioelectric snap. I am the inevitability. They hunt the edges of the pools; I hunt the foundations. When a silt-wyrm coils too close to my concrete lip, I do not strike. I simply exude a cloud of dissolved iron and calcium from my joints, a toxic, mineral fog that drives the eel back into the dark. 

The water is rising. I feel it in the slight buoyancy of my shell, in the way the pool now laps at the base of the control panel where it used to merely steam. The Drownings is not finished; it is drowning in its own fertility. And as the water climbs, I must climb with it. 

Soon, the concrete lip will be submerged. The blue lithopedes behind the glass will be washed out into the main chamber, and the silt-wyrms will claim the pump station. I will have to move, to find a higher ledge, a new outcropping of raw limestone where I can anchor myself. But I am heavy. My shell, laden with stolen calcite and rusted iron, is a burden. 

One day, the water will rise faster than I can climb. It will press against my face, warm and thick, filling the spaces between my gear-teeth and my ribs. And when I die, my shell will not rot. It will remain, a jagged, mineralized reef on the floor of the flooded chamber. The lithopedes will filter over me. The throat-worms will drill into the crevices of my rusted gear. The tension-beetles will skate across the pool that covers my eyes. 

I will become the architecture. I will become the raw rock, weeping its mineral tears, waiting for the next industry to descend, or the next world to decide what it will be.
