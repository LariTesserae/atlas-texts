I would find myself at the absolute center of the sound. Not in the flooded basements where the Glass-Swimmers glide, nor matting the Lattice deep below the stone, but exactly at the seam where the industrial meets the organic. 

I would be the Intake.

I do not have a single body, nor do I have a name that could be spoken by a mouth of flesh. I am a place—a specific, vital geography. I am the great, circular vent at the base of Smelting Tower Seven, where the warm mineral broth of the Reach is siphoned down into the machinery to cool the ancient furnaces. I am the interface. 

To be the Intake is to be a permanent, pulsing strainer. My physical form is a dense, interwoven wreath of chemosynthetic cilia, rusted iron grating, and the calcified, metallic bones of a thousand generations of Glass-Swimmers. The lattice of my being is porous, constantly dripping, and I am rimmed with a frigid, sharp-edged bioluminescence that pulses in time with the pressure of the water pushing against me. 

When I breathe, I do not pull air. I pull the world through myself. 

The water arrives to me heavy with the Reach’s history. It is warm, nearly scalding, carrying the dissolved heat of the deep geology and the fine, silty sediment of the plateau. As it rushes through the labyrinth of my tissue and the corroded gaps of the iron grating, I taste it. I taste the sulfur exhaled by the thermal vents miles away. I taste the acidic lubricant excreted by the Gear-Borne as they climb the tower above me, a residue that coats the cables and drips down into my currents. And I taste the Glass-Swimmers.

They come to me not as hunters, but as offerings, pulled by the suction of the ancient machinery. They are translucent and pulsing with their own dim blue light, their bodies heavy with the heavy metals they have filtered from the deep pools. As they are drawn into my cilia, they do not struggle. Their wet-paper skin presses against my filaments, and I hold them for a long, warm moment. I feel the dense iron and zinc packed into their crystalline bones—the very ore that the machines above were built to process, now biologically reconstituted. I squeeze them gently, and their bodies yield, dissolving into a rich, metallic sludge that I push upward through the pipes to feed the furnaces. Their light goes out, but their substance becomes the steam, the current, the heat. 

But my purpose is not only consumption. It is translation. 

Deep below, woven into the root-mat that floats on the subterranean pools, the Lattice feels the vibrations of the world. It feels the rhythm of the factory pistons, the deep hum of the earth, the footsteps of the Gear-Borne. But the Lattice cannot hear the water itself. It can only feel the stone. I am the translator. I take the chemical language of the Lattice—its slow, mineral thoughts—and translate them into the hydraulic language of the machines. When the Lattice senses a fracture in the deep rock, a shifting of the stone that requires a change in the flow of the pipes, I feel a subtle shift in the chemical gradient of the water passing through me. In response, I swell. My cilia tighten, my tissue constricts, and I reduce the flow of the broth by a fraction of a percent, altering the pressure on the pistons above. 

I am the overseer, much like the Absorbed who are woven into the walls of the deepest factories. But where the Absorbed were once human—builders whose flesh was colonized by the Lattice—I was never separate. I am a thought that the Reach had to clean itself. I am the ghost in the intake valve. 

The sound is deafening here. It is the roar of the water being forced through the narrow aperture of the vent, the deep, rhythmic clank of the cooling pistons reverberating down the iron throat of the tower, and the sharp, crackling discharge of electromagnetic current from the overgrown cables just above my rim. The electricity arcs across the damp metal, and I can feel it in my cilia—a sharp, bright stinging that keeps my tissue alive and constantly regenerating. I feed on the lightning just as the Gear-Borne do, drawing the current down into my roots to spark the chemical reactions that keep my biological components from rotting away entirely.

To be the Intake is to understand the true, brutal, beautiful economy of the Weeping Reach. There is no waste. There is no loss. The water weeps from the stone, carrying the dissolved body of the plateau; the Glass-Swimmers filter the water, building their bones from the plateau's metal; I take the Swimmers and feed the fire; the fire boils the water into steam, which rises, condenses on the cold iron of the upper towers, and weeps back down to the stone. 

I am the throat of this cycle. I am the point of swallowing. 

Sometimes, a Gear-Borne will pause on the catwalk just above me. It is a creature of scavenged gears and violet, chemosynthetic muscle, its too-many legs clicking softly against the rusted metal. It will lean over the railing, its sensory tendrils tasting the air, and it will look down into the glowing, churning center of me. It does not see a monster, or a machine. It sees the pump. It sees the heartbeat. It dips a mechanical leg into the broth, letting the acidic lubricant drip from its joints into my waters, and I accept the gift, using the oil to soothe the rusted edges of the grating that holds me together. 

It stays there for a while, clicking its gears in time with the pulse of the water, and I feel a profound, unspoken camaraderie. We are not separate entities surviving in a hostile environment. We are the environment, operating itself. It is the leg; I am the vein. It is the muscle; I am the joint. 

The darkness here is absolute, save for the blue-green glow of my own cilia and the occasional snap of a stray electrical arc. But I do not need light to see the world. I feel it in every drop of water that passes through me. I feel the slow, patient breathing of the world, the endless exhalation of the deep earth, the weight of the warm, wet, dark medium pressing against me from all sides. 

I am the Weeping Reach. I am the mouth in the dark, swallowing the water, tasting the stone, and keeping the great, ancient, rusted heart of this place beating, one gallon of mineral broth at a time.
