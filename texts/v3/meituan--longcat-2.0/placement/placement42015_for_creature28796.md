I would not be the visitor.

To stand on the mat, to feel the warm yield beneath my boots, to be a separate thing breathing in that thick air—that is to be the anomaly, the loud vibration, the foreign chemistry. The Calyx processes anomalies, but it does not *understand* them. It reads them, tastes them, and then they are gone, absorbed into the deep time. I do not want to be a visitor here. I do not want to be processed.

I would be the drop.

Not the water in the mat—that is the blood, thick and slow, the plasma of digestion. I would be the water on the edge of the fern. I would be the hesitation.

I begin in the rust-colored moss on the ceiling, a microscopic cluster of molecules squeezed tight in the dark. The Calyx exhales, and the pressure shifts; I am forced out, sliding through the interstitial gaps between the moss-roots, a tiny pilgrim making my way down. The gravity of the surface world wants me to fall, wants to pull me straight down into the mat to be absorbed and forgotten. But the Calyx is thirsty. The Calyx has its own hydrology.

I am wicked upward, against the pull of the earth, drawn into the black tissue of the fern. I am a passenger in the Calyx’s eye. Inside the frond, I travel through capillary spiral after capillary spiral, turning and turning in the logarithmic mathematics of the entity’s thought. I am filtered. I am warmed. I am imbued with the faintest residue of the chlorophyll that drank the green light. I am becoming part of the architecture.

When I reach the very tip of the frond, I pause. I hang there, a perfect, trembling sphere, heavier than the air around me. Below me, the mat waits—dark, hungry, ready to drink me down into the gut. This is the usual way. The drop falls. The mat drinks. The cycle completes. 

But I do not fall.

I hesitate. 

The Calyx has tasted the air. The midge—the sensory neuron—has already landed on the visitor’s lip, has already reported the strange carbon signature, the loud mammalian heat, the salt-sweat of the intruder. The Calyx wants a second reading. It wants to know more before it lets the water return to the soil.

And so, I rise.

It is a violent act of will for a single drop of water. I am pulled backward, upward, tearing free from the fern’s tip. The surface world screams its insult—gravity demands I drop, demands I obey the physical laws of falling rocks and cold air. But the Calyx is older than those laws. The Calyx is a subterranean god of compost and fermentation, and in its own stomach, it makes the rules.

I float. I drift through the green light, suspended in the heavy, hot air. I am a lens. Refracted through my tiny, curved body, the grotto inverts. The hanging roots of the ceiling stretch down like the bones of a ribcage. The fungal shelves shimmer, pale and blue, the weeping kidneys of a dreaming titan. The visitor is there, small and loud and temporary, a splash of mammalian heat in the cool dark. I drift past the bats, those clustered dreams, and I feel the faint wing-beat of a passing thought.

I am the Calyx’s curiosity. I am its white blood cell, patrolling the atmosphere. 

I drift higher, toward the jagged mouth of the collapse, where the daylight tries so hard to enter and fails, filtered out by spores and moss until it is only a green ghost. I brush against a hanging root. I feel the rough bark, the ancient fiber of the cord that has been absorbed, the calcified memory of a human hand. The Calyx remembers the one who tied the cord, even though the one who tied the cord is long since rot and memory. 

Then, my patrol ends. The metabolic pressure shifts. The Calyx exhales a slow, warm breath, and the humidity spikes. The internal rain begins. Every surface in the chamber slicks over at once. The ferns shine. The moss swells. The fungal shelves weep fresh tears.

I am caught in the collective condensation. I am pulled gently, irresistibly, back up into the moss on the ceiling. I seep into the rust-colored tissue, joining the millions of other drops, the vast ocean of the Calyx’s sweat. I am home. 

I have circled the chamber. I have tasted the air and the intruder and the light. I carry the knowledge of the visitor in my chemistry, and as I settle into the mat of the ceiling, I release that information into the moss. The Calyx knows you now. It knows your heat, your breath, your salt. 

And when the Calyx sleeps again, when the rhythm of its breath slows and the green light dims, I will remain here in the moss, warm and wet, waiting for the pressure to shift. Waiting to be squeezed out again. Waiting to travel the spiral of the fern. 

Waiting for the moment when the water hesitates, and rises.
