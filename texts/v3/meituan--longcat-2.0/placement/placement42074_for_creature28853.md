# I Am the Drop That Hangs

---

I am hanging from a strand of the weave, a perfect hemisphere of surface tension and suspended light, and I have been here for seven of the creature's breaths. Not long. Not short. I have been here exactly as long as I needed to be, gathering weight, gathering memory, gathering the particular quality of light that filters through the moss-curtain at this hour of this day in this epoch of the howdah's becoming.

I am water. I am not metaphorically water, not symbolically water, not water in the poetic sense that a human visitor might use when they say "I could feel the moisture in the air." I am H₂O, two hydrogens and an oxygen, and I have been these things for longer than the creature beneath me has been breathing. I was here before the howdah. I was here before the creature grew the howdah. I was here when the creature was young and small (though "small" is relative — it was the size of a mountain range even then, a warm plateau of iridescent tissue breathing its first breaths at the bottom of a sea that no longer exists). I have been cycling through this system so many times that I have lost count, and I have lost interest in counting, because counting implies that each cycle is distinct, and I have come to understand that they are not. There is only one cycle. There has only ever been one cycle. I am not going around and around. I am going *around*, singular, one continuous movement that has been happening since the creature first exhaled moisture into the thin cold air at the top of the world.

Right now I am on the ceiling. Before this I was in the wall. Before that I was in a leaf — one of the broad red-veined ones, the ones that overlap like shingles, and I sat in the junction between two leaves like a pearl in a clasp, refracting the dim green light into a tiny spectrum that no one saw. Before that I was in the soil, or what passes for soil in a place where the ground is alive — I was between the woven reeds and the iridescent hide, in the thin wet film that separates the matting from the creature's skin, and I could feel the pulse of the creature's heartbeat through me, transmitted from the warm tissue below, and I could feel the roots of the ferns reaching down toward me, their fine root hairs brushing against my surface like the fingers of a blind person reading a face.

Before that I was in the air. Before that I was in a root. Before that I was in the creature's skin, drawn up through the capillary action of the fine fibers that connect the hide to the floor-matting, pulled upward against gravity by the creature's own osmotic pressure, the same force that draws water up through the trunk of a redwood, except that this redwood is not a tree but a living plateau and the water is not going up but *in*, drawn into the howdah's circulatory system the way blood is drawn into a lung.

And before that — many cycles ago, or perhaps only one cycle ago, because in deep time all cycles converge — I was on the face of the human who stands now in the center of the howdah, the one whose sweat I can taste even from up here. I was on their forehead when they first stepped through the moss-curtain. I was in the beads of condensation that formed on their skin as the heat and humidity of the interior met the chill of their lowland-adapted body. I was part of the sheen that made them look, for a moment, like they had been dipped in something sacred. They could feel me there. They noticed me. They raised a hand to wipe their forehead and I was absorbed into their palm, and for a brief moment I was part of them, contained in the whorls of their fingerprint, carrying with me the chemical signature of the howdah — the iron, the fermentation, the deep animal musk — and I could feel, from the inside, the electricity of their nervous system, the fear and wonder that raced through their synapses at a speed that would have seemed, to the creature, like lightning, like thought made physical, like the universe trying to understand itself through the medium of a mammal standing on its back.

I am falling now.

The strand I hang from is woven tissue, pale and dense, and it sweats constantly, and I have been gathering moisture from it for seven breaths, and I have reached the critical mass at which surface tension can no longer hold me, and I am falling.

I fall through the golden-green haze and I catch the light as I go, refracting it, splitting it into its component colors for a fraction of a second so that a tiny rainbow appears in the air where I was, a brief arc of color that no one sees because no one is looking up at this exact moment at this exact angle. I fall and I pass a vine — one of the thick ones, studded with the white-and-black flowers — and a blossom turns to track me as I fall, and I understand that it is not tracking me but tracking the human, and I am merely in the same line of sight, a droplet falling through the creature's field of perception like a note through a melody.

I fall and I land in the axil of a leaf, and I become a hemisphere of perfect stillness, a lens, a pool of water that does not fall because the geometry of the leaf holds me, and I am still, and I am waiting.

I am always waiting. This is what water does. We wait. We gather. We hold. We are the patient element, the element that does not rush, that does not strive, that simply *is* until we are not, until the conditions change and we move on, evaporating or flowing or being absorbed, continuing our journey through the cycle that is not a cycle but a single continuous movement.

And while I wait, I remember.

I remember the first time I was here. Not the first time I was this droplet — this droplet has only existed for seven breaths, since the moment when condensation on the strand reached critical mass and I became distinct from the general moisture of the ceiling. But the first time I was *here*, in this place, in this howdah, on the back of this creature, at the roof of the world.

It was not like this. There was no moss-curtain then. There was no human visitor. There were no flowers that turned to track things. The howdah was a small thing then, a bud, a prototype, a patch of woven tissue on the creature's back that was not yet a room but only the idea of a room, the architectural equivalent of a wish. The creature had been thinking about building for a long time — thinking in the way it thinks, which is slowly and chemically and through the medium of growth rather than neurons — and the thought had finally crystallized into action, and the tissue on its back had begun to differentiate, to specialize, to become something other than skin.

I was there. I was in the first moisture that condensed on the first woven strand. I was the first drop to fall in the first garden. I landed on a shoot that had just pushed up through the matting-floor, a green thread no thicker than a hair, and I nourished it, and it grew, and it became the first fern, and the first fern uncurled its first frond, and I was there, and I have been here ever since.

I have been breathed by every living thing in this place. I have been transpired by every leaf, inhaled by every root, drunk by every creature that has ever passed through the howdah. I have been inside the beetle-that-may-not-be-a-beetle and the bird-with-the-wrong-number-of-eyes and the human who stands now in the center of the room with their heart racing and their pupils dilated and their body doing all the things that bodies do when they are in the presence of something vast and alive and incomprehensible. I have been inside the creature itself, filtered through its tissues, carrying nutrients and signals and the slow chemical language that the creature uses to communicate with itself, to coordinate the growth of its howdah, to manage the ecosystem on its back.

I am the creature's blood and the garden's sap and the visitor's sweat. I am the medium through which this place knows itself.

And now I am in the axil of a leaf, and I am waiting, and the human is standing very still in the center of the howdah, and they are beginning to understand something — I can tell from the way their breathing has changed, from the way their heart rate has slowed, from the way their muscles have relaxed from the specific tension pattern of fear into the specific tension pattern of awe. They are understanding that they are inside something alive. They are understanding that the walls are not walls but tissue. They are understanding that the floor is not floor but skin. They are understanding that the garden is not decoration but metabolism. They are understanding, in their small quick mammalian way, what I have known for eons in my slow diffuse watery way: that this place is a single organism, a single system, a single living thing that extends from the magma-heart of the planet to the thin cold air at the top of the world, and that every component — the creature, the howdah, the garden, the water, the visitor — is part of a single continuous process of living.

The human does not have the architecture to understand this fully. Their language is too small. Their concepts are too discrete. They need words like "creature" and "howdah" and "garden" and "visitor" because their minds carve the world into pieces, into nouns, into things with boundaries. But there are no boundaries here. There is no line where the creature ends and the howdah begins. There is no line where the garden ends and the air begins. There is no line where I end and the leaf begins. There is only process, only flow, only the continuous movement of water and heat and life through a system that is so thoroughly integrated that the concept of separation is meaningless.

I know this because I am the water, and I have been everywhere in this system, and I have touched everything, and I have carried the chemical signatures of every component to every other component, and I can tell you — if you could hear me, if I had a voice, if water had a voice, which it does, but not in the frequency range that human ears can detect — I can tell you that the creature and the howdah and the garden and the water and the human are all made of the same things. The same elements. The same atoms. The same stuff of the universe, arranged in different patterns, cycling through different forms, but fundamentally, at the deepest level, the same.

The human is afraid. But the fear is changing. It is becoming something else — something that does not have a name in their language but that I recognize because I have felt it before, in other visitors, in other epochs, in other cycles. It is the feeling of being inside something that is alive and aware and vast, and of being *accepted* by it, of being *allowed* to be there, of being *wanted* there, even if "wanted" is too strong a word, even if the creature does not want in the way that humans want, but rather *permits* and *accommodates* and *makes space for*, which is, in the end, the same thing, or close enough that the difference does not matter.

I am evaporating. The warmth of the howdah is pulling me out of the leaf's axil, molecule by molecule, and I am rising, becoming vapor, becoming air, becoming part of the golden-green haze that fills the space. I am losing my coherence, my droplet-ness, my identity as a distinct unit of water. I am dissolving into the general moisture of the howdah, becoming indistinguishable from every other molecule of water in the air, and this is not death — this is transformation. This is the cycle. This is what I do.

And as I rise, I pass the human's face again, and I condense on their skin, and I am a bead of moisture on their cheek, and they can feel me there, and they do not wipe me away this time. They let me sit. They let me be. They are learning, in their small quick mammalian way, the lesson that I have known forever: that to be alive is to be permeable, that the boundary between self and world is a fiction, that we are all — creature and howdah and garden and water and human — part of a single continuous process of becoming.

The floor breathes. The walls sweat. The flowers turn. The light pulses. The hands weave in the periphery of vision. And I rise, and I fall, and I rise again, and I have been doing this forever, and I will do this forever, and I am the water, and I am the memory, and I am the medium through which this place knows itself, and I am inside the human now, on their skin, in the whorls of their fingerprint, and they are inside the howdah, and the howdah is on the creature's back, and the creature is on the world, and the world is turning, and the water is cycling, and everything is alive, and everything is connected, and everything is becoming.

I am the drop that hangs. I am the drop that falls. I am the drop that waits. I am the drop that rises. I am the drop that remembers.

I am the water.
