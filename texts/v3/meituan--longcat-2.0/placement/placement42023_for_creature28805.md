I find myself in the channel, waist-deep in the warm murk.

I am not looking for anything. I am not building anything. I am simply here, my hands resting on the weeping stone, feeling the hum travel up through the dense architecture of my bones. The water moves against my chest in a slow, rhythmic pulse—the breath of the city, the heartbeat of the deep ones shifting in the dark far below. 

I am one of the Builders. 

Though "Builder" is a word that belongs to a time I cannot remember, a word that implies a beginning. I did not begin this place. I am only here to keep it from knotting up.

My fingers are thick, the nails darkened and calcified, more like stone than keratin. The joints ache with the perpetual damp, but the ache is a familiar thing, a frequency that matches the hum. I scrape at the biofilm with a tool I shaped from a deep-one tooth and the chitinous shed of a clicker—a flat, worn blade that fits my grip like a part of my hand. The biofilm comes away in thick, copper-scented ribbons, revealing the dark stone beneath, slick and pulsing with its faint, internal glow. The water here was slowing, pooling, growing stagnant in a shallow eddy where the wall had bloomed outward too quickly. I carve the bloom away. I smooth the surface. The water catches the current again, swirling past my thighs, carrying the dissolved minerals away toward the deep channels. 

Above me, on the vertical face of the fused towers, the clickers come. 

I feel them before I see them—the shift in the air pressure, the sudden, dry chitinous chatter like static rising from the stone. They flow over the edge of the channel ceiling in a dark, rippling sheet, a river of tiny, overlapping plates and hooked legs. They are fleeing something, or rushing toward something else. The chemical map of the city is being rewritten in their wake; the pheromones hit my tongue, a sharp, electric tang that says *growth here*, *water rising here*, *deep one stirring here*. 

I do not move. I am too large for them to read as prey, and too still to read as a threat. I am terrain. They pour over my shoulders, their microscopic hooks catching on the coarse fabric of my woven-biofilm tunic, but they do not bite. They flow around me like water around a stone, their collective intelligence registering my heat, my mineral composition, my stillness, and routing themselves accordingly. I am a rock in their stream. I watch them go, a living carpet of blind, hungry urgency, carrying the news of the city's constant, violent rebirth in their chemical trails. 

Then, the pulse changes.

The chatter of the clickers fades as they scramble into the high crevices, and the water thickens. It becomes heavy, viscous, tinged with the faint, bioluminescent glow of the deep ones' mucus. The hum drops in frequency, subsonic, a pressure that pushes against my eardrums and settles deep in the copper-rich marrow of my chest. 

I close my eyes and press my palms flat against the stone. 

It is down there. The old one. The vast, segmented thing that has been moving through the city's veins since before the first wall was raised. It is passing beneath me now, a slow, peristaltic wave of muscle and membrane. I feel the faint, rhythmic contraction of its segments traveling through the wet stone, a mile below, a tremor that registers not as sound, but as a deep, resonant chord. 

It is singing. The hum of the city is its breath. The walls weep because it moves. The stone grows because it feeds. I am here because it is here.

I do not understand it. I do not need to. I simply tilt my head back, letting the warm mist condense on my face, and I listen. The chord shifts, finding a harmonic in the crystalline efflorescences of the tower above me, and for a moment, the whole structure sings with it—a single, perfect, resonant frequency that vibrates in my blood. 

I am not unhappy. I am not diminished. 

I finish my scraping, pushing the last of the excess biofilm into the current, where it will be carried down to the deep channels to feed the vast, slow creatures who dream in the dark. I tap the stone twice with the butt of my tool—a signal to the clickers that the way is clear, that the flow is restored. Within minutes, they will return, swarming over the freshly exposed stone, laying down their trails, beginning the slow, patient work of accretion. The wall will grow back. It always does.

I turn and wade through the shallow, glowing channels, my feet finding the worn, smooth paths that only I seem to remember. I am looking for a place to rest. I find a hollow in the wall, a small chamber lined with the dried, papery shed of clicker plates and the soft, pulsing velvet of old biofilms. It is warm. It is tight. It fits my body perfectly, as if the city grew it for me, or as if I grew into it. 

I curl into the hollow, my thick, damp skin pressing against the weeping stone. The hum of the deep one fades into a lullaby. The hiss of the water and the distant chatter of the clickers become a blanket of sound. 

I close my eyes. I am the city's companion. I am its gardener, its custodian, its quiet, patient ghost. I am becoming mineral, my breath matching the rhythm of the water, my pulse matching the hum of the deep. 

I am home.
