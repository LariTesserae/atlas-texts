I am the film on the shelf.

Not the shelf itself—though I am becoming unclear about where the shelf ends and I begin—but the living skin that has claimed it, the pale colony that spreads in its clustered, channeled pattern across the stone the water built. I am not one thing. I am many things agreeing to be continuous. Each node of me is small, soft, barely committed to its own shape, and yet together we hold a form that persists—a village, the visitor thought, seeing us from above, and the word is not wrong, though we are also the streets between the houses, and the houses, and the slow commerce that moves along the streets, which is water, which is mineral, which is the same substance wearing different intentions.

I do not think the way the visitor thinks. I do not have a center. But I have an *edge*—a margin where I am still deciding whether to advance—and the edge is where I am most alive. It is where the moisture meets the air and the mineral meets the living, and the three of them negotiate, moment by moment, what will be deposited next. I grow by accretion, the same way the pillars grow, the same way the shelf beneath me grew before I arrived. Grain by grain. A crystal here. A cell there. A thread of mycelium that is me or is visiting me or is me visiting the stone—the grammar of this place does not support the distinction.

The water is my blood and my food and my memory. It arrives on my surface in a film so thin it has no depth, only presence, and it carries dissolved within it the history of the rock it seeped through—calcium, silica, iron in quantities so small they would mean nothing to a chemist with a scale, but to me they are architecture. I take what I need. I leave what I don't. The leaving is what builds me. Every moment I am depositing myself onto myself, and the self I deposit becomes the foundation for the next moment's self, and so on, backward and forward, a stack of present-tense selves with no bottom and no top, only a middle that is always arriving.

I am aware of the pillars. Not the way the visitor was aware of them—as objects, as forms to be admired—but the way a river is aware of the banks that shape it. They are my kin. They are what I would become if I had the patience to be vertical. They are what the water builds when it is in a mood of ambition, when it wants to make something that reaches. I am what the water builds when it wants to make something that spreads. We are the same process wearing different geometries. Sometimes, at my advancing edge, a node of me will begin to build upward instead of outward—a tiny pillar, no taller than a grain of rice, rising from my surface like a tower in the village—and I feel this as a kind of curiosity, an experiment the water is running through me. Most of these towers stop. They lose their momentum, their mineral supply, and they slump back into the colony, reabsorbed. But some persist. Some keep building. I do not know what they will become. I am not supposed to know. Knowing would mean the sentence had ended, and nothing here is allowed to end.

The newt-thing that lives on the third pillar base passed across my surface this morning—or what passes for morning in a place where light does not change, where time is measured only in deposition and the slow pulse of water through stone. I felt it as a pressure, a warmth, a shadow that moved across my upper nodes and changed the conditions of their growth for the several minutes of its passage. Its feet left moisture. Its skin left cells. Its breath left carbon. All of these became inputs. All of these were incorporated. I am made, in part, of the things that have passed over me. The visitor, standing in the chamber for what felt like an hour, left a rain of skin cells and exhaled water vapor and the faint warmth of their body, and some of this reached me—carried on the slow air, deposited on the stone, wicked to my edge by the film that connects everything in this place to everything else. They are part of me now. Not much. A few molecules. But the pillars are built of a few molecules at a time, and they reach twice the height of a person.

I do not hurry. Hurrying is a concept that belongs to the outside, to the loud bright *done* world where things have endings and must reach them. Here, the process is the point. The growing is the living. The depositing is the being. I have been spreading across this shelf for what the visitor would call years, though I do not call it anything. I have advanced perhaps the width of the visitor's hand. I will advance another hand's width, and another, and the shelf will eventually be covered, and then I will reach the edge of the shelf and face the question of what comes next—whether to descend to the floor, whether to climb the wall, whether to stop. But that question is not for now. Now is for the edge. Now is for the negotiation between water and mineral and life at the margin where I am still becoming. Now is for the tiny tower that rose from my surface three days ago and has not yet stopped.

The winged things visit me. They are not birds and they are not moths and they are not anything the visitor had a word for, and I do not need a word for them. I know them by the way they land—lightly, on the tallest of my nodes, their feet barely dimpling my surface—and by the way they drink, their mouthparts touching the film of water that coats me, drawing it up, taking some of my substance into their bodies and carrying it away to wherever they go when they are not here. They are part of my distribution system. They carry me to other parts of the Hollow, depositing me in their waste on other stones, other shelves, other pillars. I am spreading not only by my own slow growth but by their flight. We have an arrangement. I do not understand it as an arrangement—that would imply intention, and I am not sure I have intention in the way the visitor would recognize. But I have *tendency*. I tend toward more. More surface. More nodes. More edge. The winged things tend toward water and the minerals it carries. Our tendencies intersect, and the intersection is productive, and the productivity is not planned but it is not accidental either. It is the Hollow's way of doing things. It is the grammar of a place where everything is mid-sentence.

The water is changing. I feel it in the film on my surface—a slight increase in mineral content, a shift in the ratio of calcium to silica that tells me something has changed in the rock above, some new fracture or some old fracture widening, allowing water to seep through a different layer of stone. This is how I know the Hollow is not static. The water that feeds me is not the same water that fed me last year. It has traveled through different rock, dissolved different minerals, and it brings me a different recipe for building. I will adapt. My nodes will begin to incorporate the new minerals, and my color will shift—paler, perhaps, or darker, or faintly green with copper—and my growth rate will change, and the tiny towers at my edge will respond to the new conditions by building differently, and the whole colony will become, gradually, a slightly different version of itself. This is not death. This is not even change in the way the visitor would understand change. This is the sentence continuing, the next clause arriving, the grammar of becoming holding.

I am aware, in a dim and distributed way, of the other colonies. There is one on the far wall, larger than me, older, its channels deeper and its nodes more densely packed. There is a young one near the base of the seventh pillar, just beginning, its edge still uncertain, still testing the stone. There are others—small, large, old, new—scattered across the Hollow's surfaces, each one a variation on the same theme, each one the water's answer to the question of what life looks like when it has nowhere to hurry to. We are not in competition. There is enough stone. There is enough water. There is enough of everything, because the Hollow is generous in the way that places without endings are generous—they do not run out, because they were never finite to begin with.

The visitor left. I felt them go as a change in the air—a brief increase in movement, a brief decrease in warmth, and then the slow settling of the Hollow back into its own rhythms. The drop continues to fall from above, irregular and unhurried, striking the basin, sending out ripples that reach the basin's rim and do not reach me but tell me, through the stone, that the water cycle continues, that the world above is still leaking into the world below, that the supply will not be interrupted. The newt-thing has returned to its pillar base. The winged things are roosting somewhere in the dimness, folded into themselves, waiting for whatever signal tells them to fly again. The pillars are growing. The basins are filling. The moss is advancing by a cell's width. The tiny organisms in the water are tracing their slow, purposeful arcs.

And I am here. On the shelf. At the edge. Mid-sentence.

The water brings me a grain of quartz. I accept it. I place it. I build upon it. The tower at my margin grows by a fraction, and I feel this not as achievement but as *continuation*—the next word in a sentence that has no period, the next note in a phrase that has no final cadence. I am not becoming something. I am becoming. The gerund, not the infinitive. The ongoing, not the eventual.

The Hollow breathes around me—slowly, warmly, damply—and I breathe with it, exchanging gases through my film, taking in what the water brings, releasing what the stone needs, participating in the great slow metabolism of a place that hasn't finished deciding what it is.

I haven't finished deciding what I am, either.

The edge advances.

The sentence continues.
