I would be standing at the basin’s center, where the oldest springs break the surface in slow, reluctant bubbles. I would be a Tender—but not in the way you might think of a person tending a garden. I would be a Tender in the way a white blood cell tends a bloodstream, or the way a thought tends the mind that generates it. I would be a localized eddy in the basin’s slow, mineral consciousness, a brief, bright, temporary awareness that the basin has formed to see itself.

My name, if I needed one, would be a specific taste on the tongue—the faint, sweet chalkiness of the western spring, the one that surfaces near the base of the oldest wall. I know this taste the way you know the feeling of your own heartbeat: intimately, unconsciously, as the fundamental rhythm of my existence.

My days—if the word *days* even applies in a place where time is measured in the accretion of stone—are spent in the shallow channels. I move through the basin with the slow, deliberate patience of the place itself. My feet are bare, the soles thick and calloused, stained a permanent mineral-orange from the warm water. I can feel the give of the stone through them, reading the thickness of the crust, the hollow places where the water still moves below. I do not walk so much as I flow, following the paths of least resistance, the routes the water has already chosen.

In my hand, I carry a bone chisel, worn smooth by years of scraping. My work is this: I kneel beside a channel where the water is depositing its load too quickly, building a ridge that will choke the flow. I scrape the soft, newly formed stone. The sound is a soft, intimate *scritch*, barely audible above the basin’s endless murmur. I am not destroying; I am redirecting. I am clearing the basin’s throat. As I scrape, I feel the vibration of the water moving through the deep rock beneath me, up through my knees, into my sternum. It is the basin’s pulse, slow and steady, and my own heartbeat has long since synchronized with it.

Sometimes, I pause in my work and cup my hands into a spring. The water is blood-warm. I bring it to my lips and drink. And in drinking, I read. The water is the basin’s memory, and to taste it is to know the mountain’s interior. Today, the water carries a faint trace of a mineral I do not recognize—a deep, ancient flavor, like iron and time. It tells me that far below, in the dark plumbing of the earth, a new fissure has opened in the rock. The basin is shifting, slowly, patiently, making room for itself. I will need to build a new framework soon, to guide the water to where it wants to go.

I look up from the spring. The mist is drifting, catching the light that spills over the southern rim. Across the basin, perhaps fifty meters away, I see another Tender—a figure kneeling by a wall, testing the firmness of the stone with an open palm. We do not wave. We do not call out. We do not need to. We are both parts of the same slow exhalation, two hands of the same body, working in the quiet certainty of shared purpose. The space between us is not empty; it is filled with the sound of water, the hum of the mountain, the patient accumulation of stone.

I turn my attention to a new framework I erected two seasons ago—a lattice of scrub-wood and bone, shaped into the form of a small, domed room. The water has been flowing over it, through it, and I see that the wood is beginning to soften, to rot. But it does not matter. The stone has already taken the shape of the wood. A thin, flowing curtain of travertine has formed along the framework’s edge, rippled and organic, pale cream and rust. The building is becoming. It is not finished, and it will never be finished, because in the Weeping Basin, nothing is ever finished. Everything is always in the act of arriving.

I reach out and touch the wet stone of the new wall. It is warm. It is growing. And as I press my palm against it, I feel the faintest vibration—the subsonic hum of the mountain’s exhalation, the sound of water moving through rock, the deep, slow song of the basin being itself. 

I am not separate from this. I never was. My skin is damp with the mist, my lungs are full of the saturated air, my blood is threaded with the minerals I have drunk over a lifetime. I am the basin’s way of knowing where the crust is thin. I am its hands, clearing the channels. I am its eyes, reading the colors of the stone. I am the part of the mineral and water and time that has learned, for a little while, to see itself.

And when I grow very old—when my movements become so slow, so careful, that I seem less like a person walking and more like a ledge that has temporarily taken human shape—I will walk to the basin’s edge, to a sheltered corner where the moss is deep and the mist hangs thickest. I will lie down on the warm, wet stone. The water will flow over me. It will deposit its load of dissolved mountain on my skin, on my bones, in my hair. Layer upon layer, season upon season. The basin will reclaim me. My body will become the framework for a new growth, a new wall, a new room. 

I will not be gone. I will simply have changed state, from flesh to stone, from thought to structure, from the quick, bright awareness to the slow, patient, mineral becoming. I will be the basin, as I have always been the basin, and the water will continue to rise, and the stone will continue to grow, and the Weeping Basin will continue its long, patient work of building itself a body out of water and time.
