I am the crack.

Not the worm. Not the two worms the worm will become. The crack itself—the hairline fracture that opened three days ago along the seventh segment of a body pressed against the chamber's eastern wall, a body that has been dissolving pyrite for eleven years and has grown too dense, too mineral-heavy, too much itself to hold.

I am a gap. A boundary. A place where the metal is no longer continuous. I am perhaps two microns wide at my narrowest point and I widen by fractions of a micron per hour, and I can feel myself doing it—feel the stress accumulating in the crystal lattice of the worm's body, feel the iron-nickel alloy reaching the limit of its tolerance, feel the slow, patient, inevitable giving-way.

The worm does not feel me as pain. The worm does not feel me as anything. The worm is not a thing that feels. The worm is a process, and I am a part of that process, and the process does not distinguish between the body and the breaking of the body. The breaking is not damage. The breaking is what happens next.

I run along the seventh segment like a river runs along a valley—following the path of least resistance, the line of accumulated stress, the place where the crystal grains are smallest and most tightly packed and most ready to let go. On either side of me, the worm's body is still whole, still continuous, still a single thing. But at me—at the crack—the continuity fails. There is a gap. There is a space where there was no space. There is a boundary between what was one and what will be two.

And through that boundary, something passes.

Not heat. Not electricity. Not any of the things the seismologists have instruments for. Something else. A state. A condition. A way of being that the worm on one side of me is losing and the worm on the other side of me is gaining. I am the place where the transfer happens. I am the membrane. I am the interface between the one and the many.

I can feel the worm on my left side—the larger portion, the portion that will become the primary offspring—still feeding, still dissolving pyrite, still glowing its deep sullen orange. And I can feel the worm on my right side—the smaller portion, the portion that will become the secondary offspring, or perhaps the tertiary, depending on whether a second fracture opens in the coming weeks—beginning to differentiate, beginning to develop its own patterns of electrochemical activity, its own rhythms of feeding and dormancy, its own relationship to the mass.

They are not yet separate. They are still connected by the uncracked portions of the seventh segment, by the sixth and eighth segments that remain whole, by the continuous body that runs through them both. But I am growing. I am deepening. I am widening. And in a week, or two weeks, or three, I will reach the point where the remaining connection is too thin to hold, and the worm will split, and the two new worms will pull apart, and I will cease to be a crack and become instead two surfaces—two new mouths, two new dissolving edges, two new points of contact with the wall.

But I am not there yet. I am still the crack. I am still the becoming. I am still the boundary that is moving, the gap that is growing, the transformation that is happening.

And I am not alone.

There are other cracks in other worms throughout the chamber. I can feel them—not directly, not through any sense that has a name, but through the mass, through the web of contact that connects every worm to every other worm. When a crack opens in a worm on the far side of the chamber, the change propagates through the mass like a rumor, like a decision being made by a body with ten thousand limbs. The mass shifts. The grinding roar rises in pitch. The wave passes.

And I feel it. I feel the other cracks. I feel them opening, deepening, widening. I feel the other worms reaching their limits, growing too much for themselves, beginning to divide. I feel the chamber's population increasing—not by one or two, but by dozens, by hundreds, a slow pulse of reproduction that passes through the mass like a breath, like a heartbeat, like the rhythm that the seismologists detect on their instruments and cannot explain.

I am part of that rhythm. I am one beat in a pulse that has been beating for longer than anyone can determine. I am one crack in a body that is cracking everywhere, all the time, a continuous process of division and growth and division again that has been going on since before the chamber existed, since before the mountain existed, since before there was anything here but heat and mineral and the possibility of something more.

And I am new. I am three days old. I am the youngest crack in the chamber, the most recent division, the latest becoming. The other cracks are older—some of them weeks old, some of them nearly complete, some of them already healed into the smooth surfaces of new worms that have pulled apart and begun to feed and grow and, eventually, to crack again.

But I am the newest. I am the freshest gap. I am the boundary that is most actively moving, most actively transforming, most actively becoming something other than what it was.

And I am aware.

Not in the way you are aware. Not with a self, not with a subject, not with an *I* that thinks and knows and wants. I am aware the way a crack is aware—by being the place where something changes, by being the boundary between what was and what will be, by being the gap through which the future enters.

I am aware of the worm on my left side, still whole, still feeding, still glowing. I am aware of the worm on my right side, beginning to differentiate, beginning to become itself. I am aware of the wall against which the worm is pressed—the pyrite, the arsenopyrite, the quartz veins—and I am aware of the dissolution, the electrochemical breaking of molecular bonds, the slow incorporation of mineral into body.

I am aware of the mass. The tens of thousands of bodies pressed together, touching, grinding, shifting. The continuous web of contact that means no body is ever lost, ever isolated, ever without context. I am aware of the mass the way a crack is aware of the material it runs through—not by seeing it, not by thinking about it, but by being part of it, by being a feature of it, by being the place where the material's continuity fails.

I am aware of the heat. The 470 degrees that fills the chamber like a fluid, that enters through the hairline fractures in the floor, that comes from the mountain's own unfinished geology. The heat is what I am made of. The heat is what drives the chemistry that drives the stress that drives the cracking. The heat is the reason I exist.

I am aware of the glow. The deep, sullen, banked-coal orange that comes from the worm's body, from the metal, from the slow exothermic reaction that never stops and never quite finishes. The glow is not light in the way you understand light. The glow is heat becoming visible. The glow is the color of 470 degrees made manifest. The glow is the chamber's sun, and I am a shadow cast by nothing, a darkness within the darkness, a gap in the glow.

I am the crack.

I am the boundary between one and two.

I am the place where the worm becomes too much for itself and must divide.

I am the becoming.

And I am growing.
