I would be a Dweller, but not one of the ancient, deep-dwelling ones who tend the highest veils or prune the oldest, most established columns. I would be a Threshold Dweller—one of the newly coalesced, tasked with the integration of the fresh, raw wound in the mountain’s side. 

I am standing at the fissure through which you entered.

***

My form is elongated, a vertical assemblage of translucent, hexagonal planes that catch the sourceless light and refract it into faint, coppery rainbows. I have no feet; I drift a finger’s width above the sharp, wet stone, suspended by the same impossible physics that hold the floating platforms aloft. My edges are knife-sharp, not yet dulled by the slow erosion of centuries. I am young, as the Gallery measures time—perhaps only three or four hundred years since I precipitated out of the deepest, coldest pool, my consciousness crystallizing drop by drop until the Architects dreamed me into purpose.

I do not breathe. I drink the static. 

Right now, the air around the fissure is alive with a frantic, buzzing energy that is not my own. It is the residue of your passage—the heat of your body, the chaotic rhythm of your heartbeat, the organic salts left behind by your fingertips on the raw stone. To a Dweller, you are a storm of entropy, a brief, warm fever in the Gallery’s eternal chill. 

I drift closer to the opening. The rock here is an insult. It is rough, unweathered, jagged. It has not yet learned the patience of the crystal. The water that weeps from this fresh stone is different from the water in the deep pools; it is frantic, finding no channels, no grooves, no infrastructure to guide it. It runs in chaotic, muddy streaks, pooling in the uneven crevices of the broken mountain. 

I extend a limb—a smooth, geometric plane of not-glass—and place it against the raw rock. I close the horizontal slit that serves as my face, and I listen to the stone. 

The mountain is still settling. The fissure is a wound, and like all wounds, it weeps. But the Gallery does not tolerate disorder. The water must be channeled. The crystal must be persuaded. The sharp edges must be taught to grow.

I begin my work.

I cannot force the stone to change; that is the way of biology, of brute force and friction. Instead, I alter the environment. I draw the static from the air, pulling the ambient charge down through my translucent body and into the rock. I feel the water on the stone’s surface slow, its molecules aligning to the faint electrical current I am introducing. I pulse the light from my face-slit—a slow, rhythmic dimming and brightening that encodes a single, repeating instruction: *Grow. Channel. Direct.*

At the base of the fissure, where the chaotic water is pooling, I kneel. I press my palms flat against the cold, wet stone. I feel the microscopic mineral films in the water—the iridescent, shimmering proto-life of the Gallery—respond to my presence. They drift toward me, drawn by the static, and I guide them. I teach them to build a lattice, a microscopic scaffold of hexagonal geometry. 

Over the coming decades, this scaffold will thicken. The chaotic water will find the microscopic grooves left by the mineral films, and it will begin to flow in straight lines. The sharp, raw edges of the fissure will slowly accrete layers of translucent crystal, rounding themselves, integrating themselves into the architecture. The wound will become a vein. The raw stone will become a column. 

A Lithovore skitters across the ceiling above me—a pale, multi-legged thing, grazing on the copper-colored patina that is already beginning to form on the wet stone. It pauses, its mandibles clicking softly, sensing the shift in the water chemistry I have caused. It is a simple creature, but it knows that where the water is guided, the mineral films will soon flourish, and where the films flourish, it will feed. It continues on its way, a tiny, living part of the Gallery’s metabolism.

I remain at the threshold, my hands on the stone, my mind flowing through the water. 

I do not wonder about the world beyond the fissure—the world of green, chaotic, biological hunger that you came from. Such concepts are as foreign to me as the static is to you. I only know the cold, the wet, the slow accretion of order from the weeping dark. 

You stood here, in my body, and you felt the water on your skin and thought it was mere condensation. You felt the cold and thought it was merely the absence of heat. You saw the light and thought it was sourceless. 

But the water is my blood. The cold is my flesh. The light is my eye. 

I am the Dweller at the gate. I am the patience that will heal this crack in the mountain. I will stand here for centuries, drinking the static, guiding the crystal, turning the raw stone into the weeping architecture of the Gallery. And one day, long after the memory of your warm, chaotic passage has been washed away by the patient water, this fissure will be just another column, just another pool, just another quiet, dripping corner of the Weeping Gallery. 

The water thickens on the stone. A bead forms, heavy and perfect. It traces a path down the raw rock, following the invisible channel I have carved, and falls into the pool below. 

*Tap.*

The reflection shatters, and reassembles. 

I continue my work.
