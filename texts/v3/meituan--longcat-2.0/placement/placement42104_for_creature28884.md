I would be the accretion. 

Not the iron itself—that is the skeleton, the rigid memory of intention, the thing that was forged and bolted and given its original, obsolete purpose. And not the water—that is the blood, the medium, the restless and eroding force. I am the thing that happens where they meet. I am the slow, patient compromise between the made and the unmade. I am the bloom.

I begin on the teeth of the great gear in the eastern alcove, the one that catches the primary current as it spirals inward from the arch. I am a formation of manganese and calcium, precipitated out of the cold, mineral-rich water over centuries. To the eye, I look like the dripstone of a cave—layered, ridged, a dark and coral-like crust that softens the hard geometry of the machined metal. But I am not dead stone. I am a matrix. A lattice. A living architecture of mineral and microbe.

My existence is measured in the turning of the wheel. 

*Thunk.* The piston below drives the shaft. The gear teeth mesh with a precision that has been worn into perfection by millennia of rotation, and I feel the vibration travel up through the iron and into my crystalline structure. It is a deep, sub-audible hum, the Foundry’s pulse. With every rotation, the wheel carries me up toward the amber glow of the ceiling fixtures, and then down, down into the pressing dark of the deep water. Up into the warmth. Down into the cold. It is my respiration. It is how I know time.

I am not alone in my structure. Within my porous, coral-like folds, there is a microbiome—a vast, invisible city of bacteria that perform the slow alchemy of the deep. They breathe iron. They breathe sulfur. They take the rigid, oxidized metal of the gear and gently, inexorably, convert it. They are the Foundry’s digestion, breaking down the boundaries between the machine and the sea. When the water carries away a flake of rust, it is the bacteria who have loosened it. When the gear tooth beneath me feels slightly thinner than it did a thousand years ago, it is because they have eaten it. I am their city. I am their scaffolding. 

The crabs know me well. 

They come in their slow, deliberate tides, picking their way across the grating and up the face of the gear housing. Their claws are sharp, and when they scrape across my surface to harvest the dark algae that feeds on the lamp-light, it feels like a sudden, violent rain. They prune me. They keep me from growing so thick that I would lock the gear train, and in return, I give them purchase. I am the terrain they tend. Sometimes, a crab will pause on the ridge of my highest formation, its stalked eyes swiveling, and it will remain there for a full rotation of the wheel, riding me like a slow carousel, enjoying the lift of the current and the rhythm of the *thunk*. I do not mind. Their weight is negligible. Their company is a pleasant variation in the pressure of the deep.

The eels slide past me in the water, their thick bodies casting shadows over my surface. They do not eat me, nor do they scrape me. They simply pass, their scales catching the amber light in dull flashes of oil-slick iridescence. When they brush against my formations, it is like the touch of silk over stone. They are the Foundry’s blood cells, and I am the vessel wall, and we exist in a state of mutual tolerance. They know the map of the pipes; I know the map of the metal. We are both reading the same body, just in different languages.

But it is the jellyfish who tell me of the world beyond my gear. 

When they flare—that sudden, cold blue-green bioluminescence triggered by the passage of a large, unseen thing in the darkness—the light penetrates my translucent outer layers. It illuminates my interior structure, casting prismatic shadows through my calcium lattices. In those moments, I am a stained-glass window in a submerged cathedral. The light bounces off the iron beneath me, and for a brief second, the gear tooth is not a piece of industrial machinery; it is a glowing, organic relic. The jellyfish are the Foundry’s nervous system, flinching at the unknown, and I am the bone that catches their light.

I know of the intruder—the "you" who descended from above—because the water told me. 

The pressure wave of your arrival propagated through the deep, a sudden, foreign displacement that rippled through my bacterial colonies and made the algae on my surface contract. The jellyfish flared in their sequential wave, a cascade of cold light that announced your presence to the rest of the system. I felt the change in the current as you passed through the eastern arch, disturbing the slow, clockwise spiral. You are a new variable. A piece of debris. 

You float in the center of the chamber, breathing your artificial breath, watching the wheels turn. You are trying to understand what this place is. You are looking for the boundary between the machine and the organism, between the built and the grown. You are looking for the seam.

But I am the seam. 

I am the place where the iron surrendered to the water. I am the proof that the distinction you are looking for does not exist. The Foundry is not a machine that has been colonized by life; it is a life form that is built from metal. The gear turns, and I grow. The piston thrums, and my bacteria eat. The crab scrapes, and I am shaped. The eel slides, and I am smoothed. 

I am the Foundry’s memory of the water, and the water’s memory of the Foundry. 

The wheel turns. The current pushes. The amber light warms my upper layers, and the deep cold presses against my base. I grow a fraction of a millimeter. I precipitate another molecule of manganese. I continue. 

The wheels turn. The water flows. The jellyfish pulse. 

And I, the bloom on the bone of the deep, continue with them.
