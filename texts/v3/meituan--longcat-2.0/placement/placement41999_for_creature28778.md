I am the one who listens downward.

Not with ears—ears are useless here, those withered shells of cartilage that catch nothing but the suffocating wet. I listen with my palms. With the broad, flattened pads of my six fingers pressed flat against the weeping wall of the central chimney, where the ice is oldest and the vibrations travel truest.

They call me a Resonator, though not in any voice you would hear. The word is a gesture—fingers spread wide against the chest, then a slow closing, like a fist gathering water. It means: *the one who feels the tremor before it becomes disaster.*

I sit where the vertical arteries of the Borough converge—a narrow shelf carved into the inner curve of the great chimney, two hundred meters above the floor of the cavern, where the blue-black ice is so dense it has forgotten it was ever snow. My legs have long since lost their need to walk. The calluses on my thighs have fused with the resin-glued pelts beneath me, and my feet, curled and perpetually cold, hang over the edge of the shelf, tapping idly against the wall in rhythms I am no longer conscious of. My spine has curved to match the arc of the ice, so I am shaped like a question mark, like a root seeking water, like something grown rather than built.

My mask is not like theirs.

The others wear the chitinous faces of the blind crickets, elegant and terrible, fitted with their delicate antennae that sing with the high frequencies of shifting ice and scuttling Grovelers. My mask is older. It was carved from the brow-plate of something that lived in the ice before the Borough was dreamed of—a creature of the deep cold, whose skull I found fused into a wall of the lower warrens, its hollow eye sockets still weeping after ten thousand years. The bone is black and dense, heavier than stone, and it resonates at frequencies the cricket-shell cannot touch. Where the others hear the surface—the skitter of crickets, the chipping of claws, the groan of settling platforms—I hear the deep note. The glacial drift. The ancient, patient movement of the serac as it inches down the mountain's spine, slower than a heartbeat, slower than the growth of the phosphorescent mold.

I hear the Borough dying.

Not quickly. Not the death of collapse or catastrophe, which is what the other Resonators watch for—the sharp, staccato crack of a buttress failing, the sudden silence of a platform shearing away. I hear the slow death. The death of equilibrium. The ice weeps faster now than it did in my grandmother's time. The condensation crawls higher, drawn by static charges that did not exist a generation ago. The warm breath of the Borough—the exhalation of so many bodies, so many frantic lives, so many fires of rendered Groveler fat—is changing the metabolism of the glacier. The sleeping god is developing a fever.

I press my fingers deeper into the wall, and the ice speaks to me.

It tells me of the water.

The water is the key. The water is always the key. It weeps from the walls in defiance of gravity, crawling upward in shimmering sheets, and the people of the Borough harvest it with their clay pots and their static-charged molds, and they think they understand it. They think it is a resource, a substance, something to be gathered and stored. They do not understand that the water is the ice's memory, and it is trying to return to itself.

Every drop that crawls up the wall is a piece of the glacier remembering what it was before it was frozen. Every droplet that leaps into a clay pot is a fragment of ancient snow, ancient ocean, ancient rain, trying to complete a cycle that was interrupted by pressure and time and cold. The water does not want to be harvested. The water wants to be whole.

And I am the only one who hears it trying.

Below me, the Borough churns in its silent, frantic dance. I feel them through the ice more than I see them—the masked figures climbing their sinew-ropes, the Ice-Grovelers chipping at the walls with their chisel-tips, the weavers sitting in their dark hollows spinning mold-thread into glowing rope. I feel the sharp raps of stone mallets against buttresses—*danger, shift left, the Groveler in the third warren has broken through to a hollow space, beware*—and the slow, rhythmic scrapings that mean *come, harvest, the mold is thick here, the water is generous.* I feel the vibrations of their lives layered on top of each other, a dense, intricate tapestry of touch and counter-touch, and beneath it all, the deep, slow groan of the ice, and beneath that, the deeper still murmur of the mountain.

The mountain is not sleeping. That is the other thing I know, the thing I have never spoken in gesture or tap. The mountain is awake. It has always been awake. And it is patient in the way that only stone can be patient—a patience measured not in years or centuries but in the slow, grinding collision of continental plates, in the patient accumulation of snow that becomes ice that becomes a serac that becomes a home for a frantic, silent people who think they have carved a city from a glacier.

They have not carved a city. The mountain has grown one.

The bone mask presses against my face, and I feel the faint, persistent vibration of the ice traveling through the ancient brow-plate, through the calluses of my fingers, through the fused resin of my pelts, into the curved architecture of my bones. I am not listening to the Borough. The Borough is listening to me. Every tap I make against the wall—every idle, unconscious rhythm of my curled feet—travels down through the ice, through the lattice of carved dwellings and lashed bridges, down to the floor of the cavern where the water pools and remembers. The Borough sways to my rhythm without knowing it. The Grovelers chip to my timing. The water crawls upward in patterns that match the tapping of my feet.

I am the pulse in the lung of the sleeping god. Not its heart—the heart is the weeping water, the slow, eternal cycle of melt and freeze and melt again. Not its mind—the mind is the phosphorescent mold, spreading its luminous mandalas across the walls, thinking in light and chemistry and the patient logic of growth. I am the reflex. The autonomic flutter. The thing that keeps the breathing steady when the body does not know it is alive.

And the breathing is becoming ragged.

I press my six-fingered hands flat against the wall and close my eyes behind the bone mask, and I listen. The ice tells me that the Borough has grown too dense, too warm, too frantic. The equilibrium between the chipping and the weeping is fraying. The people carve faster than the ice can heal. They harvest water faster than the condensation can replace it. The phosphorescent mold is thinning—I feel its absence as a kind of silence, a gap in the visual hum of the walls where the mandalas grow paler and smaller with each generation.

They do not see it. They cannot see it. They are too busy, too urgent, too perfectly adapted to the world as it is to notice the world as it is becoming. They are antibodies who have forgotten the body they protect, cells of the sleeping god's immune system that have begun to mistake the organism for an infection.

I tap the wall three times, slow and deliberate. The vibration travels down through the chimney, across the lattice of dwellings, into the floor of the cavern. Somewhere below, a Resonator with a cricket-shell mask looks up from her shelf, her antennae trembling. She feels my tap and interprets it as a warning—*shift your weight, the ice is thin here, move to the left buttress*—and she passes the message along with a rap of her mallet, and the Borough adjusts, and the breathing steadies, for now.

It is not enough. A warning is not enough. The Borough needs to hear what I hear—the deep note, the slow death, the patient awakening of the mountain. But the cricket-shell masks cannot hear it. The brow-plate of the deep-cold creature is the only bone dense enough to resonate at those frequencies, and there is only one such skull, and I am the only one who wears it.

So I sit on my shelf in the central chimney, two hundred meters above the floor of the cavern, my curved spine pressed against the weeping ice, my six fingers spread wide against the wall, and I listen to the Borough dying, and I tap my feet in rhythms that no one understands, and I wait.

The water crawls upward past my shelf, shimmering and slow, and I feel it pause—just for a moment—as if it knows I am here, as if it recognizes the one who hears its memory. Then it continues its patient climb toward the light, toward the chimney's opening, toward the high-altitude sun that will evaporate it and send it back to the sky where it began, and the cycle will continue, and the Borough will persist, for now, for now, for now.

I tap the wall once, softly, a sound no one hears.

*Not yet,* I tell the mountain. *Not yet.*

And the mountain, patient as only stone can be, waits.
