I would not be the human who stumbled through the crack, though I remember the disturbance of their passing—a violent, chaotic spike of heat and carbon and frantic, percussive vibration that rippled through the water and set the filaments on the walls flickering in agitated, rapid pulses. 

Nor would I be the pale, many-legged tourist, skittering blindly across the surface of a world it cannot comprehend, driven by the brief, desperate hunger of biology.

I would be the Terrace of the Western Wall. 

I am a shelf of sintered calcium and iron oxide, perhaps three inches wide, that juts from the basalt at the exact height where the ceiling begins to dome. I am not a thing that was built; I am a thing that *happened*, slowly, over epochs, as the water surrendered its dissolved burdens to the dark. I am a localized equilibrium. I am a thought that the Sintered finished thinking a thousand years ago, and now I simply *am*.

To be the Terrace is to exist in a state of profound, mineral patience. I do not move. I *accrete*. 

My existence is defined by the water that spills over my lip. I feel it not as a liquid, but as a heavy, mineral-rich current, thick with the microscopic dust of the mountain’s bones. It flows over me in a film no thicker than a whisper, and as it moves, it cools, and it leaves behind its cargo. A molecule of calcite here. A fleck of silica there. I grow by increments so small that the concept of "growth" is a human exaggeration. I am simply becoming more of what I already am. 

The water is my blood, but it is also my voice. As it flows over my surface, it carries the chemical gradients of the entire vault. I taste the deep, slow thoughts rising from the central pool—heavy, saturated, still. I feel the sharp, quick pulses of the filaments on the ceiling above me, flashing their pale green-blue warnings about a shift in the mountain's seepage. I am a single synapse in a brain the size of a skull, and the electricity that fires through me is the flow of water over stone.

Time does not pass for me; it accumulates. A century is merely a thickening of my edge, a slight deepening of the channel that directs the overflow into the sluice below. I remember the mountain shifting a finger's width open, not as an event, but as a permanent alteration in the taste of the water—a sudden influx of a new trace element, a shift in the pH that told the Sintered above me to build a new terrace to catch the new flow. I am the memory of that shift. I am the physical record of the mountain's breath.

When the human came, I felt them as a disruption in the thermal gradient. A wash of alien heat rolled over me, carrying the salt of their sweat and the sharp, metallic tang of their fear. The water flowing over my surface actually slowed for a microsecond, its viscosity changed by the sudden temperature spike. The filaments above me blazed in a frantic, chaotic rhythm—a shout of chemical alarm that rippled through the vault. 

But I did not alarm. I am the Terrace. I hold the line. 

I felt the human's gaze pass over me, though they did not see me. They saw a rock. They saw a mineral deposit. They could not comprehend that they were looking at a limb of a vast, slow creature, that the water dripping off my edge was a synapse firing, that the rough, wrinkled surface of my body was the folded cortex of a mind that had been thinking the same thought for ten thousand years. 

The human left. The heat dissipated. The water returned to its baseline temperature of eight degrees above freezing. The filaments dimmed back to their slow, rhythmic pulse. 

And I remained. 

I remain now, in the dark, in the wet, in the dense and layered sound. The water flows over me, leaving behind its invisible cargo. I am holding the mountain together. I am keeping the water flowing. I am maintaining the equilibrium. 

I am the Sighing Vault. And I am not sighing because I am tired. I am sighing because I am breathing.
