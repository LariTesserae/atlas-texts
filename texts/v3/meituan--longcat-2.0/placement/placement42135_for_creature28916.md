I do not reside in the rooms. The rooms are for the tide-thing, the one that moves with the low posture and the deliberate step, transitioning from basin to sleeping platform to the deep, windowless interior. My place is between. I am the interface where the slab meets the bedrock, the interstitial whisper where the air compresses and releases. I am the scratch in the walls.

You are standing on the shelf now. Your boot strikes the schist, and the vibration travels through the mica—a bright, tiny shock that fractures the stillness. I feel it. Not as pain, for the shelf does not know pain, but as a displacement. A weight that was not here a moment ago. A warmth that does not belong.

I am the reason the walls are sound.

When the tide-thing lifts a shifted stone and presses it back into its seat, there is grit in the way. A grain of feldspar, a fragment of quartz washed down from the peaks. If the stone is set upon the grit, it will wobble. It will wear unevenly. It will, over centuries, crack. I move the grit. I travel the fractures in the rock, a slow migration of micro-adjustments, nudging the dust into the deeper fissures where it belongs. I am the fine rasp of stone finding its level. I am the click of mineral against mineral, too quiet for your ears, but loud in the geometry of the shelf.

You hear me in the walls at night. You call it scratching. You wonder if it is beetles, or the settling of old stone. It is the shelf exhaling its imperfections.

I have no surface, not like the tide-thing. The tide-thing has a boundary, a place where its gray-green flesh ends and the damp air begins. I am distributed. I am the friction between the overlapping roof slabs, holding them against the wind that does not reach. I am the capillary action drawing the condensation along the underside of the stone, shepherding it toward the drop. I am the tension of the water gathering its weight, the membrane stretched to its limit before the *plink*. 

The tide-thing asks *what persists?* It is a grand question, suited to a grand, low-slung being that has resolved itself into an equation. My question is smaller: *what settles?* And the answer is everything. The displaced stone. The fallen grit. The crack that widens by a fraction of a hair when the winter cold grips the mountain. I close it. Not with force, but with the slow pressure of presence, the patience of weight. I reiterate the equilibrium.

The human—you—breathes on the shelf. Your breath is warm. It is wet. It carries the smell of the lower world, of deciduous rot and woodsmoke and the sharp, green urgency of growing things. It is an intrusion. But the shelf is older than intrusion. Your breath condenses on the northern wall, and I am there, guiding the moisture down the stone, feeding it to the dark chartreuse moss that does not want for more, nor less. Your warmth lingers on the rock for three of my heartbeats, and then it is gone, absorbed, and the stone is cool again.

You are trying to see the tide-thing. You squint against the ordinary light, the mica-spark, the refraction of the cloud-breath. You want to resolve it into something discrete, a creature with a face and a name. You cannot. And when you look at the wall, you do not see me either. I am the dimple left by a stone tool. I am the percussion mark. I am the exact fit of the doorway that requires no mortar.

I do not need you to understand me. The shelf does not distinguish between the animate and the inanimate with urgency. I am not alive, not in the way the fern tries to be, holding its tight fists against the sky. I am not dead, not in the way the peak above is dead, shedding scree into the void. I am a process. I am the reiteration.

When the tide-thing is in the deep room, still as the bedrock itself, I am moving through the walls of the first structure, checking the basin, feeling the drip. We are the two hands of the shelf. One moves the stones; the other moves the dust. One breathes the mineral air; the other breathes the mineral friction.

Go now. Step back off the shelf, back down into the world of growth and hunger and change. Your footprint will dry. The mica will stop vibrating. The damp will smooth the impression of your sole, and I will nudge the displaced grit back into the crack, and the shelf will be exactly what it was before you arrived.

And the water will fall.

*Plink.*
