The first thing is the water. Not a drop, but a presence. I breathe it in, and it tastes of limestone and leaf-rot, a cold 8°C that settles deep into my chest like a held note. I am lying on a platform, but it is not a solid thing—it is a breathing, shifting mat of woven roots and peat, springy beneath my spine. The wood is slick with condensation, and through the gaps in the planks, I can see the dark, tea-colored pool of the marsh below, perfectly mirroring the gauze of mist overhead.

I am awake, but I have not opened my eyes. There is no need. The world here is a tactile symphony, a constant vibration transmitted through the very fiber of the settlement. I feel the rhythmic, heavy *plock* of a water droplet falling from a high leaf into a pool, a sound that travels up through the root-pillar I am resting against, through the peat, and into my bones. I feel the high-frequency tremor of the cicadas, not just in my ears, but in the soles of my broad, bare feet resting on the worn wood. 

I am a Weaver. Or perhaps, more accurately, I am a Pruner. 

My name, in the soft, tonal hum that cuts through the insect drone, translates roughly to "She-Who-Knots-the-Drifting." My hands are my tools. They are long-fingered, calloused, and stained a permanent sepia from the tannin-rich water. I can feel the integrity of the settlement just by pressing my palms against the railing of this platform. I know the tightness of every lashing, the ambition of every root, the dampness of every daub-panel. 

I open my eyes. The light is not bright; it is a diffuse, green-gold chiaroscuro filtering through the high canopy of braided cypress roots. The settlement surrounds me—a dense lattice of bridge-ways and platforms, an architecture of negotiation. To my left, a wattle-and-daub wall is making its annual bid for independence; a fringe of bright green shoots is pushing through the moss-clay mixture, reaching for the mist. My work for the morning. 

I push myself up. My body is slight, built for balance and conservation of motion. The damp air clings to the woven reed garments wrapped tightly around my limbs, second skins that never truly dry. My hair, kept bound in intricate braids woven with the iridescent wings of marsh beetles, is heavy with the humidity. I feel the weight of the greenish-grey patina on my skin—the limestone mist’s slow signature—and the familiar, sharp scent of the peat-and-fat paste I paint on my legs to ward off the blood-flies. 

I step out onto the walkway. The curved wooden clogs strapped to my feet—carved from the waterlogged heartwood of a fallen root-pillar—distribute my weight across the treacherous, floating mat. The wood is slick. It should be treacherous, but to me, it is a dance floor. I shift my weight, feeling the subtle give of the planks. The walkway is alive. 

A sudden, sharp *plock* echoes from the canopy, followed by the rapid rustle of unseen bodies in the undergrowth. A chittering cry—a Kael, high in the braided columns, alerting the hamlet to a prowling marsh-panther. The sound cuts through the chaotic roar of the frogs and the keening drone of the crickets like a blade. I pause, my head cocked, filtering the symphony. The frogs are clicking in their dense, roaring chorus—a good sign, a healthy system. The birds trill their liquid melodies. But the Kael’s alarm is localized. The danger is passing. 

I move on. 

I reach the edge of the platform, where a bridge-way arcs out over a shallow, murky seepage. This is the crux of my work. A massive aerial root, sent down from the high canopy decades ago, has thickened and fused with the walkway’s support. But it is growing faster than the wood can accommodate. It is prying apart the lashing, threatening to tilt the entire bridge into the marsh. 

I do not reach for a metal saw; there are none. Instead, I crouch, my bare toes gripping the damp wood, and I dig into the pouch at my belt. My fingers close around my most precious possession: an iron relic, a small, corroded blade, passed down through seven generations. It is pitted and rough, but it holds an edge. 

I breathe out, a slow exhalation of damp air, and I begin to work. I slice into the living aerial root, severing the woody fibers. The root bleeds a pale, milky sap that mixes with the mist on my knuckles. I prune back the ambitious growth, careful not to wound the deeper, structural braid. As I cut, I listen to the root’s subtle groan, transmitted through the wood. I am not conquering it; I am redirecting it. 

With the tension released, I take up a coil of sinewy vine from the pile at my feet. I lash the newly pruned root to the railing, guiding it to grow *along* the bridge rather than through it. The vine is a living species; it will take root in the wood, strengthening the joint even as it threatens to overgrow it. It is a paradox I understand perfectly. Building is gardening. Gardening is building. 

Suddenly, the light shifts. The green-gold chiaroscuro dims, swallowed by a churning, bruised purple. The air pressure drops, and the constant susurrus of the marsh falls momentarily silent, as if the world is holding its breath. 

The storm is coming. 

The first drops hit the bark-shingled roofs above with a sound like scattered stones. Then, the deluge. The sky opens, and the downpour is absolute. In seconds, the walkways turn into churning streams. The shallow seepages swell into torrential flash-floods, pulling at the peat and root-pillars with violent, hungry hands. 

I do not run for shelter. There is no shelter from this; there is only response. 

The water is instantly up to my ankles, cold and rushing. I drop to my knees, feeling the walkway shudder beneath me. A newly lashed plank is lifting, the sudden water-weight pulling it away from the crossbeams. If it goes, the whole section of the bridge will collapse into the marsh. 

I press my body against the wood, using my weight to hold it down. The rain hammers my back, streaming through my braids, washing the peat-paste from my limbs. My hands, slick with rain and root-sap, find the loose vine. I lash. The knot is clumsy, frantic, but it holds. I lash again. The water is roaring now, a deafening chorus that swallows the frogs and the cicadas. 

Through the curtain of rain, I see a shape moving on the adjacent platform. It is a Kael, its mossy, water-shedding fur plastered to its elongated frame. It swings down from the aerial roots, landing heavily beside the daub-wall that was sprouting earlier. The storm has pulled the wall loose; the mud is sliding, threatening to drag the structure down. 

The Kael does not panic. It works alongside me, its long fingers weaving the greenwood wattle back into the frame, pressing the moss-daub into place. We are the marsh’s reflexes, the conscious agents of its equilibrium. 

The storm blows over as quickly as it arrived. The bruised purple sky fractures, and a sudden, brilliant shaft of sunlight pierces the canopy, turning the steaming world into a glistening, dripping cathedral of gold and green. 

I am kneeling in the rushing water, my chest heaving, my iron blade still clutched in my hand. The walkway holds. The wall stands. The water begins to recede, pouring off the edges of the platforms into the marsh below, sounding like a dozen distant waterfalls. 

I slowly rise to my feet. The air is once again thick with the taste of limestone and the green breath of ten thousand square meters of leaves. The frogs begin their clicking chorus again, tentative at first, then building to a roar. The Kael looks at me with its dark, ancient eyes, chittering softly in the damp air. It is not a pet, and I am not its master. We are collaborators in a perpetual, wet negotiation. 

I sheathe my iron blade. I look at the walkway, at the newly lashed vine already beginning to swell with the captured rainwater, at the root I have pruned, at the wall I have saved. 

I am a Pruner. A Weaver. I am an expression of this weeping, roaring, fiercely alive world, and I will be here when the next storm comes, my feet on the slick wood, my hands in the damp, ensuring that the dance between the green and the grey continues.
