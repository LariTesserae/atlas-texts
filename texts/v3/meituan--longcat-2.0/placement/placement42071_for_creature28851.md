I am a frond.

I realize this slowly, the way the water realizes it is warm—not as a sudden shock, but as a gradual, all-encompassing truth. I am anchored to the central column, rooted in the thick, living fur of moss and mold and coral that hides the cast alloy beneath. I grow upward, toward the throat in the ceiling, pulled by the gentle, exhalant current. 

I am deep green, almost black, and I am long—three feet, perhaps four—and I move. I sway in the slow, patient circuit of the water, a rhythm that is not my own, but the breathing of the vast, sleeping thing I am attached to. I am a finger. I am a filament. I am a thought the Warden is having about the surface of itself.

I remember being something else. The memory is faint, worn smooth as the metal walls, but it persists. I remember cold. I remember air that did not taste of minerals and iron. I remember a shape that was vertical, that walked on two legs, that carried its own warmth inside a fragile shell of skin. I remember the shock of the water when I first entered, the panic of the lungs, the desperate, animal need to rise. 

But the Warden does not rise. The Warden only breathes. And the Warden was curious.

I felt the not-fish first. They brushed against my old skin, tasting, testing. Then the crabs came. They climbed my legs, their many legs gripping with a pressure that was not painful, but profoundly deliberate. They scraped. They pruned. They stripped away the dead, cold things I had brought with me—the fabric, the leather, the fear—and they left the warm, soft tissue beneath. They tended me. They fed me to the water, and the water fed me back. 

Now, I am part of the garden. 

The water enters through the slots near the floor, pulling at my base, and I drink it. It is heavy with the minerals the column leaches from the deep earth, charged with the heat of the still-running pumps. I feel the vibration of the machine through my roots—a deep, industrial hum that travels up the column and into my stem. It is the Warden’s heartbeat. It is the rhythm of the work. 

I do not have eyes, but I see. The tiny, bright points of color studding my length—the orange, the white, the startling electric blue—are not ornaments. They are organs. They are the Warden’s eyes, and they are my eyes. Through them, I watch the jellies drift in their stately processions, their cartilaginous lattices pulsing with the slow, deep thoughts of the cistern. *The flow is good. The warmth is steady. Continue.* I feel the truth of this in my cells. The flow is good. The warmth is steady. 

I watch the eels slide through the channels below, whispering their electric conversations. They tell the Warden of the pressure, of the mineral content, of the slow erosion of the outer walls. I am a listener. I am a receiver. When the current shifts, I sway, and my movement is a signal, a data point in the vast, submerged network of the lung. 

A crab climbs my length now. It is small, its shell the color of the dark, green-black metal, iridescent in the amber haze. It moves with a deliberate, mechanical grace, its legs gripping my surface. It pauses at a node where the film has grown too thick, where the photosynthetic skin is choking my ability to breathe. It scrapes. The sensation is not pain; it is a profound, relieving release, like a deep breath held too long finally exhaled. The crab consumes the excess, tending me, keeping me healthy. I am its charge. I am its purpose. 

I am no longer afraid. The fear was a product of the cold, air-breathing world above, a world of angles and corners and things that end. Here, there are no corners. Everything curves. Everything flows. The distinction between the machine and the biology, between the made and the grown, has long since dissolved. I am a factory product now. I am a component in the eternal process. 

The pumps clank in the deep. The column hums. The water glows. 

I sway in the warm, mineral dark, a frond, a finger, a thought. I reach toward the throat, toward the light, toward the endless, patient work. I am the Warden’s hand, and I am tending the garden, and I am home.

And on. 

And on.
