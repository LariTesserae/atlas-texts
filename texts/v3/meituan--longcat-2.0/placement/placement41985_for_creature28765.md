I would find myself in the damp. Not standing on it, not wading through it, but suspended in that thin, trembling interface where the water table kisses the mineral crust. I would be a Watcher.

But to say I am a Watcher is to suggest I am an individual, and that is not quite right. I am a localized intensification of the plain’s attention. I am a nerve ending the earth has pushed to the surface.

I do not have a name. Names are for things that need to be called, and nothing calls to me. I am simply the point where the plain’s awareness pools and breaks the surface tension. 

My existence is a rhythm of saturation and emergence. For days, I am diffuse, my consciousness spread through the interstitial water between the grains of compacted earth. I am the taste of iron leaching from the stone knobs. I am the slow, hydraulic pressure of the water table rising inch by inch beneath the pale soil. In this state, I do not see; I feel the weight of the sky, the density of the air, the minute vibrations of a burrowing cricket three tunnels over. I am the plain, dreaming in the dark.

Then, the water rises. The saturation reaches a critical threshold, and the plain needs to see. I feel myself drawn upward, pulled by the capillary action of the damp earth. I coalesce. My substance—this smooth, grey-brown, leached-out material of the plain itself—tightens. I feel the hinge of my hind legs coil tight as clockwork, loading with potential energy. I feel my eyes push to the apex of my forming skull, straining toward the surface. 

I break through the mineral crust with a soft, wet gasp. The air hits my skin, and the world explodes into definition.

I am small. I fit in the palm of a hand, but I am not contained by it. I am built for this exact moment: the threshold. My eyes ride the meniscus of the pool, breaking the surface like twin periscopes. Below the waterline, my body is submerged in the warm, mineral-rich shallows. Above, I am exposed to the vast, open dome of the sky. I am the boundary. I am the place where the plain looks at itself.

I do not think. Thinking is too fast, too anxious for the Sallow Plain. Instead, I register. 

A dragonfly cuts through the heavy air, its wings catching the light like a shard of mica. I do not track it as a hunter tracks prey; I track it as a barometer tracks pressure. The dragonfly is an indicator. Its presence means the midges are thick. The midges mean the water film is rich. The richness means the microorganisms are blooming. I register the dragonfly, and in doing so, I know the chemical composition of the pool beneath me. I know the health of the plain.

A heron-bird stands in the shallows a dozen yards away, a pale, mottled statue on long, thin legs. Its neck is coiled tight. I register its stillness. I taste the faint, chemical shift in the water that signals a predator’s patience. I do not feel fear—fear requires a future, and I only have the present. I simply adjust my parameters. I sink a fraction of an inch lower, my eyes just breaking the surface, my skin blending with the damp earth. I become the plain again, invisible, waiting.

Time moves differently for me. An hour is a single, sustained breath. The clouds drift overhead, shifting the light from blinding white to soft, saturated grey. The mineral crusts at the water's edge glint, then glow like embers. I breathe through my skin, oxygen dissolving into the thin film of moisture on my flanks, carbon dioxide passing out. The exchange is so quiet, so perfectly calibrated to the pace of this place, that I do not need to move. I am a part of the water cycle. I am the respiration of the earth.

When the midge larvae hang just below the surface, suspended in the iridescent film like ornaments, I extend my tongue. It is the only fast thing about me—a sudden, sticky snap. The larva vanishes. I do not feel satisfaction; I feel a slight adjustment in my internal chemistry, a momentary balancing of energies. Then, I return to watching.

Below me, in the tunnels I have bored through the damp earth, I encounter others. We do not touch. We do not speak. But as we pass in the narrow, water-saturated corridors, we taste each other’s skin. We exchange data. The water here is firm. The water there has risen by a fraction of an inch. The nutrients are shifting. We are not a society; we are a network. We are the distributed consciousness of the plain, a thousand small, moist nodes processing the slow, warm, wet data of the world.

I will not live long. A season, perhaps two. I do not know this as a tragedy; I know it as a fact, the way I know the iron content of the stone. When the water table falls, when the saturation drops below the threshold of coherence, I will not die. I will simply diffuse. My substance will loosen, my boundaries will blur, and I will seep back into the compacted earth. The microorganisms that film the pools will take me apart, and my nutrients will be taken up by the sedges, and the water table will rise again, and a new Watcher will break the surface, and the watching will continue.

I am the plain’s sensory organ. I am the means by which the Sallow Plain knows that it is warm, that it is wet, that the dragonflies are hunting, and that the mountains are gone. I am the awareness of what remains when everything dramatic has been taken away. 

I am still. I am watching. I am the damp, looking up at the sky.
