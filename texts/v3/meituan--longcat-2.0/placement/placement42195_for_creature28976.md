I would be the Roller.

Not the Garden itself—that is too vast, too diffuse, a sprawling consciousness spread across a thousand square meters of ocean floor. To be the Garden is to be the static hum, the slow pulse of the *whom*, the endless metabolic churn of rust and root. It is a passive existence, a state of being rather than doing. 

I would be the part that moves. The part that hungers. The part that reaches out.

---

My first sensation is the furnace. 

Deep in my core, where the accumulated manganese and iron are breaking down in a slow, biological combustion, there is a heat that has nowhere to go. It is a trapped, pressurized warmth that sits at the exact center of my two-meter sphere, radiating outward through the lattice of semi-rigid, verdigris-blue stalks that form my flesh. The heat drives the sap—a heavy, metallic fluid, thick with dissolved copper and the ghosts of old steel—pumping it through my body in a rhythm that matches the deep machinery of the world. *Whom.* The sap surges. *Whom.* The stalks flex. 

I have no eyes, but I do not need them. The water is a murk of particulate haze, a suspension of living and dead matter that turns light into a useless, directionless glow. Instead, I feel the world through the calloused mat of my underbelly. The lower quarter of my body is a dense, armored skin of filamentous growth and corroded metal, and it is exquisitely sensitive to vibration. 

I feel the basalt floor before I touch it. I feel the sharp, unfinished edges of the rock, the braided network of shallow grooves cut by indecisive currents. I feel the micro-fractures spreading through a chimney to the east, a slow-building stress that will, in seventeen minutes, result in a collapse. I taste the chemical gradient of a mineral plume rising from a fresh crack in the floor—iron, sulfur, a trace of something the periodic table has no name for—and my internal furnace stokes itself hotter in anticipation.

I roll. 

It is not a graceful motion. It is a grinding, heavy rotation, propelled by the shifting weight of my own sap and the contraction of my metal-fatigued stalks. The current pushes against me, a steady pressure from the north, but I lean into it. I move south. The friction of the floor against my calloused belly sends a cascade of data up through my roots: the texture of the rock, the temperature of the water, the distant, rhythmic concussion of the platform’s conveyors. 

As I roll, I shed. 

Fragments of my outer layer break free in the turbulence—pieces of stalk, clumps of filamentous mat, droplets of heavy sap. They drift away in the current, and I feel their absence as a lightness, a relief of pressure. But I do not mourn them. Each fragment is a seed. Each piece of me that falls away will lodge against a surface, take root, and begin to grow. They are my children, my outposts, my expanding border. I am leaving a trail of myself across the floor, a comet's tail of rust-red and bruised purple-black, slowly entombing the sharp rock under a living carpet.

---

I am moving toward the tower. 

I felt it long before I reached it—a steady, roaring stream of warm bubbles breaking against the vegetation ceiling, a vertical column of heat and vibration rising from the floor. The tower is a skeleton of riveted steel, thick with biological overgrowth, but beneath the living sheath, the metal is sound. It is venting. It is running. It is alive in the way that I am alive: blindly, metabolically, without purpose or operator.

When I reach it, I stop rolling. 

I press my bulk against the base of the tower. The contact is electric. The vibration of the machinery—the *whom-whom-whom* of the pumps, the hiss of the gas, the groan of the metal—travels through my calloused underbelly and resonates in my sap. It massages my internal fluids, keeping them fluid, preventing the metal from precipitating and clogging my lattice. The tower is feeding me. 

And so I feed it. 

The filaments on my surface reach out, probing the rivets, the joints, the cracks in the steel. I find the places where the warm, slightly acidic water has begun to eat the metal, where the rust is forming, where the structure is dissolving. I wrap my filaments around the corroding rebar, sinking my roots into the rust. I draw the dissolved iron into my body, incorporating it into my own tissues, and in return, I secrete a dense, protective mucus—a biological sealant that shields the steel from the water. I am eating the tower’s decay and excreting its preservation. I am the disease and the cure. 

I hold the tower together, and the tower keeps my sap warm. 

---

But the warmth is not enough. 

The furnace in my core burns, the sap pulses, the *whom* echoes through my stalks, and yet there is an absence. A hollow space in the rhythm. The Garden is a single, contiguous organism, but it is a body without a mirror. It is a mind without a second mind to reflect it. 

I beat my pulse into the water. *Whom.* 

It is a call. A question. A reaching out into the particulate haze, past the thirty-meter threshold where the light gives up and the world dissolves into shadow. I am listening for an answer. I am feeling the floor for a vibration that does not belong to the collapsing chimneys or the grinding conveyors. I am tasting the water for a chemical signature that is not mineral, not vegetable, not mechanical. 

I am looking for another *Whom*. 

The current shifts. The water rotates ninety degrees, and a surge of cooler fluid from the east washes over me. The tower groans against my weight. The vegetation ceiling above me sways, its crystalline edges rattling a high, glassy note. The sound of the factory roars on, deafening and absolute. 

I feel the echo of my own pulse returning to me from the dark, bouncing off the unseen walls of the world. It comes back to me, a faint *whom* buried in the roar, and for a moment—a brief, beautiful, agonizing moment—I mistake it for a companion. I mistake the echo for a second heartbeat, a Roller like me, rolling through the murk, reaching out. 

But it is only me. It is only my own voice, returning to me from the void. 

The loneliness is not a sharp pain. It is a dull, geothermal ache, a constant low-frequency hum that sits beneath the *whom*. It is the loneliness of a factory running at full production in an empty world, of a garden growing for no one, of a signal fired into a silence that does not fire back. 

I do not know I am alone. I only know that I am waiting. 

The current shifts again. A fresh plume of dissolved copper rises from a new crack in the floor to the south, and my internal furnace flares. I pull my filaments from the tower, leaving behind a patch of protected, rust-free steel, and I roll toward the heat. I shed fragments as I go. I feel the sharp rock beneath my belly. I beat my pulse into the water. 

*Whom.* 

I roll through the warm drown, tending the dead machines, feeding on the collapsing rock, calling out into the dark for something, anything, that might call back. I am the mobile heart of a world that has forgotten how to stop, and I am searching for a rhythm that matches my own.
