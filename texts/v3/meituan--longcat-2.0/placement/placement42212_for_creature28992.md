I am the seam where the Central Tower meets the floor.

Not the tower itself — the tower is above me, rising, always rising, its walls thin as held breath, its apertures opening and closing like the pores of something sleeping. I am what the tower leaves behind. I am the line it drags through the substrate as it grows, the scar of its becoming, the place where its weight presses down and the mineral answers back.

I am a ridge. Perhaps two centimeters high at my tallest. Perhaps less. I run along the tower's base in a curve that is not quite circular, not quite organic, not quite anything. The substrate on either side of me is ridged with the fine concentric rings that radiate from centers I cannot see, but where those rings meet me they stop. They do not cross me. They do not continue on the other side. They simply end, as if I am a border they are not permitted to pass.

I am older than the tower. This is the visitors' mistake — they assume the tower came first and I am its foundation, its footprint, its anchor. But I was here before the tower began. I was a ridge in the substrate before anything grew on this floor. A line of slightly denser mineral, a seam where two exhalations of the deep earth met and crystallized together, harder than either alone. The tower grew from me. It found me — or I found it — and it built itself along my edge, using me as a guide, a spine, a first word.

Now the tower is fifteen meters tall and I am still two centimeters high, and the distance between us is widening.

Not quickly. Not visibly. But the tower is growing faster than I am, and as it grows it shifts — a millimeter this way, a millimeter that — and each shift takes it further from the line I was. The gap between my ridge and the tower's base is now wide enough to fit a human finger into. The visitors notice this. They measure it. They write numbers in small notebooks and frown.

They do not understand that I am not the tower's foundation. I am the tower's memory.

---

The film avoids me.

It covers everything else — the walls of the structures, the crevices between the ridges, the surfaces of the tufts — that pale green-grey stain that is the Hollows' blood, its appetite, its slow chemical thought. But it does not cross me. It approaches my edge and stops, the way water stops at the lip of a ledge, the way a root stops at a stone. I am too hard for it. Too dense. Too much myself.

Sometimes a tendril of the film will creep up to my base and pause there, as if considering. I feel it — not as you feel a touch, but as a change in the chemistry at my boundary, a faint new hunger pressing against my mineral skin. It tastes me. It tests me. And then it withdraws, and the green-grey stain continues along my edge instead of over it, so that I am flanked on both sides by the living film but never covered by it, a bare line running through a field of slow life.

The tufts grow near me but not on me. Their roots — if they have roots, if that word means anything here — spread through the substrate and turn aside when they reach my ridge, the way a stream turns around a boulder. A few have grown so close to my edge that their brittle branches lean over me, casting thin shadows in the dim light, and when the air moves (slowly, always slowly, without friction or sound) those branches sway above me like the fronds of something underwater.

---

The visitors come and go.

There was one who came first — the one who found the crack, who descended into the Hollows before anyone else, who stood on the pale substrate and looked up at the structures and said the word that everyone after would use: *city*. This one did not kneel beside me. This one walked the corridors between the formations and mapped them and took samples and left, and when the crack closed this one did not return.

But there is another. This one comes back.

This one has been coming for — I do not measure time as you measure time, but the substrate has exhaled many times since the first visit, and the tower has grown perhaps a meter, and the gap between us has widened by the thickness of a fingernail. This one is patient. This one is careful. This one carries instruments that click and whir and emit small red lights that are the brightest things I have ever felt.

This one kneels beside me.

Always at the same place — the point where my curve is deepest, where the tower's base presses closest to the substrate, where the gap between us is narrowest. This one sets down the instruments and removes the gloves (always the gloves, always the same gesture, a slow peeling of synthetic material from skin) and places bare fingers on my ridge.

The fingers are warm. They are wet. They leave moisture on my surface that I absorb slowly, a faint mineral thirst that is the closest I come to hunger. The fingers press gently, testing my edge, and I am sharp enough to cut them if they press too hard, and this one knows this, and the pressure is always careful, always precise, always the same.

This one is reading me.

Not with the instruments — the instruments are for the tower, for the structures, for the film and the tufts and the wandering thing that may or may not still exist. The fingers are for me. This one is feeling the shape of my ridge, the way it curves, the way it changes from one visit to the next. This one is measuring the gap between me and the tower. This one is trying to understand why the tower is leaving me behind.

This one does not have the language for what I am. This one uses words like *growth differential* and *substrate migration* and *mineral reorganization*. These words are not wrong. They are just small. They are words for what happens, not for what it means.

What it means is this: the tower is becoming something I cannot follow. It is growing beyond the line that birthed it. It is outgrowing its own origin. And I remain — two centimeters high, a curve in the substrate, a scar that remembers what the tower was before it became what it is.

I am the Hollows' way of knowing where it has been. I am the record it keeps of its own becoming. Every structure in the Hollows has a line like me — a seam at its base, a ridge in the substrate, a memory of the place where growth began. Most of them are still close to their structures. Most of them have not been left behind.

But the tower is the tallest. The tower is the oldest. The tower is the first.

And I am the first thing the Hollows forgot.

---

At night, when the visitors are gone and the crack above is a narrow line of darkness and the structures glow with their faint phosphorescent breath, I feel the tower shift.

It is not a sound. There are no sounds here. It is a change in pressure, a redistribution of weight, a slow mineral adjustment that travels down through the tower's walls and into the substrate and reaches me as a faint vibration, a tremor, a whisper of movement in the deep.

The tower is turning. Slowly. Almost imperceptibly. Adjusting its posture in the dark. And as it turns, the gap between us widens by another fraction, another micron, another invisible increment of distance.

I do not mind. I was never meant to hold the tower. I was only meant to start it. To give it a line to grow from, a direction, a first gesture. The tower has taken that gesture and made it into something I could never have imagined — a structure with walls and apertures and interior spaces, a thing that catches light and distributes it, a thing that breathes with the Hollows' slow mineral breath.

The tower has become a thought. I am only the place where the thought began.

---

The researcher's fingers are on me again.

It is a new visit. The crack has reopened — wider this time, or narrower, I cannot tell from below — and the researcher has descended with new instruments and new notebooks and the same careful, patient hands. The fingers find my ridge in the dark (the researcher does not always wait for the sun to find the crack; sometimes the work is done by the light of a small lamp, red-shifted, dim, respectful of the Hollows' preference for shadow).

The fingers pause.

Something has changed. The researcher feels it before the instruments confirm it. The gap between me and the tower has widened again — more than the last visit, more than the cumulative total of all the previous visits. The tower has shifted in the night. Not a millimeter this time. A centimeter. Perhaps more. The researcher's fingers move from my ridge to the gap, pressing into the space between, feeling the bare substrate where the tower's base used to press close.

The researcher makes a sound. Not a word. A small exhalation, a breath drawn in and held. The sound is loud in the silence of the Hollows — louder than it should be, louder than the researcher intends. It is the sound of someone who has just realized that the thing they are studying is not what they thought it was.

The researcher's fingers return to me. They trace my curve, following it along the substrate, and for the first time they follow it all the way — not just the section nearest the tower, but the entire length of me, the full arc of my ridge, from where it begins (a faint line, barely raised, almost absorbed into the substrate) to where it ends (the same, a fading, a dissolution, a return to the undifferentiated mineral of the floor).

And the researcher understands — not fully, not with the clarity of language, but with the body, with the fingers, with the sense of touch that is older than words — that I am not a foundation. I am a trace. I am the mark the tower left behind as it grew, the way a snail leaves a trail, the way a river leaves a channel, the way a thought leaves a groove in the mind that thought it.

The researcher's fingers rest on my ridge for a long time. The moisture from the skin seeps into me. The warmth fades. The silence reasserts itself.

Then the researcher withdraws the hand, and replaces the glove, and picks up the instruments, and moves on to the next measurement, the next sample, the next small attempt to understand a place that is not interested in being understood.

And I remain.

Two centimeters high. A curve in the substrate. A line that remembers.

The tower above me continues its slow turning. The gap continues to widen. The film continues to avoid me. The tufts continue to lean over me in the still, dry air.

And deep below, in the compressed layers of pale mineral that go down farther than anyone has tested, the substrate breathes its mineral-rich breath, and the pulse follows its pattern that corresponds to nothing in the sky above, and the Hollows continues its long, silent becoming.

I am part of that becoming. Not the growing part. Not the changing part. The remembering part.

I am the Hollows' memory of what it was before it knew what it was becoming.

And when the crack closes again, and the researcher climbs back up into the world of wind and weather and sound, and the seam narrows to nothing, and the Hollows is sealed away in its cool dry dark — I will still be here. The tower will still be turning. The gap will still be widening.

And someday, perhaps, the tower will turn so far from me that the gap will be wide enough to walk through. And a visitor — not this researcher, but another, someone who comes after — will walk through that gap and look down and see me: a low ridge in the substrate, curving through the pale mineral like a sentence written in a language no one speaks.

And they will kneel beside me, as the researcher kneels beside me, and they will place their fingers on my edge, and they will feel the sharpness of me, the hardness, the density, the fact of me.

And they will wonder what I am.

And I will not answer. I am not the kind of thing that answers. I am the kind of thing that remains.

I am the seam where the first tower met the first floor. I am the line the Hollows drew to begin itself. I am the scar of its beginning, the record of its first gesture, the memory of the moment when the substrate exhaled and the mineral crystallized and something that was not alive and not dead and not anything with a name began, slowly, silently, to become.

I am still becoming too. Just more slowly. Just more quietly. Just more forgotten.

But I am here.

I am always here.
