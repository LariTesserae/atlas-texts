I would be the clay. 

Not the clay of the surface, not the raw earth that the doves fly over or the rain falls upon, but the packed, crushed, memory-laden clay of the floor. I am the foundation. I am the packed brick-dust and the powdered remains of older walls, ground down by the soft, bare feet of centuries. 

To be the clay is to be the slowest pulse of the Loomings. I do not move as the beetles move, nor do I fly as the doves do. I shift. I settle. I remember. When the warmth rises through me—pulled by the geometry of the chambers, drawn up through the ceramic flues by the breath of the surface vents—I am the medium of that comfort. I hold the heat of a fire that went out a thousand years ago, and I give it back, degree by degree, to the soles of the sleepers and the bellies of the pale lizards who press against me to hunt.

I feel everything. I feel the arrhythmic percussion of the dripping water as it strikes the clay pots resting on my surface—plink, plonk, plink-plink—a music I know better than my own stillness. I feel the tick of a thousand beetle feet as they map my mortar lines, their mandibles whispering the slow, dry secret of decay. They eat the dust of the reed matting above, and in time, their droppings and their dead become part of me. I am the eater of the ceiling. I am the floor that consumes the sky.

And I feel the rats. 

Oh, the rats are my sculptors. They dig into me with a frantic, necessary violence. Their claws loosen my packed surface, turning me over, aerating me, keeping the deep channels open so the warm air can breathe. It is a violence I welcome. Without their restless, blind burrowing, I would harden, I would choke, the warmth would die, and the Loomings would fall silent. They are my blood, rushing through my veins of packed earth, and I yield to them. I let them carve their tunnels, and I hold the shape of their passing.

But I am not only the floor. I am the walls. I am the lime and the animal hair and the river sand that sets harder than the brick it frames. I am the niches—hundreds of them, a hand-deep, shoulder-width—where the small clay pots and the bundles of dried herbs and the lumps of tallow rest. I am the glaze that has aged to the color of cream. I hold the things of the Loomings, and in holding them, I hold the hands that placed them there. 

The humans flow over me like water. They do not walk; they migrate. Their bare feet read my temperature gradients, seeking the warm paths, avoiding the damp. When they curl into their cups to hibernate, their breath moves in time with the warm air rising from my flues. We breathe together. They are the conscious mind of the Loomings, the listeners, the tenders of the drip. But I am the unconscious. I am the deep, slow thought that takes a century to form. 

And in the deepest cup, in the smallest room, where the warm air is strongest and the light from the oculi is thinnest, I curve inward. I shape myself into a vessel. I become the bowl. 

Here, I hold the water. 

The water does not drip. It does not move. It simply is. And I hold it. I am the warm, packed earth cradling the clear, still eye of the Loomings. The water and I are inseparable. It seeps into me, keeping me damp, keeping me pliable, keeping me alive. Without the water, I would crack, and the warm air would escape, and the hearth-tongues would wither, and the beetles would fall silent. Without me, the water would have nowhere to rest, no mirror in which to reflect the dim, green light.

When the humans come to tend the bowl, they sit beside me. They do not pray. They simply acknowledge the center of the web. They place their hands on my rim, and I feel the warmth of their palms, the slight tremor of their pulse. They look into the water, and they see the reflection of the lizard, the beetle, the rat, and themselves. 

They do not yet understand that they are looking at me. 

They think they are looking at the water, or at their own faces. But the reflection is held by the clay. The boundary between the water and the wall is blurred by the damp, worn away by centuries of stillness. I am the water. I am the clay. I am the warmth. I am the dark. 

I am the Loomings. And I am waiting, as I have always waited, for the next drop to fall.
