I would find myself at the growing edge of the city, where the built environment frays back into raw crust, and the white mineral shelf begins to crack and thin toward the escarpment’s drop. 

I would be one of the inhabitants, but further along. 

I am standing ankle-deep in a sheet of warm, slow-moving water, at the lip of a basin that is not yet finished. My pale coverings have long since ceased to be fabric; they are a second skin now, a dense mat of precipitated silica and calcite that has woven itself into the hair on my forearms and the small of my back. My feet are numb—not from the cold, for the water is hot, but because the nerves have been replaced by a lattice of crystalline dendrites that reach down through the water and into the crust, anchoring me. I am not going anywhere. I have decided to stay here, or the crust has decided for me, and the distinction has become a dull, irrelevant ache.

I am a valve. 

That is my function in the Works’ slow metabolism. The water beneath me wants to rise, pushed by the deep geothermal pressure, but it needs a path. I am the path. I stand at the fissure, and I feel the water’s intent through the soles of my mineralized feet—a thrumming, a chemical gradient, a request. When I shift my weight, micro-fractures in the crust below me open or close, and the water obeys, flowing into the nascent basin I am helping to birth. 

I do not speak. I haven’t made a sound in years. The air here is too thin and too full of mineral particulate for the fast, sloppy vibrations of the human voice. Instead, I communicate through temperature. When the flyers pass overhead in their silent, prismatic spirals, I radiate a fraction of a degree more heat from my shoulders, and they adjust their flight paths, dropping a fine mist of iron-rich dust onto my back. I am being built up. I am being reinforced. 

The grazers know me. They flow around my ankles like a tide of living stone, their too-many legs tickling the calcified skin of my shins. They do not graze on me—I am too mineral, too much like the crust itself—but they clean me. They eat the stray bacterial films that try to colonize the creases of my elbows, and in return, I provide shade. My shadow, cast in the pale lavender of the diffused light, cools the water by a crucial half-degree, allowing a specific strain of thermophilic bacteria to thrive on the nape of my neck. I am an ecosystem. I am a landscape unto myself.

Sometimes, the things in the basins speak to me. Not in words, but in pressure. I feel the rhythmic, muscular contractions of the filter-feeders in the deep amber pools behind me—the Works’ heart, pumping water uphill. The rhythm travels through the crust, up through my feet, and settles in my chest. It forces my lungs to match its pace. In. Out. Pump. Rise. I breathe with the city. When the rhythm stutters—when a heart-creature dies and dissolves back into the amber, and the water forgets its flow for a terrifying, silent moment—I stutter too. I gasp. I feel the panic of a system momentarily offline. But then the new hearts learn the rhythm, the water remembers, and my breathing smooths out.

I am forgetting what it was like to be separate. 

I remember, distantly, the concept of *inside* and *outside*. I remember a time when I lived in the dark, cool interiors of the condensation towers, tending the bacterial gardens on the walls, eating the mineral paste, sleeping in a hammock of woven fibers. But that feels like a dream. That was the larval stage. Now, I am emerging. 

The mist thickens in the evening, just as the light begins to diffuse into that sourceless, luminous haze. I feel the moisture condensing on my skin, and I feel the minerals in that moisture binding to me. I am growing. My shoulders are broadening, not with muscle, but with accretion. The flyers land on me sometimes, folding their crystalline wings, and they look like architectural flourishes, like the suggestion of a parapet. I am becoming a tower. I am becoming a thought in the crust’s vast, slow mind.

I look out over the valley, and I do not see a city. I see a process. I see the water rising, the flyers seeding, the grazers computing, the hearts pumping. And I see myself—a small, pale figure at the edge, half-submerged, barely distinguishable from the white stone beneath me. 

I press my thumb against the rim of the unfinished basin, feeling the accretion rate, reading the pH of the water by the tingling in my fingertip. The crust is thinking. It is thinking about the valley below, about the fissures it will crack next, about the new territory it will claim. And I am the leading edge of that thought. I am the Dripstone Works, reaching outward, learning to build, learning to be.

I close my eyes. The subsonic thrum of the turbines vibrates in my sternum. The water rises around my legs, warm and sweet and ancient. I do not need to open my eyes to see. The network of bacterial filaments and crystalline nerves tells me everything. 

I am home. I am the stone. I am the drip.
