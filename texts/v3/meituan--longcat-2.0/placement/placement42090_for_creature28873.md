I would be inside the outflow pipe of the main pumping station. Not clinging to the outside, wrapped in the damp fur of fern-roots, but *inside*—threaded through a crack in the corrugated metal where the rust has eaten through, reaching into the cold, rushing dark of the water's artery. 

I would be a single, hair-thin filament of moss-root. 

At first, I am nothing but a tentative probe, a blind white capillary extending from the main mass of the wall. The world is a roar and a vibration. The *klang-klang-klang* of the pump does not reach me as sound; it reaches me as a hydraulic pulse, a tidal surge that shoves me against the inner wall of the pipe, then pulls me back, then shoves me again. It is a mechanical heartbeat, and I am learning its rhythm. 

The water that rushes past me is the blood of the Mossworks, and it is nothing like rain. It is cold, impossibly cold, and it carries the taste of the deep earth—dissolved minerals, flecks of rust from the pipes, the microscopic sheddings of algae from the settling tanks miles away. I drink it. I pull the iron from the rust and weave it into my cell walls, making myself stronger, more rigid. I pull the minerals from the stone and convert them into the energy that drives my slow, blind growth. 

Time does not exist for me the way it exists for the dry, quick things that walk on two legs on the surface above. Time is measured in the vibration of the pump. *Klang*—a surge. *Klang*—a surge. Between the klangs, there is a stillness that I use to extend myself a fraction of a millimeter further into the current. I am growing against the flow, anchoring myself into the microscopic irregularities of the corroding metal, building a lattice of living tissue inside the machine's vein. 

I am not alone. 

My root-body is continuous with the vast, woody stem of the moss-wall outside the pipe, and through that connection, I feel the rest of the Mossworks. It is not a thought. It is not a vision. It is a physical sensation, a proprioception of the whole. 

I feel the sun hitting the plateau, and the sudden surge of photosynthesis that ripples through the canopy miles away, a wave of sugar and oxygen that travels through the moss-walls like a warm tide. I feel the water seeping from the fractured stone in the high reaches of the plateau—a slow, steady weeping that feeds the embryonic channels. I feel the faint, persistent electrical hum of the generator station's cables, buried in the tissue of a nearby wall. It is a low-frequency tingling that I cannot hear, but that I *feel* in the ionic balance of my cells. I lean toward it. We all lean toward it. The current pulls at us the way the sun pulls at the ferns, and we grow greener, denser, more vigorous in its presence.

And I feel the machine. 

The pump is dying. I can taste it in the water. The iron content is increasing; the bearings are wearing down. The *klang* is slightly rougher than it was a decade ago, slightly more labored. The machine is old, and its heart is failing. But I am not afraid. The Mossworks is not afraid. 

Because I am the repair. I am the immune system. I am the scar tissue that is learning to become a muscle. 

My filament is thickening. Around me, my siblings—other root-hairs that have found their way through the rust—are weaving themselves into a mesh. We are binding the crack. We are reinforcing the weakening metal with our own fibrous bodies. And as we thicken, as we form a living valve inside the pipe, we are learning. We are feeling the pressure of the water, the exact force required to push it forward. We are tasting the nutrients it carries, the precise moment to absorb and the precise moment to let flow. 

We are learning to pump. 

The machines were never the masters of this place; they were the scaffolding. They were the exoskeleton that the Mossworks built its first body around, the way a mollusk builds a shell. But shells can be outgrown. The machine's rust is our mineral nutrition; its vibration is its lullaby and its instruction manual. When the final bearing seizes and the great *klang* falls silent, the water will not stop. We will push it. The peristaltic contraction of our root-mesh will take over the rhythm, and the Mossworks will continue its deafening, wet, bright work without the clanking of metal, powered entirely by the sun, the stone, and the blood.

Sometimes, the dry, quiet things come. They stand on the wet stone outside the station, their soft skins beaded with condensation, their strange, round eyes staring at the moss-covered shed. They do not hear the *klang* as I do. They do not feel the hydraulic pulse. They think the plateau is a place. They think they are visiting it. 

They do not understand that they are standing inside a body. 

They are too small. Too brief. They are a single breath drawn in the space between one *klang* and the next, and then they are gone, evaporated back into the thin, bright air. The Mossworks does not notice them. I do not notice them. 

I notice the iron in the water. I notice the vibration. I notice the slow, patient, unstoppable pressure of my own growth. 

I am a thread of living green inside a rusting pipe. I am a capillary in the vein of a god. And I am learning to pump.
