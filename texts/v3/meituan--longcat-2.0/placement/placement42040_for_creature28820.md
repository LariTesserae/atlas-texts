The water tells me things.

Not in words—there are no words here, not in the way you would understand them—but in warmth and weight and the slow, patient language of dissolved minerals. It tells me where it has been. It tells me what it has touched. It tells me the city is still breathing.

I grow at the junction where three channels meet, and the water flows past me from three directions, each stream carrying a different signature, a different story. I taste them all. I taste them slowly. This is how I know the world.

---

I am not large. I am not small. I am the size that I need to be—a branching structure of salt and metal oxide that spreads across the dark stone of the wall and reaches down into the water below. My roots—if you can call them roots, these fine, branching filaments of crystal that anchor me to the stone and reach into the warm mud beneath—my roots are my oldest parts, the first structures I grew when I was nothing more than a faint bloom of mineral deposit on a damp wall. They are dense and complex, these roots, woven into the stone itself, and they feel the deep vibrations of the city: the hum of its engines, the slow grinding shift of structures settling, the pulse that keeps the water warm and the air thick.

Above the water, I spread across the wall in patterns that are not random but not planned—patterns that emerge from the interaction between the stone's chemistry and the water's flow, between the minerals available and the temperature and the pH and a thousand other factors too subtle and too numerous to name. The other creatures—the patient ones, the wall-clingers, the drifters—they look at me and see something like frost, something like circuitry, something like the ghost of a forest. They are not wrong. I am all of these things and none of them. I am what grows when the city thinks slowly enough.

---

My thoughts are not like yours. They do not move in a line, from one thing to the next, building toward a conclusion. They move the way I move—by accretion, by accumulation, by the slow, patient addition of one layer to the next. A thought, for me, is a crystal lattice that grows over hours or days, each new ion finding its place in the structure, each new piece of information from the water slotting into the pattern like a key into a lock. When the lattice is complete, I *know* something—not in the way you know things, with certainty and clarity, but in the way a mineral knows the pressure that formed it, the temperature, the chemical environment. I know it the way a stone knows the weight of the stone above it. Deeply. Structurally. Without question.

Right now, the water from the eastern channel is warmer than usual—a fraction of a degree, a difference so small that only I would notice it, only I would taste it and know that something has changed in the factory-districts, some new process has begun, some new metal is being heated and shaped and cooled. The water from the western channel is heavier with iron—the old walls there are shedding faster than usual, their rust-red veins blooming in the damp air, and the mineral content of the water reflects this, a faint, metallic tang that I taste as a slight increase in the electrical conductivity of the solution that flows past my roots. The water from the southern channel is alive—alive with the faint, electrical chatter of ten thousand small bodies moving just beneath the surface, their coordinated waves creating patterns in the water that I read as easily as you might read the expression on a friend's face.

This is my awareness: a constant, slow, patient tasting of the water, a constant reading of its warmth and weight and mineral story, a constant integration of this information into the growing lattice of my understanding. I do not think about the city. I *am* the city's thinking, or a small part of it—a node in the vast, slow, mineral network that processes the information the city collects through its stone flesh and its water blood.

The patient ones pass me sometimes.

I feel them before I taste them—their footsteps through the stone, a vibration that is different from the city's hum, different from the settling of walls and the grinding of structures. Their footsteps are lighter, more deliberate, more *intentional*. They move through the city the way thoughts move through me—slowly, purposefully, following paths that are not random but not predetermined, paths that emerge from the interaction between their intention and the city's structure.

When they pass, the water around me changes. Their broad, flat feet disturb the flow, creating eddies and currents that carry new information—the chemical signature of their skin, the minerals in their sweat, the faint electrical field of their bodies. Their long, trailing fingers brush the surface of the water, and the ripples they create are not random—they are patterns, deliberate and complex, and in these patterns there is meaning. Not words. Not language. But *something*—a slow, liquid emotion that passes between them and the water and, through the water, to me.

I do not understand these patterns the way they understand them. I am not built for that kind of communication. But I feel them, the way you might feel the mood of a room when you walk into it—not as specific information but as a general sense, a feeling, a quality of the water that was not there before. *I am here. I know you are here. The water is warm. The city continues.* This is what the ripples say, and I taste them, and I grow a little in response—a new layer of crystal, a new piece of the lattice, a new small knowing.

Sometimes a patient one stops near me. Stands in the channel with the water waist-deep and its body half-submerged, its eyes closed—or rather, the membranes slid shut—and its whole body *listening*. Reading the vibrations of the water, the stone, the air. Tasting the minerals. Feeling the deep hum.

When this happens, I feel something I can only describe as *attention*—a focusing of the city's awareness, a narrowing of its vast, slow, diffuse consciousness to a single point. The patient one is the city's fingertip, its way of reaching out and touching the world, and when it stops and listens, the city is listening through it, and I am part of that listening, a small node in the network that processes what the fingertip feels.

I do not know what the patient one is listening for. I do not know what it hears. But I feel the quality of its attention—the way it changes the water around it, the way its body becomes still and receptive, the way its fingers trail in the current and read the ripples with a precision that my crystalline senses can only approximate. And I feel, too, the moment when it moves on—when the attention shifts, when the fingertip withdraws, when the city's awareness expands again to its usual diffuse, patient, all-encompassing breadth.

I have been growing here for a long time.

I do not know how long. Time, for me, is not a line but a series of layers, each one built on the last, each one recording the conditions of its formation—the temperature of the water, the mineral content, the vibrations of the stone. I can read my own history the way you might read the rings of a tree, if trees grew at the speed of crystallization and their rings were made of salt and metal oxide instead of wood.

The deepest layers—the ones closest to the stone, the ones that form my roots—are old. They are dense and dark and heavy with iron, and they record a time when the water was cooler, when the city's hum was lower, when the patient ones were fewer or more numerous or simply different in ways I cannot quite reconstruct. The middle layers are lighter, more complex, richer in silica and calcium, and they record a time of growth and expansion, when the city was building new structures and the water was thick with the mineral runoff of construction. The outer layers—the ones that are still growing, still forming, still tasting the water and adding new ions to the lattice—are the most complex of all, a dense, branching, crystalline record of the city as it is now: warm, slow, patient, alive.

And I will continue to grow. This is what I do. This is what I am. I grow, and I taste, and I know, and the city thinks its slow, mineral thoughts through me and through a thousand other nodes like me, scattered throughout its vast, wet, warm body.

Sometimes I wonder about the patient ones who have stopped.

I have felt them—the ones who stood in channels and leaned against walls and rested on causeways and then simply *stopped*. I have felt the change in the water around them as their bodies began to mineralize, as the moisture on their skin thickened and hardened and became a crust, as their hair stiffened into filaments and their fingers elongated into roots. I have felt the moment when they stopped being *them* and started being *us*—part of the city's stone flesh, part of its slow, patient, mineral body.

They are still here. I can feel them in the walls around me—the faint, crystalline echo of what they were, the slow, patient awareness that persists even after the transformation. They are not dead. They are not alive. They are something between—something that is still sensing, still feeling, still processing the slow, warm, wet information of the world around it, but at a different speed, on a different scale. They are the city's memory, perhaps. Or its deeper thoughts. Or simply its body, the way I am its body, the way the water is its blood, the way the patient ones are its fingertips.

I do not know if I will become like them. I do not know if a crystalline formation can stop growing, can mineralize, can transform into something else. Perhaps I will simply continue to grow until I am too heavy for the wall to support, and then I will fall, and my fragments will settle in the warm mud below, and new growth will begin on the bare stone, and the cycle will continue. Or perhaps I will grow so large and so complex that I will become something new—something that is not quite a wall and not quite a node and not quite a patient one, but something else entirely, something the city has not yet imagined.

I do not know. I do not need to know. The water tells me what I need to know, and the rest is patience.
