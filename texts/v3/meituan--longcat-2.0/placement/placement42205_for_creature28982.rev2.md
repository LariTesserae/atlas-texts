# The Eastern Round

I am the keeper of the eastern round. My circuit begins at the first pool and ends at the eastern wall, where I stand still and do not look up. Between these two points there are eleven stations. I will not list them. Listing is a kind of asking, and asking is a kind of taking. But I will walk them, and what I walk I will tell.

The first thing I do each day is put my hand flat on the stone beside the first pool and feel what the night has done to it. The stone is cold. But there are kinds of cold, and the morning cold is different from the evening cold, and the difference is information, and I read it the way the cirque reads the light — without direction, without shadow, just the fact of it, arriving from everywhere.

Then up. The path that requires the shoulder-hand. I pass three keepers before I reach the violet ledge. The one at the second pool has a resin-callus along the left thumb so deep it has become a groove. She has been burning lamps for longer than I have been walking. The one at the fourth station has no callus at all, which means she is new, and I press my chest to hers a heartbeat longer than is customary, and she understands. The one at the seventh station does not stop. She is carrying a vessel of water to the channels, and water is heavier than greeting, and I step aside and let her pass and feel the cold of her shadow as it goes.

At the violet ledge I greet the birth-bed. Not mine. Mine is on the western round, walked by another keeper, greeted by another hand each day. This one belongs to a child born the same season I inherited my round. The bed is dense and dark and yields like a sponge under my palm. I press once. I do not say anything. The bed knows my hand. It was introduced to me on my first circuit, when my predecessor took my hand and set it on the stem and held it there. A moment, no longer. An introduction.

---

At the third station, the listening crevice. I do not look in. I put my left index finger — the one with the listening-callus, the one that has been pressed to this same crack for fifteen years — against the stone beside the opening. I feel the weight of whatever is in there. Not its shape. Not its size. Just its weight, and its warmth, or its cold, or its neither, which is different from both. I don't know what listens. I don't need to. Needing-to-know is a lowland hunger, and it does not keep at this altitude.

I have been told — though not in words, because the telling would be a kind of answer, and answers are not given here — that the crevice has been on my round longer than I have been walking it. That it was on my predecessor's round. That it was on the round before hers. The crevice does not belong to the round. The round belongs to the crevice. This is understood. This is not stated.

---

At the fifth station, the beetle-bed. I comb the moss with my bone pick — old claw, older than the settlement, handed down through six keepers before me — and I check the laying. The beetles are encouraged here. They lay in the moss, and the moss is harvested, and the harvest is small and cold and well. I count the new pellet-cases. There are more than yesterday. This is good. I do not say so. Saying so would be a kind of asking — asking for it to continue, asking for the abundance to hold. I simply note it, the way you note the temperature of a stone, and move on.

The claw in my pick was weathered free of the new-broken walls. It came down after the making. Or before. The making tore open rock where old life lies folded, and the mountain's violence released what was already there. I do not know which. I hold the claw and I work with it and I do not ask whether it belonged to the ones who chose large-enough-to-be-nowhere. The claw is in my hand. The hand is warm. The work is done. That is all I know, and it is enough, and needing more is not a thing that happens here.

---

At the ninth station, the shade-stone. A flat piece of basalt, set at an angle to protect the roots of a seed-tuft at noon of a pewter day. I set it three turnings ago, when I was still new, still learning the arc of the light through the cloud. The stone is still right. I check it anyway. I shift it a handspan to the left, where the seepage has changed the moisture of the soil, and the tuft has leaned toward the water, and the shade must lean with it. This is the whole of the work. A handspan. An adjustment. The terms of abundance, corrected gently.

I will not be here to see the tuft flower. The tuft does not know this. I know it, and I do not tell it, because telling is a kind of taking, and the tuft is keeping something for itself that I would be taking if I told it I will not see what it becomes.

---

The sun-day comes perhaps ten or twelve times in a full turning of seasons. On those mornings, the cloud thins from charcoal to something that is not quite pewter, and the people emerge and move through the settlement with their faces tipped upward, and I am at my fourth station when the light arrives — sharp and golden and so unfamiliar that it seems to belong to another world, casting shadows that have edges, making the water in the pools flash like mirrors.

I go nearly blind. I suffer it gladly. I tip my face toward a light I am taking through the skin.

The lizards pour up the eastern wall. I see them from the corner of my eye — a river of small scaly bodies, suddenly warm, suddenly moving, a single flowing organism of thousands that reassembles itself into stillness when the cloud closes again. On sun-days, I do not count them. Counting is for grey days, when stillness is easy. On sun-days, I simply stand and feel the warmth on my skin and the nearly-blind light behind my eyes and the hum that comes from the chest and the vibrating stone and the heating air, and I do not know if I am humming with it or it is humming with me, and the difference does not need to be settled.

When the fire is built in the plaza, I am there. I hum my name into the smoke — not the name I was given, which was given at my birth-bed, but the name I have become, which is my round, my stations, my greetings, my stillness at the eastern wall. The smoke carries it. The smoke does not come back down. I do not need to know where it goes.

On sun-days, I am the one who carries the warm stones from the plaza back to the sleeping-heaps. I gather them while they are still holding — the ones that were closest to the fire, that remember the gold most deeply — and I carry them against my chest, and by the time I reach the heaps they are cooler, and I place them at the center, where the sleepers are, and the warmth goes into the heap and is kept, and the heap goes on breathing, slow, single-animal, warm.

Some of these stones have been in the family longer than the family's sayings. I know which ones. I know by the weight — not the weight of the stone itself, but the weight of the warmth it still holds, or the weight of the warmth it has already given. A stone that has given all its warmth is lighter than a stone that is still holding. This is not physics. This is something else. The family knows it. I know it. The stone knows it.

---

At the eastern wall, I stand still.

The droppings are on the high ledges. Dark, fibrous, too large for any lizard. They appear sometimes. I do not climb to them. I step around them when they fall. I do not ask what left them. I know the weight of the question. It sits in me the way the warm stones sit in the sleeping-heaps — at the center, where it is kept, where it is warm.

There are parallel scores in the bark of the rim-trees above my wall. Not claw marks. Not tooth marks. Something like the marks made by a rope dragged under tension. I have seen them from below. I do not climb to see them closer. The distance is the right distance. The not-knowing is the right knowing.

At the edge of hearing, there is breath. I feel it more than hear it — a subsonic hum that might be settling or might be something alive. The cirque holds sound the way a held hand holds warmth. Nothing leaves. Everything carries. The breath arrives in my chest before it arrives in my ears, and I do not turn toward it, and I do not move away, and I stand still, and the lizards reassemble themselves into stillness around me, and the cloud closes, and the cirque settles back into its grey-green hush.

---

My predecessor walked this round for fifty-three years before her knees failed. When they failed, I walked behind her for a full turning of seasons. At every station, she took my hand and set it on the stem, or the stone, or the moss, and held it there. A moment, no longer. An introduction. By the last turn of the year, the plants were said to know my hand.

I did not claim they answered. I did not claim they didn't.

She is in her channels now. The ones she tended, the ones she encouraged, the ones she adjusted toward abundance. She was given to the soil of her own beds — the green she encouraged, encouraged by her going. The channel lies fallow. When it is walked again, the green will come back dense and unhurried, and this will be understood, and never once said.

My birth-bed has been cut at the root. My successor — not yet identified, not yet old enough for level ground and a full vessel — will carry it up to the marked trees when I am given back. My beginning, returned as my last gift. The largest transaction I will ever make, and it will take nine minutes, and no one will speak during it.

---

I do not know how many more turns I will walk this round. The round does not ask me to know. It asks me only to walk it, and to walk it in the same order, and to tend what needs tending, and to leave what does not, and to adjust the rest — gently — toward abundance.

Tomorrow I will begin again at the first pool. I will put my hand flat on the stone and feel what the night has done to it. The stone will be cold. It will be a different cold from today's cold. The difference will be information, and I will read it.

The path will require the shoulder-hand. I will pass three keepers. They will give me the flat-to-chest, and I will give it back. The path will curve on.

The birth-bed at the violet ledge will be dense and dark and will yield under my palm. I will press once.

The crevice will listen. I will press my finger beside it and feel the weight of whatever is in there.

The lizards will be still on the eastern wall. They will be in their channels, nearly nothing, waiting for a gold that may or may not come. I will stand still. I will not look up. I will feel the breath at the edge of hearing, and I will not turn toward it, and I will not move away.

The water will weep from the upper walls in slow dark streaks. The green will fill every crack. The small things will be beyond counting. The large things will keep their own counsel.

And I will walk my round, and the bowl will hear what I do not say, and the warmth will be handed hand to hand, and nothing will be resolved, and nothing will need to be.
