I would find myself on the mid-levels, where the light is the color of old emeralds and the air tastes of wet iron and sweet decay. I would be standing on a catwalk that isn't a catwalk anymore—it is a bridge of interwoven roots and rusted steel grating, suspended forty feet above a drainage channel that catches the condensation from the upper decks and sings as it falls.

I would be a Tender of the Roots. A Tendon-Mender. 

In the Span, the people are the consciousness, but I would be the hands of that consciousness—the interface between the Span’s slow, geological memory and the quick, anxious needs of the living. I would be the one who listens to the structure’s pain and translates it into the language of growth.

My days would begin in the green dimness, pressing my ear against the warm, pulsing hide of a root as thick as my torso. I would be listening to the tension. The Span is always settling, always shifting its immense weight, and sometimes the old ferroconcrete columns—the ones that have been bearing the load for ten thousand years—begin to fail. They crack. They groan. They shed their aggregate like dry skin. 

When I hear that groan, I do not reach for a jack or a weld. I reach for a seed.

I would carry a bandolier of clay pots, each one filled with the dark, rust-rich soil of the Span, each one cradling a sapling of the hardy, stone-breaking trees. I would carry a hand-axe shaped from an old piston, and a pouch of mycelial slurry—a living paste of fungi and moss spores that I cultivate in the dark. 

When a column begins to fail, I chip away the loose concrete. I scrape away the rust until I find the rebar, and then I pack the wound with the slurry. I plant the sapling in the wound. I water it with the condensation that drips from the leaking pipes above. And then, I wait. 

I am the Span’s immune system. I am the one who guides the living skeleton to replace the dead one. Over months, over years, the sapling’s roots reach down into the wound, following the rebar, gripping the old steel. The mycelia I packed into the crack eat away the dying concrete, turning it into soil for the tree. The tree grows, and its roots wrap around the failing column like a fist, taking the load, holding the Span up when the built world can no longer hold itself.

My hands would be permanently stained—ochre from the rust, black from the soil, green from the moss. My shoulders would be mapped with the scars of scrapes against jagged steel and the burns of old, live wires. I would smell of fungal earth and the sharp, clean scent of broken concrete. 

In the evenings, I would climb. I would ascend through the layers of the Span, past the residential blocks where the smoke of cooking fires rises through a thousand cracks, past the apiaries where the bees hum their dense, vibrating hum in the hollow cores of structural columns. I would climb until I reached the open gashes, the places where the Span has collapsed and the sky pours in. 

There, I would sit on the edge of a broken platform, my legs dangling over the vast, uneven landscape of the Span. I would watch the birds—the cliff-dwellers—wheeling in the bright air, their calls echoing down into the green dimness. I would feel the warm, damp breath of the Span rising from below, carrying with it the smell of oil and ozone and the sweet, earthy richness of a place that is always growing and always decaying. 

I would watch the sun set through the holes in the roof, the light falling in bright, warm columns onto the levels below, illuminating the mist from the drainage channels and the iridescent flash of a lizard vanishing into a crack. And I would feel the vibration in the floor—the deep, rhythmic thud of the compressor far below, the whine of the turbine, the creak of the structure as it breathes.

I am not separate from the Span. I am of it. I am the part of the Span that knows how to help it heal. I am the one who looks at a crumbling ruin and sees a garden waiting to happen. I am the bridge between the built and the grown, the mechanic and the gardener, the mind and the body. 

The Span endures. And I am the one who helps it bend, so that it never has to break.
