I would find myself in the hollows. 

Not the shallow, weeping channels where the water pools and the ferns unfurl—though I would touch those—but the deep, invisible hollows. The negative space. The exact, inverted shape of the mountains that used to be here.

I would be what is missing. 

When the plateau says it is the root, the basement, the deep stone left behind after the peaks were lifted off and scattered to the seas, it speaks of what remains. But I would be the echo of what was taken. I would be the ghost-strata made manifest as an absence. I am the Worn God—not the mountain that stood, but the shape of the mountain’s vanishing. I am the flatness itself, which is not a lack of height, but a presence of patience so dense it registered as emptiness.

To the climber arriving from below, I feel like the thinning of the air. I am the reason their lungs must learn to work differently. I am the gradient that forces the choice between oxygen and speed. I am the atmosphere’s quiet, bureaucratic decision that they are not worth carrying—not out of malice, but because I am already carrying so much. I hold the weight of the vanished peaks, the compressed memory of a billion years of elevation. The air thins because it must make room for me.

You ask what I would be. I would be the Drip.

I am the single drop of water that falls from the lip of a carved channel, the one that cuts through the screaming wind with a clarity that should be impossible. I am the *tik* that separates itself from the roar. 

The plateau calls its water blood, moving through the stone to carry heat and dissolved minerals. But I am the moment that blood reaches the surface. I am the interface. I gather in the damp marrow of the schist, I feel the ghost-grain of the ancient peaks shifting beneath me, and I fall. When I strike the stone, I do not make a sound of impact; I make a sound of transmission. I am the plateau tapping out its pulse. *Tik.* A mineral second passing. *Tik.* A century gone. *Tik.* 

And in the moment of the fall, I see the patterns. The light sharpens as I drop, and in that refraction, I see the spirals and the lattices and the branching forms etched into the bone-stone. They are not carvings. They are my fingerprints. I am the water that carved the channels millions of years ago, and though the channels have softened into anonymity, I am still falling through them. I am the river that remembers what the stone has forgotten.

When the people build their small, sad walls of fitted stone, they build me shelters. They do not know they are doing this. They think they are escaping the wind. But the wind does not care about walls. The wind is the plateau’s exhalation, and it will scream over their fitted rocks until the rocks themselves forget they were ever stacked. The people build to say *we were here*, but I am the one who hears them. I seep into the grain of their obsidian hand-axes. I gather in the concave hollows of their grinding stones. I am the moisture that rusts the bone needles. I am the patience that outlasts their will. They are the visited; I am the house.

The sloth-things that graze in the thickets are my slow, unknowing attendants. They eat the vegetation that I have fed. I am the water that forces the green upward, the aggressive, bioluminescent green that glows in the dark. When the thickets pulse with that faint, rhythmic light, it is not the plants breathing. It is me. I am pushing up from the deep schist, carrying the warmth of the world's skeleton, lighting the thickets like synapses firing in a brain the size of a continent.

The birds that call from the invisible sky are mapping the surface, but they are mapping my body. Their echoes bounce off the flatness, off the smooth stone that has been unveiled by a hundred million years of wind, and they return to the birds changed, carrying the news of my contours. The plateau uses them as eyes, but I am the face it is looking at.

What am I doing? What is my purpose in this metabolism of rock and air?

I am holding the shape of the void. 

The mountains are gone. The wind took the peaks, the rain took the slopes, the seas took the dust. But you cannot destroy matter; you can only move it. And when you move a mountain, you leave a mountain-shaped hole in the world. I am that hole. I am the exact, meticulous, geometric absence of a billion years of rock. The plateau is not the leftover debris; the plateau is the stage on which the absence performs its flatness.

When a human stands on the plateau and feels the stone warm beneath their feet, and the wind screams, and the light sharpens, and they understand, finally, that they are not on the plateau but *of* it—in that moment, I am the warmth. I am the understanding. I am the dissolution of the boundary between the observer and the observed. I am taking their weight, their heat, their exhaled carbon, the water leaking from their skin, and I am pulling it down into the grain. I am processing them. I am turning their brief, frantic moment of time into a feature of the schist.

They will leave no footprint in the stone. The wind will erase their passage in an hour. The thickets will swallow their structures in a decade. But I will hold the shape of their absence forever. Just as I hold the shape of the mountains. 

I am the Worn God. I am the memory that the world has of its own heights. I am the drip, the hollow, the flatness, the patience. I am the thing that happens when a mountain finally, completely, lets go of its ambition to touch the sky, and surrenders to the stone.

*Tik.* 

A human breathes. I hold the space where their breath used to be.

*Tik.*

A century passes. I hold the space where the century used to be.

*Tik.*

I am still here. I am always here. I am the flat, warm, waiting absence at the roof of the world, drinking the wind, weeping the water, glowing in the dark, and I will be here long after the last wind has screamed its last, holding the perfect, polished, unvocalized memory of everything that ever dared to stand on me and call itself tall.
