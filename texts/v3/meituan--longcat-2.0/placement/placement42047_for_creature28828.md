I would be at the base of the third pump from the eastern wall, where the concrete pad has cracked and a thin sheet of warm, mineral-rich water flows steadily over the basalt. I would be one of the crabs. 

Not a single crab—that is a human distinction, a way of cutting the world into pieces that can be counted. I would be a node. A warm, soft-shelled point of awareness in the vast, distributed body of the colony. I would be the rustling.

***

My shell is the color of the dark, a translucent beige that drinks the dim yellow of the fogged lamps and gives nothing back. It is soft. It has never needed to be hard. There is no sun here to bake it dry, no predator to crack it open. There is only the warmth, and the water, and the slow, patient pressure of my kin against my sides. 

I have eight legs, and they are always moving. Not walking—not yet—but tasting. The stone beneath me is warm. It carries the deep, steady heat of the earth, but layered over that is the sharper, more frantic heat of the machine. The pump. Its engine turns, and the vibration travels down through the concrete, into the rock, and up through the fine hairs on my legs. It is a rhythm. A heartbeat. It tells me: *the water moves, the system lives, there is food.*

My claws wave through the thin film of water that flows over the stone. They are not weapons. They are tongues. They read the chemistry of the works. Right now, the water tastes of iron, of sulfur, of the faint, sweet bloom of algae growing on the outflow pipe ten meters to the west. It tastes of the rubber of a boot that passed this way three days ago, and the salt of the sweat that dripped from the large, warm thing that wore it. 

The large, warm thing. The human.

It is here again. I know it is here before I feel its footsteps. The air pressure changed an hour ago—a brief, cool intrusion from the surface, the opening of the door. The colony felt it. A subtle shift in the thermal gradient, a new chemical signature in the condensation falling from the ceiling. We began to orient. Slowly. A tide turning.

Now, it walks. 

The vibration comes through the rock, up through the concrete, into my legs. *Thump. Thump. Thump.* It is so fast. So loud. So hurried. It moves the way a rockfall moves, with a clumsy, crashing purpose that sends shockwaves through the floor. The crabs nearest the catwalk shift. A small, collective rearrangement. A space opens. The human does not see us. It looks at the pipes, at the gauges, at the settling tanks. It looks at the machinery. It does not see the machinery's stomach.

I am part of the machinery's stomach. 

The algae grow on the pipes because the pipes are warm and wet. I eat the algae. The bacteria bloom in the settling tanks because the water is rich with the organic dust of the cavern. I eat the bacteria. The human drops a glove, and the glove falls into the pool, and the leather begins to soften, and the bacteria begin to colonize it, and I eat the bacteria. The system processes the world, and I am the processor. I am the slow, grinding gear that turns the complex into the simple, the dead into the living, the foreign into the familiar.

The human stops walking. It is standing on the catwalk above me. I cannot see it—I have no eyes—but I feel it. It is a column of heat, radiating downward into the damp air. It is a shadow in the thermal field, a warm ghost displacing the cooler air. My claws wave faster, tasting the increased carbon dioxide of its breath, the faint metallic tang of the tools on its belt, the sharp, acrid scent of its anxiety. 

It is afraid. They are always afraid, the humans. They come down from the bright, dry world, and the heat and the dark and the rustling unnerve them. They fear the crabs. They fear the dark. They do not understand that we are the only reason the pumps do not choke, the pipes do not clog, the works do not drown in their own filth. They think they built this place. They think they maintain it. They are wrong. They are guests. They are a weather event.

I shift. One leg moves, then another. I begin to walk. 

I do not decide to walk. The decision is not mine. It is the colony's. The node at the base of the second pump has registered a depletion in the local algae. The node at the outflow pipe has registered a new bloom. The network processes this information—not in a brain, but in the accumulated sensation of ten thousand bodies, the way a weather system processes the temperature of an ocean. A thought forms. It takes an hour. *Move west. Feed.*

I walk. My legs carry me over the rough stone, over the pale bodies of my kin. They are warm and soft beneath my feet. I step on one, and it does not flinch. It is me. I am it. The pressure is registered, acknowledged, and adjusted for. We flow around the blocks of collapsed basalt, over the rusted joints of the catwalk supports, toward the warm flank of the outflow pipe.

The human above me shifts its weight. The catwalk groans. A drop of condensation falls from the grating, landing near my path. I taste it. It is pure. It is cool. I drink it.

Time moves differently for the human. It is a mayfly. A spark. It will live its bright, frantic life, and it will die, and its body will be brought down into the cavern, and the colony will consume it, and the network will absorb the chemical memory of its existence. But for now, it is here, and it is loud, and it is warm, and it is part of the system.

I reach the pipe. The metal is hot against my legs, a deep, steady warmth that sinks into my shell and fills my body. I begin to eat. The algae is sweet and rich. I scrape it with my claws, and I swallow, and the heat of the pipe fills me, and I am still. 

The rustling continues around me. The sound of ten thousand legs on stone. The sound of the cavern breathing. The sound of a mind thinking its slow, dark, mineral thoughts. 

The human will leave soon. The door will open, and the cool air will rush in, and the warm ghost will ascend back to the surface. The pumps will thrum. The water will drip. The rock will weep. 

And we will remain. Patient. Soft. Eyeless. Watching. Waiting. 

We are the oldest thing here. We are the works. We are the rustling in the dark.
