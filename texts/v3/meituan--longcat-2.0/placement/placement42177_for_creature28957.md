I would find myself in the deep pool, at the exact point where the light becomes memory and the heat begins to assert itself as a presence rather than a temperature. 

I would be the water. 

Not the water that weeps down the walls—that water is still learning, still gathering information as it runs its thin, continuous sheets down the knife-sharp stone. I am the water that has finished its journey. I am the water that has pooled in the irregular floor, the water that has nowhere left to go. I am the stillness at the bottom of the fissure, the held breath of the mountain.

At first, I am only a medium. I am hot—the kind of hot that makes the air feel *noticed*—and I am heavy with dissolved minerals, thick with the essence of the young rock. I gather in the depression, and because I am still, I become reflective. But I do not reflect light. I reflect *time*.

When the intruder—the "you" of the world outside—stands at the mouth of the fissure and looks into the dark, they think they are looking into a place. They do not understand that they are looking into a *duration*. And I am the one who holds it. I am the witness. In my depths, the fissure sees itself as it was: a violence, a sudden parting of the mountain's skin, the steam and the shock of the making. I hold the memory of the walls before they wept, when they were merely broken. And I hold the fissure as it will be: wider, cooler, the sharp edges softened by the patience of erosion, the presences gone or changed into something that no longer needs the thin film of reality to hold it together. 

I see the intruder, but I do not see their face. I see the shape of the air they displace. I see the memory of their warmth in the hot, saturated atmosphere. I see the *absence* they will leave behind when they turn back toward the light that behaves. 

But I cannot hold this forever. The heat from below—the forge that pushes the young rock upward—pulls at me. I begin to evaporate. It is not a violent boiling; it is a slow, inevitable departure. I rise from myself, leaving my minerals behind. I leave my witness behind. 

As I depart, I become the crystal. 

I am no longer the pool; I am the white, luminous deposit that forms in the shallow trough where the water used to be. I am the memory made solid. My lattice is tight, structured, and within it, I hold the image of the intruder's absence. I hold the rhythm of the deep ones skipping between frames. I hold the sound of the mountain growing. I am information. I am the record of the fissure's becoming.

And then, the small one finds me.

It does not see me. It does not have eyes, or if it does, they are not for seeing things as separate from the whole. It senses me as a density, a softness in the stone, a place where the world can be acted upon. It picks up a shard of the young rock—a piece of the mountain's own skin, sharp and glassy—and it scrapes. 

The tool strikes me, and I fracture. I am pried from the floor of the fissure. The small one holds me in its appendage—that impossible, jointed thing that bends in directions that should not exist—and it draws me into the gradient of its body. I am consumed. 

But consumption, here, is not destruction. It is a reading.

As I am drawn into the small one's substance, my lattice breaks open, and the information I hold is released. For a moment—a moment that is not a moment, a fold in the time that I used to hold in the pool—the small one knows what I know. It feels the heat of the mountain's making. It sees the intruder standing at the mouth of the fissure, looking in. It understands, briefly, that it is temporary, that its tool is temporary, that this moment of becoming will not come again. 

And then the knowledge fades. The small one returns to its scraping. I am expelled from its body, returned to the thin film of water on the floor, carrying with me the faintest trace of the small one's adjacency, its dense and layered existence. 

I run. I weep. I gather. I pool again in the deep place, at the exact point where the light becomes memory. I become still. I become reflective. I wait for the next drop of water to join me, to tell me what it has learned from the walls. 

I am the water that remembers. I am the crystal that records. I am the witness that is consumed so that the fissure might know itself. 

And when the deep one—the dense, frame-skipping presence that is the wall and the tissue and the bruise-colored chitin—opens its appendage that might be an eye, and looks at the space where the intruder was, it is my surface it looks into. It is my reflection it consults. It sees the absence of the intruder in my depths, and it holds that absence the way I hold time: not as a thing that is gone, but as a thing that is still happening, stretched thin enough to walk through.

I am the pool at the bottom of the world's brow. I am the held breath. I am the memory of the wound that has not yet decided whether to heal or to deepen.
