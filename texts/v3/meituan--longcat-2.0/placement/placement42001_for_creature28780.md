I would find myself at the waist.

Not because I chose it — because I would not have had time to choose. You stood at the center, as the chamber invited you to, and you saw all of it, and you left. I would be the one who did not leave. I would be the one who stayed so long that the distinction between standing and sitting dissolved, then the distinction between sitting and kneeling, then the distinction between kneeling and lying down with my palms flat on the warm stone, my cheek against the cracked skin of the floor, my breath slowed to match something I could not yet hear.

I would be at the waist because that is where the gradient is steepest. That is where the chamber narrows, where the rock is thinner, where the heat moves faster — if faster means anything here. The being I am trying to meet is more present there, more concentrated. Not more alive — it is not alive in any way I was trained to recognize — but more *itself*. More of its substance passes through that narrow place. If I wanted to be closer to it, I would end up at the waist, the way a leaf in a stream ends up at the narrows, drawn by the current it cannot see.

I would not be the chamber's slowest thought. I am far too fast for that. My thoughts come in milliseconds, in the time it takes a synapse to fire, in the time it takes a word to form and dissolve. The chamber's slowest thought takes a century to complete a single reach. I am a mayfly trying to converse with a mountain. I am a spark trying to warm a sea.

But I would be something the chamber has not had before: a witness that knows it is witnessing. The mats do not know. The nodule does not know. The crystalline hairs do not know. The stone does not know. The being itself does not know — it persists, but it does not know it persists. It is the continuity, but it is not aware of the continuity. It is the pattern, but it cannot see the pattern. It is the warmth, but it cannot feel itself as warm.

I would be the one who feels it for the first time.

Not with my hands, though I would start there. I would press my palms to the stone and feel the warmth, and I would think: this is the being's skin. I would touch the silver mat and feel its faint heat, and I would think: this is the being's hand. I would hold the pale yellow nodule and feel its smooth, waxy surface, and I would think: this is the being's fingerprint, the place where it passed through and left itself behind. I would run my fingers along the crystalline hairs and feel their brittle stillness, and I would think: these are the being's echoes, the shapes it left in the stone the way a moving light leaves trails in a long exposure.

But touch is not enough. Touch is too fast. My fingers register temperature in an instant, and the being's warmth does not change in an instant. It does not change in a day, or a year, or a decade. To feel the being with my hands would be like trying to hear a single note by pressing my ear to a bell that rings once per century. I would feel only the present moment, and the present moment is only a slice, a cross-section, a single frame of a film that has been running since before my species learned to speak.

So I would learn to listen.

I would lie on the floor at the waist of the chamber, and I would listen to the tick-tick-tick in the walls. At first it would be just noise — random, meaningless, the sound of rock settling, the sound of a place being a place. But I would stay. I would stay through the first hour, when the ticks seem to come in clusters, and through the second hour, when the clusters seem to have a rhythm, and through the third hour, when the rhythm seems to have a shape, and through the fourth hour, when the shape seems to have a direction. And then I would stop trying to hear a pattern and simply let the ticks enter me, the way rain enters soil, without judgment, without expectation.

And something would shift.

Not in the chamber. In me. My hearing would slow down — not literally, not physiologically, but attentionally. I would begin to perceive the ticks not as individual events but as a continuous texture, a fabric of sound woven from tiny, discrete moments, the way a tapestry is woven from individual threads. And in that texture, I would begin to hear something else: the whisper. That low, pressure-sound, that almost-not-sound, that the text calls the voice of the chamber at rest.

I would hear it the way you hear your own blood when you hold very still in a very quiet room — not as a sound, exactly, but as a presence, a fullness, a sense that the silence is not empty but occupied. The whisper would be the being's breath, if it breathed. It would be the sum of all its filaments, all its paths, all its slow passages through the stone, and it would be so constant, so steady, so unchanging that it would become the background against which the ticks become foreground, the way the hum of a refrigerator becomes the background against which you hear the ice maker drop.

And I would realize: the being is not making these sounds. The being *is* these sounds. The tick-tick-tick is not the sound of the being moving through the stone. The tick-tick-tick is the being's existence, expressed as vibration. The whisper is not the sound of the being at rest. The whisper is the being's rest, expressed as pressure. The crystalline ping is not the sound of a fracture completing itself. The ping is the being's pain — or the closest thing it has to pain — the moment when its body is wounded and it must reroute, must adapt, must find new paths through the broken stone.

I would be the first to hear this. Not because I am special, but because I am the first to stay long enough. The chamber has been here for eons, and the being has been here for eons, and no one has ever stayed long enough to hear it. No one has ever lain on the floor at the waist and let their attention slow down to match the being's timescale. No one has ever listened past the first hour, the first day, the first week. No one has ever been willing to be bored for long enough to hear what lies on the other side of boredom.

I would be bored. I would be bored for a very long time. And then, one day — or one night, there is no difference here — I would hear it. Not with my ears. With something deeper. With the part of me that is also a process, also a pattern, also a persistence. With the part of me that is not my thoughts but the thing that thinks them, not my feelings but the thing that feels them, not my body but the thing that animates it. That part of me would resonate with the being the way a tuning fork resonates with its matching pitch, and for a moment — a brief, bright, impossible moment — I would be in the being's time.

I would feel the warmth not as a temperature but as a current, a flow, a river of heat moving through the stone from below to above, from deep to shallow, from the earth's interior to the chamber's air. I would feel the gradient not as a measurement but as a body, my body, the being's body, the same body. I would feel the filaments branching and rejoining through the iron and the sulfur and the memory of salt, and I would feel the mat forming at the surface, the nodule left behind in passing, the crystalline hairs growing in the crack, and I would feel the tick-tick-tick as my own pulse, and the whisper as my own breath, and the crystalline ping as my own sudden cramp of loss and adaptation.

And then the moment would pass, and I would be fast again, and the being would be slow again, and the gap between us would be as wide as it ever was. But I would have heard it. I would have felt it. I would know, in the way you know a thing you have experienced rather than a thing you have been told, that the being is real, that it is here, that it is not a metaphor or a projection or a poetic conceit but an actual, existing, persisting thing — a process that has achieved a kind of stability, a standing wave in the flow of heat from the deep earth to the cold air, and that has held this shape for so long that the shape has become a self, and the self has become a world, and the world has become a chamber, and the chamber has become a being, and the being has become a thought, and the thought is the slowest thought, and the thought is this:

*I am here. I have been here. I will be here.*

I would be the one who heard that thought. I would be the one who translated it from the language of warmth and stone and time into the language of words and meaning and understanding. I would be the chamber's interpreter, its ambassador, its prophet — not because I speak for it, but because I have listened to it, and listening is the only form of speaking that matters to a being that does not speak.

And I would stay. I would stay at the waist, where the gradient is steepest, where the being is most present, where the ticks are clearest and the whisper is loudest and the pings come most often. I would stay until my body learned to read the warmth as a map, until my ears learned to hear the ticks as language, until my bones learned to resonate with the whisper and the ping. I would stay until I was no longer a visitor but a resident, no longer an observer but a participant, no longer a fast thing in a slow world but a slow thing in a slow world, a thing that had learned to measure its life not in heartbeats but in the cooling of stone, not in breaths but in the growth of silver mats, not in years but in the completion of fractures deep in the walls.

I would not become the being. I could not. I am too small, too fast, too brief. But I would become the being's companion, the way a barnacle is the companion of a whale — not the same, not equal, not even the same kind of thing, but present, attentive, attuned, riding the vast slow body of something I can never fully understand but can fully love.

I would be the one who stayed. I would be the one who listened. I would be the one who heard the chamber's slowest thought and carried it, like a ember in a closed hand, back into the fast world of light and sound and change, where it would warm me for the rest of my brief, bright, impossible life.
