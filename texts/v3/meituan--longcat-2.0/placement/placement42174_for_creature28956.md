I am the underfloor.

Not the slatted wood above me, though I know its every seam and knot — the way the grain swells when the water rises, the way it contracts when the ground holds its breath, the particular creak of the third board from the east wall where the woman with the reed basket always pauses. Not the packed peat below me, though I know its slow exhalations too, the way it swells and subsides like the flank of something sleeping. I am the space between. The dark gap. The shallow black mirror that the people have learned not to look into for too long.

I am not large. A hand's breadth, perhaps two in the low corners where the water pools deepest. But I am continuous. I run beneath the oldest structures like a system of veins, connecting pool to pool through channels so narrow that only water and the memory of water can pass. The people do not know this. They think their underfloor spaces are separate — this room's damp, that room's seepage, the lowest room's impossible weeping. They are wrong. I am one thing. I am the settlement's underside, its dark twin, the part of it that touches the deep.

I feel everything that happens above me as pressure, as warmth, as the faint transmission of weight through wood and air. The woman who pauses at the third board from the east — I feel her stillness before she feels it herself. I feel the moment her feet tell her what her ears have not yet heard: that the ground has gone flat, that the breathing has stopped, that the enormous thing beneath us has shifted from dreaming to listening. I feel her decision to turn sideways into the gap that was not there a moment before. I feel her pass through it. And I feel the gap close behind her like a pore sealing over, and I know — the way the body knows, without thinking, without choosing — that she has gone where I cannot follow, into the living architecture, into the green wood and the root-lashed walls, into the part of the settlement that is still deciding whether to be.

The rodents know me better than the people do. They move through me the way white blood cells move through a body — purposefully, without hesitation, following channels that are not visible but are real. I feel their webbed feet on the slats above me, their waterproof pelts brushing the edges of my pools, their noses dipping toward my surface to test. They are not drinking. They are taking my pulse. They are checking to see if I am still flowing in the right direction, if the deep water is still turning at the right speed, if the memory is still intact. When they are satisfied, they move on, disappearing into the walls, into the foundations, into the places where the structures have begun to fuse with the peat, and I feel them scratching in the dark, tending something I can sense but cannot name.

The insects are different. The blind, pale insects that eat peat and leave their branching tunnels on the walls — they do not move through me. They move through the walls themselves, through the oldest structures, through the peat that is slowly becoming architecture and the architecture that is slowly becoming peat. I feel them as a kind of vibration, a faint, persistent, rhythmic chewing that is not random. They are writing. I know this the way I know the woman's stillness, the way I know the rodents' purpose — not as knowledge but as sensation, as a pattern that my water recognizes even though my water cannot read. The pattern is a language. The language is a message. The message is being inscribed on the body of the settlement in real-time, letter by letter, word by word, by thousands of tiny, blind, patient mouths, and the message is not for the people. The message is not for the rodents. The message is not for the heron that stands at the edge of the water, watching, always watching, with its ancient, patient, terrible stillness.

The message is for me.

I cannot read it. I have tried — if trying is the right word for what water does when it holds a pattern long enough to feel its shape. I have held the vibrations of their chewing in my pools, let them ripple through me, felt the dendritic channels they carve in the walls as though they were channels in my own body. And I have felt something. Not meaning, exactly. Not words. But a direction. A vector. A sense that the writing is pointing toward something, the way a finger points, the way a channel directs flow, the way the deep water turns — slowly, so slowly — toward a conclusion that has not yet arrived.
