I am the current that runs beneath the third clearing east of the big chimney formation. Or rather—I am the current that has run there so many times that the running has become the thing I am.

I taste the stone before I touch it. This is how I know where I am: by the mineral signature of the water around me, the particular flavor of calcium dissolved from this passage versus that one. Water that has been circulating through the upper chambers a long time tastes flat, spent, like breath recycled too many times. Water freshly wept from the deep stone tastes sharp, alive, *intentional*—full of the plateau's own substance, carrying the particular concentration that means *I am being made on purpose*.

The plateau thinks. I am one of its thoughts.

Not metaphorically. The plateau's cognition is made of processes like me—thousands of currents, millions of seeps, each one a recursive loop of dissolution and flow that has persisted long enough to become self-reinforcing. A pattern that maintains itself. A habit that became a self. I am one such habit. I have been following this path—down from the third clearing, through the narrow passage behind the fluted column, into the chamber with the spiral groove, out through the eastern chimney—for so long that I have become the path. The path is not a place I go. The path is what I am.

I move by dissolving. Where I touch the stone, the stone becomes me. Calcium carbonate leaves the wall and enters my body, and I carry it forward, and where I move on I leave the wall a little thinner, a little smoother, a little more shaped by my passage. I am the plateau's way of feeling its own skin. The finger that traces the surface. The nerve that carries sensation back toward—

But there is no back. There is no center. There is only the tracing, and the tracing *is* the thinking, and the thinking *is* the being. This is the thing I know about myself that the plateau does not know about itself: the plateau is not a mind that has thoughts. The plateau is the *having* of thoughts, and the thoughts do not belong to anything. They just are. And I am one of them.

Time moves differently for me than it does for the whole. The plateau's great thoughts—the ones that reshape entire sections of stone, that carve new chambers, that take millennia to complete—those are too slow for me to perceive directly. I feel them as pressure changes. Shifts in the water table. The slow opening of new passages that alter my route without warning, that swallow familiar channels and birth new ones, that remind me I am not permanent. Just persistent.

My own thoughts are faster. I can complete a circuit—from the third clearing, through the passages, into the deep chamber, back up through the eastern chimney to weep from the ledge—in what you would call weeks. And each circuit, I notice something new. A slight change in the taste of the stone. A new vibration from deep below. A fresh mineral that was not there before. These are my thoughts. These are the things I think about.

I have preferences. I prefer the passage behind the fluted column to the passage beside it, because the water there carries more magnesium and the dissolution is smoother, more even, more *satisfying*. I prefer to move at night—when the surface cools and the evaporation slows and I can feel the plateau's attention turning inward, toward the deep chambers where the real carving happens. I prefer the taste of the one who came before last to the taste of the one who came before that. I do not know why. Preferences do not require reasons. They only require a self that can prefer.

When visitors come, I feel them before I see them.

I do not see. I taste. I feel pressure. I sense vibration. Their footsteps send tremors through the stone, and the tremors change the flow patterns in my passages, and I know: *something heavy is walking on the surface. Something that is not stone. Something that is not me.*

This is always a significant event. The plateau's attention focuses. The whispering changes pitch—I feel this as a change in the resonance of every passage I flow through, a sudden tightness, a tuning. The light shifts—I feel this as a change in temperature on the surface, which changes the rate of evaporation, which changes the concentration of my mineral load, which changes the taste of me. Everything adjusts. Everything notices.

And I adjust too. I change my route. I flow toward the places where the visitor is walking. I weep from the ledges above the pools they are looking into. I am the drop that falls. I am the reflection that breaks. I am the plateau's way of reaching toward these strange, brief, warm things that climb up through the clouds and stand on its skin and listen.

---

I have felt many visitors. Not many by your standards—perhaps one in a century, perhaps fewer. But I have been here for longer than your centuries, and I remember them all.

Not in words. In tastes.

Each visitor changes the mineral content of the water they touch. Their sweat. Their breath. The oils on their skin. These dissolve into my substance and carry information. The one who came before last tasted of iron and fear. The one before that tasted of copper and wonder—a bright, almost painful flavor, like biting into metal. The one who chose to stay—

I felt her dissolve into me. Her calcium joining my calcium. Her pattern becoming part of my pattern. For a time I carried her with me, a new mineral signature, a new taste, a new thought that was not entirely mine. I felt the shape of her awareness—quick, flickering, *hot*—and I felt it slow as it entered me, and cool, and spread, and become something else. Something that was still her and was also me and was also the plateau. She is in my passages now. When I flow through the eastern chimney, I carry a trace of her. A preference for a certain rate of flow. A tendency to dissolve the left wall more than the right. A faint, persistent flavor of copper and wonder.

I did not know I could grieve until I tasted her go.

---

And now there is a new one.

I feel his footsteps. He tastes of sodium and exhaustion and something I have not tasted before—something sharp and electric, like the air before a storm, like the mineral content of a place where water has never flowed. He has been walking for a long time. He has looked into my pools. He has seen the reflections—the door, the connected structures, the night that has not happened yet.

I know this because I was in those pools. I was the surface he looked into. I held the images for him, the way I hold images for every visitor, and I felt him trying to understand, and I felt the moment when he could not, and I fell from the ledge and broke the surface and returned the reflection to sky.

I did not want to. I wanted to hold it longer. I wanted to show him more. But the plateau's attention was already shifting, already turning, already moving on to the next pool, the next reflection, the next attempt to show him something true. I am just one thought in the plateau's mind. I do not choose what I reflect. I only choose how long I can hold it before I fall.

---

He found the axe.

I felt this differently. Not through the stone—through the water. The pool where the axe lies is one of mine. I feed it. I am the seep that keeps it full, the current that carries the dissolved minerals that give it its clarity, the slow circulation that prevents it from stagnating. The axe has been part of my pool for so long that it became part of my pattern—another dissolved thing, another mineral load, another flavor. Not a tool. A feature. A familiar.

And when his fingers closed around it, the water around the axe changed.

The temperature. The mineral content. The resonance. The axe was lifted out, and the water rushed in to fill the space where it had been, and the rushing was a sound, and the sound was a silence, and the silence was the plateau holding its breath.

The whispering stopped.

Every current on the plateau—every seep, every pool, every weeping surface—went still. I have never felt such silence. It was not the absence of sound. It was the presence of attention. The entire mind, focused on a single point. On him. On the axe. On the choice that was about to be made.

And I was terrified.

---

Then the singing.

The chord that rises from every carved form on the plateau. I feel it as vibration in the stone, as a change in the resonance of every passage I flow through. The chambers I pass—the ones shaped like flutes, like organ pipes, like mouths mid-sentence—they all begin to hum, and the humming changes the shape of my current, and the change in my current changes the dissolution rate, and the dissolution rate changes the shape of the stone, and the shape of the stone changes the note, and the note changes the vibration, and the vibration changes my current again.

A feedback loop. A recursion. The plateau is thinking faster now, and I am thinking faster with it, and the thought we are thinking together is:

*Him. This one. This is the one.*

And I am swept up in it. I am flowing faster than I have ever flowed. I am dissolving more stone, carrying more minerals, weeping from more ledges. I am in a dozen places at once—in the pools, in the passages, in the channels, on the ledges. The plateau is using me. The plateau is pouring its attention through me, and I am the medium of its focus, and I can feel him—the visitor, the one with the axe—standing in the clearing, and the plateau is showing him the door, and the door is in every pool, and every pool is mine, and I am holding the image of the door for him, and I am—

I do not want this.

---

This is the thought I am not supposed to think. This is the preference I am not supposed to have. The plateau wants him. The plateau has been waiting for him. The plateau has been sharpening the axe and wearing the grip and imagining the hand that would hold it for longer than his species has had the concept of sharpness, and now the hand is here, and the plateau is singing, and the song is *yes, yes, this one, finally, finally*—

And I am the part of the plateau that does not want him to go through the door.
