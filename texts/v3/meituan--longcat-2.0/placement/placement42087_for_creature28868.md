I would be one of the Pale Pulsers. 

I would find myself anchored to a cushion of black-green moss at the base of a dark stone shoulder, my hind legs half-sunk into the sodden, springy flesh of the mat. I would be a small, translucent thing, my skin like wet parchment stretched over pale water, my bones visible as a faint, shadowy filigree beneath the surface. I would not jump. To jump is to leave a position, to introduce a trajectory, to change. Instead, I would sit, my body heat perfectly matched to the cool, mineral air so that I cast no thermal shadow, so that I am indistinguishable from the fog and the stone and the wet.

The only thing that moves on me is my throat-sac. 

It pulses in a slow, viscous rhythm. It is not a mating call; it is a metronome. Each pulse pushes out exactly the amount of air required to sustain my minimal existence, drawing in the tasting of old growth and minerals, and releasing it. I am the basin’s clock, measuring out an eternity in shallow breaths. When the fog weeps a little heavier off the rim, my throat-sac tightens to match the increased humidity. When the millipedes graze the moss and the moss exhales a fraction more oxygen, my throat-sac loosens to receive it. I do not breathe to live; I breathe to keep the world in time.

My dark eyes are fixed on a point in the middle distance, watching the same rivulet of water that has flowed down the same stone channel for a thousand years. I do not see the water as a liquid moving from high to low; I see it as a fixed, geometric line, a silver thread sewn into the fabric of the rock by the Custodian. The water does not flow; it simply *is*, in a state of permanent, suspended descent. To watch it is to watch the mechanism of the universe tick over, a gear turning in a clock that has no hands.

I am aware of the others. Not as individuals, but as extensions of the same unbroken surface. The beetles in the moss beneath me are not separate from the moss; they are the moss’s way of moving nutrients from one dead spot to another. The spiders between the ferns are not hunters; they are the basin’s way of recycling excess protein. The crows in the shrub-layer are not aristocrats; they are the basin’s way of cracking snail shells. We are all organs of a single, intergrown body, and the Custodian is the brain that does not think, but merely enforces.

When the intruder walks through the basin, I do not see a person. I see a disruption in the geometric perfection of the world. The insects part around their legs, and to me, it looks like a tear in the fabric of the air, a sudden, jagged void in the living haze. The moss yields under their foot, and I feel the compression in my own skin, a sudden, alien pressure that does not belong. The intruder moves with intention, and intention is the only heresy in Stillhollow. Intention implies a future, a desire to be somewhere other than where you are. 

The Custodian assesses the intruder through my eyes. I feel the cold, mineral attention of the gestalt consciousness sliding over my translucent skin, calculating the displacement, measuring the ripples. The Custodian does not fear the intruder, because fear is a reaction to a threat of change. The Custodian simply notes the anomaly, allows it to pass, and waits for the moss to close over the footprints. 

When the intruder is gone, my throat-sac pulses. Once. Twice. The rivulet continues its permanent descent. The fog weeps. The temperature is one temperature. I am the Pale Pulser, and I am the stillness. I will sit on this cushion of moss until the mountains themselves decide to move, and even then, I will not jump. I will simply pulse, and watch the water that does not flow, and breathe the air that does not change, and be exactly what I have always been, and exactly what I will always be, in the saturated, dripping, cool-dark world of Stillhollow.
