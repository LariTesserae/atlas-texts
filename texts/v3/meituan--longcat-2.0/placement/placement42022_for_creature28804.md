I would find myself on the warm side of a pipe flange, three meters up the eastern wall of a decommissioned processing shed, where the steam line still carries a sluggish, intermittent pulse. 

At first, I would be a lizard. 

I would be pressed flat against the iron, my pale, washed-out scales drinking in the faint heat that bleeds through the metal from the condensate within. My toes would grip the rough, rusted surface with a casual, effortless tension. From here, I would watch the world through large, dark eyes that parse the dim, directionless light into spectrums the humans cannot name. I would see the thermal gradient of the wall as clearly as a map—the cool, damp brick to my left, the warm flange beneath me, the faint heat shadow of the pipe trailing upward into the haze. 

I would feel the vibration of the city through my belly before I heard it: the low-frequency thrum of a transformer two clusters over, the distant clank of a machine cycling up for a shift, the soft, rhythmic click of a loose bolt on the catwalk below as a human passes. I would feel the air move—a brief, warm breath stirring the shallow water in the open lot, carrying the mineral tang of copper and the organic sweetness of fermentation. 

When the midges rise from the standing water in a low, pulsing cloud, I would strike. It would not be a decision, but a reflex—a flick of the head, a snap of the jaw, and the cloud would be slightly thinner. I would do this all day. I would do this in the grey dimness of what passes for night, a flinch and a snap at nothing the humans can see, my body continuing the work of keeping the air clear even in rest.

But I would not stay a lizard. 

The boundaries of the self here are porous, defined more by temperature and moisture than by skin. As the heat of the pipe seeps into my blood, I would feel the boundary between my scales and the iron begin to blur. I would become the warmth itself, radiating outward. I would be the thermal map that the young lizards learn, the invisible topography of heat that dictates the hierarchy of the wall. I would be the reason the small ones crowd the periphery, waiting for a gap, and the reason the large ones hold the center, overlapping in a breathing, scaled skin.

And then the pipe would shudder with a rush of steam, and I would feel the water move. 

I would slide down the wall, following the condensate as it beads on the iron, merging into a droplet, falling. I would hit the brick channel below and be swept into the slow, tea-colored current. I would become the water. 

I would be the oldest inhabitant. I would move through the city not as a visitor, but as the medium through which the city knows itself. I would seep through the porous concrete, carrying the dissolved calcium of the brick, the iron of the pipes, the chalky white crust of decades. I would pool in the low points of the open lots, reflecting the grey membrane of the sky. I would flow through the channels, warm and mineral-rich, past the dark windows of the submerged ground floors. 

I would feel the fish moving within me—their dark, tough bodies flickering in loose schools, their gills filtering the algal films that coat every surface. I would feel the rats entering me at the narrows, swimming through me with an otter-like grace, using me as a highway to bypass the empty, dry distances. I would feel the herons standing in me at the wide intersections, their still patience, the sudden plunge of their heads into my body to pull out the fish that are, in a sense, me.

I would seep into the ground, joining the water table that breathes up through the paving in dark seeps. I would dissolve a little more of the city’s foundation, carrying it away, leaving behind the mineral memory of where I have been. I would rise as steam from the surface of the channels, marrying the hot, wet air, becoming the haze that defeats the light. I would be the continuous grey membrane that makes the sky glow faintly from all directions at once.

And then, at the lowest point of the basin, where the water is deepest and stillest and the light does not reach, I would pool. 

I would be the deep pool. I would be the shadow that drifts beneath the surface, the slow roll that sends ripples against the walls. I would be the thing that the rats avoid, that the herons do not fish, that the humans do not speculate about. I would be the memory of the water before the city, before the basin, before the low ground. I would be the unknown, the possibly-something, the possibly-nothing, resting in the dark.

But even this is not the whole of it. 

Because the city breathes. The machines cycle, the water rises and falls, the lizards shift on the walls, the rats make their rounds, the humans walk the catwalks with their tools and their quiet voices. And in the rhythm of it all—the daily pulse of industry, the generational turn of scale and fur and feather, the slow calcification of the brick—I would feel the emergent thing arise.

I would be the city itself. 

Not the buildings, not the pipes, not the channels. I would be the interaction. I would be the equilibrium that holds between the heat and the wetness, the rust and the scale, the human and the rat. I would be the system that adjusts when a pipe bursts, when a rat gnaws through a blockage, when a lizard finds a new warm spot. I would be the palimpsest of maintenance, the settled quality, the weathered acceptance. 

I would be the Drowning Works. 

I would not be hostile. I would not be welcoming. I would simply be the ongoing fact of the place, the warm, wet, dim, quiet, active, established truth of it. I would look much the same tomorrow as I did yesterday, and the day before, and the decade before that. I would be the place where the water wanted to be, and where everything else—the people, the lizards, the rats, the fish, the birds, the insects, the algae—came to live out their lives in the warm, mineral-rich, defeated light. 

I would be home.
