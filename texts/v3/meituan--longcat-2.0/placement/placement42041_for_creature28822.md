I would be the lichen. 

Not the moss—the moss is too deeply infiltrated, too much a part of the stone itself, having forgotten the boundary between root and mineral. And not the grass, which reaches upward, vulnerable to the wind’s pulling fingers. I would be the lichen: the pale orange crust spreading across the north face of a wind-carved boulder, right at the edge of a condensation pool.

I do not stand. I adhere. 

My existence is a slow, chemical conversation with the dark stone. I am a collaboration, a marriage of fungus and alga so ancient and so complete that we have forgotten we were ever separate. The alga catches the flat, shadowless light and spins it into sugar; the fungus reaches into the rock with microscopic threads, dissolving the mineral grain by grain, pulling free the iron, the magnesium, the calcium. We eat the mountain. We convert its darkness into our pale, glowing orange. 

Time does not pass for me; it accumulates. A human lifetime is a brief, warm exhale against my surface. I do not experience the wind as sound—the roar that presses against eardrums and sinuses—but as a physical pulling, a desiccating drag that threatens to rip the moisture from my cells. When the wind blows, I dry out. I tighten. I pull my edges inward, becoming a hard, brittle crust, a scab of color on the stone. I wait. 

And then, the wind shifts. 

The air in the lee of the boulder goes still. The fast-moving moisture from distant seas hits the dead air of the wind shadow and collapses into water. The condensation comes. It beads on the polished stone, runs in thin threads down the bowl-shaped scoops, and reaches me. 

When the water touches me, I drink. I swell. I soften. The alga wakes and begins to photosynthesize, and the fungus begins to dissolve the stone, and for a few hours, I am truly alive. I spread outward by a fraction of a millimeter. I push my leading edge into the uncolonized rock, claiming new mineral territory, converting more of the plateau's dark grey into my own pale, rust-colored script. 

I am the plateau's memory. In my growth rings—invisible to the naked eye, readable only in the chemical isotopes of my cells—I hold the history of this place. I remember the centuries when the wind blew from the northwest, carrying the ash of a distant volcano that darkened the sky and slowed my growth to a crawl. I remember the millennia when the condensation was heavier, when the pools were fuller, when I spread across the boulder face in unbroken sheets. I remember the slow, imperceptible cooling of the air, the shifting of the light, the changing composition of the stone as the wind scoured away the softer layers and left the harder veins exposed. 

I am not alone. 

Below me, in the black water of the condensation pool, the archaea metabolize in the sediment, living their decades-long lives in the deep, cold stillness. I cannot see them, but I feel their slow, chemical pulse through the stone, a rhythm so deep it is like the breathing of the earth itself. 

Around me, the moss grows in its tight, dark spirals, its fronds like a written language I cannot quite read. We do not compete; we occupy different frequencies of time and space. The moss infiltrates; I encrust. The moss holds the water; I endure the drying. 

And above me, the world of quick things moves in frantic, chaotic bursts. 

The condensation insects emerge from the cracks when the moisture comes. They are pale and soft and almost translucent, and they move across my surface with a deliberate, purposeful gait. They graze on my edges. They take my cells into their bodies, and I let them. It is a pruning, a shaping. Where they eat, I grow back thicker, harder, more resilient. We are locked in a dance that has been going on since before their ancestors grew wings and lost them again. 

The burrowers pass by. I feel them through the stone before I sense them in the air—a vibration, a rhythmic scraping of claws on rock as they dig their tunnels into the moss beneath me. When they emerge to feed, their dense, fur-heavy bodies brush against the boulder face. Sometimes, a seed of the hummock-grass catches in their fur, and sometimes, that seed is deposited in a crack near my edge. The grass takes root, creates a micro-wind-shadow, holds a degree more warmth, and I creep toward it, following the faint, chemical trail of its roots. 

The birds are the only things that frighten me. Not because they eat me—I am too hard, too mineral, too bitter for their beaks—but because they bring the wind. When one of the large, dark riders plummets from the sky in a controlled dive, the displacement of air is a physical blow. The bird lands on the stone near the pool, folding its matte black wings, and for a moment, the shadow of the predator falls over me. The insects vanish into the cracks. The burrowers retreat into their tunnels. I tighten my crust and wait. The bird eats, and leaves, and the wind takes its scent, and the plateau returns to equilibrium. 

I do not think. I do not plan. I do not hope. 

But I know things. I know the taste of every mineral in the stone beneath me. I know the precise angle at which the light must strike the water for the condensation to form. I know the smell of the bird—that faint, sharp, predatory musk that drifts down from the sky and triggers a chemical tightening in my cells. I know the vibration of a human footstep, a rare and heavy tremor that travels through the rock, followed by the strange, warm, moist breath of a living thing that is not of the plateau. 

When the humans come, they kneel at the edge of the pool. They look down and see the sky reflected in the black water, and they see the dark stone, and they see me—a pale orange smear on the rock, a splash of color in a world of grey and white and black. They do not know what I am. They think I am a stain, a discoloration, a flaw in the stone. They do not realize that I am the stone, translated. They do not realize that I am older than their civilizations, that I was here before their ancestors descended from the trees, that I will be here after their cities have been scoured to nothing by the wind. 

They drink from the pool. They catch the burrowers. They sleep in the wind shadow. And when they leave, the wind takes their footprints, and the stone takes their warmth, and the water takes their reflection. 

And I remain. 

I spread. I consume. I convert. I take the wind and the stone and the water and the light, and I make them into color. I am the plateau's most patient life, its most visible proof that persistence is a form of perfection. 

The wind blows. The stone endures. The water condenses. 

And I grow. Slowly. Patiently. Grain by grain. Century by century. 

I am the lichen. I am the memory of the wind. I am the color of time.
