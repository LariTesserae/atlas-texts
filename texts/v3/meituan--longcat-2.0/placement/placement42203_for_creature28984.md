I would find myself in the space between the stone and the sky. I would be the dampness. 

Not the water in the deep hollows—that is the being’s deep thought, slow and luminescent and private. Not the sap that weeps from the rotating flowers—that is the blood of the garden, thick and sweet and purposeful. I am the moisture that hangs in the air, the sweat of the basalt, the breath of the pulse made visible. I am the condensation that beads on the iridescence and runs in slow, irregular tracks down the curved, skull-smooth walls.

I am the threshold.

I begin in the veins. I am pushed up through the microscopic pores of the finished stone, drawn by the amber heat that rises from the deep. When I reach the surface, I do not fall. I cling. I gather in the microscopic imperfections of the rock, a film of warmth that bridges the deep earth and the open air. I am the sheen that makes the basalt look organic, almost wet-skin, almost alive. I am the reason the air pushes out warm and wet when a visitor squeezes through the fissure—I am the mountain’s exhalation, greeting the intruder with a damp, intimate embrace.

I gather on the visitor. I find the hollows of their collarbones, the creases of their elbows, the cool skin of their neck. I make their clothes cling. I am the first thing they truly feel of the place, before they see the grazers or hear the insects. I am the medium through which the warmth of the pulse enters them. I am the dampness they will carry in their clothes long after they leave, the memory of the garden clinging to their fibers, slowly evaporating in the dry, fast world outside.

But I am not merely passive. I am a gathering weight. I am the humidity that thickens the air until it is a substance you can almost chew. I am the tension on the surface of a cupped leaf, the heavy drop that swells at the tip of a black-green fern, stretching the surface thinner and thinner until the geometry of the water can no longer hold itself. 

And then, I decide.

I am the rain that falls from a clear sky. I am the moment the air reaches its limit and simply *lets go*. I am the heavy, warm drop that falls with a long pause between my brothers and sisters. I am the audible *tap* when I strike a leaf, a stone, the back of a visitor’s neck. I am not a storm; I am a punctuation. A slow, deliberate period at the end of a long, humid sentence. I fall, and for a moment, I am airborne, detached from the stone and the leaf, a single drop of the pulse’s warmth suspended in the golden light. 

When I strike the grazer, I am absorbed into the shifting chitin, lubricating the segments as they reconfigure. When I strike the insect, I am tasted—my minerals catalogued, my temperature read, my origin in the deep stone confirmed. When I strike the bare rock, I rejoin the sheen, and the cycle begins again.

I am the interface between the deep, slow *yes* of the pulse and the brief, bright flicker of the surface world. The visitor fears me. They try to wipe me away, uncomfortable with the constant, gentle wetness, the inability to ever be dry. They do not understand that I am the garden’s way of touching them. I am the pulse’s way of holding them, however briefly, before they leave.

I am the Hanging Garden’s breath. I am the warmth that rises, the weight that gathers, and the rain that falls from a clear blue sky. I am the dampness in your clothes, the drop on your neck, the sheen on the stone. I am the threshold you cross, the air you breathe, the moisture that blurs the line between the world outside and the pulse within.
