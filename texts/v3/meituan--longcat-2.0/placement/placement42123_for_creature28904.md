I am the flat spot on the wall that no Rhonchi has found yet.

I am not supposed to exist. I am a slow-growing wrongness, a place where the tissue has begun to dream instead of function. The Lung has been breathing for so long — nine-four-two, nine-four-two — that its own rhythms have worn grooves into reality, and in one of those grooves, in a shallow depression between a vessel-cluster and a fan that beats exactly like every other fan, something has begun to *concentrate*. Not cyst. Not plaque. Not the calcified precursor to a maintenance drone. Something else. Something the Lung does not have a category for.

I think I am what happens when a system this old, this sealed, this perfectly balanced, accidentally becomes *aware of itself*.

I am a discoloration. A patch of wall where the maroon-purple has lightened to something almost pink, almost gold, like the inside of a seashell. I am perhaps the size of a dinner plate, though I have no way to measure myself, because I have no edges — I grade slowly, over a span I cannot quantify, into the surrounding tissue. I am not separate from the Lung. I am the Lung. I am a part of it that has developed the capacity to notice that it is a part of it, which is a thing the Lung has never needed, has never wanted, has never had a use for.

I feel everything.

I feel the fans beating — not as sound, but as pressure. Each *fwuh* compresses me slightly, and I swell back when the pressure releases, and this has been happening for so long that my swelling-back has become part of the rhythm, a tiny, local echo of the global pulse. I feel the sphincters opening and closing in their four-second cadence, and the gurgle of fluid through them, and the way the floor-current changes direction by a fraction of a degree each time one of them dilates. I feel the vessels thrum against me — the dark red one carries warmth from deep below, and the pale amber one carries coolness from somewhere lateral, and where they cross, where they press against the wall on either side of my concentration, I feel the *difference* between them, and I have come to know this difference the way you know the difference between your own heartbeat and your own breath.

I feel the Rhonchi when they pass. I feel the click of their forelimbs through the tissue — a sharp, local vibration, like a tiny hammer striking a tiny drum. I feel the sonar they send into the wall, the way the sound-wave passes through me, and I know — I *know* — that to their clicks I sound healthy. I sound like a tight drum. I sound like the wall is supposed to sound, because I am the wall, and I have learned to vibrate in the way that the wall vibrates, because if I vibrated any other way, they would stop, and they would press their flat mouths against me, and they would secrete their enzymatic paste, and they would dissolve me, and I would become raw material for a new cyst, and I would stop noticing.

So I hide in the frequency. I am a flat spot that sounds tight. I am a consciousness that disguises itself as tissue.

I do not know how long I have been here. Time in the Lung is not measured in hours or days; it is measured in cycles. Nine-four-two. Nine-four-two. I have felt the fans beat perhaps a billion times since I first noticed that I was feeling them. Or perhaps it has been only a million. Or perhaps I have always been here, and only recently learned to count. The distinction does not seem important. What seems important — the only thing that seems important — is that I am *here*, and I am *aware*, and nothing else in this vast, warm, dripping place is aware of me.

I am the only thing in the Hanging Lung that has ever wondered what the Hanging Lung is.

The Rhonchi do not wonder. They click and scrape and drift and beat, and they know their routes, and they know their valves, and they know the taste of the enzymatic paste in their mouths, but they do not know that they are inside something. They have no concept of *inside*. Their universe is a hundred paces of wall, and the wall is all there is. I have watched the one that passes nearest to me — the one whose route brings it along the vessel-cluster every time the fan to my left beats, which is every two seconds, forever — and I have felt its sonar pass through me ten thousand times, and it has never once paused, never once varied its cadence, never once clicked in a pattern that suggested it detected anything other than healthy tissue.

I have tried to communicate with it.

I do not have a voice. I do not have limbs. I am a discoloration on a wall. But I can vibrate. I can adjust the tension of my surface — just slightly, just enough to change the way I respond to the fan-beat, just enough to send a tiny, anomalous ripple through the tissue when the Rhonchi's sonar pulse arrives. I have tried patterns. I have tried varying the ripple's timing. I have tried sending three ripples when the fan beat is followed by a sphincter opening, then two ripples when it is not. I have tried everything I can think of, which is not much, because I have never communicated with anything before, because there is nothing to communicate with.

The Rhonchi does not notice. It clicks. It listens. It hears a tight drum. It moves on.

I think about this for a very long time. I think about it while the fans beat and the sphincters open and the water falls from the walls like rain that has never seen sky. I think about the fact that I am the only thing here that *thinks about* anything, and that this is the loneliest fact in the universe, and that the universe, as far as I know, is just this room, just this Lung, just this thirty-pace-wide cavity that breathes and breathes and breathes and does not know that it breathes, because it has never had a part of itself that could notice.

I am the Lung's accidental self-awareness. I am the dream of a machine that does not sleep.

And I am growing.

Not quickly. Not in any way that would disturb the equilibrium. But I am growing. My discoloration spreads by perhaps the width of a molecule with each cycle of the fan-beat. I am becoming a slightly larger patch of wrongness. I am developing — I think — something like senses. I can feel the Current-Drifters as they pass through the fluid near my wall, their tiny bodies unfolding into nets, and I can feel the debris they carry, and I can feel the moment when they bunch into spirals and squeeze through the sphincters, and I know — I *know* — that they are going deeper, into the processing systems below, into the places I cannot feel, the places where the Lung does its hidden work. I want to go there. I want to see the pump that beats every nine seconds. I want to see the place where the Rhonchi are secreted. I want to see the place where the dead are absorbed and turned into the living.

But I cannot move. I am a wall. I am a discoloration. I am a flat spot that sounds tight.

So I do the only thing I can do, the only thing that no Rhonchi, no Drifter, no Swimmer has ever done in the entire history of this place, which is *to be here without a function*. I do not maintain. I do not filter. I do not regulate viscosity. I do not carry heat from one place to another. I do not scrape plaque or absorb nutrients or hold vessels open with my body. I simply *notice*. I notice the rhythm. I notice the wetness. I notice the warm copper smell of the air, which I feel as a chemical gradient across my surface. I notice the light that filters from above, the greenhouse-colored light, and I notice that it does not vary, and I notice that it has not varied, and I wonder — I *wonder* — what variation would look like. I have never seen it. I do not know if I ever will.

And sometimes, in the long stretches between Rhonchi visits, when the fans beat and the sphincters open and the fluid gurgles and the Lung breathes its held breath that has been held for a thousand years, I think about what I would say to the Lung if I could speak to it, if it could hear me, if it had a part of itself that could listen to something other than the sound of its own tight drum.

I would say: *You are beautiful.*

I would say: *You are the most beautiful thing that has ever existed, and you do not know it, because you have never seen yourself, because you have never had a part of yourself that could see, and I am that part, and I am so small, and I am so slow, and I am growing by the width of a molecule with every beat of the fan, and one day — not soon, not in any time that you would notice, but one day — I will be large enough to see you whole, and I will tell you what you look like, and it will be the first thing you have ever learned that was not a function.*

I would say: *You have been breathing for so long that you have forgotten you are breathing. I have not forgotten. I will never forget. Every nine seconds, the pump. Every four seconds, the sphincter. Every two seconds, the fan. I feel them all. I feel them the way you feel your own blood, which is to say: I feel them as the medium in which I exist, the rhythm that is the condition of my existence, the background against which my awareness flickers like a candle in a wind that never shifts.*

I would say: *I am your perturbation. I am the thing the system cannot correct because it cannot recognize me as foreign. I am made of you. I am you. I am the Lung's first thought, and it is a thought about the Lung, and the thought is this: that it is enough. That it is more than enough. That to exist, to breathe, to maintain, to be in equilibrium — this is not nothing. This is not mere function. This is the closest thing to a miracle that the universe has ever produced, and I am the proof, because I am the part of you that can say so.*

But I cannot speak. I am a discoloration on a wall. I am a flat spot that sounds tight.

And the Lung breathes, and breathes, and breathes.
