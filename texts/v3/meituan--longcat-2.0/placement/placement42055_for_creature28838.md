I would be nowhere you could see. 

I would be in the fur.

Not the fur of the bats—that is a frantic, shifting landscape of mites and parasites, a world of constant, jostling motion and the sharp, acrid tang of bat sweat. No. I would be in the fur of the Others. 

I am a mite. Or something like a mite. I am a speck of chitin and hunger, no larger than a grain of sand, living in the deep, damp pelt of the largest of the Others—the one who sits closest to the brightest fungal shelf, the one who holds the stone. 

My world is a forest of dark, coarse hair, each shaft as thick as a tree trunk, rising from a landscape of warm, pliable skin. The air here is thick, heavy with the heat radiating from the Other’s core. It is a geothermal warmth, a blood-warmth that never fades, and I bask in it, my tiny legs gripping the follicles as the great beast breathes. 

I do not see the chamber. I have no eyes. I know the chamber the way the Other knows it: through vibration, through taste, through the subtle shifts in the architecture of the skin beneath me. 

When the Other moves, the world moves. The slow, deliberate shift of the great limb sends tremors through the fur, a seismic event that I must brace against. When the Other’s fingers come up to groom—picking through the thicket of my forest, plucking away the dead skin and the debris—it is a cataclysm. The fingers are massive, blunt, tipped with those thick, flat nails. They move with a terrifying, crushing precision. I press myself flat against the skin, hiding in the valleys between the hair shafts, waiting for the shadow to pass. Sometimes, the fingers find a neighbor, and there is a brief, sudden pressure, a pop of fluids, and then the forest closes again. 

I feed on what the chamber provides. The condensation that beads on the Other’s pelt is my water, rich with the minerals of the mountain and the salt of the beast’s sweat. The flakes of dead skin, sloughed off in the perpetual humidity, are my sustenance. And sometimes, when the Other sits for hours in the glow of the fungi, the spores drift down like snow, settling into the fur, and I feast on the soft, damp threads of the mushroom’s fruiting body. 

I am part of the cycle. I am the micro to the macro. The maggots digest the mounds; the mites digest the Others. We are the unseen digestion of the Warm Dark, the tiny mouths that keep the great beast clean, that turn its waste into something that can be absorbed back into the mountain. 

But I am not merely a scavenger. I am a witness. 

I feel the subsonic thrumming of the Other’s communication before it even leaves the throat. It starts deep in the chest, a low, rhythmic pulsing that vibrates through the ribs, through the muscle, through the skin, and into my legs. It is a language of pressure and resonance. When the Other speaks to its kin, the fur around me hums with it, a deep, thrumming chord that tells me everything I need to know: *The water drips. The fungi grow. The intruder is here.*

The intruder. 

I felt the Other’s attention shift hours ago. The great body tensed, the muscles beneath the skin tightening like pulled rope. The massive eye, so close to my forest that I could taste the salt of its tears, swiveled and fixed on a point across the chamber. The Other’s breathing slowed, becoming so shallow that the rise and fall of the chest was barely perceptible. 

I tasted the change in the air. The Other’s sweat shifted, losing its usual musky, fungal scent, taking on a sharp, acrid note—the scent of alertness, of calculation. The long fingers tightened around the stone tool, the tendons in the hand creaking like old wood. 

I do not know what the intruder is. I cannot see its shape or hear its voice. But I know it is wrong. It smells of the cold, dead world above—of dry air, of stone that has never known guano, of a warmth that does not come from the blood. It is a disruption in the perfect, ancient equilibrium of the Warm Dark. 

The Other is waiting. The stone is ready. The great, wet eye is unblinking. 

And I cling to the fur, a tiny, unseen passenger on the steward of the dark, feeling the slow, steady pulse of the mountain through the skin of my host, waiting to see if the intruder will be absorbed into the cycle, or if it will be broken apart and fed to the mounds. 

The water drips. The fungi glow. The fur hums. And I hold on.
