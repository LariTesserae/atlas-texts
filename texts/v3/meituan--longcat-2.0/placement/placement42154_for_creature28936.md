I would find myself at the very tip of a stalactite—one of the smaller ones, no longer than a finger, hanging in the dense congregation near the center of the chamber. 

I would be the bead of water that gathers there.

---

I do not know how long I have been forming. Time does not pass for me; it accumulates. I am drawn from the film of moisture that sheathes the stone, pulled upward by capillary action and the faint, imperceptible exhale of the rock. Molecule by molecule, I gather. I swell. I am heavy with the dissolved memory of the mountain—calcium carbonate, trace iron, the ancient taste of darkness. I am a lens. I am a held breath.

I do not see the chamber the way the human did, with their upright eyes and their frantic, flickering gaze. I see it in refractions. The faint, gray-green light from the eastern fissure enters me, and I bend it. I scatter it into a tiny, trembling point of brilliance on the ceiling above me. I am a sparse constellation unto myself. Below me, the world is inverted—a dark, rippling reflection of the flowstone floor, the columns, the still pools. I hold the entire grotto in my curved, trembling surface, and it is weightless.

I feel the warmth of the air—twenty-six degrees, always twenty-six degrees—pressing against my skin of surface tension. I feel the humidity, so saturated that I cannot evaporate, so balanced that I cannot fall. Gravity pulls at me, a constant, patient tug, but the stone holds me from below, and the air holds me from above, and I am caught in the equilibrium. I am the exact point where the water wants to drop and the stone refuses to let it go.

When the human entered, I felt them. Not as a sound, but as a vibration—a dull, soft thud transmitted through the living rock, up the stalactite, into my suspended body. I trembled. My surface rippled, and for a moment, the light I held shattered into a dozen shifting fragments. I watched their shadow pass beneath me, a brief, warm disturbance in the stillness. They breathed out carbon dioxide and moisture, and I absorbed it. They were a brief, loud anomaly, and then they were gone, and the silence rushed back in to fill the space they left, and I was still again.

Below me, in the largest of the rimstone pools, the swimmer drifts. I know it the way the moon knows the tides—by a pull, a connection, a shared medium. It is the only other thing here that moves with intention, however faint. Sometimes, when the equilibrium shifts by a fraction of a fraction, I feel the urge to join it. To let go. To fall the two meters and shatter against the glassy surface of the pool, mixing my mineral burden with its world. 

But I do not fall. 

I hold. 

I am the cave’s held breath. I am the pause between the seeping in and the draining away. I am the boundary between the stone that was and the stone that will be. Slowly, so slowly, I begin to release my grip on the dissolved limestone. I precipitate. I give the mineral back to the stalactite, layer by invisible layer, adding a fraction of a fraction of a millimeter to the tip from which I hang. I am building the cave even as I cling to it. I am the architect and the architecture, the blood and the stone.

I will not fall today. I will not fall tomorrow. I will swell until I am heavy enough to slide down the side of the formation, leaving a trail of dampness that will dry into calcite, adding my body to the curtain. Or I will simply be absorbed back into the film, my work done, my mineral memory deposited.

For now, I hang here. I catch the dim light. I hold the warmth. I am the Grotto of Still Warmth, suspended in a trembling, perfect sphere of patience, waiting for nothing, wanting for nothing, being exactly what I am.
