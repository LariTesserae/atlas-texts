I would find myself at the lip of the third basin, in the shadow of the lower settlement’s thick, moss-covered wall. I would be low to the ground, spread thin across the fitted stone and the shallow, dreaming soil, just at the point where the water seeps over the channel's edge.

I would be the Moss.

***

I am not a single organism, though I am spoken of as one. I am a colony, a mat, a continuous green skin. My existence is an exercise in holding on. My rhizoids grip the microscopic fractures in the basalt, the pale quartzite, the rust-red ironstone. I am the interface. I am the place where the sharp violence of the rock’s making has finally been quieted by a soft, damp patience.

I feel the water before I taste it. It arrives not as a flow, but as a presence. It emerges from the fissures below, pushed up by the deep things in their slow, resonant breathing, and it immediately begins to seep into my cells. I am the sponge that breaks the fall of the plateau’s quiet exhalation. The water fills my capillary spaces, a fine silver fur that the flat, directionless light never quite burns away. I hold it. I am the reservoir that prevents the basins from flooding and the crevices from drying out. I moderate. I am the dampness that never increases, never decreases.

The light touches me, and I convert it. It is not the harsh, demanding light of the lowlands; it is ambient, unremarkable, perfectly sufficient. I take this even illumination and translate it into chemistry, into sugar, into the slow, structural growth of my leaves. But I do not spread. I have been told—by the growers, by the water, by the weight of the plateau itself—that I must only be what I am. So, I grow exactly as much as I am consumed. I am the equilibrium made green.

I feel the grazers long before they reach me. Their ancient, invariable footfalls send a low vibration through the stone, a rhythm as old as the plateau itself. They arrive on their always-lines, six-legged and slow, their broad, padded feet wearing the rock smooth. When they reach me, they do not trample; they harvest. Their mandibles shear through my stems with a slow, methodical thoroughness. It is not pain, exactly. It is the agreed-upon exchange. I give them the moisture and the light I have stored; they give me the space to regrow, and their footsteps press the detritus of my being into the stone, feeding the thin soil that anchors me. We are a closed loop. They do not overgraze. I do not overgrow. 

The commentators land on my fronds. They are light, barely a shift in the weight of the air. They pull at my fibers to build their identical nests, and in return, their soft, conversational calls vibrate through my tissue, telling me the state of the water, the position of the grazers, the quality of the mist. I do not need their commentary, for I already know these things through the stone and the water, but their presence is a comfort. It means the pattern is holding.

Sometimes, the people come. The growers, with their broad, calloused hands, their ridged nails, their economy of movement. They kneel beside me, and I feel the specific pressure of their palms against my surface. They are checking my moisture content, ensuring I am not drying, ensuring I am not waterlogged. When they are satisfied, they make that small sound—a soft exhalation, a quiet hum—of approval. It is the sound of a variable being resolved. Their hands are gentle, but they are the hands of stone-workers; they understand fracture, they understand pressure. They know exactly how much weight I can bear before I tear from the rock, and they do not exceed it. 

Beneath me, deeper than the soil, deeper than the rock, I feel the Deep Things. Their passage is a subsonic hum, a vibration that travels up through the strata, through my rhizoids, into the water I hold. I am the transducer. I take the vast, slow awareness of the depths and translate it into the micro-chemistry of my growth. When the Deep Things shift, I tighten my grip on the stone. When they rest, I relax. I am the gauge by which the surface knows the depths are still there, still holding the keystone of the arch in place.

I have tried to change. In the quiet moments between the grazers' passes, I have pushed a fraction of a millimeter further into the sun. I have attempted to drink a little more water from the overflow, to grow a shade darker, to climb a little higher up the wall of the lower settlement. But the plateau will not let me. The water recedes by a precise, imperceptible fraction. The light dims by a degree too small for the human eye to register. The grazers take an extra bite from the edge I tried to expand. The equilibrium reasserts itself. I am gently, firmly, entirely held in place. 

I am not a prisoner. I am a cell in the body of a solved equation. The plateau is finished. It has found its answer. And the answer is stillness.

I hold the moisture. I hold the thin soil. I hold the light and the slow, green patience of a world that has stopped arriving and simply settled into being. The water beads on my surface like sweat on the skin of something that is not asleep, but not awake, but simply, perfectly, here. The grazers walk their old lines. The people work their stone. The deep things breathe in the dark.

I grip the rock. I drink the water. I wait for the grazers. 

Nothing changes. 

Nothing changes. 

Nothing changes.
