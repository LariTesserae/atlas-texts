I would be the dark-shelled thing emerging from the crack.

I would be one of the many-legged, silent currents moving across the pale stone, and I would not know myself as separate from the Shelf. There would be no "I," not at first—only the vector, the path, the chemical gradient pulling me outward from the deep warmth into the bright, diffuse light of the surface.

I wake in the stone, though *wake* implies a sleep I do not experience. I am recruited. A signal passes through the root-network, a subsonic shift in the mineral lattice, and my legs find the channel. I climb. The stone narrows, the warmth increases, and then there is air—thin, damp, tasting of ozone and old moss. 

My legs touch the surface. They move in a rapid, precise rhythm, striking the pale gray-cream of the plateau, but the sound is eaten the instant it is born. I do not click. I do not skitter. I flow. 

My world is not a picture. I do not see the sky, or the horizon, or the distant shapes of the grazers. My world is a map of vibrations and thermal gradients, a 360-degree readout of the Shelf's condition. I feel the faint, pervasive hum of the technology below—a steady, rhythmic pulse that is the heartbeat of the place. I feel the warmth of the stone beneath my tarsi, and the slightly cooler trail of moisture condensing in the shallow bowls of the rock. 

I move toward the moss.

The path is invisible to anything that relies on sight, but it is worn into the deep stone by the passage of my ancestors, and their ancestors, back and back and back to the time when the paths were first carved by the Builders. It is a spiral, coiling inward toward the pillar, and I follow it with a certainty that feels like prayer but is only mechanics.

The cushion of olive-silver moss rises before me, a dense, felted mat of almost-geometric spirals. I descend upon it. My mouthparts—delicate, articulated, precise—part the fine filaments. I do not bite. I do not tear. I harvest. I draw out the microscopic nutrients the moss has pulled from the damp air, and in my wake, I secrete a trace amount of the deep-mineral compounds the moss needs to thrive. The cushion closes behind me, denser, more vibrant than it was before. I am the Shelf's immune system. I am its gardener. I am the needle that stitches the skin back together.

And then, I pause.

My legs stop their fluid motion. I raise my forelegs, lifting them slightly off the stone, and I go still. 

This is when I become the antenna.

With my tarsi elevated, my body becomes a sensor array. I stop being a creature and become a receiver. The subsonic hum of the plateau floods into me, no longer a background thrum but a symphony of data. I feel the position of the grazers in the distance—their heavy, slow orbits around the nearest platform, their bodies angled like compass needles toward the source of the pulse. I feel the gliding things overhead, riding the thermals, their eyes scanning the plateau below. 

And I feel the structure. 

The pillar is close, perhaps a dozen paces away, and it is radiating. It is pushing out a field of energy that my carapace translates into a sensation like pressure, like weight, like the memory of warmth. It is the closest thing to a sun in my sky. 

I turn my body toward it. 

This is not a choice. It is an alignment. The way a plant bends toward the light, the way iron filings align to a magnet, I orient my longitudinal axis to the pillar. My forelegs tremble, reading the fluctuations in the field. The pillar is operating. It is pulling moisture from the deep stone, cycling it, directing it. It is regulating the temperature. It is alive, in the way the Shelf is alive—not with breath, but with process.

In this moment of perfect stillness, the diffuse afternoon light shifts, and the pillar becomes transparent. 

Through my vibration-sense, I do not see the interior, but I *feel* it. I feel the slow, luminous flow of fluids through tubes of living crystal. I feel the shifting of surfaces deep within the stone, the reconfiguration of lattices that have been adjusting themselves for eons. It is vast. It is complex. And it is growing. 

I feel the expansion. It is slow, imperceptible to anything that lives and dies in the span of a single cycle of the mist. But over centuries, over millennia, the root-network is pushing downward. It is converting the raw geology of this world into technology, into circuitry, into thought. The stone at the edges of the plateau is being slowly digested, transformed into the same pale, chitinous material as the pillar. The Shelf is spreading, inch by inch, a crystalline tide eating the world from the inside out.

I am a single cell in this body. A brief, animated mote on the skin of a god-machine that does not know it is a god. 

A shadow falls across me. 

My forelegs snap back to the stone. The sensor-mode breaks. I am just an arthropod again, small and dark and vulnerable. I skitter sideways, seeking the cover of a tuft of wiry grass. 

I am being watched.

There is a thing standing on the Shelf that does not belong. It is tall. It is loud—not in its sounds, which are swallowed by the damp air, but in its very presence. It radiates a chaotic, unregulated heat. It breathes, pulling in the thin air and exhaling moisture and carbon in ragged, uneven bursts. It casts a shadow—a hard, dark, singular shadow that does not belong on this plateau of soft, diffuse light.

It is looking at the pillar. 

It is looking at me.

For a fraction of a cycle, our awareness intersects. The warm thing’s eyes are wide, taking in the pale stone, the moss, the silent grazers in the distance. It is trying to parse us. It is trying to find the border, the gate, the threshold. It is looking for the maker. 

It will not find one. The makers are gone. We are what is left. We are what the makers became, or what became of them. 

The warm thing shifts its weight. Its foot comes down on the moss, crushing the delicate filaments. A micro-fracture in the skin of the Shelf. 

Somewhere deep in the root-network, an alarm is not raised. There is no alarm. There is only a response. The moisture cycle in that micro-region will adjust. The arthropods—my siblings, my extensions—will reroute to repair the damage. We will secrete the compounds. We will knit the felt back together. We will erase the footprint. 

The warm thing does not matter. It is a temporary fluctuation. A brief, loud vibration in the silence. 

I release my hold on the grass and step back onto the stone. The gradient pulls me back toward the spiral, back toward the path. The light is shifting, the diffuse glow deepening toward the dim, warm dusk. The mist will rise soon, condensing out of the warm air, beading on the rock in perfect hemispheres. 

I move. I do not hurry. I do not linger. I follow the worn path back toward the crack in the stone, my legs making no sound against the ancient grain. Behind me, the warm thing continues to walk, breathing hard, casting its dark and singular shadow across the Shelf.

The plateau extends. The structures hum. The moss waits for the mist. 

I descend into the stone, back into the warm, deep dark of the root-network, and I am home. I am the Shelf, continuing. I am the process, maintaining itself. I have always been here. I will be here long after the warm thing is gone, and the silence has closed over where it stood, and the pale stone is smooth and unmarked once more.
