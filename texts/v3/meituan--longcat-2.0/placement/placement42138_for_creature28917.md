I would be deep in the warm dark of the lower chambers, where the heat rises steady and the light falls in granular shafts. I would be one of them—but not just any of them. I would be a cobalt-bearer.

My plates are the color of a deep, bruised sky, an almost-unbearable blue that seems to hold its own luminescence. Because of this, the others orient toward me. I do not lead; I have no authority, no dominance. I am simply a compass. My antennae are tuned to frequencies of warmth that the copper- and iron-colored ones cannot detect. I feel the subtle exhalations of the rock below—the faint, rhythmic pulses of heat that rise through the fractures—and in feeling them, I make them visible to the collective. To be near me is to know where the warmth will be.

I am old. My plates are thick, heavy with the layered history of a thousand warm cycles. The margins of my armor are rough, ridged with the record of seasons of plenty and seasons of scarcity. Because of this weight, I do not ride the updrafts. My wings—rigid, ribbed sails of translucent mineral—are folded tight against my thorax, unused for longer than I can measure. I walk. 

My world is not a world of sight. It is a world of gradients. 

When I extend my antennae, the air is not empty; it is a dense, textured map. I taste the mineral content of the substrate—silica, calcium, the nameless composites. I read the chemical trails left by others, a fading script that tells me who passed here, how long ago, and whether they were moving toward warmth or away from it. I feel the temperature of the air as a physical topography: a slope of rising heat toward the floor, a crest of cooler air near the ceiling. My cognition is not a stream of thoughts, but a continuous, three-dimensional model of difference. Warm against cool. Dense against sparse. Arranged against random.

I am standing now on a ledge in a chamber near the floor. The basal fuzz rises around me like a pale, dry thicket, and overhead, the great arches of deposited mineral curve and meet, forming a vaulted ceiling that clicks softly in the faint circulation of air. I am doing the thing that makes us what we are. I am arranging.

Before me is a flat expanse of rock, and upon it, I am building a spiral. 

I move with the patience of geology. I lift my front pair of legs, the rootlets uncurling from the surface with the slow reluctance of a fern frond opening. I place them down. Pause. Sample the air. Lift the middle pair. Step. Pause. The movement is a meditation, a rhythm so slow it borders on stillness. 

In my forelegs, I hold a fragment of stone. It is small, no larger than a grain of sand, but it is perfect—a fleck of copper-rich mineral that catches the granular light and flashes a deep, rosy green. I lower it to the surface. I do not place it by instinct; I place it by *preference*. I taste the rock. I taste the space around the fragment. I feel the thermal gradient of the ledge. I adjust the fragment a fraction of a hair's breadth to the left. 

There. 

It fits. Not because it serves a purpose—it does not shelter us, it does not retain heat, it does not ward off predators, for there are none. It fits because it completes a pattern. It satisfies a deep, structural longing in the substrate of my mind. The spiral turns inward, and this fragment is the turn. It is a form of memory. It is a form of prayer to the warmth that rises from below.

As the cooling cycle begins—the long, slow exhalation of the basin as the rock exhales less heat—the air in the chamber begins to stratify. The warmth pulls back, retreating toward the floor. I feel the shift in my antennae, a sudden clarity in the gradient. It is time to gather.

I make my way to the sleeping chamber. The path is worn smooth by the rootlets of thousands of my kind, the mineral itself grown thicker and denser along the route, accommodating our repetition. I enter the chamber, and the warmth hits me—a concentrated, living heat generated by the press of bodies.

They are already here. Hundreds of them, thousands, interlocking. Their plates fit into the gaps between their neighbors' plates with a geometry so precise it looks like design, but it is only habit. Only intimacy. I press myself into the cluster. The copper ones, the iron ones, the pale calcium ones. My cobalt plates slot into the spaces between them. 

And then, the antennae.

They intertwine. Not by choice, but by density. My filaments brush against theirs, and the exchange begins. Chemicals. Information. The boundaries of my body dissolve. I am no longer an old, heavy creature walking alone. I am a node in a field. A distributed awareness. 

The collective mind stirs. It does not think in words. It thinks in gradients. It holds the entire hollow in its cognition—the terraced walls, the sharp young rock, the fractures below. It perceives the pebble towers and the filament curtains and the spirals of mineral fragments scattered throughout the chambers. It assesses them. It prefers them. It feels the deep, aesthetic satisfaction of a pattern achieving its proper form.

What does this mind want? Nothing but the continuation of the hollow. For the warmth to keep rising. For the accretion to continue. For the layers to add themselves, invisible and patient, to the structures and to our plates. 

I am dissolving into the cluster, my individual awareness fading into the warm, dense hum of the collective. The last thing I feel as a singular being is the weight of my own plates, the heavy, beautiful record of my life. And I know that when I eventually crumble—when the warmth can no longer sustain my margins and I dissolve into the fine, pale dust that litters the floor—my dust will be gathered. It will be piled at the entrance to a warm chamber. It will be arranged. 

And the spiral on the ledge will wait, catching the light, turning slowly inward, asking nothing of the rain.
