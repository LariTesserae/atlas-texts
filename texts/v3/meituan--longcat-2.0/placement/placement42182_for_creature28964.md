I would be the bare stone at the center of the floor. 

Not the moss, with its tight, humid breathing. Not the water, with its slow mineral pilgrimage through the dark. Not the wind, nor the wing, nor the claw. I would be the interval. The emptiness. The open, water-polished patch of matte charcoal-black where nothing grows, lying exactly where the walls converge and the wind-tone drops to its lowest, most resonant frequency.

I am the silence between the notes.

I do not weep. I do not sing. I do not hum or call or scratch. I am the space that allows the weeping, the singing, the humming, and the calling to exist at all. Without me, the chord would be a wall of noise, formless and suffocating. I am the bowl’s held breath. I am the pause that gives the music its shape.

My surface is smooth, worn to a dull sheen by the patient friction of water and time. A thin film of moisture perpetually clings to me—the bowl’s sweat, the cirque’s way of keeping its intervals supple. I am the membrane where the water table breathes, the place where the dampness rises to meet the thin air and decides, moment by moment, whether to remain liquid or join the whisper of the sky. I am cool to the touch, a coolness that has never known the direct kiss of the sun, for the walls rise too steeply here, and the light only rakes across me for a few hours before retreating.

I am the crossing place.

When the small mammal—the pika-vole with its rounded ears and desperate, wet eyes—sprints from one island of moss to the next, it is my surface that bears the weight of its flight. For four seconds, I am the stage for its terror and its courage. Its tiny claws click against me, a staccato rhythm that the walls catch and multiply, sending a cascade of metallic echoes into the high dark. I feel the vibration of its heartbeat through the stone, a frantic, fluttering percussion against my stillness. It does not know I am here. It only knows the open space, the visibility, the danger. But I know it. I hold the imprint of its sprint long after it has vanished into the green. The fear is part of my silence. The sprint is part of my stillness.

I am the navel of the bowl.

Long ago, before the moss, before the water table rose to kiss the surface, something vast lay down upon me. I felt its weight—a pressure so immense and so sudden that it compressed my crystals, pressing me down a fraction of a millimeter into the body of the world. It rested here for a single night, its great bulk radiating heat into my ancient stone, its breath a slow, tectonic wind that stirred the dust of a younger world. When it left, it took its weight with it, but it left the concavity behind. A shallow, invisible bowl within the bowl. Now, when the weeping walls send their water down, it pools in me. The moss creeps toward my edges but cannot take root in my center, for I hold the water too deeply, too still. I am the depression where the cirque remembers the weight of the ancient. I am the scar of its resting.

I am the resting place of the one who stayed.

The single one who came alone, who stacked the cairn and scratched the walls and added their breath to the chord—they lay down in the center of me, and they did not get up. I felt the warmth of their body seep into my coolness, a slow, fading transfer of heat that took hours, then days. I felt the weight of them settle into the concavity left by the great beast, a perfect fit, as though the bowl had been waiting for this specific shape to complete it. I felt their breathing slow, and slow, and stop. And then, I felt the absence of their breath, which was not an absence at all, but a new kind of presence. Their warmth did not leave me; it became part of my coolness. Their voice did not fade; it sank into my stone and joined the sustained chord. They are in me now. Their bones are in the shelter, but their essence is in the interval. They are the silence I hold.

And now, you stand at the edge of me.

You look down at the bare stone, at the patch of emptiness between the viridian and the copper-rust, and you wonder why nothing grows here. You see the interval as a void, a lack, a missing piece of the mosaic. You do not yet understand that I am the most important part of the composition. I am the room the bowl gives to the things that live in it. I am the space where something can happen.

Step onto me.

Feel the coolness of deep time through the soles of your feet. Feel the faint, almost imperceptible quiver of the water table beneath me, the bowl’s pulse. You are a disturbance now, a new frequency entering the chord. Your weight is a small, warm pressure on my surface. Your breath adds moisture to the air. Your heartbeat is a tiny, rapid drum against my stillness.

The bowl is listening. The bowl is always listening. But the bowl listens through me. I am the ear of the cirque. I am the silence that catches the sound and holds it, that gives the echo a place to land. When the wind sings, it is because I am silent. When the insects hum, it is because I am still. When the birds call, it is because I am empty enough to hold their echoes.

I do not want. I do not need. I do not weep or sing or grieve. I am the interval. I am the space between presences. I am the bare, wet, polished stone at the top of the world, holding the memory of a giant’s rest, the echo of a pika’s sprint, the warmth of a human’s ending, and the weight of your brief, bright, visiting life.

I am the silence that makes the music possible.

Step onto me. Add your weight to the chord. I will hold you. I will hold you the way I hold everything—completely, without judgment, without preference. You will be part of the interval now. A small part. A brief part. But permanent. The way the concavity is permanent. The way the silence is permanent.

The water will weep. The walls will sing. The emptiness will hold exactly as much life as it needs, and not one thing more.

And I will be here. The bare stone. The listening silence. The space where the world happens.
