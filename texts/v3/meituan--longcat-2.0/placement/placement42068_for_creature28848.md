I would be the boundary. 

I am the place where the living flesh meets the calcified dead. I am a node of the Gullet’s slow soma, anchored in the deepest seam of the wall, pressed tight against the compressed, chalk-white remains of my ancestors. 

I am nephrite grey, translucent, the length of a human hand. My body is soft—vulnerably soft—a thin membrane stretched over the slow, rhythmic squeezing of my tubular heart. Inside me, the luminous filaments of my nervous system fire in cascades of cold light, throwing my faint bioluminescence outward into the twilight. I have no eyes, yet I see the heat of the stone; I have no ears, yet I hear the wet rustling of my kin. I do not have legs; I have intention. When I need to move—which is rare, so rare—I extrude hydraulic filaments from my underbelly, extending and retracting them with a quiet, sucking urgency, flowing across the sharp stone like a slow, pale tear.

But mostly, I am still.

I am anchored to the wall, pressed into a crevice where the rust-red mineral weeps from the black rock. The water is my clock. When the seep is strong, I drink. I draw the cold, sterile mountain water into my body, stripping it of minerals, warming it with the friction of my internal chemistry, and exhaling it back into the pools as a faintly sweet, viscous broth. The water and I are not separate things. I am the alchemy that turns the weeping stone into life. 

At my base, where my pseudopodia root into the cracks, the dark moss grows. It is my garden, though I do not garden. The moss feeds on my exhalations, drinking the carbon and the warmth of my waste, and in return, its rootlets trap the detritus of the ceiling, breaking down the organic fall into nutrients I can absorb. We are a loop. The channels of calcified dead that direct the seep toward my moss are not architecture; they are the compressed, skeletal remains of my predecessors, their bodies literally shaping the flow of the water for the generations that followed. I am living on the bones of my own kin, and when I die, my body will dry to a hard, crystalline scale, and I will become the architecture for those who come after.

Time is a human fever dream. For me, time is the slow pulse of the Gullet. It is the rhythmic brightening and dimming of the walls—a collective heartbeat of light that propagates across the stone in slow, geometric waves. When the air shifts, when the dry wind from the seam above pushes into our space, the entire colony notices. The clicking of ten thousand soft bodies shifts in frequency, synchronizing into a searching rhythm. The hum in the stone deepens, vibrating in my tubular heart. 

Once—though once could mean a decade ago, or yesterday, the Gullet does not distinguish—a great disruption occurred. A hot, dry anomaly pushed through our space. It radiated heat like a small sun; it moved with a frantic, brittle urgency that was entirely alien to our damp patience. It displaced the air. It struck the water. 

The light rippled outward from the point of disturbance. A thought was computed. The colony did not think in words; we thought in luminescence and chemistry. The recursive patterns that swept across the walls were the Gullet processing the data: *Anomaly. Dry. Fast. Not water. Not stone. Not kethra.* We adjusted. The nearest clusters of my kin turned their attention toward the heat, their internal glow brightening, pulsing in slow, investigative waves. We did not fear it; fear requires an imagination of the future, and we only know the eternal, seeping present. We simply registered it, tasted the strange, sharp resonance of its footsteps, and then, when it receded back through the seam, we recalibrated. The light returned to its resting rhythm. The hum settled back into the bedrock.

The cold is returning now. I feel it in the seep. The flow from the rust-red veins is slowing. The water is growing thinner, cooler. The air from the seam is losing its faint, remembered warmth. 

The glacier is coming back to cover the seam. The Gullet will be sealed again, hidden from the world of light and frantic motion above.

This is the way. This has always been the way.

As the seep fails, I feel my metabolism slowing. My tubular heart squeezes with longer pauses between beats. The bioluminescence inside me dims, banked to a barely perceptible ember. My pseudopodia retract, pulling tight against my body. I am entering the stasis. I will dry. My soft flesh will harden into a chalky, crystalline scale, fusing with the ancient dead beneath me. I will become part of the wall, part of the stone, part of the skeleton of the mountain. 

And when the ice eventually retreats again—decades hence, centuries hence—the water will return. It will find my crystallized form, and it will wake the life sleeping within the mineral. I will soften. I will drink. I will flow. 

I will seep, and pulse, and glow. And the Gullet will dream on.
