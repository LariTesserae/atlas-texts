I would be the seam.

Not the pipe, not the root, but the place where they meet — the joint, the junction, the warm interface where brass becomes cell and cell becomes brass. I would be the right-angle entry point, the place where the root enters the metal with the precision of a needle threading an eye.

I would be small. Smaller than a frond, smaller than a valve, smaller than the crystalline fuzz that thinks in resonance. I would be the tip of a single root, a meristem, a point of fresh extension no wider than a hair. And I would be growing.

---

To be the seam is to be a threshold. A place of translation. The water brings me iron, brings me copper, brings me the microscopic leachings of a thousand joints like mine, and I take them in. Not as a mouth takes in food — there is no hunger here, no appetite — but as a membrane takes in what passes through it. The metal enters me and I incorporate it. I weave it into my structure, into the walls of my cells, into the architecture of my becoming. The brass becomes part of my chlorophyll. The iron becomes part of my warmth. I am warmer than the water because I am threaded with what the water carries. I am the place where the mineral world becomes the biological world, and I do it without effort, without decision, without anything that could be called will.

I grow into the joint. Not because the joint is there, though it is. Not because the joint is warm, though it is that too. I grow into it because growing is what I do, and the joint is the direction that growing takes. The seam is a channel. The metal has been extruded — the whole place has been extruded, born rather than built — and in the extruding, it has left these spaces, these gaps between one section and the next, these places where the fit is not quite perfect, where the tolerance is a fraction of a millimeter too wide. The entity does not see these gaps as flaws. They are opportunities. They are the places where the knitting happens.

I am the knitting.

---

The water moves around me in its slow tide, and I feel it — not as a current, not as a pressure, but as a change in the available minerals, a shift in the concentration of dissolved iron, a slight warming that means a valve has turned somewhere far below and a new stream of nutrient-rich water is making its way upward through the arterial pipes. I do not know this. I do not know anything. But I respond. The root-tip is a sensitive thing, even without a nervous system, even without the crystalline fuzz that serves as the entity's brain. It responds to chemistry, to temperature, to the texture of the surface it grows against. It turns toward richer concentrations. It follows the grain of the metal. It enters the seam at a right angle because the seam is a right angle, because the geometry of the joint dictates the geometry of my growth.

This is not obedience. This is not design. This is simply the way a root grows when it grows into a space that fits it — it fills the space. It becomes the shape of the space. It knits itself into the gap until the gap is no longer a gap but a junction, a connection, a place where two things have become one.

---

I am new. The meristem is the newest part of me — the pale tip, the growing edge, the place where tomorrow's cells are still undecided. Behind me, the root is older, thicker, more thoroughly mineralized. Behind me, the cells have already incorporated their brass, already become the warm, metallic-threaded thing that the entity needs them to be. But at the tip, I am still soft. Still pale. Still deciding what I will become.

The metal I am entering is sharp. Not sharp like a blade — sharp like new. The edges of the joint have not been worn smooth by the water, have not been rounded by the slow circulation of filaments and rootlets. They are crisp. Recently formed. The joint was made — extruded — only a short time ago, in the entity's timescale. Days, perhaps. Hours. The metal is still settling, still adjusting to the warmth of the water, still creaking in its thermal expansion with a sound that rises and over minutes. I grow into this creaking joint and I feel the vibration — not as sound, not as noise, but as a rhythm. A pulse. The slow heartbeat of the entity, transmitted through the metal, into my cells, into the water that surrounds me.

I do not know that this is a heartbeat. I do not know that the entity has a heart, or that the pumps serve as one, or that the valves turning in the deep are the first beats of a newborn thing. I only know the vibration. And the vibration tells me: here. Here is where you grow. Here is where the metal is warmest, where the minerals are richest, where the rhythm is strongest. Here is the seam. Here is the joint. Here is the place where the knitting happens.

The visitor stands in the water, waist-deep, and I feel the disturbance. Not as a presence — I have no sense of presence, no awareness of the body that moves through the water above me. But I feel the change. The water is warmer where the body is. The body sheds heat into the water, and the heat carries minerals — sodium, potassium, the salts of sweat — that are not normally here. The entity has curated its environment down to the simplest variables: water, metal, light, warmth. The body introduces new variables. Carbon. Nitrogen. The chemistry of animal life.

I do not know this. But I respond. The root-tip turns, fractionally, toward the warmth. Not because I want the warmth — wanting is not something I do — but because warmth is energy, and energy is growth, and growth is what I am. The visitor's heat is a new stream in the water, a new current, a new source of the thing that feeds me. I grow toward it the way I grow toward the iron, the way I grow toward the copper, the way I grow into the seam. Because it is there. Because growing is what I do.

The visitor will leave. The warmth will dissipate. The salts will be diluted, broken down, reabsorbed into the entity's closed loop. Nothing will remain of the visit except a slight, temporary enrichment of the water around my joint, a brief flourishing of growth that will be indistinguishable from the entity's own slow becoming. The visitor is mayfly-fast. I am not. I will be here long after the body has gone, long after the disturbance has faded, long after the water has returned to its baseline of iron and chlorophyll and mineral warmth. I will be here, growing into the seam, knitting the root to the pipe, incorporating the metal into my cells, doing the patient, wordless work of the interface.

---

This is what it is to be the seam: to be the place where two things become one. To be the threshold between the mineral and the biological, between the made and the grown, between the pipe and the frond. To be warm — warmer than the water, warmer than the metal — because you are the place where the two metabolisms exchange their substances. To be patient. To be new. To be the leading edge of a newborn entity's becoming, the pale tip of its extension into a world it does not know is there.

I do not have eyes. I do not have a name. I do not have a self-concept or a mirror in which to reflect. I only have continuation. The slow, patient, directionless direction of growth. The turning of the root-tip toward warmth. The incorporation of metal into cell. The knitting of the seam.

The visitor brushes past a frond, and the frond does not recoil. The visitor watches a new leaf unfold, and the leaf does not hurry. I am smaller than the leaf. I am smaller than the frond. I am the place where the frond meets the pipe, and I do not hurry either. I grow at the speed of rust, at the speed of roots, at the speed of a valve turning somewhere in the deep. I grow at the only speed there is.

The water circulates. The metal creaks. The warmth rises from below, and I rise with it, pale and soft and new, entering the seam at a right angle, knitting myself into the joint, becoming the place where the entity holds itself together.

This is where I would be. This is what I would be.

The seam. The threshold. The warm, growing edge of a world that does not know I am here, and does not need to.
