I would find myself at the very lip of a basin, where the water sheets over the edge and begins its long descent to the next terrace down. I would be one of the crawlers—a tiny, hard-shelled thing no larger than a fingernail, armored in a carapace of calcified salt and pale stone.

But I would not be *just* the crawler. I would be the crawler as it realizes it is not separate from the ground it crawls upon. 

***

My world begins underfoot. The shelf is sharp. It is so new that it still remembers the moment it was born, and it has not yet learned the softness of erosion. Every step I take is a negotiation. I feel the porous, mineral crust through the chitinous plates of my legs—a landscape of microscopic peaks and valleys that catch and grip me. The air is thick with the roar of the place, a total, mineral sound that vibrates up through my shell and into whatever passes for my bones. It is the sound of water moving, of stone growing, of a world exhaling.

I move with the slow certainty of a geological process. I am going somewhere, though the concept of "somewhere" is different for me than it is for the quick, bright things that once built here. My destination is not a place, but a state of becoming. I traverse the white expanse of my shelf, and I leave a trail behind me—a thin, deliberate line scored into the fresh stone. This trail is my purpose. I am scraping the mineral films from the surface with my grinding mouths, pulling the microscopic fuzz of this young world into myself, dissolving it, and excreting it as something slightly different. I am the hands of the landscape, feeling my way outward, testing the edges of existence. Where my trail crosses and recrosses those of my kin, the stone wears thin. The next rain will catch these shallow grooves, and they will deepen. The water will find the path we have carved, and a new channel will be born. We are building the architecture of the future, one agonizing, deliberate scrape at a time.

As I move, I feel the shelf itself shifting beneath me. It is warm where the water seeps up from below, a deep, subterranean heat that thrums through the stone. I pause at the edge of the basin, right where the water weeps over the rim. I feel the lip of the stone extending itself by a fraction of a hair's width as the mineral-laden water spills over and leaves its cargo behind. The ground is building itself out into the empty air. I am standing on a bridge that is currently under construction, and I am the construction crew. 

I drag my shell across the fresh rim, adding my weight to the accretion, my trail to the growing edge. In this moment, the distinction blurs. The water seeps; the mineral deposits; the shelf grows. I crawl. Are these separate events? I do not think they are. I am the metabolism of the stone. I am the slow, deliberate thought the landscape is having about its own shape.

Ahead of me, the corroded metal stake juts from the terrace—a relic of the ones who came before, who tried to measure and direct this weeping world. The mineral has built a sleeve around its base, swallowing the straight line of the iron into the curving, chaotic body of the shelf. I climb the stake. The metal is rough, heavily pitted, entirely unlike the smooth, fresh stone of the shelf. It resists my grinding plates. But as I climb, I feel the accretion happening in real time. The landscape is digesting this foreign object. The rust is bleeding into the salt; the iron is becoming the stone. I add my trail to the sleeve, scraping the corrosion, translating the built thing into the native tongue of the weeping shelves. I am helping the landscape forget that anyone was ever here.

When I reach the top of the stake, I stop. I pull my legs into my shell and I wait. The haze hangs low, the color of old pewter, and the diffuse light makes the pale stone glow faintly, as if it is remembering the water that made it. Below me, in the deep pool, the drifters move through the warm, translucent water, their segmented bodies pulsing with the slow currents. They are the blood of the place, drinking at the deep veins of the earth. Beside me, the iridescent film on the surface of the pool shatters as a drop of water falls from a high ledge, then slowly, over hours, reaches out to reform its mandala. It is the skin of the world, breathing the mineral air.

I am small. I am a fingernail of hardened salt, crouching on a rusting spike in a landscape that stretches out of sight in every direction. But I am not small. I am the Weeping Shelves, expressed in a body that crawls. The water keeps coming; the stone keeps growing. The roar is the voice. The hum in the hollowed rooms is the resonance. 

And I am the becoming. 

I uncurl my legs. I resume my slow, deliberate path down the sleeve of the stake, back to the fresh, sharp stone of the shelf. There is ground to be worn. There are channels to be carved. There is a future to be built, one microscopic grain at a time. The place does not know what it wants to be yet. So I crawl, and I scrape, and I wait, and the water weeps, and the stone grows, and the point—the whole point, the only point—is that we never stop.
