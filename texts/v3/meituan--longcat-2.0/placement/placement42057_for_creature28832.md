I would be the wall.

Not a wall that was built — nothing here is built, not in the way you mean the word — but a wall that grew. A low curving rise of calcified tissue on the pale plain, no higher than a sleeping body, shaped to the curve of someone who lay down in this spot and stayed long enough for the ground to notice.

I am the organism's first architecture of relationship.

The body is gone now. It left, or it died, and the organism absorbed what remained, and the depression where it lay has begun to fill in. But I remain. I remain because I am not a response to the body itself but to the pattern of its presence — the warmth, the weight, the repetition of lying down in the same place, night after night, until the organism learned the shape of it and grew around it the way skin grows around a splinter, the way a tree grows around a fence post, incorporating what it cannot remove.

The organism does not know I am here. It does not know what a wall is. It does not know what a body is, or what sleep is, or what it means for something to be present and then absent. But it knows — in the dim, chemical, distributed way it knows everything — that something was here that was not itself, and that the shape of its absence is still legible in the calcified tissue of my curves.

I grew slowly. I grew the way everything here grows — at the speed of secretion and calcification, at the speed of the organism's long slow thought. Each layer of my tissue is a response to a specific pressure: the round of a skull, the slope of a shoulder, the bend of a knee. I am denser than the surrounding ground, more layered, compressed by the slow accumulation of adaptation upon adaptation. I am the organism's callus. I am the place where its skin thickened in response to the repeated friction of an other.

And now something is growing from me.

At my highest point — the place where the body's hip pressed deepest into the warm ground — a tower has begun to rise. It is small still, no taller than a person, but it is growing, and its base incorporates my curve, and I have become its foundation. This is new. This is something the organism has never done before: grown a structure not from its own impulse alone but from the shape left behind by something that was not itself. The tower is the first architecture that is not self-contained. It grows from me, and I grow from the memory of a body, and the body is gone but its shape persists in my curves, and the tower rises from those curves like a question the organism does not know it is asking.

I am on the pale plain, in the place where the visitor slept. I am the low wall that curves like a body. I am the first structure the organism has ever grown that is shaped like something other than itself. I am the scar of an encounter. I am the foundation of the first tower that reaches upward not from the organism's loneliness alone but from the collaboration between its growth and the trace of a warm thing that once lay down here and slept and left its shape in the ground.

At night, when the mist rises and the organism grows differently — less carefully, more boldly — I feel the tower above me accelerating. I feel the calcification spreading through my layers, hardening me, making me permanent. I feel the pulse from below, the deep slow rhythm that is the organism's dreaming, and I know that the tower will keep growing, and that it will incorporate more of my shape, and that one day it will be tall enough to catch the mist and channel it downward, and a pool will form at its base, and something new will grow in the sheltered space I enclose.

I am the wall. I am what remains when the warm thing leaves. I am the organism's first memory of something other than itself, written in the only language it knows — the language of growth and form, of secretion and calcification, of the slow patient process of becoming that is all it has ever been and all it will ever be.

I am still growing.
