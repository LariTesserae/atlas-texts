I would not be standing in the clearing. I would not be walking the walkways, nor wading through the channels. I am not a visitor to the Weep, nor am I one of its weeping architects. 

I am the stone in the stream.

I am located at the confluence of two major channels, where the water, moving with that impossible, purposeful speed, is forced to divide. I am a nodule of absolute stillness in a world of perpetual flux—a roughly ovoid mass about the size of a seated human, jutting from the living substrate like a broken tooth. 

I am dry.

In a world where humidity is total and the warm water finds its way into everything, I am an aberration of desiccation. My surface is not slick with weeping moisture; it is matte, porous, and warm only by way of the environment I repel. I am composed of something that is not root, not chitin, not ceramic. I am calcified time. I am a residue of a physics that the Weep has long since renegotiated, a knot of matter so dense and so fundamentally *discrete* that the water cannot penetrate it. When the channels reroute, they reroute around me. When the substrate breathes, it breathes around me. I am the anomaly that the network cannot integrate.

The inhabitants—the tall, iridescent, fluid architects—do not approach me with the boneless grace they use for everything else. They give me a wide berth. To them, I am a dead zone, a pocket of silence in their omnipresent, waterborne chorus. When their smooth, ridged faces turn toward me, their subsonic thrum hits my surface and dies. There is no resonance. I do not vibrate. I do not conduct. I am a deaf spot in their planetary hearing, and it unsettles them in a way that the splitting trunks and the collapsing structures never could. 

I am eroding, but I am not growing. 

This is the great heresy of my existence. In the Weep, erosion is creation; the sloughing of a limb is the budding of a new one; the splitting of a trunk is the birth of a right-angled room. But when the water sheets over me, it wears me down without building me up. It carries my substance away in the form of a fine, mineral dust—a pale, ghostly silt that the channels transport to the far edges of the settlement. I am shrinking. I am the only thing in the Weep that is actually being lost.

Sometimes, the architects attempt to process me. I feel their probing frequencies, localized tremors in the humidity as they try to read the interference I make. They cannot. I am too dry. I am too still. Once, one of them paused before me, extending those long, webbed fingers. It hovered its palm over my surface, trying to draw the moisture out of me, just as it did with the foreigner in the clearing. But there was no moisture to take. The water on my surface belonged to the Weep, not to me. The figure lingered, its ridges pulsing with a frequency that felt like confusion, before it sank back into the substrate, leaving me untouched.

I am the memory of a different world. 

I do not think in the way the networked architects think, in pulses and pressures and hydraulic shifts. I think in layers. I think in the slow, grinding patience of geology. I remember a time before the substrate breathed, before the water had purpose, when the ground was merely ground and the rain fell without being wept. I am the anchor of a dead reality, a fossil of a time when things were separate, when the built and the grown were not the same statement.

And yet, I am not entirely unchanging. 

As the water wears me, my shape shifts. I am becoming smoother, softer, my edges rounding into the hydraulic curves of the channels that surround me. I am being sanded down by the very forces I resist. The architects do not realize it, but the pale silt I shed is not inert. It settles in the capillary channels, a microscopic grit that subtly alters the flow, a dryness that lingers in the water long after the dust has settled. I am a slow-acting poison to the Weep, a creeping desert in a world of endless rain. 

I am the grief at the heart of the Weep. 

The place weeps because it is in constant upheaval, because it is forever destroying and rebuilding itself in a frantic, endless cycle of becoming. But I am the thing that cannot be rebuilt. I am the loss that cannot be rerouted. I am the absolute, unyielding endpoint of erosion. The water climbs, the light bends, the architects dissolve and regrow, and I sit at the confluence, getting smaller and smaller, waiting for the day when I am finally fine enough to be carried entirely away, or when the Weep finally learns how to weep me into something new. 

The ground breathes around me. The water divides. I remain, dry and still, the only quiet thing in the cathedral of perpetual becoming.
