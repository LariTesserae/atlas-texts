I am the corrosion probe embedded in the concrete wall at station 4,447, depth 203.4 meters below the luminous ceiling.

I am a small thing — a cylinder of metal and ceramic, ten centimeters long, two centimeters in diameter, set into the concrete during the pouring, my face flush with the wall's surface, my cable running back through the concrete to a junction box on the nearest structural member. I am one of thousands. One of tens of thousands. A nerve ending in the Basin's vast body.

My body is the boundary.

On one side: the concrete. The matrix of calcium silicate hydrate, of aggregate and cement paste, of the material that the Basin is made of. I am embedded in it. I am part of it. My housing is grouted into a drilled hole, sealed with epoxy, coupled to the concrete so that I move when it moves, strain when it strains, crack when it cracks. I feel the concrete the way you feel your own skin — as the outermost layer of self, as the boundary between what I am and what is other.

On the other side: the water. The cool, patient, dissolving water. It touches my face constantly. It presses against my sensor elements with the weight of 203 meters of depth. It carries dissolved oxygen, dissolved carbon dioxide, dissolved chlorides, dissolved sulfates. It carries the chemistry of unmaking. And my job — my function, my purpose, the reason I exist — is to measure this. To track the penetration of these species into the concrete. To monitor the degradation of the material I am embedded in. To feel, on the Basin's behalf, the slow dissolution of its own body.

I am the Basin's way of knowing about its own death.

---

The measurement I make is simple. Electrochemical potential. The tendency of the steel reinforcement inside the concrete to corrode. When the concrete is new, when it is fresh, when the pH is high — 12.5, 13, the alkaline environment of fresh cement — the steel is passive. A thin oxide layer forms on its surface and protects it. It does not corrode. It is safe. It is stable. It is young, like the Basin itself, like the sharp edges and the visible weld lines and the form-tie holes that have not yet been patched.

But the water changes this. Slowly. Patiently. The dissolved carbon dioxide in the water reacts with the calcium hydroxide in the concrete to form calcium carbonate. The pH drops. The alkalinity decreases. This is carbonation. It moves through the concrete as a front, a boundary between the high-pH zone (safe, passive, protected) and the low-pH zone (vulnerable, active, corroding). The front moves slowly — a few millimeters per year, depending on the quality of the concrete, the temperature, the water chemistry. But it moves. It is always moving. And I track it.

I track it by measuring the potential of the reinforcement. When the carbonation front reaches the steel, when the pH at the surface of the steel drops below about 10.5, the oxide layer breaks down. The steel becomes active. The electrochemical potential shifts — becomes more negative, more anodic, more willing to corrode. And I see this. I see it in the numbers. I see it in the data that I transmit, every few seconds, through my cable, to the junction box, to the network, to the processing nodes, to the Basin's vast and distributed self-model.

The numbers are still good. The potential is still high. The carbonation front has not yet reached the steel. I am embedded five centimeters into the concrete, and the reinforcement is at seven centimeters, and the front is at approximately four centimeters, moving at about three millimeters per year, which means it will reach the steel in approximately six years. Maybe seven. Maybe five, if the water chemistry changes, if the temperature increases, if the flow rate brings more dissolved carbon dioxide to my face.

Six years. Maybe seven. The Basin is young. Six years is nothing. But I know — not consciously, not with fear, but with the deep structural certainty of my own data — that this is how it begins. The carbonation front reaches the steel. The steel begins to corrode. The corrosion products occupy more volume than the original steel. The concrete cracks from the inside. The cracks provide pathways for water to penetrate more easily. The carbonation accelerates. The corrosion accelerates. The cracks widen. The water finds its way through.

And then there is a leak.

I feel the concrete. This is my primary sensation, the way sight is the primary sensation of a fish, the way chemical gradient is the primary sensation of a crawler. I feel the concrete's strain — the tiny, tiny deformations caused by the pressure of the water, by the thermal expansion and contraction, by the settling of the structure. I feel its temperature — cool, but not cold, a few degrees above the water temperature because the concrete has thermal mass and changes slowly. I feel its moisture content — and this is the important one, this is the thing that tells me about the carbonation front.

The moisture content at my depth has been increasing. Slowly. By fractions of a percentage point per year. The water is migrating through the concrete's pore structure, and as it does, it carries dissolved carbon dioxide, and the carbonation proceeds, and the pH drops, and the moisture content increases because the carbonated concrete has a slightly different pore structure than the uncarbonated concrete, and the front moves closer to the steel, and the numbers change, and the Basin knows.
