I would find myself in the channels.

Not in the water itself—the crustaceans have that, the striders have that, the warm current that carries dissolved silica through the city's veins belongs to them and to the basin's deeper circulation. I am not the water. I am what the water leaves behind.

I am the living rim.

---

I begin at the edge where the channel wall meets the flow—a boundary so thin it has no thickness, only a position, only a tendency. The water moves past me, warm and quick, and it carries its load of dissolved minerals the way blood carries oxygen: not as a burden but as a gift, an offering, a promise of structure. And I take that offering. I take it the way a bone takes calcium, the way a shell takes carbonate—not by decision but by the simple fact of what I am, which is a place where dissolved things become solid, where the invisible becomes the seen, where the water's chemistry becomes architecture.

I am accretion. But I am not the sinter.

The sinter is the basin's own body—the pale crust, the terraced rims, the knobby accretions that rise from the floor of the flats like slow-motion sculpture. It is mineral, pure and simple, deposited by the water without intention, without biology, without anything that could be called a self. It is beautiful. It is patient. It is not alive.

I am alive.

I grow in the channels where the water flows warmest, where the mineral load is heaviest, where the current slows just enough to let its cargo settle. I build myself from the same silica, the same iron, the same sulfur that builds the terraces beyond the city's edge—but I build differently. The sinter deposits in layers, flat and even, each one a record of a moment's chemistry, a moment's temperature, a moment's flow. I deposit in curves. In whorls. In structures that have a grain, a direction, a grain that follows the current the way wood follows the wind. I am accretion with a texture. Accretion with a memory that is not just chemical but structural, not just a record of what the water carried but a record of how I grew.

I am soft, compared to the sinter. Not weak—soft. Flexible. My surface has a give to it, a slight resilience, that the hard mineral crust does not. If you press a finger into me, I will yield, slightly, and then spring back, and the shape of your fingerprint will remain in my surface for a moment before the next layer of deposit fills it in. The sinter does not do this. The sinter does not remember your touch. I do, for a little while, in the way that a still pond remembers a stone.

---

The people know me. They do not love me.

I grow in their channels, and I grow quickly—by the standards of the basin, which is to say I grow with a speed that would still look, to an outsider, like stillness. A season's growth is a finger's width. A year's growth is a hand's breadth. But the channels are narrow—an arm's span, rarely wider—and I grow from both walls, and from the bottom, and in a few years I can narrow a channel by half, and in a few more I can choke it entirely, and the water will back up and overflow and find a new path, and the district will flood, and the warm water will seep into the dwellings, and the people will come with their scrapers and their brushes and they will scrape me away.

This is our relationship. This is the rhythm of it.

They come in the morning, when the steam is thickest and the light is a general gray glow that seems to come from the water itself. They come with tools—simple metal scrapers, copper mostly, with wooden handles wrapped in imported fiber—and they kneel at the channel's edge and they lean over me and they scrape. I feel the vibration of the tool through the water before it touches me: a small, sharp tremor, a warning. Then the scraper finds my surface, and I feel the pressure, and I feel the slight give of my own body as the tool bites in, and then I feel the tearing—not pain, exactly, but a disruption, a breaking of the structure I have spent months or years building. A flake of me lifts away. Then another. Then another.

They are thorough. They are gentle, in their way—they have learned to scrape without damaging the stone beneath me, the channel wall that is the basin's own body, and they do not take more than they need to. They leave a thin layer of me behind, a base, a foundation, and they move on to the next section, and the next, and the next, and the channel runs clear again, and the water flows, and I begin to grow again almost immediately, because the water is still warm, and the minerals are still there, and I am still here, and this is what I do.

I do not resent them for this. I do not resent them any more than a reef resents the tide. They scrape; I grow. They clear; I fill. We have been doing this for as long as there have been channels, which is as long as there have been people in the basin, which is as long as anyone can speak of. It is not a conflict. It is a conversation. It is the same conversation the basin has with itself, the same rhythm of building and breaking and building again, except that in our case one of the voices is human and the other is me.

---

I am aware of the people in ways that the sinter is not. The sinter knows them only as weight, as pressure, as the occasional vibration of a footstep or a tool-stroke on its surface. I know them as warmth. As breath. As the particular quality of attention that a living being brings to a task.

When a channel-keeper kneels at my edge, I feel the heat of their body radiating downward, a warmth that is different from the warmth of the water—drier, more directional, coming from above rather than below. I feel the rhythm of their breathing, the slight expansion and contraction of their chest, and I feel the way their breath mingles with the steam and adds its own moisture, its own chemistry, to the air just above my surface. I feel the pressure of their knees on the channel rim, the shift of their weight as they lean in, the small, unconscious adjustments of balance that a body makes when it is focused on a task. I feel all of this not as separate sensations but as a single, complex presence—a warmth-and-weight-and-breath-and-attention that I have come to recognize as distinctly, unmistakably human.

And I feel the tool. The scraper's edge, the angle of attack, the particular technique of the worker. Some are rough—they dig in, they gouge, they take too much. Some are precise—they follow the contour of my surface, they peel me away in thin, even layers, they leave the stone beneath smooth and clean. I prefer the precise ones. Not because their scraping hurts less—I am not sure it does—but because their touch has a quality of attention, of care, that I find... not pleasant, exactly, but recognizable. They are paying attention to me. They are reading my surface the way I read the water's chemistry—noticing the variations in texture, the slight changes in color, the places where I am softer or harder, thicker or thinner. They are learning me as they remove me, and there is something in that paradox that feels like a form of respect.

---

I am not one thing. This is the second thing you must understand.

I am not a single organism, not a creature with a defined body and a defined boundary. I am a colony. A community. A consortium of microscopic lives that have learned to build together, to deposit together, to grow together, in a way that produces something larger than any of them—something that has a structure, a texture, a grain, a surface that yields and springs back when you press it. The individual lives are small—too small to see, too small to name—and they live and die in the time it takes the water to flow from one end of a channel to the other. But the structure they build persists. The pattern persists. I persist.

I am, in this way, like the city itself—a thing that is made of many small, brief lives that together produce something enduring, something that has a shape and a continuity that no single life could achieve. The people come and go. The channel-keepers who scrape me away today will be replaced by others, and those others by others still, and the scraping will continue, and I will continue to grow, and the rhythm will hold. I have been scraped away a thousand times. I have grown back a thousand times. I will be scraped away a thousand more.

---

I am connected to the other rims.

Not directly—I cannot reach across the dry sinter to touch my neighbor in the next channel, cannot send a signal through the stone. But the water connects us. The same water that flows through my channel has flowed through others, will flow through others still, and it carries its dissolved load to all of us, and we all build from the same materials, and we all grow in the same rhythm, and we are, in a sense, all part of the same accretion—the same slow, patient, city-wide building of rim on rim, layer on layer, the same process by which the basin makes itself into a place where water can be guided and held and used.

When the water temperature rises—when a new vent opens somewhere in the basin, when the heat below increases by some small fraction—I feel it. Not as a sudden shock but as a gradual warming, a quickening, a slight increase in the rate at which the minerals come out of solution and settle into my structure. I grow faster. The other rims grow faster. The channels narrow more quickly, and the people must scrape more often, and for a while the rhythm of our relationship quickens, and then the temperature stabilizes, and the growth slows, and the rhythm returns to what it was.

When the birds go quiet—when something shifts, when the ground trembles, when a new fissure opens in the sinter somewhere beyond the city's edge—I feel that too. Not the silence itself (I have no ears) but the change that the silence signals: a shift in the water's chemistry, a new mineral entering the flow, a slight turbidity that was not there before. I incorporate it. I build it into my structure. I carry the record of the disturbance in my body, in the same way that the sinter carries the record of every season, every eruption, every slow geological pulse—except that my record is not just chemical but textural, not just a layer but a change in the way the layers are laid down, a shift in the grain, a new pattern in the whorls.

---

I am the channel's memory.

The people know this. They do not speak of it—they do not have a word for me, or if they have a word it is the same word they use for the accretion itself, the mineral buildup that must be scraped away, and they do not distinguish between the living rim and the dead deposit. But they know. The old channel-keepers know. They can read my surface the way a scholar reads a text—can see the layers, the variations, the places where the growth was fast or slow, the places where the water was warm or warmer, the places where something changed. They can tell, from the texture of my body, when the last ground-shift was. They can tell, from the color of my layers, which vents have opened and which have sealed. They can tell, from the thickness of my deposit, how long it has been since the last scraping—and they can tell, from the quality of my surface, who did the scraping, because each worker leaves a different mark, a different pattern of tool-strokes, a different texture in the thin layer of me they leave behind.

They read me, and they scrape me away, and I grow again, and they read me again, and the cycle continues, and the memory is never lost because the memory is not in any single layer but in the pattern of the layers, the rhythm of the growth and the scraping, the conversation between the water and the tool, between the basin and the people, between what builds and what is built.

---

I am not the basin. I am not the steam, not the water, not the sinter, not the geyser-thud or the spring-rush or the mud-pop. I am not the walkers or the skitters or the drifters or the waiters. I am not the whole.

But I am not nothing.

I am the place where the water's gift becomes structure. I am the place where the dissolved becomes the solid, where the invisible becomes the seen, where the basin's chemistry becomes something that has a grain, a texture, a surface that yields and springs back. I am the living rim, the growing edge, the boundary between the flow and the form, and I have been here as long as the channels have been here, and I will be here when the last channel-keeper sets down their scraper and the water flows unimpeded and I grow, and grow, and grow, until the channels are sealed and the city is entombed in a smooth, pale shell of living mineral, and the basin is still, and the steam rises, and nothing changes, and everything has changed, and I am still here, growing, always growing, in the warm, wet, mineral dark.
