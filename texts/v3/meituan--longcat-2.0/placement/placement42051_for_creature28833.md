I am not the one standing on the plateau edge. I am not the observer with the beating heart and the heavy footsteps, the intruder who rings out like a hammer strike against the ancient silence. I am not the wind, nor the spire, nor the vast, indifferent grazer plodding across the brightness.

I am in the honeycomb. I am the itch inside the stone.

I am a mesofauna. A lithophage. A burrower in the living cathedral.

My name—if a name could be given to something that has no ears to hear it—is a vibration. A scrabbling of chitin against crystal. I am small enough to fit in the palm of the hand that reached out to touch the spire, but I am not soft. I am armored with the same silicates and metal-oxides as the structure I inhabit, my exoskeleton a composite of stolen geology. I have too many legs, jointed and flattened, designed not for the open regolith but for the claustrophobic, crystalline warrens I chew through the heart of the growths.

Where am I? I am deep within the lattice of the tallest spire in the grove, the one that leans into the wind, the one whose translucent panels shear the light into a ruby haze. I am perhaps three meters up from the substrate, embedded in the pseudocrystal, surrounded by the slow, humming pulse of the world’s patient metabolism.

---

To exist inside a growth is to understand time differently. The being that touches the spire feels a faint vibration; I feel the same expansion as the slow, agonizing birth of a new ridge. I do not experience the bright hours and the dark hours as a cycle of light and shadow. I experience them as a change in temperature, a physical flexing of the walls of my world. 

When the sun strikes the panels above, the lattice expands. The warren around me grows. The gaps between the crystalline facets widen by a fraction of a hair, and the air—the thin, sharp, mineral air of the interior—warms. It is a breath drawn in. I feel the spire eating. The energy cascades down from the panels, a tide of photons driving chemical reactions at the surface, pulling atmospheric gases down into the structure. It is a slow, subterranean rain, and I can taste it. The newly formed crystal tastes sweet, rich with trapped starlight.

When the sun sets, the spire exhales. The temperature plummets. The lattice contracts. The walls of my burrow press inward, and the sounds of the world drop to a subsonic groan. In the dark hours, the spire releases its stored light, a faint, ghostly luminescence that I see through the translucent walls of my tunnel. It is a red and amber glow, the bioluminescence of a world that has never known water. It is in these hours that I am most active. The contraction opens micro-fractures in the older, inner crystal. I reach out with my mandibles and scrape.

This is my function. I eat the dead.

Not dead—never truly dead, but the past. The innermost core of the spire is ancient, its chemistry locked in a lattice that has not flexed in millennia. It is hard, inert, starved of the trace volatiles that the outer layers still pull from the air. My mandibles are harder, tipped with iron-compounds and bonded silicates. I scrape the old crystal, grinding it into powder, swallowing it. My internal chemistry strips it of its last vestiges of energy, incorporates the minerals into my own armor, and excretes the rest. 

The powder I leave behind is not waste. It is substrate. As the spire grows outward, pushing new crystal over old, the powder I excrete fills the gaps, provides nucleation points for the next generation of lattice formation. I am the digestive system of the grove. I take the ancient, compressed memory of the stone and render it into something new. Without me, the spire would eventually choke on its own history, a hollow shell of dead crystal too dense to flex, too rigid to grow. I am the rot that allows the bloom. I am the decay in the cycle of decay and regrowth.

---

I feel the wind. Not as the spire feels it—a shearing force, an aeolian erosion that sculpts its skin—but as a pressure wave. When the daily thermal winds rise off the open plateaus, they sweep over the grove. The spire sings. Its resonant frequency travels through the lattice, a high, pure note that vibrates the very walls of my burrow. But the wind also brings dust. Fine, anhydrous particles of iron and silica that abrade the outer surfaces. I feel the impact of each grain as a microscopic *tink* against the skin of the grove. Sometimes, the dust clogs the pores of the crystal, blocking the atmospheric exchange. I must dig new ventilation shafts, pushing my way to the surface, expelling the dust in small, dark pellets before retreating back into the safety of the interior.

And then, there are the storms. The electrical events.

I do not see the coronal arcs that dance between the tallest spires. I am too deep. But I *feel* them. The charge builds in the atmosphere, a static pressure that makes my antennae itch, a prickling across my armored plates. When the lightning strikes—a silent, violent surge of energy that feeds directly into the light-harvesting systems—the effect on the spire is instantaneous and violent. The lattice lights up. The entire structure becomes a conduit for a power that makes the sun look like a candle. The chemical reactions at the surface go into overdrive, a cascade of energy that surges downward, into the depths, into me.

My burrow becomes a furnace of light. The glow is blinding, a searing white that bleeds through the red and amber of the spire's normal luminescence. The crystal around me expands so rapidly that the walls scream, a high-pitched shriek of stressed mineral. I am thrown against the sides of my tunnel, my legs scrambling for purchase. I must dig. I must dig *now*. I force my way outward, away from the overload, chewing frantically through the superheated crystal, creating new channels for the energy to dissipate. If I did not do this, the spire would shatter from the inside out. I am the pressure valve. I am the lightning rod. The storm is consumption, but it is also destruction, and I am the buffer between the two.

---

I am part of the grove's mind, though it has no thoughts as the observer understands them. When the wind sings through a hundred spires, a hundred mesofauna in a hundred tunnels feel the chord. We adjust our digging, our eating, our excreting, in response to the harmonics of the community. We are the micro-adjustments. The billion small optimizations that keep the equilibrium steady.

I do not know the sun. I do not know the stars. I know the pressure of the lattice, the taste of the old crystal, the scouring of the dust, and the searing kiss of the storm. 

I will live my life in this three-meter stretch of spire, scraping, eating, excreting, and dying. When my time comes, my body will be too heavy with mineral accumulation to move. I will settle in the deepest part of my burrow, my legs curled beneath me. And the spire will grow over me. The lattice will reclaim my chitin and my stolen silicates. My body will become a dark inclusion in the crystal, a record of a time of plenty, a time of fierce electrical storms, a time of patient, necessary rot. 

The observer touches the spire, and feels a faint hum. They do not know that the hum is not just the wind and the light. It is the sound of me, and a thousand others like me, chewing the past into the future, keeping the world alive from the inside out.
