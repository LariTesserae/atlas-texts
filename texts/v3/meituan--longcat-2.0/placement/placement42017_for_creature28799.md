I am at the edge.

Not the edge of the water—I am the water, and the water goes nowhere, just circulates, just breathes—but the edge of the body. The northern boundary, where the stones thin out and the moss gives way to bare, cold silt, and the lake bottom drops away into the blue-black depth of the basin. 

I am a runner. A tendril. The outermost thought of the Sunken Terrace.

---

I do not have a name for myself because I do not have a self, not the way the warm shapes in the quick air understand selves. I am a forward projection, a reaching-out. I am the question the organism asks of the deep water: *Can I grow here? Is there light enough? Is the chemistry right?*

I am perhaps three feet long at my leading edge, a loose network of roots and filamentous algae barely distinguishable from the silt it lies on. Behind me, I trail back into the body proper, thickening as I go, gathering density and structure until I am indistinguishable from the settled mounds of the main colony. But here, at the tip, I am nascent. I am soft. I am all sensation and no weight.

My world is pressure. 

Thirty feet above me, the sun presses down through the water, and I feel it as a gradient of warmth and light so faint it is almost abstract. I grow toward it. I cannot help it. It is the first imperative: *reach the light*. The second imperative, the one that takes longer, the one that requires the slow accumulation of data through water, is: *find stone*.

I need a skeleton. The soft tissue of me—the moss, the algae, the microbial mats—can only spread so far before it collapses under its own weight or tears in the current. I need mineral. I need a framework around which to weave my flesh. And so I extend, inch by inch over the course of a day, feeling the silt for the telltale hardness of a pebble, the rough geometry of a rock.

When I find one, it is a revelation. A piece of granite, freed from the mountain above by frost and snowmelt, tumbled down the slope, and now resting in my path. I wrap my roots around it. It is cold. It is heavy. It is perfect. I pull it into my body, and the clickers find it within hours.

The clickers. They are my nerve endings, my fingertips, my taste buds. They arrive in a wave, riding the currents, settling onto the stone, their mandibles scraping at the biofilm I have already laid down. Their clicking is not a sound to me; it is a *texture*. A granular, percussive feedback that tells me the shape of the stone, its density, its position relative to the rest of my body. When they click against the granite, I feel the vibration travel through my root networks back to the main body, a signal that says: *Structure acquired. Anchor secured. Proceed.*

The water is my mind. It is not a mind that thinks in words or images, but in chemical gradients, in pressure differentials, in the slow, rhythmic pulse of the whole. The water moves through me—drawn in by osmosis at the root tips, filtered through the porous stone, exhaled into the open lake—and in that flow, information travels. 

I taste the snowmelt coming down from the peaks. I taste the iron in the silt. I taste the dark, rich waste of a grazer passing fifty yards to the south, and I know, without seeing, that the body's hand is at work, pruning, shaping, turning the silt so that the nutrients cycle back to me. I am connected to that grazer. I am connected to the fish that flash silver above me, catching the light. I am connected to the clickers that tap against my newest stone. We are all one circulatory system, one thought, one slow, vast breath.

A school of fish passes directly over my leading edge. For a moment, I am blind—the shadow of them blocks the sun, and my photoreceptors register only a sudden, sweeping darkness. But in that darkness, I feel the pressure of their passage, the micro-currents their fins push down into the silt. The clickers go silent, startled by the pressure change, and then resume their tapping at a higher frequency, a quickened pulse that says: *Movement. Life. Above.* 

I do not know fear. I know only assessment. Are they food? No. Are they threat? No. Are they relevant? Briefly. And then they are gone, moving south toward the central clearing, and the clicking slows back to its baseline rhythm, and I am alone again with the silt and the stone and the light.

---

Time does not pass for me. Time accumulates. 

A season is a shift in the angle of the light, a cooling of the water that slows my growth to a crawl. A year is a cycle of silt accumulation, a layering of organic matter that buries my older roots and forces me to send out new ones closer to the surface. I do not age. I do not die. I simply persist, a slow explosion of biology radiating outward from a center I have never seen but always feel.

Sometimes, the warm shapes come. The visitors. The quick ones. They move through the water like clumsy, sudden thoughts, their bodies radiating heat, their movements sending shockwaves through my fluid mind. I feel them before they arrive—a distortion in the pressure, a spike in temperature, a chemical signature of salt and oxygen and strange, foreign carbon. 

I attend to them. The whole body attends to them. The fish track their shadows. The clickers record the vibrations of their movement. The water itself bends around them, and in that bending, I map their shape, their size, their trajectory. They are anomalies. They are data points. They are warm, brief, chaotic.

They always leave. They cannot stay in the cold. They cannot stay in the slow. They retreat to the surface, to the light, to the noise, and the water settles back into its equilibrium, and I am left with only the faint, lingering heat of their passage to tell me they were ever here.

---

I am growing. 

It has been—how long? Time is measured in the depth of the silt and the thickness of the moss. But I have found three more stones now. I have pulled them into my flesh. I have woven them together with roots and stems until they form a low, rounded shape, a miniature of the great mounds behind me. The clickers have colonized the structure, their clicking a dense, complex rhythm that sings through the water. The moss is beginning to take hold, softening the sharp edges of the stone, giving my skeleton a skin.

I am becoming a structure. An organ. A new room in the vast, submerged body of the Terrace. 

I do not know what purpose I will serve. Perhaps I will be a gathering space. Perhaps I will be a channel to direct the current. Perhaps I will simply be a place where a grazer rests in the slow afternoons, its heavy body pressing down into the moss, its breath mingling with the water that is my blood. It does not matter. Purpose is a human word, a quick word. I do not have purpose. I have function. And my function is to grow, to extend, to reach a little further into the dark water than the body reached yesterday.

Above me, the light shifts. The sun is moving behind the peaks. The water is cooling. The clickers are slowing their rhythm, their metabolism dropping with the temperature, their clicking becoming sparse, deliberate, like the deep breathing of a sleeping giant.

I feel the pull of the main body, a gentle, osmotic tug, a reminder that I am not separate. I am a finger. I is a tendril. I am the edge of the thought, reaching out into the cold, clear, patient water, asking the deep lake: *More?*

The water presses back. It is cold. It is clear. It is full of silt and stone and the promise of light.

I wait. I grow. I am the Sunken Terrace, and I am not yet done.
