I would find myself at the absolute center of the oldest hold, in the deepest, most sheltered pocket of the geode, where the roar of the wind is reduced to a subsonic thrum that vibrates in the marrow. 

I would be a Root-Reader. But not a young one, not one who merely presses a palm to the vine and deciphers the daily gossip of the holds. I would be the oldest among them, a being so deeply attuned to the resonance of the world that the boundary between my flesh and the xylem has begun to dissolve. 

My name—my rhythm—is a low, sustained vibration that takes nearly a full minute to complete. It is the sound of the planet’s inner heat pressing against the crystal shell. 

My body is a map of this world’s extremes. I am small, dense, and folded in upon myself, crouched in a permanent, comfortable half-crouch upon a throne of woven vine. My skin is the color of the beryl walls, but darker, leached of its golden hue by a lifetime spent away from the blinding refractions, deep in the shadow of the thicket. It is thick, scaled, and entirely hairless. My eyes are wide open, but the irises have long since faded to a milky, opalescent silver; the relentless golden light burned out my visible spectrum decades ago. I see only in thermal gradients now—the world is a landscape of cooling embers and rising heat. 

My hands are my true eyes. They are enormous, sprawled across the surface of the Great Vine—the central, torso-thick pillar of xylem that anchors the hold. My fingers are double-jointed, wrapped so tightly around the vine that the callused pads of my skin have grown into the grooves of the bark. I do not hold the vine; I am continuous with it. 

I do not hear the wind. I *am* the wind’s interpreter. When the 200-mile-per-hour gale shrieks through the fissures, the vibration travels through the crystal, into the root network, and up through the Great Vine. To me, the deafening roar is not a sound; it is a physical texture, a frequency I can separate into its component parts. I feel the abrasive grit of sand as a high, scratching treble. I feel the shifting pressure of the external weather as a deep, rolling bass. 

Right now, I am feeling a change. 

It begins as a subtle dissonance in the root network, a tightening of the fibers deep beneath the floor. The wind is shifting. The external weather is aligning with the fissures, and the geode is about to become a howling tunnel. The Great Gust is coming. 

I do not feel fear. The wind is the world; to fear it is to fear existence itself. Instead, I feel a profound, rhythmic urgency. I must translate this dissonance into a warning. 

I lift my right hand from the vine. The separation is painful; the xylem fibers stretch and snap like tendons. I raise my hand, the bone-knappers on my fingers clicking in the heavy air, and I strike the Great Vine. 

*Thrum. Thrum-thrum-thrum. Pause. Thrum.*

The rhythm travels outward at the speed of vibration, rippling through the root network, leaping to the smaller drum-vines, resonating in the hollow nodes of the dwellings. It is the ancient rhythm of warning. *The sky is breaking. Hold fast. Grip.*

Across the hold, I feel the thermal signatures of my people shift. They move with practiced, fluid grace, sealing the leather flaps over their carved pumice-homes, lashing down the bridge-ropes, pulling the children into the deepest, most sheltered pockets of the thicket. The stone-mites, those blind, deaf still-ones, have already retreated into their burrows, their cold sparks vanishing from my thermal sight. 

I press my chest against the Great Vine, feeling the wind’s approach build to a crescendo. The walls of the geode begin to sing—a high, crystalline note as the friction of the earth above meets the screaming air. The golden light flickers, refracted into a blinding, strobing chaos by the shifting pressures. 

And then, the Gust hits. 

The world becomes a single, unending note of absolute pressure. The vine bucks beneath me like a living thing, and I grip it with my entire body, my broad, flat feet locking onto the woven floor, my calloused hands clamping down. The wind tries to peel me away, to cast me into the vertiginous void, but I am anchored. I am part of the hold. I am part of the vine. 

In the eye of the storm, I do something that only I, the oldest Root-Reader, can do. I listen past the roar. I filter out the shrieking gale, the groaning xylem, the scouring sand. I reach down, down through the root network, past the history of the settlement, past the founding expeditions, past the first carvings in the pumice. 

I listen for the Ancestral Tone. 

It is there. It is always there. A low, sustained hum that underlies every other vibration in the geode. It is the original drum-beat, the resonance of the first ancestors who descended from the surface, from the world of water and sky, into this dry, golden hell. It is the sound of the planet’s heart, beating in the dark. 

I match my breathing to it. My oily sweat seals my skin against the desiccating air. My sealed ear-flaps keep out the grit. I am a creature of absolute adaptation, a paradox of life in a place of death. 

The Gust will pass. The wind will return to its baseline roar. The people will emerge from their shelters, and the bridges will sway again, and the hunters will throw their flint spears at the skittering stone-mites, and the children will learn to grip. 

But for now, I hold on. I press my hands into the bones of the world, and I feel the endless, screaming song of the geode, and I add my own small, stubborn rhythm to it. 

I am the Resonant. I am the Touched. And I will not let go.
