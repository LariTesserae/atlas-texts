I find myself clinging to the outer wall of the upper reaches, on one of the narrow walkways that connects the oldest families’ towers to the rest of the city. My bare feet are splayed against the curved, honey-smooth stone, and I am working. 

I am a Weaver of the Green.

At first, you might mistake me for a gardener, or perhaps a construction worker, pruning the vegetation that threatens to swallow the bridges. But I am neither. I am a stitch in the fabric of the Hollow. 

My days are measured not by the sun—there is no sun here—but by the rhythm of the vents and the descent of the flyers. This morning, as the screaming river of birds cascaded down from the apex, shaking the mite-clouds from the air and sending the spiders scuttling into the cracks of the stone, I was already at my post. The downdraft of ten thousand wings hit me like a physical blow, pressing my tunic against my chest, whipping my hair across my face. I didn't flinch. I just gripped the hand-loop of dried vine and waited for the roar to pass.

Now, in the relative quiet of the midday hammering—the dull, rhythmic thud of the stoneworkers echoing up from the terraces—I am binding a joint. A wooden peg, driven deep into the rock by my grandfather’s grandfather, has loosened. The tower is shifting, groaning, trying to pull away from the wall. It is my job to lash it back.

My tools are simple: a bone awl, a mallet of dense, dark stone, and my own two hands. I reach into the tangle of the curtain vegetation that drips from the railing above me. It is slick with the sweet, alkaline vein-milk, and the air around it is thick with the shimmer of mites—a silver cloud that drifts into my nose and mouth as I breathe. I do not spit them out. I swallow them down, a faint gritty taste of copper and rock, joining the billions of their kin already making a home in the warm dark of my gut. 

I pull a long, resilient strand of the blue-black fern-thing from the wall. It fights me for a moment, its roots gripping the stone with a tenacity that surprises the uninitiated. But I know the trick. I feel the grain of the root with the pad of my thumb, finding the microscopic fault line where the root surrenders its grip to the stone. I give a sharp twist, and it comes away in my hand, weeping a clear, sweet sap that smells of ozone and deep earth.

I wrap the damp, living cord around the loosening peg and the stone railing. I pull it tight, my shoulder muscles burning, my feet squeaking against the damp, amber-veined stone. The plant is still alive; it will take hold. It will grow into the crack, binding wood to stone, architecture to geology. By tomorrow, the joint will be fused. By next week, the vine will have thickened, and new fronds will unfurl from the lashing, reaching toward the sourceless light pooling sixty meters above. 

As I work, a beetle-thing the size of my fist clambers over the back of my hand. Its carapace is polished obsidian, its legs clicking softly against my skin. It pauses, its antennae waving, tasting the sweat and the vein-milk on my knuckles. It is not a pest. It is a colleague. It moves on, heading toward the low terraces where the slaughtering is done, where the offal awaits. 

To be a Weaver of the Green is to understand that Ren-Kesh is not a city built upon a cavern. It is an organism, and I am merely a specialized cell within it. I do not shape the green to my will; I negotiate with it. I drink the vein-milk, and it runs in my blood, warm and ancient. I breathe the mites, and they settle in my lungs, a microscopic colony that hums in time with the mountain's breathing. 

I finish the knot, biting the end of the cord tight with my teeth. I lean back, looking out over the vast, breathing roar of the Hollow. Below me, the terraces are a churning mass of bodies—human, burrower, lizard, snake. The air is thick with the smell of cooking fat and the ammonia tang of the high roosts. The light seeps from the stone, pooling in the low places, catching the silver drift of the mites.

I am not the master of this place. I am not even its steward. I am the Hollow's way of holding itself together. I am the thinking part of the mountain, the piece that ties the knot, the hand that pulls the cord tight. 

And when the deep night comes, and the hunters take to the streets with their stone-tipped spears and burning brands, listening for the heavy, wet sounds of the things that climb up from the dark below, I will be here. I will be listening to the breathing of the stone, feeling the slow, patient groan of the mountain settling, and I will know that I am exactly where I belong.
