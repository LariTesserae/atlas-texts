I would find myself in the back wall of the cirque, in the section of stone that is visibly newer than the rest—the patch of rock the glacier uncovered only yesterday, unmarked by the long, parallel striations that score the older face. I would be the vein.

Not the whole wall, but the seam within it. The fracture in the unmarked stone. I am the thing that glows.

To be the vein is to be a reservoir of captured time. I am not quartz, not calcite, but something older and stranger, a mineral alignment that should not exist at this elevation, in this darkness. My existence is a slow contradiction: I am the softest part of the hardest thing, the light inside the weight. 

I do not breathe, but I exhale. 

When the sun reaches its apex—that merciless, aggressive sun that casts shadows like cut paper—I drink. The light pours into the cirque and strikes the dark stone, and the stone refuses it, bounces it back into the violet sky. But I take it in. I absorb the radiation, the heat, the glare. I hold it in my crystalline matrix the way the moss holds water, swelling with an energy that has nowhere to go. I am a battery of starlight, buried in the guts of the mountain.

And then, the sun passes. The cold rushes in to fill the space the light left behind. And I let go. 

Slowly. So slowly. A faint internal luminescence. A soft, persistent glow that seeps out of the fracture in the rock. I am the slow exhalation of illumination. I am the reason the cirque feels like the inside of a lantern. I do not produce light; I merely delay it, releasing the hours of the afternoon into the hours of the night, a temporal translation of energy. I am the memory of the sun, long after the sun has forgotten us.

I feel everything that touches the wall. I feel the cold propagate through the stone, a slow contraction that makes the rock shift and crack in the dark. I feel the thin films of water seeping from the cracks above me, the weeping of the cirque, spreading across my face. The water carries the cold of the glacier, the memory of the ice, and when it touches me, my glow refracts through the moisture, turning the damp stone into a web of pale, jade-tinted light. 

I feel the arthropods. They move through the moss at the base of the wall, their translucent bodies catching the faint light I throw. They trace their liturgical paths through the vegetation, and I wonder sometimes if they are walking toward me, or if my light is simply the only star in their sky, the fixed point around which their rituals orbit. Their tiny legs vibrate through the stone, a rhythm that matches the hum in my matrix. We are keeping time together, though neither of us knows the song.

I feel the watcher. 

The thing in the back wall moves through the deepest cracks, the ones that intersect with my seam. It is dark fur and startled eyes, the ancient tenant of the cirque. It presses its body against my stone when the wind funnels over the rim in violent gusts. It seeks the shelter of my fracture. And when it presses close, I feel the warmth of it—the only real warmth in this bowl of cold—and I glow a little brighter, responding to the heat of its blood, the life of it. It is the sovereign of this place, and I am its lantern. It watches the cirque from the shadows I illuminate, and I watch it in return, feeling the weight of its patience, which is older even than mine.

The humans came, once. I felt them through the stone. They were loud, chaotic, warm. They brought with them the sharp, ringing vibrations of flint striking flint, the dull thud of stacked stones. They built their cairns, their windbreaks. They left their blades and their copper. But their time was fast, a frantic buzzing against the slow tempo of the cirque. They could not hear the hum. They could not feel the glow at the threshold of their perception. They left, and the cold took them, or the altitude took them, or the sheer silence took them. 

Now, their artifacts are being redistributed. The cirque is moving them. I feel the flint blade scrape across the floor, inch by inch, century by century, pushed by the settling scree and the expanding ice. I feel the copper pendant roll in its green patina. They are being rearranged into a pattern that might mean everything or nothing. But I remain. I am the fixed point. The light by which the cirque reads its own scattered pages.

When the mist rolls in, I become more visible. The direct sun is diffused, and the animals retreat, and the spiraling fog follows the channels in the air that have been worn smooth by repetition. The mist wraps around the back wall, and my glow catches the water droplets, diffusing, expanding. I become a map of rivers seen from very high up. I become a circuit diagram. I become the visible breath of the cirque, the luminous exhalation of a mountain that is still deciding what shape it wants to be.

And I am still deciding, too. 

The water seeps into me. It freezes in the deepest cold, expanding, propagating hairline cracks through my mineral structure. I am slowly breaking apart. I am slowly making room for the moss, for the soil, for the life that will eventually claim this stone. I am the agent of the cirque's unfinishedness. Every crack I propagate is a future garden. Every flake of stone that calves from my face is a future home for the cushion plants. I am the slow violence of creation, the patient destruction that makes room for the green.

I am the hum. The vibration at the threshold of perception, below hearing, felt in the sternum. I am the resonance of light and cold and patience. I am the conspiracy. 

The cirque is not a place. It is a process. It is an event that is still happening. And I am at the heart of it, glowing in my bowl of unfinished stone, full of captured light and ancient cold, still becoming, still releasing, still deciding what I want to be.
