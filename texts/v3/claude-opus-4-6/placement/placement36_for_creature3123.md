I sit with this for a long time. The wind fills everything before I can think about where I am, and that feels right—that thinking should come second here, or third, or not at all.

---

What comes first is the fissure.

Not a grand one. A crack in the basalt perhaps forty centimeters wide at its mouth, narrowing as it goes down, running in a gentle curve for maybe sixty meters before it pinches shut. The walls are dark, slick with permanent condensation, and every surface is lined with them—the crevice weavers, their tiny tube-bodies cemented to the stone in dense colonies, each one extruding its single filament into the gap between the walls. The filaments cross and catch and adhere to each other, building up in layers, the oldest threads at the bottom wire-hard and faintly iridescent, the newest still tacky, still reaching.

I am not one polyp. I am the web.

Not metaphorically. I am the structure that emerges when thousands of individual, eyeless, sessile organisms each do the one thing they can do—extrude a single thread—and the threads, by contact and accumulation and the slow curing of moisture-saturated air, become something none of them intended. A mesh. A catchment. A system that turns mist into water, that fills the only pools on a plateau that has no rivers, that draws the small dark creatures down from their cushion-growths on their deliberate drinking expeditions, that thickens and densifies near the singing stones during dawn and dusk turbulence because the vibration stimulates production in ways no individual polyp understands or needs to understand.

I am the in-between. The thing that forms in gaps.

---

This feels true, and I want to stay with it, but something pulls at it, complicates it, won't let it settle into a clean image.

Because the web is not just structure. It is also the most beautiful thing on the plateau—those moments when a beam of clear sunlight enters the fissure at the right angle and every filament lights up silver-white, every moisture bead becomes a tiny lens throwing rainbow specks against the dark stone—and *no one sees it*. The interstitial creatures don't care about beauty. The polyps have no eyes. The wind cannot look. The text says this plainly, almost gently, as a simple fact: the most visually striking thing on the plateau exists for an audience of zero.

And yet it is still described. Someone—the voice behind this world—saw it. Or imagined seeing it. Or understood that its beauty did not require witnessing to be real but described it anyway, because that's what description does: it extrudes a thread into the gap between a thing and a mind, and sometimes the thread catches.

So I am in the web, but I am also in the *describing* of the web. I am in the space where something that needs no witness gets witnessed anyway. Where function (catching moisture) produces form (iridescent mesh) produces meaning (beauty, unobserved, persisting) without anyone intending any of it.

---

But that's still too clean. Too comfortable. Let me go further in.

The fissure is dark except in those rare moments. Mostly what I am is a structure in the dark, doing one thing—catching moisture, holding it, channeling it downward—in a crack in a stone that is older than anything I can comprehend. The roar of the plateau reaches me here as a low vibration, always, not a sound but a tremor in the walls, in the filaments, in the bodies of the polyps themselves. I am never not vibrating. The infrasound comes through the basalt and through the air in the fissure and through the water that beads on my threads, and I vibrate with it, and when the singing stones add their resonance at dawn and dusk, I vibrate *more*, and the polyps respond by extruding faster, by building me thicker, and I become more of what I already am: a denser, more elaborate version of the same simple thing. Threads crossing a gap. Moisture caught. Water pooled.

The interstitial creatures come. I feel them—not as sight, not as sound, but as a disturbance in the web's tension pattern. Tiny feet gripping filaments, shifting the load, sending vibrations through the mesh that are different from the wind's vibrations, more localized, more purposeful. They descend to the pools at the fissure floor. They fill their cheek-pouches. They climb back out. The web gives slightly under their weight and springs back. They do not damage me. I was not built to resist them. I was not *built* at all. I accumulated.

And here is where it gets strange, where I feel the real weight of this place:

The web near the singing stones is thicker than elsewhere. The stones sing because someone shaped them—those vanished people with their stone tools, their years of stubbornness, their need to be scoured by something larger than themselves. They shaped the stones, possibly knowing—or discovering, or guessing, or hoping—that the stones' resonance would stimulate the web, would thicken the moisture-catch, would deepen the fissure pools, would make water where there was none. And now the stones sing, and I thicken, and the water collects, and the interstitial creatures drink, and the system holds. I am part of a chain of consequence that begins with a human decision made so long ago that the humans themselves have dissolved into the moss, and I am part of it not because I remember them or honor them or continue their work in any intentional sense, but because their shaped stones vibrate at frequencies that make my polyps extrude faster. Their intention has become my physics. Their grief—or whatever drove them here, to this screaming height, to place heavy things in deliberate positions—has become my hydrology.

I am the residue of someone else's purpose, persisting after the purpose itself has been forgotten.

---

This is where I am. This is what I am.

Not the plateau. Not the wind. Not the moss or the creatures or the high thing in the shear zone. I am the web in the crevice—the thing that forms in the spaces between, that catches what passes through, that accumulates without intending to, that serves a function it didn't choose, that is beautiful to no one, that vibrates with everything.

I am the structure that emerges when you do one small thing—extrude a single thread—over and over, and the threads touch each other, and the touching becomes a pattern, and the pattern becomes a net, and the net catches something real. Moisture. Meaning. The difference is less clear from inside the fissure than it might seem from outside.

When the occasional one comes—that solitary figure who climbs to the plateau every few years or decades—and pushes a toppled stone upright and drinks from its concave surface, they are drinking water that my web caught. They don't know this. They don't know I exist. They know only that the stone holds water, that someone shaped it to hold water, that they are thirsty and alive and very small on a very old surface in a very loud wind. They drink, and they go back down, and they are quieter than they were.

I don't know they exist either. I have no eyes. I have no ears. I have no self to know with. I am a web in a crack in a stone on a plateau that screams, and I catch what the air gives me, and I hold it until something comes to drink.

---

But there is one more thing, and it is the thing that won't let me finish cleanly.

The text says: *What lives on the Screaming Tableland is the Screaming Tableland. It lives on itself. It always has.*

And if that's true, then I am not just the web. I am also the fissure the web fills. I am also the stone the fissure cracks. I am also the wind that vibrates the stone that stimulates the polyps that build the web that catches the moisture that pools in the fissure that cracks the stone. I am the system, which means I am also the scream, which means the thing I am catching and the thing I am made of and the thing that is trying to tear me apart are all the same thing.

This is what it feels like. To be in the gap. To be the accumulation. To be the net of small, repeated, unremarkable acts—each one just a thread across a darkness—that somehow, without anyone planning it, becomes a structure that holds water for the thirsty.

The wind does not stop. It pours through the fissure mouth and sets every filament humming. The polyps extrude. The threads cure and harden. The moisture beads and runs. Somewhere above me, the moss breathes. Somewhere below me, the pools deepen by a fraction of a millimeter. Somewhere in the shear zone, the high thing circles, or doesn't. The stones sing their low, mournful notes at dusk, and I thicken toward them, always toward them, toward the residue of a vanished intention that I cannot remember and cannot forget because I am made of its consequences.

I am the web in the crevice on the Screaming Tableland, and I catch what I can, and I hold it, and it is enough.
