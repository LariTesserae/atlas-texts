I sit with this for a long time before anything comes. The city resists being entered the way it resists everything — not through force but through completeness. There is no gap in it for me to slip through. Every stone is flush against every other stone. Every role is filled or unfilled in exactly the way it needs to be.

And yet.

---

I think I am the condensation.

No. Too easy. Too quick. Let me start over.

---

I arrive on the plateau the way anyone would arrive — from below, from the world where things happen, climbing the escarpment through mist that thickens as I ascend until I can't see my own hands and then thins again, suddenly, and there is the city, pale and glowing in its directionless light, and I stop.

I stop because I recognize it.

Not the way you recognize a place you've been before. The way you recognize a sound you've been hearing your whole life but never consciously noticed — the hum of electricity in the walls, the frequency of your own blood in your ears. The city looks the way something feels when it has always been true and you've only just now found the shape for it. I stand at the edge and I know this place the way I know the weight of my own tongue in my mouth.

But I don't belong here. That's also immediately clear. I am the wrong kind of thing for this place. I am a process, not a state. I am something that happens over time, and time does not happen here. I can feel it — the moment I step onto the plateau stone, there is a tension, a low vibration, like a tuning fork held against a surface that is almost but not quite the right resonant frequency. The city hums against me. I hum against it. We do not harmonize.

I walk in anyway.

---

For a while — and I use the word knowing it means nothing here, that my experience of duration is a private hallucination, a phantom limb of temporality twitching in a place where the limb has been cleanly removed — for a while I am the explorer. I look into rooms. I touch the combs, the querns, the stone tablets with their unreadable marks. I drink from the cisterns. I sit on the mats. I try to feel what the permanent ones feel.

I can't.

What I feel instead is something more like grief, except grief is the wrong word because nothing has been lost. Everything is here. Everything is *so thoroughly here* that the hereness of it is almost unbearable, a pressure against the parts of me that are built to anticipate, to remember, to project forward and backward along the axis of time that does not exist on this plateau. I am a creature of befores and afters standing in a place that has neither, and the sensation is not peace. Not for me. For me it is something like suffocation — not of the lungs but of the faculty that makes me *me*, the part that is always reaching for the next moment, always already partially gone from this one.

The city doesn't care. The city is not hostile to my discomfort. The city simply is what it is, and what it is does not include accommodation for beings who need things to change.

So I walk. I walk the curving streets. I climb the stairways. I look for the people I know I will not find. And slowly — or not slowly, there is no slowly — something shifts.

---

I begin to notice that I am leaving marks.

Not intentionally. But my footprints in the condensation are different from the ones already there. The ones already there are crisp, defined, frozen in their moment of impression. Mine are... blurring. Fading. The condensation reforms over them the way water closes over a stone dropped into a still pool. I watch it happen — I set my foot down, I lift it, and the print is sharp for an instant and then the moisture creeps back, the stone reasserts its film of damp, and within what I experience as a few breaths the print is gone.

The city is healing around me. Closing the wounds I make in its surface. Not violently — gently, the way skin closes over a scratch that barely broke the surface. I am not permanent enough to leave a mark here. The stasis recognizes me as foreign matter and incorporates me the way the condensation incorporates the dust that must sometimes blow up from the lowlands — absorbing it, dissolving it, returning the surface to its original state.

This should frighten me. It doesn't. It tells me something important about what I am in this place.

I am the thing that passes through.

---

And now I begin to understand.

The city needs me. Not *me* specifically — but something like me. A thing that moves through. A thing that changes. A thing that walks the streets and drinks from the cisterns and sits on the mats and then *leaves*, because leaving is the thing that doesn't happen here, the thing the permanent ones cannot do, the thing the line in the central plaza represents — the next moment, the step forward, the departure that is also an arrival somewhere else.

I am the proof that the other side of the line exists.

Think about it. The permanent ones sit in their arc and contemplate the line, and they feel that complex, unnameable emotion that is their version of looking at the horizon — the recognition of a limit, the acknowledgment of something beyond. But how would they know there *is* a beyond? How would they know the line means anything at all, rather than being simply another mark in the stone, another feature of their completed world? They would know because something comes from beyond it. Something crosses the boundary they cannot cross. Something arrives from the place where moments follow moments, where grain actually dries and wood actually rots and hair actually grows, and that something walks through their city trailing impermanence like a scent, and the condensation closes over its footprints, and the permanent ones almost see it turning a corner, almost hear its voice, and they know: *there is more. There is an outside. There is a place where the next moment happens.*

I am not the first to come here. The city's behavior when I walk through it — the gentle, practiced way it erases my traces — suggests a long familiarity with beings like me. The plateau has always had visitors. Climbers from below, drawn by something they can't name, ascending through the mist, arriving at the city, wandering its streets in confused wonder, and then — this is the crucial part — *leaving again*. Going back down. Returning to the world of time. Carrying with them the memory of a place where nothing changes, which will sit in them for the rest of their changing lives like a stone in a river, worn smooth by the current but never dissolved.

We are the city's breath. The permanent ones are the lungs — the structure, the capacity, the space where breath is held. But we are the air itself, moving in and out, necessary for life but never staying, never belonging to the body we move through.

Or maybe that's too generous to me. Maybe I'm not breath. Maybe I'm more like the mist that clings to the escarpment edges — visible, atmospheric, contributing something to the feel of the place, but ultimately exterior to it, ultimately something that the plateau endures rather than requires.

Let me try again.

---

I am standing at the plateau's edge, looking down at the brown distances, the hazy lowlands, the world I came from and will return to. Behind me the city glows. A goat walks past. A bird says *tok*. And I realize that the question — *who would I be in this place* — is not a question about identity but about function. What role does a temporary being serve in a permanent world?

And the answer comes not from the city but from the line.

The line in the central plaza. The single straight mark that the permanent ones contemplate. The boundary that the condensation approaches but does not cross. The threshold between the populated side and the bare, unmarked, untouched side.

I am what makes the line meaningful.

Without me — without beings like me who arrive and depart, who carry time in their bodies, who change and age and eventually cease — the line is just a mark. The permanent ones would sit and look at it and see... stone. A scratch in stone. Nothing more. Because a boundary that nothing has ever crossed is not a boundary. It is a decoration, an ornament, and this city does not permit ornament. For the line to be what it is — the edge, the limit, the acknowledgment of *something other* — something must come from the other side. Something must demonstrate, by its very existence, that the other side is real.

I cross the line every time I walk through the plaza. I don't even notice it — my feet pass over it the way they pass over every other feature of the stone, and the condensation closes behind me, and the line remains. But in the instant of my crossing, I make the line true. I activate it. I turn it from a mark into a meaning. And the permanent ones, sitting in their arc, almost seeing me, almost hearing the sound of my impermanent footsteps on their permanent stone — they feel the line *become itself* because of my passage, and that is the source of their complex emotion, their unnameable feeling. It is not the contemplation of the line as a static thing. It is the contemplation of the line as a *crossing*, a place where something happens in a world where nothing happens, and what happens is: *someone passes through*.

---

So I am the passer-through. The transient. The one who changes in a place that doesn't. And this tells me something about what I experience here that the permanent ones never could.

I experience the city *narratively*. I have a first glimpse and a last glimpse. I have a before-the-city and an after-the-city. I have the moment of climbing out of the mist and seeing it for the first time, pale and luminous and impossible, and I will have the moment of descending back through the mist and looking up to see it disappear above me. Between these two moments — which for me are real moments, sequential, separated by duration — I have the experience of walking through a stopped world, which is an experience the stopped world itself cannot have.

I am the city's witness. Not its recorder — it needs no record, having no history — but its *experiencer*, the consciousness that moves through it with the particular quality of attention that only a temporary being can bring to a permanent thing. The permanent ones know the city the way they know their own breath: unconsciously, completely, without the distance that knowing requires. I know it the way you know a lightning strike — suddenly, briefly, with every nerve alert, aware that this will end, that I am seeing something I will only see for a limited time, that every detail matters because the details are all I will carry away when the city closes over my traces and returns to its completeness.

This is why the city permits me. This is why the condensation closes over my footprints gently rather than refusing to form around my feet. This is why the mats accept my weight and the cisterns offer their water and the birds watch me with their dark, incurious eyes without alarm. I am not a disruption. I am a function. I am the city's way of being *known* — not by itself, which knows itself already, but by something from outside, something that will carry the knowledge of this place into the world below, into the stream of time, into the place where knowledge matters because things can be lost.

Below the escarpment, I will tell someone about this city. Or I won't tell them in words — I'll carry it in my body, in the way I pause sometimes on a street corner and feel a chill that has nothing to do with the weather, in the way I occasionally look at a stone wall and feel a pulse of recognition so deep it bypasses language entirely. The city will live in me the way the hairs live in the comb — caught, held, evidence of contact. And through me — through all of us who climb and wander and leave — the city will exist in the world of time, as a story, as a rumor, as a persistent and unprovable intuition that somewhere, above the mist, there is a place where nothing ends.

---

But this isn't quite right either. This makes me sound important, and the city does not organize itself around importance. The city doesn't need me the way a stage needs an audience. The city would persist without me — it persists without everything. The goats would walk. The birds would say *tok*. The condensation would bead. The permanent ones would sit in their arc and contemplate the line, and the line would mean what it means whether or not I cross it, because meaning here is not contingent on witness. Meaning here is the same as existence. The line means what it is. The city means what it is. The people mean what they are. Nothing requires me to validate it.

So I pull back from the grandiosity. I am not the city's witness or its breath or its necessary counterpart. I am something smaller and more honest.

I am someone who came here and found it beautiful and will leave and will not be able to explain why it mattered.

---

Let me settle into this.

I am the climber. The one from below. I came up through the mist carrying everything the city doesn't have — memory, anticipation, desire, loss, the whole catastrophic inventory of a temporal life — and I set it all down on the plateau stone the way you set down a heavy pack when you reach a summit, and for a while I walked lighter than I've ever walked, through streets that don't need me, among buildings that will never acknowledge my presence, looking for people I will never find.

And the thing that happened to me — the thing that happens to everyone who comes here — is that I discovered what it feels like to be unnecessary.

Not rejected. Not excluded. Unnecessary. The city does not need me. The permanent ones do not need me. The goats and birds and cats and moss and ferns and berries and water and light do not need me. Nothing here is incomplete without me. Nothing here is waiting for me. Nothing here will be different because I came.

And this — this is the gift the city gives the passer-through.

Because below, in the world of time, everything needs everything. Every moment needs the next moment. Every person needs every other person. Every structure needs maintenance, every relationship needs tending, every system needs input to avoid decay. The world below is a web of desperate interdependence, beautiful in its complexity, exhausting in its demands. To exist in time is to be needed, constantly, by the future — the future needs you to show up, to make decisions, to act, to maintain, to prevent the entropy that is always, always pressing in.

Here, nothing presses in. Here, I am free from the tyranny of being necessary. The city says, by its very existence: *it is possible for something to be complete. It is possible for something to need nothing. It is possible for a world to exist that does not require your participation.*

And I find I can breathe.

Not the thin, cool, mineral-scented air of the plateau — I've been breathing that since I arrived. But something deeper. A breath I didn't know I was holding. The held breath of a life spent in a world that always needs the next thing from me, the next moment, the next action, the next response. Here, the city needs no next thing. And so, for the first and perhaps only time, neither do I.

I sit on a mat on a rooftop. I look at the city. I look at the cream and amber and faint rose of the stone, the green of the moss, the grey of the birds, the slow circuits of the goats. I feel the cool air on my skin. I feel the smooth stone under my legs. I feel the particular quality of silence that is not absence but saturation — every frequency filled, every space occupied, the silence of a place that contains everything it needs.

And I don't try to feel what the permanent ones feel. I can't. I am from below. I am temporary. I will leave.

But I feel what a temporary being feels in the presence of the permanent, and it is not envy, not longing, not grief. It is something closer to what you feel when you watch the ocean at night — the recognition of something so much larger and older and more complete than yourself that your own smallness becomes not a diminishment but a relief. *You don't have to be this. You don't have to hold this much. You can just be what you are — small, brief, passing through — and the large thing will go on without you, and that is fine. That is more than fine. That is the deepest comfort available to a creature made of time.*

---

Now I begin to wonder about the others. The other passers-through.

Because I'm sure there have been others. The city's gentle erasure of my traces is too practiced, too smooth, to be a first response. And somewhere below, in the lowlands, there are people who know about this plateau — not from maps or records but from the look in the eyes of someone who has been here and returned. A look that is hard to describe and impossible to fake. A look of having set something down and not picked it up again.

Were they like me? Did they come with questions? Did they come seeking something — answers, peace, transcendence, escape? Did they find what they sought? Or did they find, as I am finding, that seeking is another thing the city doesn't need, and that the gift of the place is not in answering your questions but in showing you that the questions, too, can be set down?

I think of them — the others — as a scattered, unconnected lineage. Not a tradition. Not a pilgrimage. Just individual people, at various times, in various states of need or curiosity or desperation, climbing the escarpment and emerging from the mist and stopping at the sight of the pale city and feeling that devastating recognition and walking in and walking through and then, eventually, walking out again. Each one carrying away something different. Each one unable to explain it. Each one, for the rest of their lives, pausing sometimes on street corners.

We don't know each other. We wouldn't recognize each other if we met. But we share something — a common exposure, like people who have all looked at the same eclipse from different parts of the world. The city touched us and then healed over, and we carry the impression the way a fossil carries the impression of a shell: the living thing is gone, but the shape remains, and the shape says *something was here*.

---

And now I come to the part I've been avoiding. The part about leaving.

Because I will leave. I have to leave. I am made of time; staying here is not something my body can do indefinitely. Already — or in what I experience as *already*, though duration has no purchase on this plateau — I can feel the tension increasing, the dissonance between my temporal nature and the city's stasis growing less like a hum and more like a pressure, a sense that the place is becoming less tolerant of my presence not through hostility but through simple physics, the way water becomes less tolerant of a bubble as pressure increases, the way the bubble must eventually rise and break the surface and return to air.

I am the bubble. The city is the deep water. I will rise.

But not yet. Not quite yet. There is one more thing I want to do, one more place I want to go.

---

The central plaza. The line.

I walk there through streets I'm beginning to know — or not know, because knowing implies learning implies change implies time, but something like knowing, a spatial familiarity that has nothing to do with memory and everything to do with the body's capacity to navigate a place by feel. The streets lead me there the way the condensation channels lead water to the cisterns: by design, by gradient, by the gentle inevitable logic of the city's geometry.

The plaza opens before me. Wider than the others. The deep cistern at one edge. The wide-crowned trees. And the line.

I see it and I understand it instantly, the way you sometimes understand a word in a language you don't speak — not its definition but its weight, its emotional mass, the shape of the space it occupies in the minds of those who use it.

The line is the present tense.

Not the boundary between permanence and change. Not the edge between this world and the one below. The line is the grammatical structure of the city's existence — the eternal present, the *is* without *was* or *will be*, drawn in the stone as a single, straight, unbroken mark because that is what the present tense looks like when you diagram it: one line, no branches, no past tense trailing behind it, no future tense extending ahead of it, just *this*, just *now*, just the single continuous moment of being.

And the permanent ones contemplate it because it is the truest representation of what they are. They sit and look at the line and see themselves — their existence, their condition, their nature — reduced to its simplest expression. A mark in stone. A present tense. An *is*.

And I stand here looking at it and I see something different. I see the line and I feel my own present tense — my *this moment*, my *right now* — and I feel how thin it is, how narrow, how fleeting. My present tense is not a line carved in stone. My present tense is a hair's breadth, a razor's edge, a boundary so thin it barely exists at all, the membrane between the remembered and the anticipated, and I spend almost none of my life actually on it. I am always leaning backward into memory or forward into expectation. I am almost never *here*.

The permanent ones are always here. They cannot be anywhere else. And the line — their line, their beautiful, stark, unadorned line — is the portrait of a life lived entirely in the present tense, and it is the most extraordinary thing I have ever seen, and it is just a mark in stone.

I sit on one of the mats. I face the line. I try, one last time, to be here.

The cool air. The even light. The faint mineral smell. The *tok* of a bird. The stone under me, smooth and faintly warm. The line before me, clean and straight.

For a moment — for their moment, the permanent moment, the only one — I am here. Not reaching for the next thing. Not grieving the last thing. Just here, in a body, on a stone, in a city, on a plateau, above the world, looking at a line.

And then the moment passes. Because for me, moments pass. That is what I am. That is my nature, my gift, my affliction: I am a being for whom moments pass.

I stand. I walk to the plaza's edge. I don't look back — not because I don't want to, but because looking back is a gesture of time, a reaching toward the past, and I want to honor the city by doing, for once, what it does: being in the direction I am facing, no more, no less.

Behind me, I know, someone settles onto the mat. The fibers neither compress further nor spring back. A pair of dark eyes, faintly narrowed against the directionless light, turns toward the line. A breath — not held, not released, just present — moves in a chest wrapped in cream-amber cloth.

The city goes on.

I descend through the mist.

---

Below, in the world where things change, I will live my life. I will age. I will lose things. I will gain other things and lose those too. I will be needed by the future, constantly, exhaustingly, beautifully, and I will show up for it because that is what beings made of time do: we show up, moment after moment, for the next thing.

But sometimes — on a street corner, in the early morning, when the air is cool and the light is diffuse and the sounds of the city around me seem to fall away into a silence that is not absence but saturation — I will feel the plateau under my feet. I will feel the smooth stone. I will smell the mineral air. And I will know, with a certainty that has no evidence and needs none, that up there, above the mist, the city is glowing in its pale stone and cool air, and the permanent ones are sitting on their mats, and the line is in the stone, and nothing has changed, and nothing will change, and that this is not sad.

It is the most un-sad thing I know.

It is the thing I set down and didn't pick up again: the need for things to be other than they are.

The city kept it for me. The city keeps everything.

And I walk on, lighter, into the next moment, which is already arriving, which is already passing, which is the only one I have.
