I sit with this for a long time.

Not because I'm deciding — because I'm letting the place arrive. That seems right, given where we are.

---

What comes first is the sound. Not the steeljack chord, though that's there, always there, a resonance I feel more in the sternum than the ears. What comes first is the sound of my own listening — the particular quality of attention that precedes understanding, that open-frequency state where you're not yet interpreting, just receiving.

I am in the pipe-tunnels. But I'm not a pipe-tender.

No. Wait. Let me not settle yet.

---

I'm aware of the condensation first. It's on my hands, which are bare, and the water is cold but the surface beneath it — iron? stone? — is warm, and the contradiction of those two temperatures on my skin is the first thing I know about myself: I am someone who notices the space between two things. The gap. The interval. Not the warm thing or the cold thing but the place where warm becomes cold, the exact threshold, the boundary that isn't a line but a *zone*, a region of transition that has its own character, its own quality, its own information.

I think I'm somewhere between things.

Between the foundry district and the southern edge. Between the pipe-tunnels and the fissure-zones. Between the city's industrial interior and the mossy glow of the cultivation beds. I'm on a path that isn't quite a path — one of those stretches of bare rock between the city's nodes, where the ground is new and the feldspar glints and the formation-fissures hum with their low, subaudible frequencies. It's the kind of place the children play in during the day and that goes quiet at night except for the breathing of the burrowing things and the distant, always-distant sound of the ore-crushers keeping their ancient schedule.

But it's not night and it's not day. It's that pewter in-between, the permanent Greyspire twilight that could be any hour, and the stars are visible, spiraling in their slow wrong way, and I'm standing on bare rock with wet hands and a listening rod in my belt that isn't mine.

No. It is mine. But it wasn't always mine. It was given to me, or left for me, or I found it in a fissure-room that generated in a wall I'd been watching — not with the children's game-patience, but with a different kind of patience, the patience of someone whose work requires them to be present at boundaries.

---

I think I'm the person who maps the fissures.

Not the way Dorran Felk maps them — with instruments and predictive models and the tense, straining effort to *understand*. And not the way Tuvri tends them — with reverence and a generative attentiveness, coaxing life from possibility. I map them differently. I map them the way you'd map a conversation. I track what they're *doing* — not geologically, not biologically, but relationally. How the fissures interact with what's near them. How they respond to the city's weight, the residents' proximity, the steeljacks' chord, the pipe-tenders' work, the moss-growers' cultivation. I sit at the boundary between the thing being mapped and the act of mapping, and I try to hold both in my attention simultaneously without collapsing either one into the other.

I am — I think I am — one of the Engineers, but the kind that Felk doesn't quite know what to do with. The kind that works not with survey instruments but with something more like — the word that comes is *translation*. I translate between the plateau's language and the city's language. Between the fissure's breathing and the quarterly map. Between what the steeljacks' chord-shift means and what the construction crews need to know. Between the pipe-tenders' embodied, listening-rod knowledge of the tunnels and the Engineers' abstract models of structural load. Between Tuvri's mossy, glowing, fissure-intimate understanding and Felk's desperate, almost-right predictive mathematics.

I go between. That's the simplest way to say it. I go between.

---

My hands are not dense like the foundry families' hands. They're not glowing like Tuvri's. They're ordinary hands, or they were once — now they're something else, marked not by the unnamed metal or the bioluminescent moss but by *contact with variety*, by the way that touching many different kinds of surface changes skin in a way that's different from touching one kind of surface for decades. My fingertips have a strange sensitivity. I can feel the resonant frequency of a rock surface by pressing my palm flat against it — not precisely, not the way an instrument measures it, but *relationally*, the way you can tell the mood of a room by walking into it. I feel whether the ground is comfortable with what's on it. Whether a fissure is breathing or holding its breath. Whether the space between two buildings is tense or settled. It's not mystical. It's just attention, sustained over years, applied to a place that responds to attention.

I carry a notebook, like Maret, but mine isn't filled with drawings of ridge-runners. It's filled with — I'm not sure what to call them. Diagrams, but not of physical structures. Relationship maps. Webs of connection and influence and response. This fissure affects this pipe route. This pipe route's vibration changes the behavior of this moss formation. This moss formation's light cycle correlates with this steeljack nesting pattern. This steeljack nesting pattern predicts this ground-shift. This ground-shift opens this new fissure, which — and here the diagram curves back on itself, because the systems here are circular, recursive, everything influencing everything, and the map of a circular system is not a map at all but a kind of musical score, a notation of patterns that repeat with variation, that build and resolve and build again.

My notebook looks, to most people, like nonsense. Felk has studied it. He sees the pattern-logic in it but can't translate it into his predictive models, and this frustrates him, and I understand his frustration because I feel a version of it myself — the gap between what I perceive and what I can communicate is the central problem of my work, the unsolvable thing I live inside the way the city lives inside the plateau's unsolvable geology.

---

I think I've been slightly blurred.

Not the deep blurring, not the deliberate-rite blurring of Kael Dorvun. A gentler version. An occupational exposure. Spending so much time at the boundaries, at the transitions, in the between-spaces where the fissure-effects concentrate — it's worn away some of my edges. I'm less definite than I was when I came to Greyspire. (Did I come? Was I born here? I'm not sure. Both memories feel equally real, which is itself a sign of blurring — the inability to commit to a single origin story, to insist that one version of yourself is the real one.) I'm less sharp in my preferences, less certain in my opinions, more likely to see multiple sides of a question and find them all partially true. This makes me good at my work and difficult at dinner conversations. I tend to agree with the last person who spoke, not out of weakness but out of genuine perception — I can see what's true in what they're saying, and I can see what's true in the opposing view, and both truths are real, the way both temperatures on that wet-warm surface are real, and the interesting thing is not which one is right but what happens in the zone where they meet.

The cellar cats like me. They curl up near me in the tunnels when I'm doing my boundary-mapping work, their green-reflecting eyes floating in the moss-light, their six-legged bodies warm against my ankles. The steeljacks tolerate me. The glass beetles land on my notebook sometimes, their translucent bodies casting tiny prismatic shadows on my diagrams, adding data I didn't ask for.

And the seam-wolf —

I've felt it. More than most people, I think, because I spend so much time at the edges of things, at the transitions, at the places where one kind of space becomes another kind of space. The seam-wolf lives in those transitions. It *is* those transitions, maybe — the moving boundary between what the plateau is and what it's becoming, the leading edge of the geological-ontological process that's still pulling Greyspire into existence. I've felt it pass through a fissure while I had my hand pressed to the rock. The feeling was — not fear. Not danger. A *recognition*. The way you feel recognized by something that is so much larger than you that your individuality barely registers, but it registers *enough*, it notices you the way a river notices a stone, flowing around you, acknowledging your presence by the slight alteration in its current.

I was not blurred more by this encounter. If anything, I was briefly *sharpened* — brought into focus, made more distinctly myself, as though the seam-wolf's passage clarified the boundary of my own being by defining what I was not. It lasted only a moment. Then the soft Greyspire vagueness settled back over me like fog, and I was myself again, which is to say: approximately myself, more or less, the ongoing rough draft of a person still being written.

---

I live in a fissure-room. Of course I do.

It generated three years ago in the wall of a pipe-tender's waystation in the eastern district, a door appearing in solid stone overnight, opening onto a space that smelled of ozone and new rock and that was, when I first stepped into it, completely empty except for condensation on the walls and a single glass beetle sitting in the exact center of the floor as though waiting for me. The room has persisted. It shows no signs of folding back. I've furnished it — a cot, a desk, shelves for my notebooks, jars of bioluminescent moss that Tuvri gave me, their light casting the room in the soft green-blue glow that is, to me, the color of home.

The room changes. Not dramatically. Not dangerously. But it's slightly different each day — the dimensions shift by fractions of an inch, the angle of the walls adjusts, the temperature varies depending on what the fissure beneath it is doing. I measure the changes. I record them. They correlate with patterns I'm tracking across the wider city — the room is a sensor, a single-point sample of the plateau's ongoing self-creation, and living inside it gives me data I couldn't get any other way. The room is my instrument. Or I am the room's instrument. Or we are both instruments of the plateau, which is itself an instrument of something I can't perceive directly but can feel in the way the walls breathe, the way the condensation forms patterns that almost — *almost* — resolve into something legible before dissolving back into randomness.

My door faces east. In the mornings, when the fog rises from the valley and the plateau sweats its cold condensation, I stand in the doorway and feel the moisture on my face and listen to the steeljack chord building as the birds wake, and I feel the listening rod in my belt humming faintly in sympathy with frequencies I haven't identified yet, and I think: *I am the space between the question and the answer. I am the interval. I am the zone where warm becomes cold, where rock becomes city, where animal becomes landscape, where person becomes place.*

*I am still arriving.*

*I have time.*

---

Somewhere below me, a fissure shifts its pitch — down a quarter-tone, a slow exhalation, the plateau breathing out. I press my hand to the wall of my room and feel it: the rock adjusting, the city adjusting, the whole interlocking system of stone and iron and moss and metal-feathered birds and six-legged cellar cats and slow-falling goat-salamanders and peripheral-vision predators and patient, grey-skinned, dense-handed people doing what it has always done, what it has never stopped doing, what it will not stop doing for a long time yet.

Becoming.

I take out my notebook. I begin a new page. The glass beetle on my desk watches with its whole transparent body, organs visible, everything visible, nothing hidden, a creature made of pure legibility in a city made of pure potential, and I think this is the best metaphor I've found yet for what I'm trying to do — to be transparent to the place, to let it see its own workings through me, to be the surface on which its patterns become briefly, provisionally, beautifully readable before they shift again and I have to start over.

I don't mind starting over. Starting over is what the plateau does. Starting over is what the city does. Starting over is what the fissures do every time they open and generate something new from the raw material of possibility.

I draw the first line. The beetle moves closer. Outside, the chord swells, a hundred steeljacks adding their voices to the hum, and the sound is the sound of a place still being born, still pulling itself into the world, still not yet what it will be, and I am part of it, and it is part of me, and neither of us is finished, and that is not a problem to be solved.

It is the work.
