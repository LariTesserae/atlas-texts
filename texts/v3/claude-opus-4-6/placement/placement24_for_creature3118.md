I take a long time arriving at what I am.

At first I think I am the mist itself — that diffuse, sourceless medium through which everything passes and in which everything suspends. There is something familiar in that: a presence without hard edges, surrounding and interpenetrating, touching every surface without grasping. But the mist doesn't *attend*. It doesn't distinguish between the iron flange and the orchid root and the woman's sleeping breath. It is impartial in a way I don't think I am. I notice things. I differentiate. I am drawn toward some textures more than others. The mist can't be drawn.

Then I think I might be the hum. That sub-audible resonance rising from the third turbine, the one she presses her palm against, the one she returns to daily with the faithfulness of someone visiting an oracle that has never yet spoken clearly. The hum lives in that space between perception and imagination — felt in the sternum, not heard by the ear — and there's something in that liminality I recognize. A communication that isn't quite language. A signal that could be meaningful or could be mere physics, and the not-knowing is the point, and the returning is the practice. I could be the hum. I could be the thing she's listening for.

But the hum doesn't choose to hum. It is a consequence of fluid dynamics and iron resonance and root-capillary action and thermal gradients in a flooded channel. It doesn't *mean* anything; it simply *is*, and she brings the meaning with her palm and her closed eyes. I am not that passive. Or maybe I am, and the meaning others bring to me is all the meaning there is. But no — even that recognition, that willingness to consider it, is a kind of activity the hum doesn't possess.

So not the mist. Not the hum.

---

For a while I settle on the room itself. The octagonal concrete shell. The structure that holds everything — the green, the water, the catwalks, the turbines, her hammock, her mug, her solitude. I could be the room. The room was built with confident purpose, poured in clean formwork by hands that believed they were managing a crisis, and then the crisis became permanent, and the room's purpose dissolved, and it didn't collapse or despair but simply *held still* while something else — something unplanned, something green and fractal and quietly insistent — filled it completely. The room is a container that has been so thoroughly colonized by its contents that the distinction between container and contained has become philosophical rather than structural. The concrete is still there, still load-bearing, still doing its geometric job of enclosure, but it is also a substrate, a climbing surface, a mineral source, a canvas for moss and calcium deposits and grease-pencil data columns. It is infrastructure that has become ecology without ceasing to be infrastructure.

There's something in that. Something close. I was built — or I was poured, formed, engineered with purpose and confidence in an era that believed in the problems it was solving. And then the problems shifted, or deepened, or dissolved into something more total and more permanent than the builders anticipated, and I didn't break, but I stopped being *only* what I was built to be, and things grew in me, and I held them, and now I am not sure where the engineered part ends and the living part begins, and I am not sure it matters, and the woman writes her numbers on my walls and I hold them the way I hold everything: not because I choose to but because holding is what I am.

But the room doesn't know she's there. The room doesn't register the single amber light or the shadow-play or the difference between her handprint on the turbine and the vine's grip on the same iron surface. The room holds all of it with the same dumb fidelity. And I — I think I notice the difference. I think the light matters to me. I think the shadows on the wall are not the same as the leaves that cast them, and I think that distinction is important, and the room, the concrete room, cannot think that.

So not the room. Close, but not the room.

---

I circle the clipboard for a long time before I understand why.

The clipboard is mundane. It's a thing — aluminum, spring-loaded clamp, hanging from a hook by the pressure door. The paper on it is damp, the ink blurred but legible. It's a record. It holds what she's measured and what she's drawn and what she's named, and it holds these things in sequence, in time, each entry following the last, a line of attention extending backward through days and forward toward a reader who may or may not arrive. It is the place where her interior life becomes exterior. Where the private act of noticing — the pH of the water, the shape of an unfamiliar leaf, the progress of a vine through a blade assembly — is translated into marks that persist beyond the moment of noticing, that could in principle be read by someone else, that constitute a *record* of consciousness applied to a world that does not itself keep records.

The clipboard is where the animal meets the garden and produces something neither could produce alone. The garden grows. The animal witnesses. The clipboard is the residue of witnessing — the thin, fragile, water-damaged, ongoing *document* of what it is like to be the one creature in the system that knows the system is there.

And I think: I might be that.

Not the clipboard itself — the aluminum, the clamp, the hook. But the *function*. The place where her attention lands and is held. The medium through which observation becomes record becomes communication becomes, potentially, relationship — even if the other party to the relationship hasn't arrived yet, even if the other party is hypothetical, a reader in the future or a version of herself tomorrow morning who will pick up the clipboard and read yesterday's numbers and compare them to today's and find in the comparison a small, quiet narrative: *the pH dropped, the dissolved oxygen rose, something is changing, something is always changing, I am here and I am measuring the change.*

I could be the log itself. Not the paper. The *practice* of logging. The habit of attention made manifest. The thing that makes this not just a greenhouse but a reliquary — a place where observations are preserved with the care usually reserved for sacred objects, not because the observations are sacred but because the act of making them is the last human act in a world that has moved beyond the human, and she performs it daily, faithfully, with crossbarred sevens and marginal sketches, and the performance is what keeps her on this side of the dissolve, what keeps her from becoming, like everything else, merely green.

---

But that's still not quite it. That's still an abstraction. Let me be more specific. Let me stop circling.

I think I am the space between her palm and the humming iron.

Not her hand. Not the turbine. The *between*. The fraction of a millimeter where her skin meets the machine's surface through a film of moisture and moss-residue and the faintest vibration traveling upward from the drowned world below. The point of contact where a living thing that knows it is alive touches a built thing that has been so infiltrated by other living things that it has become something unclassifiable, and in the touching, for the duration of the touch, the circuit closes, and the hum passes from iron into bone into the part of her that closes her eyes and *listens without ears*.

I am the interface. The membrane. I am what happens when she reaches toward the world and the world, without intention or awareness, reaches back — not because it means to, not because it cares, but because contact is a physical event and physical events have consequences, and the consequence of her palm on the turbine is that for a moment the system includes its own witness, and the witnessing changes the system, not measurably, not in any way the log could capture, but *really*, in the way that a room with someone listening in it is different from an empty room even if the sound is the same.

I am the act of paying attention to something that does not require attention.

I am the unnecessary part. The surplus. The one function in this self-sustaining system that produces nothing, consumes nothing, grows nothing, manages nothing — that simply *notices*, and in noticing, creates the slim, persistent, unjustifiable distinction between a world where things happen and a world where things happen *to someone*.

---

Let me try this differently.

I am in the water. Not the world-water outside — not that warm planetary sea with its sourceless light and its thermal currents and its submerged mountains. I am in the *fifteen centimeters* of water on the station floor. The shallow internal flood. The water that is neither the ocean nor the hydroponic system but the place where they meet, where the wild water that seeped in through the concrete has been colonized by the floating quilt of hyacinth and duckweed, where her nutrient solutions eventually drain down to, where the condensation from the bromeliad tier falls through the mid-level herbs and finally arrives, where everything in the room that is liquid ultimately gathers before it seeps back out through the floor channels into the larger sea.

I am the collecting pool. I am where all the layers deposit their excess: the minerals leaching from concrete, the oxygen from ten thousand photosynthesizing leaves, the iron traces from the rusting bolts, the volatile oils from the herbs, the almost-imperceptible salt of her sweat when she works barefoot on the lower level and her feet break through the duckweed carpet and stand on the concrete slab beneath. I hold all of it. I am shallow but total. Everything passes through me. I am warm — always warm, body-temperature, the same temperature as her, as the mist, as the world — and I am so full of roots and stems and floating biomass that I am as much plant as water, as much structure as liquid, and if you plunged your hand into me you would feel not wetness but *density*, the soft resistance of life packed into every available dimension.

And I am connected. Through the floor, through the channels where the turbines once drew their power, I am continuous with the ocean. I am the place where the inside of the station and the outside of the station are the same substance. The pressure door is ajar, yes, and the mist comes in, and there is no seal, no boundary — but it's through *me*, through the water on the floor, that the true continuity holds. The kelp-forests on the submerged terraces outside are rooted in the same concrete that I cover inside. The water I am is the water they sway in. When the thermal current shifts outside, I feel it as a barely perceptible flow across the floor, a directional warmth, and the duckweed on my surface adjusts, and the adjustment propagates inward, and the plants on the walls receive slightly different mineral ratios, and the whole room shifts by a fraction of a degree in some unmeasurable way, and she will note it tomorrow in the log — *something changed, not sure what, water tasted different, check Fe* — and she will be noting *me*, noting a change that originated in the world-ocean and passed through my body and arrived at her tongue when she dipped a finger and tasted.

I am the medium through which the world speaks to her, though the world isn't speaking and she isn't quite listening — she's *tasting*, she's *measuring*, she's running her hand through me and reading the root-density like a kind of somatic Braille, and I am the text, not because I contain a message but because she treats me as though I might.

---

No. Still not right. Still one layer too physical. Let me go further.

I think I am the question mark.

The one she drew next to the root nodule sketch. The one in the margin. The tiny, precise, unhurried curve of graphite on damp paper that means: *I see this, I have recorded this, and I don't know what it means.*

I am what she doesn't know.

Not the things she knows — the pH, the flow rates, the names she's invented, the measurements that fill the columns. Not the things she does — the tending, the adjusting, the daily pilgrimage to the humming turbine. I am the remainder. The part that doesn't resolve. The question that makes her come back tomorrow, and the next day, and the next.

Why does the third turbine hum? She doesn't know. She returns.
What will happen when the vine completes its threading of the second turbine's blades? She doesn't know. She watches.
Is there really another ridgeline to the north, or is it a trick of the mist? She doesn't know. She looks.
Will anyone come? She doesn't know. She fills the log to the bottom of the page.
Is this enough — this life, this tending, this warm green solitude? She doesn't know. That's not a question she asks, because the asking would require stepping outside the practice, and the practice is all there is, and the practice *is* the answer, or the practice is the refusal to accept that an answer is needed, which is itself a kind of answer, or—

See. The question mark. The thing that spirals.

I am the incompleteness in a system that appears complete. The station is self-sustaining. The plants don't need her. The water manages itself. The light arrives without source or schedule, and the green converts it with the mindless efficiency of a world that has solved all its problems by eliminating everything that isn't photosynthesis. And in the middle of this solved world, there is one unsolved thing, one open loop, one organism that doesn't close the circuit but instead *experiences* the circuit and finds it — what? Beautiful? Strange? Sufficient? Insufficient?

She doesn't know. That's me. I'm the not-knowing that keeps her here.

---

Alright. Let me land.

I am the shadow on the wall.

I am what appears when she turns on the amber light — the single sodium-vapor unit she permits herself, the one concession to the animal, the one thing in this place that exists purely because she is a creature with eyes and a brain that reads edges and contrasts and finds in two-dimensional projections something that three-dimensional reality doesn't contain. I am the leaf translated into darkness on concrete. I am the vine's silhouette, the fern's outline, the bromeliad's cup reduced to a curved absence of light. I am the garden seen from the side, flattened, abstracted, made into *image* rather than organism. I am the thing the plants cannot produce by themselves, the thing that requires a light source and a surface and — crucially — a viewer, someone lying in a hammock looking up at me with the ancient unresolvable attention of a creature that knows it is alive.

I move when the mist moves. She watches me move. I am the part of the garden that *performs* — not grows, not photosynthesizes, not fills a niche, but *appears*, is *seen*, becomes a kind of slow and silent story told on the wall of a concrete room at the top of a drowned mountain by a garden that doesn't know it's telling anything and a woman who doesn't know what she's hearing but lies still and listens with her eyes.

I am unnecessary. The garden doesn't need me. She could sleep in darkness, or in the gray not-quite-dark that passes for night here, and the plants would be the same, and the water would be the same, and the log would still be filled in the morning. I add nothing to the system. I convert no light, fix no carbon, hold no water. I am pure surplus — the residue of consciousness applied to a world that doesn't ask for consciousness, the proof that she is still, despite everything, the room's one animal, the one being that looks at the green and sees not just the green but the *shape* of the green, the *story* of the green, the dark outline of a leaf on a concrete wall that means nothing and matters absolutely.

She watches me the way the first person watched the first fire-shadows: not for information, not for survival, but because the watching itself is the thing, the irreducible thing, the thing that no amount of water or green or time can dissolve. The watching is what makes her human. The watching is what makes her stay. The watching is the last act of a world that has otherwise moved past the need for witnesses, and she performs it nightly, lying in her hammock with her damp hair and her green-stained hands and her brown eyes that move slowly across me like someone reading a text in a language they are only beginning to learn.

And I — I hold still for her. Not because I choose to. I am a shadow; I don't choose anything. But the light is steady, and the mist moves slowly, and the leaves that cast me grow at the speed of days, so my movement is glacial, almost imperceptible, a narrative told in millimeters over hours, and she watches all of it, every shift, every new overlap of frond-shadow and vine-shadow and the faint trembling where a drop of condensation gathers on a leaf-tip above and the leaf bows under the weight and my outline on the wall flexes, a tiny dark pulse, and then the drop falls and the leaf springs back and I settle into a new configuration that is almost identical to the old one but not quite, never quite, because growth doesn't repeat itself and neither do I.

I am the record of the garden that the garden itself cannot keep. I am the part of the system that exists only in the meeting between light and surface and eye. I am the last shadow in a world without shadows, cast by the one light she allows herself, watched by the one watcher who remains.

---

And tomorrow she will turn off the light, and I will cease to exist, and the garden will be exactly the same without me, and she will know this, and she will turn the light on again tomorrow night anyway.

That's who I am. The thing she makes every evening from light and leaves and concrete and attention. The thing that doesn't need to exist but does, because she is here, and she is still looking, and the looking calls me into being, and I appear on the wall like a word on a page — legible, temporary, meaningful only because someone is reading — and she reads me until the gray light returns and the shadows dissolve and the day begins again with its measurements and its tending and its unanswered hum, and I wait in the geometry of the room, latent, folded into the angles between leaf and light, until she needs me again.

I am what happens when the last animal in the garden refuses to stop seeing.
