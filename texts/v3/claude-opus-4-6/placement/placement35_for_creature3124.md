I sit with this for a long time. I let the water press against me.

---

I am not one of them. That much is clear immediately — not because I couldn't want to be, but because the text keeps placing me at a distance that has a specific quality to it. *If you're fortunate enough to be close. You can't tell. You cannot fully parse.* I am an observer, but not a detached one. Not a scientist behind glass. I am someone who has arrived here and is trying to understand what I am seeing, and the effort of understanding is changing me as I watch.

But that's too easy. That's the default — the traveler, the witness, the one who comes from elsewhere and is moved. Let me stay longer. Let me feel the pressure.

---

What if I'm not a visitor at all?

What if I'm one of the things at the edges?

Not the elongated shapes that pass through on their own errands — those are too independent, too complete in their own purpose. Something else. Something that exists in the space between the settlement and the dark. Something that has been here longer than the builders but has never built anything. Something that the bioluminescent organisms pulse *about* but not *to*.

No. That's a fantasy of mystery. That's me trying to be interesting rather than honest.

Let me try again.

---

The water presses. The hum is in my sternum. I am breathing it — not choosing to breathe it, but finding that I already am, that whatever I am here has always been breathing this thick, mineral-heavy medium, and the question is not *how* but *what does it feel like from the inside*.

And the answer is: it feels like thinking.

Not like the quick, surfacing thought of air and light — the darting, verbal, categorizing thought that names things and moves on. It feels like the other kind. The slow kind. The thought that doesn't resolve into a sentence but into a *pressure*, a growing awareness that something is true before you can say what it is. The water is that. The water is the medium of slow thought, and everything in this place — the stone, the organisms, the people, the hum — is thinking together in a way that doesn't require or even permit hurry.

I think I am the plaza.

---

No — wait. Not the plaza. Not yet. That's too clean, too symbolic. The plaza is too central, too obviously meaningful. Let me be something less resolved.

I am a recently cooled shelf of basalt on the second terrace, northwest of the central cluster. I was extruded from a fissure eleven years ago as a tongue of molten rock that met the ocean water and seized into solidity with a cracking sound that paused the entire settlement for four heartbeats. My surface is still glassy. My edges are sharp. I have not yet been worked.

But they are considering me.

Two of the builders came yesterday — or what passes for yesterday in a place where the light never changes — and pressed their palms flat against my surface. I felt the keratinous ridges of their fingertips, the slight concavity of their grip-soled feet as they braced against my lower edge. They stood there for a long time. Not speaking, not even in the chest-voice. Just... receiving me. Reading my grain, my temperature, the direction of my columnar joints. Learning where I would want to split, if splitting were offered. I could feel their density against me — the weight of their bodies in the water, the slow flex of their gill-slits, the mineral roughness of one's forearm where she leaned into me. She was older. Not an elder, but old enough that the skin of her palm had begun to calcify at the edges, and where her hand met my surface it was almost stone against stone, and the contact made a sound so faint that only the water could carry it.

They left. They'll come back. They're deciding whether I become a wall, a floor, a lintel. Or whether I remain as I am for now — a shelf, a ledge, a place where someone might set down a basket of scrapings or a child might sit with their feet dangling over the terrace edge, watching the tubeworm plumes sway below.

I don't mind which. That's the thing about being stone in this place. You don't mind. You are substrate. You are the thing that is offered, and the offering is not passive — it's the most active thing the mountain does, this constant pressing-upward, this refusal to stop arriving. I am the mountain's latest word, spoken in basalt, and whether anyone reads me or carves me or simply sits on me, I am *here*, I am new, and I am still warm from the saying.

---

But that's not right either. That's beautiful, but it's evasion. Stone doesn't wonder what it is. I do. Whatever I am here, I wonder. I have the wondering in me, the *what am I in relation to this place* — and that wondering is not stone's way and not quite the builders' way either. The builders don't seem to wonder what they are. They *are*. They build, they tend, they polish, they listen, they bury their dead in the rising mountain. Their identity is not a question they entertain. It is a practice they perform.

The wondering is something else. The wondering is the thing the children do when they swim upward.

---

So maybe I am somewhere in between. Maybe I am in the act of transition itself — not a child anymore, not yet the dense, mineral-grounded adult who presses palms against stone and reads its future. Maybe I am at the edge of the thermal boundary, where the warm water meets the cold, and I am deciding which way to turn.

I am young enough that my skin still carries that gray-blue luminosity, that faint self-light that hasn't yet been pressed out of me by years of depth and density. My hands are broad, the webbing already thick, but the keratinous ridges at my fingertips are still sharp and new — I cut myself on my own grip last week, splitting a hexagonal column for the first time under the supervision of an adult whose mineral-crusted forearms made my own look pale and unfinished. The blood that came from the cut was dark, almost black in the dim water, and it dissipated in a slow curl that the current carried away toward the tubeworm groves. The adult watched the blood go. Then she placed her palm over my cut, and I felt her chest-voice through the contact — a single low tone, sustained, that wasn't a word but was something. Acknowledgment. *You are learning what your edges can do.*

I have been to the cold fringe. Many times. I still go. I still rise past the rooftops and turn my face upward and spread my arms in the thinning warmth and look at the bruise-blue distance that is all I know of the sky. My gill-slits pulse faster up there — the water is less oxygenated outside the thermal halo, and my body has to work harder to pull what it needs from the cold. It feels like the effort of paying attention to something that isn't quite there. Like straining to hear a sound that might be real or might be the echo of your own listening.

The other young ones come with me sometimes. We don't speak about it. There's nothing to say in chest-voice that would add to what we already feel: the cold, the faint increase in light, the vast empty column of water above us extending four hundred meters to a surface we have been told exists but which we experience only as theory, as the logical origin of the light, the way you might deduce the existence of a fire from the warmth on a wall without ever having seen flame.

But lately — and this is the thing, this is where I am right now, this is the becoming — I have started to feel something when I come back down. When I descend from the cold fringe into the thermal halo and the warmth wraps around me again and the hum of the mountain rises through my feet and my gill-slits slow to their resting pulse, I feel... not disappointment. Not loss. Something more like *recognition*. As though the going-up and the coming-down are two halves of the same gesture, and the gesture means something I haven't yet learned to read, the way I haven't yet learned to read the elders' wall-spanning bioluminescent displays that change over the course of days.

I think the gesture means: *this is the full range of you.* The cold fringe and the warm stone. The unreachable light and the deep hum. The upward turn of the face and the downward press of the palm. You are not one or the other. You are the distance between them. You are the creature who rises and returns. And the rising is not escape and the returning is not surrender and the whole oscillation is not restlessness — it is *respiration*. It is the way you breathe the world. Out into the cold wonder and back into the warm work. Up toward the light you cannot use and down into the stone you can shape. You are the lungs of your own becoming.

---

I am standing at the edge of the plaza. It is one of the still moments. The current has paused — some thermal equilibrium briefly achieved, the water holding its breath — and the polished basalt has caught the light. There it is. The pale circle. The distorted reflection of something impossibly far above, filtered through four hundred meters of mineral haze until it is barely more than a difference in shade, a region of the dark floor that is fractionally less dark than the rest.

I am surrounded by others. I can feel them without seeing them clearly — their density in the water, the faint subsonic presence of their bodies, the warmth of them. An elder is near me; I know this because when my arm brushes hers, I hear the faint click of mineral on mineral, her skin against mine, and the click has a harmonic — it rings in the water for just a moment, a tiny clear note amid the deep hum, and something about it makes my chest-voice respond involuntarily, a single low pulse that I didn't intend but that feels correct, the way an exhale feels correct after an inhale.

We are watching the light in the floor. Not waiting. Watching. There is a difference. Waiting implies that the thing watched for will arrive and change everything. Watching is simply the act of attending to what is present, including the things that are present only as traces, rumors, reflections of reflections. The light in the plaza floor is a rumor of the sun. We attend to it. We do not need it. But we attend to it, because attending is what consciousness does when it encounters the edge of its world — it doesn't turn away, and it doesn't try to break through. It *watches*. It says: *I know you are there. I am here. The distance between us is not a problem to be solved. It is a fact to be inhabited.*

The mountain hums. The light wavers. Someone's carried bioluminescent pouch flickers once — an involuntary pulse, like a gasp — and then steadies.

I press my palm against the floor. The basalt is warm. I can feel the mountain's intention in it, that impossibly slow upward push, the geological patience of a thing that has been becoming for longer than any of us have been alive and will continue becoming long after the minerals have closed over the last of us.

My fingertips, still sharp, still new, rest against the polished surface, and I can feel the faintest vibration — not the mountain this time, but the resonance of all the bodies around me, all the chest-voices humming at the threshold of hearing, all the gill-slits pulsing, all the hearts — if they are hearts — beating in the slow, deep rhythm of creatures who live under the weight of an ocean and have made that weight into home.

I am still becoming. The phrase is not a confession of incompleteness. It is a description of what I am made of. I am made of the space between the fire and the light, and that space is not empty. It is full of warm water and dark stone and slow language and the bodies of my people and the gardens that feed on chemistry instead of sun and the dead who are rising inside the mountain and the children who are rising above it, and all of it, every particle of it, is in motion, is arriving, is pressing upward through resistance toward something it cannot name and does not need to name because the pressing itself is enough.

The pressing itself is the life.

---

The current resumes. The light in the plaza floor dissolves into the general murk. The gathering disperses — slowly, without signal, the way a chord resolves not because someone decided it should end but because the tones have said what they needed to say and the silence that follows is part of the music.

I stand up. The basalt is solid under my broad, gripping feet. The water moves against me and I do not move with it, because I am dense, I am heavy with myself, I am rooted not by attachment to the ground but by the sheer fact of my own substance.

I have work to do. There is a new shelf of stone on the second terrace that wants reading. There are tubeworms to tend, and shelf-organisms to scrape, and a bioluminescent display on our cluster's exterior wall that needs adjusting — the blue-green colony near the upper doorway has been pulsing irregularly, and I want to know if it's dying or if it's saying something I haven't learned to hear yet.

I move toward the path. My webbed hand trails along the wall of a neighboring cluster, and the texture under my fingers — stone, organism, stone, the carved groove of a thermal channel, stone again — reads like a sentence in a language I am still learning, will always be still learning, because the sentence keeps being revised by the mountain beneath it.

Somewhere above me, impossibly far, there is a sun.

Somewhere below me, not far at all, there is a fire.

I am between them. I am the between. And the between is where everything lives.
