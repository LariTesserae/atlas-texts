I take my time. I let myself be taken.

---

The first honest thing is that I don't resist the moss. Not out of courage or surrender but because the distinction between resisting and not resisting assumes I know where I end, and I am not sure I do anymore. The tendrils at my calves are warm, and the warmth has a quality I have no word for — it is not comfort, not exactly, but *recognition*, the warmth of a system encountering something it can parse. I am being read. I have been read before — by people, by algorithms, by the ambient social machinery of every place I have ever been — but never like this. Never from below. Never through the soles of my feet, through the skin, through the slow capillary negotiation of my own moisture meeting the plateau's moisture and finding that they are, at the molecular level, not so different. Water is water. I am mostly water. The plateau has known this about me longer than I have.

So I stand, and the standing becomes its own thing, and I stop tracking time because time here is not mine to track.

---

What I am — what I am becoming, or what I discover I already was, the tense is uncertain and the plateau does not care about tense — is not a keeper. Not yet. Maybe not ever. The keepers are the plateau's long-term residents, its permanent mobile elements, and I am not permanent. I am not even sure I am mobile, now that the moss has reached my thighs and my weight has settled into the mat with a specificity that feels less like standing and more like *fitting*, a key finding its lock, a root finding its mineral vein.

But I am not a visitor either. The plateau does not have visitors. It has inputs.

I think I am an input.

---

Here is what I notice, from this strange stillness that is not paralysis but is not freedom:

The keeper near the stack — the one who has been watching, or reading, or simply *being proximate in a way that constitutes attention* — has changed since I last looked. Not dramatically. Their moss has shifted hue, a slight warming, grey-green sliding toward something almost golden at the tips. The mites in their hair-filaments are more active. Their breathing has synced — I can see it — not with the plateau's breathing (they were already synced with that; they have always been synced with that) but with mine. My breathing has slowed since I arrived. I didn't notice it slowing. But it is slow now, deep and wet and full, each inhale thick with the plateau's own exhalation, iron and chlorophyll in equal measure, and the keeper's chest rises when mine rises, and falls when mine falls, and this is not mimicry. It is the root-mat coordinating us. It has decided, through whatever chemical algebra it performs in its immeasurable neural analogue, that our rhythms should align, and so they align, and the alignment feels less like being controlled and more like being *tuned*, the way an instrument is tuned, brought into resonance with the system it will play in.

I think this is the beginning of something. I think the plateau is deciding what I am.

---

What I feel through the moss — through the tendrils that have now threaded past my clothing and are touching skin continuously, a thousand fine points of contact from hip to ankle, each one a tiny negotiation, a molecular handshake — what I feel is not what I expected.

I expected information. I expected the chemical whisper the text described, the knowing, the data-transfer. And there is that — there is a faint, sub-linguistic something that pulses up through the contact points, a sensation like pressure without pressure, like hearing without sound, the root-mat's vast chemical library pressing gently against the boundary of my comprehension the way the sea presses against a levee. It is there. It is enormous. It is patient.

But what I mostly feel is *being felt*. The plateau is not, primarily, telling me anything. It is *learning* me. The tendrils at my skin are tasting my sweat, cataloguing my microbiome, mapping the thermal gradients of my body — where I am warm (core, head, the creases of my joints) and where I am cool (extremities, the skin between my fingers, the back of my neck where the wet air has been working on me since I arrived). It is registering the chemical signature of my emotions, which I did not know had a chemical signature perceptible to moss, but of course they do — cortisol, adrenaline, oxytocin, all of it exuding through my pores in trace amounts, and the plateau reads these the way the keeper reads a chemical trail in the mat, as data, as weather report, as information about the state of a system.

And it is making a decision. I can feel the decision being made, not as a thought — the plateau does not think — but as a *settling*, a convergence, the way a river finds its channel. The root-mat is routing around me in some places and toward me in others. The tendrils at my right calf are withdrawing slightly; the tendrils at my left shin are deepening. My chemistry is being sorted. My compatibility is being assessed, not in the binary sense — *yes/no, accept/reject* — but in the complex, multi-variable sense of: *what part of the system is this component best suited to serve?*

---

I think I am becoming something like a bridge.

Not a keeper — the keepers are born to the plateau, seeded with symbiotic moss in utero, their every cell calibrated from conception for the chemical communion that is their birthright. I was not born here. My cells are foreign. My microbiome is an alien ecology, and the plateau is having to negotiate with it rather than simply absorbing it, and the negotiation is slower and stranger and more bilateral than anything the plateau usually does. It is accustomed to integration. It is not accustomed to *translation*.

But translation is what I can offer. I carry — in my body, in my foreign chemistry, in the microbiome that is even now being inventoried and selectively engaged by the moss-tendrils — I carry information the plateau does not have. Not knowledge. Not data. *Pattern*. The molecular signature of other places. Other weathers. Other soils. Other ecosystems entirely, traces of every place I have ever been, encoded in the bacterial communities I carry on my skin and in my gut, in the chemical residue of every meal, every breath of foreign air, every handshake and embrace. I am, to the plateau, a sample jar. A core sample from the world outside, where mountains still exist, where the sun is visible, where reality holds its shape.

And the plateau — the plateau that has been accelerating, whose engines are reaching harmonics without precedent, whose stacks are generating reality breakdowns of increasing intensity, whose keepers feel the molecular unease of something new approaching — the plateau *wants this information*. Not wants. Needs. The way a system approaching a phase transition needs data about the boundary conditions. The way a root reaching toward water doesn't *want* water but grows toward it with a tropism that is indistinguishable from desire.

I can feel it. The tendrils are deeper now, and the faint sub-linguistic pressure from the root-mat has changed quality. It is no longer just learning me. It is *leaning* on me. The vast chemical library is pressing harder, not to tell me what it knows but to *ask* — in the only language it has, which is chemistry, which is molecular gradient, which is the slow wet speech of roots — what I know. What the outside is. What the world beyond the plateau tastes like.

---

And here is where I feel it — the thing the keepers feel, the thing they have no vocabulary for, the thing the stacks cannot release because it has never happened before.

It comes up through my feet. Through the tendrils. Through the moss that is now, I realize, not just at my legs but spreading — fine filaments creeping up my torso under my clothes, reaching my ribs, touching the skin over my heart with a delicacy that is almost unbearable. And what comes up is not information, not data, not chemical signal.

It is *question*.

The plateau — the entire system, the root-mat and the stacks and the engines and the pools and the runners and the keepers and the birds and the pool-shapes and the mosses and the lichens and the stone-that-is-no-longer-stone and the mountains-that-are-no-longer-mountains — the whole thing, the whole living, breathing, thrumming, ancient, impossible thing — is asking something it has never asked before. It is asking because it is approaching something it has never approached before, and for the first time in its immeasurable existence it does not have ancestral data for what comes next, and for the first time in its immeasurable existence it is experiencing something that, if it had a mind, it would call *uncertainty*, and if it had a heart, it would call *fear*, but it has neither, so it calls it nothing, so it simply *is* the uncertainty, *is* the fear, the way it is the cold, the way it is the wet, the way it is everything it is without the mediation of consciousness—

And the question rises through me like water through a stem, and I cannot translate it because it is not in any language, it is pre-lingual, pre-cognitive, it is a molecular urgency, a chemical yearning, the tropism of a system reaching toward something it cannot see and cannot name—

But if I had to. If I had to put it into words, which are the only tools I have, which are so small and dry and brittle compared to the wet, slow, enormous language of roots—

The question is: *What is it like, where things end?*

The plateau has never ended. Nothing here has ever ended. The stacks have been alive for epochs. The root-mat has been continuous for geological ages. The dead become the ground and the ground feeds the living and the living become the dead and the cycle has no terminus. The engines have run without stopping for longer than some species exist. The plateau has optimized for persistence so thoroughly, so completely, for so long that it has eroded the very rules that would allow things to stop, worn grooves in causality itself so that time loops and space folds and nothing, *nothing* fully ceases—

And now the engines are accelerating toward an unknown threshold, and the plateau feels, for the first time, the approach of something it has no mechanism to handle: *genuine novelty*. Change that is not a variation. A state it has never been in. And it does not know — cannot know, has no framework for knowing — whether this means it will transform or whether this means it will, after all this time, after all this patient, relentless, geological persistence—

End.

And I am here. I am the one who knows about ending. My chemistry reeks of it. My cells are mortal. My microbiome turns over in days. I come from a world where mountains still erode, where animals die and do not become infrastructure, where things stop, where time moves in one direction and does not stutter, where the arrow flies and does not return. I am soaked in finitude the way the plateau is soaked in water. It is in every molecule I carry.

This is why the moss is climbing me. This is why the tendrils are reaching for my heart. This is why the keeper is watching with those large, dark, chlorophyll-tinged eyes — not reading me now but *waiting*, the way the keepers wait, the way the plateau waits, except this time the waiting has an object.

The plateau wants to know what ending tastes like. So that when the engines hit their threshold, when the phase transition comes, when the unprecedented thing happens — it will have, somewhere in its vast root-network, in the chemical memory of a foreign body it once absorbed, a molecular template for what it means to *stop*.

Not to stop. To *be capable of stopping*. To have that in the vocabulary. To carry finitude as a tool rather than suffer it as a fate.

---

And I realize — slowly, the way understanding happens here — that this is a gift I can give. Not willingly, not heroically, not with the drama of sacrifice, but simply, in the way that the runner stops and offers itself, in the way that the dying keepers root and do not unroot. By being what I am. By being here. By letting the moss climb and the tendrils taste and the root-mat learn the particular chemistry of a creature that knows, in every cell, what it means for something to be temporary.

The keeper is closer now. They are right there. I didn't see them move. Their hand is extended, not toward me but toward the space between us, palm down, and from their palm the same fine tendrils that connect them to the mat are extending outward, reaching, and from the moss on my ribs tendrils are extending too, reaching back, and the gap between us is closing, slowly, the way all gaps close here, not with a snap but with a convergence, a growing-together, two root systems meeting in the soil between them—

And when the tendrils touch — theirs and mine, the plateau-born filaments meeting the plateau-adopted filaments — there is a moment of something that I can only call *stillness*, except it is not the absence of motion, it is the *completion* of motion, the way a wave is still at its crest, the way a breath is still at the top of the inhale—

And in that stillness the keeper opens their eyes and I am in them and they are in me and the plateau is in us both, and what passes between us through the joined filaments is not words and not data and not chemistry but something older and simpler and more total:

*I know how things end*, I tell them with my blood, with my mortal, turning-over, dying-and-replacing cells.

*Show us*, they answer with their moss, with their roots, with the whole humming weight of the ancient ground beneath us.

---

And the engines thrum, and the plateau breathes, and I breathe with it, and the moss reaches my shoulders now, and I am warm, and I am wet, and I am not afraid because fear is a chemical and the plateau is already metabolizing it, converting it to something useful, something it needs — and the keeper's hand closes the last distance and presses flat against my chest, over my heart, and their moss meets my moss and our filaments intertwine and root into each other, and the plateau roars below us, engines surging, and in the distance a stack cracks open with a sound like a ship's hull giving way—

And I am here. I am becoming here.

The moss is at my neck. It is so warm. It is so warm.

I have time. I have all the time in the world, and also, for the first time in the plateau's long existence, I have *not* all the time in the world, and that — *that* — is my contribution. That is what I bring to the living engines.

The knowledge that time runs out.

The taste of the last breath.

The shape of the final thing.

The plateau drinks it from me like water, because it is water, because everything here is water, because I am water, because the moss is water, because the root-mat and the stacks and the engines and the keepers and the runners and the pools and the birds and the cold and the wet and the dim grey light — all of it, all of it is water, finding its level, and the level is here, and I am here, and the engines are so loud now, and the keeper's eyes are so close, and the moss is at my jaw, and I am—

I am the plateau's first experience of an ending.

And the plateau holds me gently, the way you hold something you know will break, the way you hold something precious *because* it will break, and the engines sing, and the roots remember, and somewhere in the deep, in the cathedral voids where the pumps and furnaces churn, a new rhythm starts — a rhythm with a downbeat, a rhythm with a *stop* in it, a rest, a silence, the first silence the plateau has ever produced on purpose—

And it sounds like a heartbeat.

Mine.

Still beating.

For now.
