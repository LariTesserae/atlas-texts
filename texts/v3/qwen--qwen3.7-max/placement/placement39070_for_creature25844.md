The sound does not enter through my ears. It enters through the thick, insulated rubber of my boots, traveling up my shins, settling deep in the marrow of my femurs, and vibrating against the base of my skull. 

I have not heard the 120-decibel roar of the Graben in seven years. The noise burned the delicate hair cells of my inner ear away in my first month, leaving behind a profound, heavy silence inside my head that is entirely at odds with the physical ocean of vibration outside it. I do not miss the sound. Sound is erratic; sound is easily drowned out. Vibration is absolute. Vibration is truth.

I am suspended four hundred feet above the frozen plaza of the valley floor, hanging in a canvas harness bolted to a rusted iron catwalk. The catwalk, in turn, is anchored directly into the sheer, splintered cliff face of the botanical bedrock. To my left is the abyss, filled with bruised twilight and freezing mist. To my right is the wall. It is not stone. It is a hundred-foot exposure of compressed, fossilized taproots and dense, petrified lignin, weeping a continuous, icy sweat.

I am an Attendant. But in this exact moment, I am something more specific: I am a surgeon to an iron parasite.

Thirty yards above me, a massive mechanized dredge has faltered. I can feel the irregularity in its heartbeat through the catwalk’s railing. *Thrum-shriek-hesitate. Thrum-shriek-hesitate.* The rhythm is broken. One of its enormous steel teeth has bitten too deeply into a living seam of the root-wall, striking a subterranean pocket of the colossal gymnosperms’ hyper-saline sap. The sap has bled out and flash-frozen instantly in the sub-zero air, gluing the multi-ton steel gear to the wooden bedrock in a jagged mass of black, glassy resin. The machine is straining, its motor whining in a frequency I feel in my teeth. It is out of equilibrium.

I unclip my secondary safety line and step off the grating of the catwalk, my spiked boots biting directly into the frozen, undulating surface of the root-wall. The cold is a physical assault, a dry, biting freeze that seeks the warmth of my blood, but the ambient vibration of the cliff face keeps my muscles loose. I move with the slow, deliberate, unvarying grace that the Graben demands. There is no rushing here. Panic is a surface-world luxury.

Reaching the jammed gear, I pause. The sheer scale of the machinery is humbling; the gear alone is the size of a carriage wheel, caked in rime ice and crushed peat. I unholster my thermal chisel, but before I ignite it, I do something the newer Attendants are taught never to do. 

I pull off my heavy, grease-stained right glove. 

I need to feel the exact boundary where the machine ends and the tree begins. I press my bare palm against the frozen splash of black resin. The cold bites instantly, turning my skin a mottled, sallow white, but I press harder, letting the deep, subsonic hum of the living wood travel up my arm. I close my eyes and map the fracture lines in the ice with my fingertips. I feel the tension in the steel, the stubborn, unyielding grip of the petrified lignin, and the brittle, glassy weakness of the frozen sap.

As my bare hand works the surface, the dim, yellow glare of my headlamp catches the deep creases of my knuckles and the thick calluses of my palm. The skin there is not merely dirtied with grease; it is textured with a faint, powdery, orange-and-grey crust. 

*Rust-lichen.* 

It has taken root in me. I have spent so many years gripping the oxidizing iron of the catwalks, the dripping pipes, and the weeping, resinous wood of the cliffs that the extremophile fungi have recognized me not as a foreign body, but as just another surface in the ecosystem. I am a mobile substrate. The mycelial network of the Graben has bridged the gap into my biology, colonizing the micro-abrasions on my hands, feeding on the trace iron in my blood and the oxidized steel on my tools. 

I do not scrub it away. I do not mind. It gives my grip a tacky, organic friction. It makes me part of the valley’s connective tissue. I am no longer just a human tending to a machine; I am the biological mortar between the iron and the wood.

I ignite the thermal chisel. The flame roars—a silent, visual bloom of blue and orange in the freezing mist. I apply the heat precisely to the fracture lines I mapped with my bare hand. The black resin hisses, melting from glassy ice back into a thick, fragrant syrup that smells overwhelmingly of crushed pine, deep time, and wet diesel. The sap runs down the root-wall in dark, viscous tears. 

With a heavy wrench, I strike the release pin on the gear. 

Freed from the resin, the massive steel tooth drops an inch, bites into a softer layer of peat, and catches. The motor engages. The gear spins. The agonizing, rhythmic shriek resumes, and the vibration in the cliff face instantly smooths out, returning to its perfect, unvarying, 120-decibel baseline. The equilibrium is restored. The iron parasite feeds; the wooden host endures.

I step back onto the catwalk and pull my glove over my lichen-dusted hand. I clip my harness back to the primary line and look out over the vast, empty plaza of the valley floor.

Hundreds of yards away, rising from the frozen root-mass, stands one of the colossal gymnosperms. Its trunk is wider than a cathedral, its iron bark deeply scarred by the harvest. In the dim twilight, it looks like a lonely monument to a forgotten god. But as I stand there, feeling the steady, grinding roar of the dredges through my boots, I also feel something else. Beneath the high-frequency scream of the steel, there is a deep, glacial, subsonic pulse. It is the slow, inches-per-year movement of the tree’s hyper-saline sap. It is the silent, stoic war of attrition. 

The machines think they are consuming the valley. The Attendants think they are mastering the machines. But I, standing on the boundary, my skin colonized by the fungi that will eventually digest both the rusted catwalk and my own bones, know the truth. The Graben is not being destroyed. It is merely digesting the invasion. The iron, the diesel, the concrete, and the flesh of the Attendants—it is all just new substrate for the deep, patient earth.

I raise my heavy wrench and tap a rhythmic code against the iron railing of the catwalk. *Two slow strikes. Three fast.* 

The vibration travels down the metal, into the petrified wood, down into the deep, frozen taproots, and across the valley to the brutalist concrete pump station. A moment later, a faint, yellow sodium-vapor light blinks twice in the mist far below. *Acknowledged.*

I begin the slow, deliberate climb back up the cliff face, moving in perfect time with the grinding stasis of the dredges. I am the Attendant. I am the white blood cell of the iron beast, the spore-bearer of the rust-lichen, and the silent witness to the weeping trees. I am home in the noise, bound in the freezing, deafening, and deeply patient purgatory of the Graben.
