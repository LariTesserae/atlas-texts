I would not be found in the soft, uniform gloom of the upper lattice, nor would I be dancing across the razor-thin ridges of the sunlit cliffs. I would be far below the woven reed bridges and the hollowed-out log aqueducts, down in the absolute, crushing black beneath the floorboards. I would be in the primary drainage channel, wedged into the deepest, most violent fracture of the unweathered basalt. 

I would be the Anchor. The biological fulcrum of the basin’s hydro-mechanical soul.

Down here, where the diffuse overcast light cannot reach, my body has diverged from the slate-grey, lichen-mimicking forms of my kin above. My skin is the color of the abyssal rock—a slick, lightless black, sheened with the thickest layer of insulating, lanolin-like oil. The large, light-gathering black eyes characteristic of our people have atrophied in the perpetual night; my heavy, translucent nictitating membranes have fused and calcified into smooth, blind domes of pale keratin. I do not need to see the shifting geometry of the town. I feel it. 

My limbs are locked in a permanent, flowing tension. My unusually long, multi-jointed fingers and toes are splayed wide, wrapping around the jagged, glassy edges of the master cleft. The keratinous pads on my palms and soles are thicker here than anywhere else in the settlement, hyper-calcified by years of immense, unyielding pressure. I have been wedged in this primary fissure for so long that the tough, pale-yellow wire-moss has begun to root in the microscopic dust trapped in the deep creases of my joints. 

I am wrapped around the grand counterweight—the massive, water-logged wooden baffle that governs the primary hydrostatic release of the entire basin. While the others above flow and shift in a continuous, syncopated martial art, adjusting their weight as the architecture breathes, I am the stillness. I am the fixed point.

The water down here does not hiss; it groans. It moves with the sluggish, immense weight of the subterranean table. Pressed flat against the wet wood of the master baffle, my deeply ridged, barrel-shaped chest acts as a secondary sensor. I feel the hydrostatic pressure building in the deep bedrock before it ever reaches the upper flumes. 

As the water table swells, the pressure pushes against the wood. The braided sinew lashings stretch, groaning in the dark. I breathe in—a slow, massive expansion of my lungs, drawing in the freezing, moisture-heavy air. As I exhale, a brief, pale plume of breath escapes my lips, instantly snatched away by the rushing undercurrent. 

With the exhalation, I shift my weight. Just a fraction of an inch. 

My calcified pads grind against the basalt. The friction transfers through my dense bones, into the wooden baffle, and down into the stone pivot. *Clack.* The heavy stone drops into a deeper notch. The pressure is relieved. The water rushes into the overflow channels, diverted safely into the abyssal cracks of the young bedrock. Above me, I feel the vibration of a thousand shifting floorboards, a thousand adjusting reed gates, rippling down through the lattice. The town settles. 

They know I am here. The inhabitants do not visit the deep channels—the gaps are too narrow, the water too fast and violently cold for their delicate foraging. But they speak to me. Through the hollowed logs, through the wet wood and the dry-stacked slate, I feel their chest-rumbling hums and the rhythmic tapping of their hardened fingernails. It is a language of pure resonance. I feel the specific hydrostatic pressure of their sectors. I know the tender of the eastern flume is anxious; her hum is tight, vibrating at a higher frequency against the stone. I know the weaver of the northern kelp-mats is resting; his resonance is a slow, rolling thrum. 

I answer not with a vocalization, but with a shift. I tap my keratinous heel against the master stone. A deep, resonant *thud* travels up the cliff face, vibrating in the sternums of the elders above. *I am here. The balance holds.*

In the quiet intervals between the surges, I am not entirely alone. The blind, white crustaceans scuttle over my oiled skin, picking microscopic dust and algae from my deep grooves. I do not eat them; we have an understanding, the deep-dwellers. Occasionally, a translucent water-skimmer is washed down from the upper sluices, tumbling through the dark. I catch it with a flick of a long, grooved finger, crushing it gently to drink its rich, oily nutrients. It is a rare, fleeting taste of the overcast light I no longer see.

When the subterranean water table reaches its absolute peak, the pressure is immense. The master baffle shudders, the wood bowing under the weight of the weeping cliffs. If I fail, the primary flume will burst. The delicate equilibrium of the lattice above will shatter, and the town will drown in its own mechanics. 

In these moments, I close my blind, keratinous eyes and sink deeper into the cleft. I expand my chest to its absolute limit, the ridges pressing hard against the wet wood. I become the stone. I become the water. I push against the shifting world, holding the violent, infant geology at bay with nothing but bone, oil, and friction. 

Above me, the town breathes out in a massive, collective exhalation of shifting architecture. I feel my kin curling into their narrow, dry fissures as the bruised purple light fades. They pull their knees to their chests, closing their dark eyes, sleeping in perfect time with the endless washing of the basin. 

I do not sleep. I remain in the dark, my hands gripping the razor-thin edges of the world, listening to the deep, hollow settling of the stone, waiting for the water to rise again.
