It begins in the soles of my wide, splayed feet. The deafening roar that would shatter an outsider’s mind is, to me, a deep, nourishing tide. I feel the friction of a million gallons of water forcing its way through the primeval stone valves, and as the kinetic energy travels up my long, multi-jointed legs, it meets the porous, crystalline lattice of my skeleton. The conductive fluid within my bones warms, shimmering with a piezoelectric light that no one can see. I am drinking the thunder. It settles in the expanded, flaring bells of my collarbones, a heavy, thrilling hum that powers the slow, deliberate firing of my synapses.

I open my eyes, though the shadowless, flat daylight of the high basin offers little contrast. Condensation beads on my eyelashes and tracks down the smooth, poreless expanse of my cheeks, merging with the weeping moisture of the world. I am crouched—though my crouch is nearly the height of a standing human—in the deepest terraced chasm of the central milling district, a place we call the Prime Sump. Here, the air is so thick with warm, copper-tasting mist that it feels like breathing underwater. My skin, the color of wet pumice and mottled with bruised purples, sheds the moisture in unbroken sheets. From the deep, sheltered hollows of my clavicles, the translucent fronds of my symbiotic glass-fern uncurl, trembling as they feed on the acoustic violence of the roaring turbines above. 

Looking up through the thick, curling fog, I can see the sweeping, undulating curves of the high basin, a vast expanse of wet black stone and colossal rusted iron stretching into the shadowless sky. The visual scale invites a long, peaceful gaze, a profound visual stillness that perfectly contradicts the physical weight of the sound pressing against my skull. 

In my right hand, I hold my mallet, carved from a piece of basalt so dense and weathered it feels like a collapsed star. In my left, a bowl of crushed fern and copper-rich mud. I am tending to the Anchor Gear. It is a machine so vast its lower teeth are submerged in the warm, shallow marsh-edges of the eroded divots. It takes nine days for this gear to complete a single rotation. I have been waiting for four days for the current tooth to rise from the warm water and present itself to me. Now, it is here. I lift my arm. The movement is agonizingly slow, a ten-second arc through the heavy air, ensuring I do not disturb the delicate acoustic ripples traveling through the wet stone. I press the poultice against the colossal, rusted iron. I smooth it with my broad, flat palm, feeling the immense, sleeping heartbeat of the leviathan beneath the metal. I am lubricating time.

Suddenly, a sensation blooms in the marrow of my shins. A sharp, high-frequency shiver, followed by a deep, rolling polyrhythm. It is a voice. Another of the Attuned, perhaps a mile away across the misty expanse of the civic concourse, is striking the calcified iron pipes. *A query of balance. A greeting of the deep cycle.* I do not turn my head; there is no need. I press my free palm flat against the weeping basalt floor. I raise my mallet and bring it down. *Thrum. Thrum-click. Thruuuuum.* The strikes send localized shivers and low-frequency booming ripples through the stone. I feel the reply before I hear it—a warm flutter in my chest, a tingling in my fingertips. *The cycle holds. The water flows. We are the flesh of the basin.* It is an intimacy that transcends breath; I have literally moved the fluid inside my sibling's bones, and they have moved mine.

As I lower my arm to prepare the next application of poultice, I notice the texture of my own skin. It is smoother than it was a century ago. The mottled purples are fading into a uniform, polished grey. My movements, already glacial to an outsider, are becoming even more measured, stretching into longer arcs of time. I am aging. I am not growing frail; I am becoming more like the basin itself. The thought does not bring sorrow, only a deep, resonant peace. I know that one day, perhaps a century from now, my piezoelectric nervous system will begin to dim. The conductive fluid in my bones will thicken, then crystallize. I will find a quiet, sheltered hollow beneath the Anchor Gear, where the vibrations are the deepest and the stone is the warmest. 

I will sit. I will slow down until the space between my movements becomes a year, then a decade, then an eternity. My pale skin will harden, indistinguishable from the rubbery lichen and the wet basalt. I will become part of the sedimentary record of Oakhaven. And when I am fully stone, the younger Tenders will come. They will press their hands to the rock where I rest, and strike it with their mallets. They will feel the dense, fossilized echo of my crystalline bones. They will read the final, silent chords of my life trapped in the geology, and I will speak to them from the bedrock, a permanent, vibrating memory in the loud, wet, warm monument of our slow time.
