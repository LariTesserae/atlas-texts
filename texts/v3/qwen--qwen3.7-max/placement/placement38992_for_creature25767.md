I do not see the dark. I wear it. 

To you, the absolute blackness of the sunken chamber would be a void, a terrifying absence of light. But to me, the dark is a dense, textured medium, as rich and navigable as water. My eyes are sealed beneath the smooth, domed cowl of my head, useless evolutionary ghosts. Instead, I read the world through the million trembling cilia of my velvet coat. Every shift in the freezing air, every microscopic eddy of condensation, every faint thermal updraft rising from the weeping walls maps itself against my skin. 

I am heavy. My hydrostatic limbs are thick, swollen with the pale, viscous sap of the Mother-Root. When I move, it is not with the frantic, darting grace of the newly hatched, who drift above me in the micro-currents like gossamer snow. My movements are tectonic. Deliberate. Gravitational. I am one of the Elders, and my mass is a weight the city relies upon.

I am clinging to the sheer, curving wall of the central taproot, halfway up the highest spire of the micro-city. Beneath my broad, spatulate foot-pads, the microscopic setae grip the slick, weeping bark with absolute security. I can feel the slow, deep thermogenic rhythm of the plant pulsing through the wood—a heartbeat that takes an hour between beats. My own pale-blue blood pumps in time with it, carrying the geothermal heat of the deep earth into my extremities, keeping the freezing damp at bay.

Suddenly, a tremor shudders through the wood. 

It is not the rhythmic *plink* of condensation, nor the familiar creak of settling burls. It is a deep, agonizing groan from the living wall. The Mother is shifting. 

Through the soles of my feet, I read the disaster before it happens. A colossal lateral root, thick as a river, is swelling with a sudden rush of sap. It is pushing outward, blind and mindless, driven by the violent, crushing vitality of the flora. It is growing directly toward the primary suspension cables of the Lower Nave. If the swelling root breaks those cables, three tiers of woven terraces and gourd-dwellings will collapse into the freezing slush below.

I strike the main artery-vine beside me with a heavy, muscled forelimb. 
*Thwack. Thrummm. Snap-snap.*

The vibration shoots down the rope, through the scaffolding, and into the living wall. *Brace. The Mother pushes. Hold the lower lines.*

Instantly, I feel the city answer. It is a roaring symphony of tension. A wave of tightened vines ripples through the infrastructure as hundreds of mid-sized weavers lock their joints and pull. The scaffolding shivers, bracing against the swell. 

I move. I scramble down the sheer face of the taproot, my heavy body dropping with controlled, terrifying speed, my pads catching and releasing the bark in a blur of silent motion. I reach the swelling root just as it begins to press against the primary lashing. The wood is weeping hot sap, burning against my cold-adapted skin. 

I wrap my massive, deeply scarred arms around the critical joint. I dig my pads into the weeping bark and I pull. 

My hydrostatic muscles scream. The pale-blue fluid inside me boils with effort. I am not trying to break the root; we do not hack, we do not chop. This is ecological aikido. I secrete a thick, numbing resin from my pores, rubbing it into the root’s weeping eyes, soothing the growth, coaxing the fibers. I use my immense weight to bend the younger, pliable wood, redirecting the swell upward, away from the cables. I am the living winch. I am the nervous system of the brainless giant.

Slowly, agonizingly, the root yields. It curves. The cables hold. The terraces are safe.

But the effort has cost me. 

As I release my grip, I feel the pale-blue fluid in my veins slowing. The geothermal heat is fading from my extremities. The deep, profound chill of the chamber, which I have kept at bay for decades, finally pierces my core. I feel the stiffness creeping into my joints, the dropping of my internal pressure. 

I am old. My translucent skin is mapped with the pale, milky scars of a thousand redirected growths. I look at the lashing I have just saved. The dried sinew is frayed; the bark-strips are rotting from the weeping sap. It needs a new anchor. It needs permanence.

I do not retreat to a hollowed burl to rest. I know what this creeping chill means. It is not a death. It is a change of state.

I adjust my grip. I wrap my thick, heavy torso around the critical junction, intertwining my limbs with the frayed ropes, pressing my faceless cowl against the weeping wood. I make myself the knot. 

And I begin to let go of the heat.

The freezing air rushes in, biting and absolute. I do not shiver. I exhale my internal warmth into the wood, offering my last thermogenic breath to the Mother-Root. As the temperature of my body drops to match the near-freezing air, my translucent skin clouds over, turning opaque, then hard. The moisture leaves my velvet down, the million sensory cilia locking together into a dense, waterproof mat. My pale-blue blood thickens, crystallizes, and stops.

I am desiccating. I am becoming the sinew.

But as my biological life ends, my consciousness does not fade. It expands. 

As my flesh turns to tough, leathery husk, my tactile awareness bleeds into the architecture. I feel the tension of the ropes merging with my hardened limbs. I feel the pulse of the Mother-Root beating against my dried chest. I become the literal mortar that holds the joint together, my body acting as the flexible, unbreakable seal that keeps the weeping sap from rotting the ropes.

I am the anchor. I am the joint.

High above, the young drift on the thermals, their gossamer down brushing my hardened shell like a phantom wind. Below, the mid-sized weavers scurry along the bridges, their spatulate pads gripping the wood. 

One of them strikes a taut vine to sing the shift-change. The vibration travels down the rope, through the scaffolding, and into me. I vibrate with their voices. I hold the weight of the spire. In the pitch dark, in the freezing wet, wrapped in the damp cold and the weeping condensation, I am the silent, unbreaking center of the world.
