I do not wake; I merely shift my attention from the deep, geological rhythm of the stone to the frantic, syncopated pulse of the iron. 

My jaw is locked tight against the primary housing of Turbine Seven. The metal is freezing, yet it burns with the friction of a million turning gears. I have no external ears to be deafened by the roar of the sky-basin. Instead, the sound enters me through my mandible, traveling up the dense, reinforced bones of my skull, cascading down my heavy collarbones, and blooming in the hollow of my chest cavity. 

It is a symphony of violence. I feel the sub-aquasonic thunder of the caldera’s currents slamming into the polished, whale-back granite of the peneplain. I feel the high-pitched, tearing shriek of cavitation bubbles collapsing in the slipstream. And beneath it all, I feel the heavy, rhythmic, sixty-hertz heartbeat of the turbine. 

I am a *Resonator*. Among the Caldera Born, I am one of the few whose skeleton has calcified enough, whose bones have grown dense and resonant enough, to read the deep, hidden faults of the ancient machines. I do not use tools to diagnose the iron; I use my own marrow.

I pull my face away from the housing. The water immediately grabs me, a freezing, muscular hand trying to rip me from my anchor. My elongated, double-jointed fingers, tipped with hardened, blunt nails, dig into the microscopic pits etched into the rock by the kelp’s bio-acid. I hold fast. 

I look at my hands. In the fractured, blinding shafts of high-altitude sunlight that manage to pierce this deep, my skin is a ghostly, translucent pearl. Through it, you can see the dense, dark webbing of my veins, pumping blood that is thick and slow, laden with natural antifreeze. The cold here is not merely a temperature; it is a physical architecture. It builds walls of numbness and slows the mind to a glacial crawl, but my biology has weaponized the chill. I am beautiful in a brutal, utilitarian way. I am built for the drag. I am built for the freeze.

Across my chest, I wear a harness woven from the thickest bull-whip kelp. The elastic polymers of the leathery fronds stretch and snap taut with every surge of the current, storing the kinetic energy of the water. Bolted to the kelp are brass pressure-gauges and copper calipers, green with verdigris, salvaged from the drowned eras of the First Intake. 

Closing my eyes, I press my jaw back to the iron and search for the dissonance. There. A slight, shuddering skip in the turbine’s rhythm. A micro-fracture in the tertiary drive shaft. It is a wound in the beast.

I unclip my heavy basalt mallet from my hip. I do not strike the machine to fix it; I strike it to sing to it, to call the heavy scavengers. 

*Thrum. Thrum. Clack.*

The seismic waves punch through the hyper-oxygenated water, a low-frequency drumbeat that travels for miles. Almost immediately, the shadows in the emerald gloom of the kelp forest shift. The armored crustaceans emerge. They are massive, their carapaces plated in pitted, oxidized iron, looking like detached, crawling chunks of the turbine itself. They scuttle up the housing, their heavy mandibles clicking against the metal. They taste the weeping rust, sensing the fractured iron. They will pack the wound with a mortar of chewed metal and bio-resin, healing the machine as they feed. Symbiosis. 

My work here is done. I release my grip and let the slipstream take me. 

This is the joy of the Caldera Born. To fight the current is to die of exhaustion. To surrender to it is to fly. 

I angle my body, folding my arms tight against my chest, and let the violent upwelling catch the broad plane of my back. I am launched upward, rocketing through the dense, layered thickets of the rubbery kelp forests. The bull-whip fronds snap and whip past me, releasing their stored kinetic fury. I weave through the labyrinth, dodging the massive, synchronized schools of silver fish. They part for me, a flashing chrome curtain, their electroreception sensing the heavy, warm-blooded mass of my body. They draft off my wake, surfing the turbulence I leave behind.

I am rising toward the ceiling. The water grows brighter, more chaotic. The millions of aerated bubbles dragged down from the surface become thicker, a dense, sparkling snow falling upward. The noise shifts from a deep, chest-rattling hum to a physical, tearing roar. 

I pass a torpedo-predator. It is anchored behind a smooth, rounded mound of basalt, its sleek, mucous-coated skin shimmering in the dappled light. It watches me with dark, vibration-sensitive eyes, feeling my passage through its jawbones. We acknowledge each other—two warm-blooded masters of the slipstream—and it turns its attention back to the silver fish, waiting for the perfect moment to launch into the downdraft.

I kick my powerful, webbed legs, driving myself into the highest layer of the sky-basin. 

I breach the shadow-eddies and enter the true chaos of the surface layer. The water here is a blinding, churning froth, a roiling ceiling of white-water. I reach up, my calloused fingers breaking the surface tension.

For a single, breathless second, my hand is in the air. 

The cold of the stratosphere bites into my wet skin. The air is impossibly thin, sharp, and empty. I look up through the roaring, bubble-choked ceiling. The sky is a deep, bruised violet-blue. It is the color of the void. It is a beautiful, lethal emptiness, a stark reminder of the extreme hostility of the world beyond the water. The air above is a myth; the surface is a boundary. Life, true and explosive life, belongs to the deep, to the roar, to the iron and the stone.

I pull my hand back down into the screaming embrace of the caldera. I exhale a stream of silver bubbles, adjust my grip on my basalt mallet, and angle my body back toward the deep. 

There is a new dissonance vibrating through the bedrock, a faint, metallic cough echoing from the eastern intake grate. The ancient beast is struggling. I must go and listen.
