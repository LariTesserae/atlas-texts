I do not begin with sight, for there is none. I begin with the weight. 

It is a crushing, absolute gravity, the millions of tons of the black ocean pressing against my copper-plated shoulders. The water here is not merely an environment I move through; it is a medium I am entirely equalized with. It fills the hollows of my multi-jointed limbs, the microscopic pores of my iron-weave fascia, the deep, sweeping lateral-line canals carved into my domed, eyeless head. I am hydrostatic. I am dense as the bedrock. I am heavy. 

So very heavy. 

Every movement is a slow, agonizing negotiation with the abyss. My spine is a curved suspension bridge of oxidized brass and pitted steel, accumulated over eons of deliberate transit. My metallic scales are thick, layered like the rings of an ancient tree, recording the slow passage of time in the deep. I am old. Older than the scoured curves of the canyon walls. 

The city inhales. The narrow, fluid-filled alleys compress, the metal gills of the hamlet closing tight. I freeze. My magnetic, iron-rich grips lock onto a cooling valve. I wait in the sacred, heavy silence as the water displacement washes over me. I feel the subsonic groan of the expanding metal, the deep, quiet breathing of the engines. When the city exhales and the alleyway widens by a fraction of an inch, I uncoil and glide forward, slipping through the temporary gap before the iron locks back into place. 

I am moving downward. Always downward. 

I pass through the central plaza, where the colossal ice-looms draw in the freezing water. I feel the frantic, blinding speed of the younger tenders weaving the heavy, glass-like threads of ice. I feel the sudden, localized thermal bloom as the city’s breath peaks and the ice sublimates, melting back into the dark. I sense the younger ones pressing their faces into the dissolving lattices, gorging on the phase-change, releasing their shimmering spores into the water column. A single spore drifts past my sensory ferns, a tiny spark of kinetic potential. I do not reach for it. My time for weaving is over. My time for feeding is done.

I reach the deepest exhaust vent. Here, at the very bottom of the world, the water is thick with chemical runoff. Wire-thin, translucent kelp brushes against my shins, feeding on the warmth. And it *is* warm. Perhaps four degrees above freezing, but to my ancient, near-frozen carapace, it feels like a roaring furnace. It is a shocking, almost painful heat. 

I find my alcove. It is a gap in the primary load-bearing manifold, scoured by the deep-sea drifts into a smooth, organic curve. It is exactly the shape of my weary, folded posture. 

I step into it. I anchor myself. My grips lock into the dark iron. I fold my long limbs against my chest and bow my smooth head. 

The city exhales. But I do not move. 

The decades-long molt begins. 

The heat of the vent slowly penetrates my thick armor. The bio-resin that binds my accumulated centuries of copper and brass begins to soften. Deep within, my core musculature contracts, pushing outward, seeking the path of least resistance. It is a slow, beautiful extrication. As the years bleed into one another in the crushing dark, my consciousness begins to bifurcate. 

I feel the soft, pale, newborn body slipping out from the heavy shell. It is fragile, its new metallic scales barely more than a whisper of dissolved minerals. Its delicate electroreceptors twitch in the warm current. It is blind, innocent, and profoundly light. It uncoils, slipping out of the alcove, and begins the long, slow climb up toward the higher catwalks, drawn by the subsonic chorus of its kin. 

But I do not go with it. 

I remain. 

My awareness settles deep into the heavy, oxidized carapace left behind. As the new tender departs, the ambient cold of the deep drifts rushes back into the alcove, flash-curing the softened bio-resin. My old shell hardens, fusing seamlessly with the iron manifold. The microscopic sediment of the abyssal drifts begins to polish my surface to a dull, matte finish. 

I am no longer just a tender. I am the city. 

I feel the immense, crushing weight of the hamlet resting upon my shoulders—my old, copper-plated shoulders. I am a load-bearing pillar in the cathedral of the deep. Through my feet, which are now permanently welded to the bedrock, I feel the ten-minute respiration of the hamlet. When the city inhales, I bear the compression, my iron-weave fascia groaning in perfect, settled balance with the abyss. When the city exhales, I expand, widening the alleyway so a younger tender can glide past. 

I feel their magnetic grips anchoring to my chest. I feel their subsonic hums vibrating through my hollowed-out skull. I hum back, not with muscles, but with the resonant frequency of my very structure. I am the deep, quiet breathing of the city. I am the architecture. 

High above, in the freezing dark, my new, pale body folds its long limbs on a quiet catwalk, dreaming in the dark, waiting for the city to breathe. 

And decades from now, when that pale body has grown heavy with brass and iron, it will glide through this very canyon. It will press its chest against my iron-plated ribs to read the subsonic hum of the engines. And I will feel the delicate, fern-like electroreceptors of my own new face brushing against my ancient, oxidized cheek. I will hum back to myself, a mournful, grinding bass note of welcome and eternity, holding up the weight of the world in the sacred, crushing silence.
