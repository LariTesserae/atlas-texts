I draw the thin, sharp air deep into my barrel chest, holding it for a long, measured count before letting it slip past my lips in a silent, white plume. My ribcage flares wide, the profound bellows of my lungs extracting life from the rarefied atmosphere. I am suspended on the north-facing escarpment of the Seventh Tier, a sheer, unweathered drop of two thousand feet of razor-sharp basalt beneath my soft-soled boots. 

I do not look down. To look down into the jagged V-valleys is to invite the vertigo that claims the careless. Instead, my pale, slate-grey eyes—shielded by heavy brow ridges and thick, dense lashes—track the weeping cliff face inches from my nose. 

I am a Fault-Reader. Before the masons bring the fire, before the heavy timber levers are set, before a single antler pick strikes the stone, I must find the mountain’s hidden tensions. The geology of the Spine is young, thrust upward in violent, planet-scale convulsions. The rock is hard, fractured, and full of stored kinetic energy. If we build our hearths and fire-set the wrong face, the tension will release catastrophically, shearing away entire terraces and plunging hundreds of dwellings into the abyss. I am here to read the stone’s memory of its own violent birth, and to find the exact seams where it wishes to break.

I press my palms flat against the freezing, damp basalt. The microscopic ridges on my thick, splayed callouses catch the frost-slicked micro-textures of the stone. I do not just touch the rock; I read it. Through the supple, moss-stuffed soles of my ungulate-hide boots, I feel the subtle temperature gradients of the cliff. The cold sinks deeper where the rock is solid, but where the hidden faults lie, the weeping moisture seeps through, creating micro-fissures of slightly warmer, iron-cold meltwater. 

I shift my weight. Left hand, right foot, left foot. A fluid, unconscious geometry of three-point contact. I do not climb; I pour myself over the overhang, my dense, compact body acting as a perfect counterweight against the dizzying drop. My heavy, oiled hide shell sheds the persistent, chilling mist, while the quilted pockets of raptor down in my mid-layer trap my hard-won heat. 

In my right hand, I hold a polished basalt tapping-stone, worn smooth by the thumbs of three generations of my lineage. I press my left hand firmly against a patch of black crustose lichen, anchoring myself, and strike the rock with the tapping-stone. 

*Clink.* 

The sound is soft, instantly swallowed by the damp moss, the packed snow, and the vast, freezing silence of the mountain. The air holds the noise poorly, and the vastness swallows the echo. But I do not need to hear it. I feel the resonance. It travels through the stone, up my arm, through the thick, rich blood in my veins, and settles deep in my dense bones. 

A solid block rings with a dull, dead thud. But here, near the upper vertex of the overhang, the stone sings with a high, tight, almost imperceptible hum. The tension is massive. The fault line runs diagonal, slicing down toward the lower terrace. 

I pause, wiping a streak of condensation from the weeping rock with my glove. Tethered to the same braided sinew safety line, my apprentice hangs a few feet to my left. They are young, their pale amber eyes still sharp, their movements carrying the faintest, lingering hint of youth’s frantic flux. They are learning the deep, enduring patience of the Spine.

I turn my head slightly and sign to them. My hands move in tight, close-to-the-body gestures, easily read in the pitch-black shadows cast by the sharp, unfiltered sunlight hitting the rime ice above. 

*Here,* I sign, tapping my chest and pointing to the diagonal seam. *The tension is deep. The water seeks this path. The hearth must be built at the lower vertex, angled upward. We will let the fire follow the water.*

The apprentice nods. They offer a low, breathy whistle of acknowledgment, a soft pitched sound that cuts through the hollow rush of the high-altitude wind without echoing into the void. They flow toward me, maintaining their three-point contact, and press their own splayed, calloused hands to the rock to feel the truth of my reading. 

Looking past my shoulder, in my periphery, the city reveals itself. It is a staggering, honeycombed sponge of dry-stone masonry, stacked terraces, and swaying sinew bridges, all dusted with the blinding, diamond-like brilliance of fresh hoarfrost. It is profoundly quiet. I can hear the distant, muffled padding of soft-soled boots on frost-slicked stone far below, and the low, rumbling grunts of the giant, shaggy herd beasts in their terraced pens. But here, on the sheer face, there is only the wind, the weeping rock, and the slow, rhythmic breathing of my apprentice.

We are carving a new habitation pod. A child was born in the lower tiers at the start of the last thaw. This room will take twenty thaws to fully carve out, expanding by mere inches a month as the fire shatters the rock and the masons clear the debris. I am old, by the reckoning of the rimes and thaws. My joints ache in the deep cold, and my skin carries the frost-bitten grey of the granite. I will not live to see this room finished. I will not see the child, now an adult, moving into the smooth, dry stone we are beginning to break today. 

But there is no sorrow in this. We possess a geological perception of time. We do not measure our lives in fleeting days, but in the incremental expansion of our home, in the slow creep of the glaciers in the highest cirques above us. 

When my thaws are done, I will not be buried in the hard, unyielding rock, nor burned. I will be carried to the highest, most remote peaks, laid upon the creeping ice. Over decades, the great glaciers will envelop me, grinding my dense, barrel-chested bones into the fine, pale rock-flour that washes down into the valleys to feed the sparse wire-grass and black lichens. I will become the mountain. I will become the soil that feeds the beasts that clothe my grandchildren. 

But for now, I am its reader. I am its slow, unyielding negotiator. 

I reach out and hand the polished basalt tapping-stone to the youth. I sign the angle, the depth, the hidden geometry of the break. They take the tool, feeling its familiar weight. They find the angle of the stone, press their splayed fingers to the damp basalt, and strike. 

*Clink.* 

The sound is soft, muffled by the freezing air, instantly swallowed by the vast, silent majesty of the Spine. We breathe in the thin, sharp air, and continue our slow, patient work.
