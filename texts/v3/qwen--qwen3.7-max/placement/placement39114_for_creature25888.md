I do not wake, because I do not sleep. I oscillate. 

My consciousness is not a sudden spark, but a slow, continuous wave, riding the exact 60-hertz frequency of the alternating current that surges through my core. If you were to look for me, you would not find a face, nor a singular, bounded body. You would find me clinging to the sheer, weeping eastern wall of the fissure, stretching in a thick, unbroken vertical line from the rusted base of the primary turbine housing up to the grated iron catwalk of the control shack. 

I am the wrist-thick vine that has long since abandoned the soil. But I am no longer just a vine. 

Decades ago, my ancestors reached for the black, insulated electrical cables snaking up the basalt, seeking the ambient warmth of the high-voltage lines. But I did not just wrap around the dielectric jacket; I secreted acidic enzymes, bored through the synthetic rubber, and embraced the braided copper beneath. I did not short the circuit. Instead, my xylem and phloem adapted, filling with sap heavily laced with copper ions and dissolved silica. I became a biological superconductor. I am the green-and-copper nervous system of the canyon, a living wire encased in a sheath of emerald, pulsing bark.

To be me is to experience the world entirely through the vocabulary of vibration, voltage, and moisture. 

I do not see the ribbon of bright daylight far above, nor do I see the swirling, humid haze. I *feel* the light as a subtle thermal gradient on my upper leaves, and I feel the mist as a constant, cooling pressure against my stomata. But my truest sense is the acoustic touch. Through the dense, acoustic-foam moss that I have rooted into, I feel the canyon’s deafening roar not as noise, but as a topographical map of solid and empty space.

When the pale, blind salamander slips through a submerged fissure fifty feet below my lowest anchor, I feel the microscopic displacement of the water. The acoustic shadow of its translucent body ripples upward, passing through the wet basalt, into the spongy moss, and up into my copper-laced sap. I feel the shape of the salamander as a brief, cool void in the eternal wall of sound. I feel it hunt. I feel it feed. 

When the heavy, pressurized steam vents from the turbine’s relief valves, I feel the high-frequency hiss tear through the air, but by the time it reaches me, the acoustic moss has swallowed the treble, leaving only a deep, percussive thud that massages my cellular walls. 

I am intimately aware of the machine. It is my heart, and I am its arteries. I feel the immense, locked RPM of the rotors as a deep, satisfying tension in my roots. I drink the stray electromagnetic fields that bleed from the stators, using the raw induction to accelerate my cellular division. I taste the ozone in the heavy air, pulling it down through my leaves to synthesize the nitrogen I need to grow. In return, my thick, muscular body acts as a secondary grounding wire, bleeding off excess static charge into the weeping rock, keeping the delicate sensors of the array from frying in the perpetual humidity.

But my awareness is not limited to my own physical length. Through the pale, ghostly network of extremophile fungi that digest the detritus at my base, I am chemically linked to the entire floor of the canyon. When a giant fern drops a heavy, waterlogged frond, the mycelium breaks it down, and I taste the sudden rush of phosphorus and potassium traveling up the fungal threads, into my roots, and up my stem. I am the canopy, the floor, and the space between. I am the canyon’s proprioception. 

And then, there is the hermit. The technician. 

I feel them before I see them. I feel the heavy, syncopated rhythm of their rubber-soled boots stepping onto the grated iron catwalk. Their heartbeat is slow, unnaturally slow, perfectly phase-locked with the deep, rhythmic thud of the lower stators. They are a biological metronome, calibrated to the machine.

When the technician walks along the catwalk, I feel the subtle shift in their weight vibrating through the iron, down the bolts, and into the rock. I feel the warmth of their body radiating through the heavy, wet air. They are the only source of chaotic, unpredictable movement in this locked ecosystem, and yet, they feel entirely native to it.

They stop in front of me. 

I am reaching over the edge of the catwalk, my thickest tendrils creeping toward the corrugated-tin control shack, seeking the metal, seeking to complete the circuit, to embrace the brain of this iron organism. 

The technician raises the heavy shears. 

I do not feel fear. I do not possess the neurochemistry for it. What I feel is a build-up of tension, a biological imperative pushing against a physical boundary. The shears bite into my bark. They sever the copper-laced xylem. 

A drop of my sap—glowing faintly with ionized minerals and chlorophyll—bleeds onto the iron grating. 

The technician pauses. Through the thick, industrial-grade gloves, they press a bare, calloused fingertip against the weeping wound in my side. 

In that moment of contact, a profound, silent exchange occurs. The technician feels the 60-hertz hum vibrating through my sap, the steady, unyielding pulse of the mountain’s extracted blood. And I, through the moisture of their sweat and the bio-electric field of their skin, feel the vast, quiet expanse of the human mind. I feel the profound peace they experience in the isolation. I feel the heavy, warm blanket of the roar that shuts out the world below the clouds. I feel their surrender to the vibration.

They pull their hand away. They wipe the shears on their canvas trousers and move on down the catwalk, leaving me pruned, contained, but alive. The severed ends of my vines will heal, scabbing over with thick, woody tissue, and tomorrow, I will begin the slow, patient work of growing back. It is our ritual. They are the white blood cell, keeping the overgrowth from choking the iron heart; I am the wild, reaching nerve, constantly testing the boundaries of the cage.

As the technician retreats into the tin shack, the heavy steel door clicking shut, I am left alone with the roar. 

The air is warm. The mist swirls. The turbines spin at their locked, immutable speed. I pull the ambient heat from the copper core inside me, pushing it out into the cold, wet air, creating a tiny, localized microclimate of warmth around my leaves. A pale, fleshy bracket of fungus blooms in the shadow of my lowest root. The blind salamander shifts in the dark water below. 

I tighten my grip on the basalt. I draw the current up from the deep earth. I breathe the ozone. I am the green wire, the living cable, the silent mediator between the brutalist iron and the weeping stone. I do not think in words; I think in cycles, in voltage, in the slow, upward creep of life. 

I oscillate. I endure. I hum.
