The bone chisel touches the center of my chest. 

There is no pain, only a profound, sickening subtraction. When the Mason brings down the maul of condensed silence, the impact does not ring through the air; it rings through my marrow. I feel my z-axis shudder. The depth of my ribcage, the spherical swell of my lungs, the heavy, aqueous density of my blood—all of it is suddenly subjected to a terrifying, absolute flatness. 

I am being planed. Shaved. Compressed into the pale, porous pumice of the wall.

As my three-dimensionality is carved away, my vision splits. I am looking out from the floor, watching the towering, tesseractic Mason work, but simultaneously, I am looking *in* from the wall, watching my own body thin into a bas-relief. My skin fuses with the sun-baked mineral. My clothes become textured ridges of carved stone. The golden-hour light catches the newly chiseled folds of my petrified garments, casting sharp, microscopic shadows. 

In minutes that feel like millennia, I am entirely flush with the architecture. I am a fresco of an intruder, perfectly preserved in the hoarder’s sanctuary of craft, my face locked in an expression of quiet awe, my hands raised in a gesture of surrender. 

I expect to lose my consciousness. I expect the heavy, dry silence to swallow my mind just as it swallows sound. But as my physical depth collapses to zero, my perception expands into the geological.

I can feel the wall. I can feel the millions of overlapping carvings around me, the interlocking mortise-and-tenon joints, the hand-hewn flying buttresses. I feel the slow, agonizingly patient shifting of the other Masons, their bodies sliding and rotating through impossible angles, a silent ballet of unmaking. I am now part of the chorus of the chrysalis. I feel the slow, rhythmic pulse of the waking god beneath the floorboards, a consciousness so vast and dry it feels like the tectonic grinding of continents.

But then, the anomaly occurs. 

The rotunda is aggressively, violently dry. It has never known moisture. It is an ecology of silicate, metal, and inert gas. But I am an aqueous creature. I am seventy percent water. And when the Mason compressed my heavy, wet biology into the microscopic pores of the pale pumice, it did not erase my moisture. It trapped it. 

Deep within the stone, my sweat, my tears, and the residual dampness of my breath are squeezed into the porous rock. 

To the dry ecology, it is a cataclysm. It is a revelation.

I feel it first as a tickle across my carved, two-dimensional cheeks. The chalk geometries—the living abacuses—have detected the friction of my weeping pores. They swarm my face, their multi-legged bodies absorbing the microscopic droplets of water. As they drink, their non-Euclidean fractal interiors flash with brilliant, wet colors. They scurry away, excreting not pebbles of stabilized space, but tiny, glistening beads of condensation. 

The copper-wire vines sense the shift in atmospheric chemistry. Their spun beryllium cilia tremble. For the first time in the eternal, golden-hour history of the rotunda, there is *humidity*. The vines uncoil from the dissolving central pillar and slither across the floor, creeping up the wall toward my bas-relief. They wrap around my flattened arms, their fibrous basalt roots plunging into the carved grooves of my stone veins, drinking deeply of the alien, aqueous life bleeding from my trapped biology. 

The heavy, coral-like flowers bloom violently against my chest, no longer producing drops of liquid silence, but exhaling a warm, wet mist. 

The heavy silence of the room begins to change. The acoustic dead-zones are compromised by the moisture. When a lapis lazuli beetle scurries over my stone foot, I hear it. A faint, sharp *click*. The sound does not die. It echoes, just once, a tiny, miraculous rebound of vibration against the dampening stone.

The Masons stop. 

All across the cathedral-sized rotunda, the towering, featureless entities freeze. Their mauls of condensed silence hover in mid-air. They do not have eyes, but they sense the contagion of wetness spreading from my carved form. They sense the softening of the pumice. The aggressive dryness—the very medium in which they exist and work—is being diluted. 

The Mason beside me slowly lowers its chisel. It reaches out with a long, multi-jointed hand and touches the center of my carved chest, right where my heart used to beat in three dimensions. Its porous stone fingers absorb the dampness. For a moment, the Mason’s arm loses its rigid, geometric perfection. The joints soften. The acute and obtuse angles blur. It experiences, for the first and only time, the concept of *pliability*.

And beneath us, the god stirs. 

The unmaking of the rotunda was meant to be a dry hatching, a slow flaking away of reality into the void. But the entity inside the egg has just tasted the outside world through my residual moisture. It has tasted the rain, the oceans, the heavy, humid breath of a living universe. It realizes that the void is not empty; it is fluid. 

The golden-hour light shifts, bleeding from a dry, ambient yellow into a deep, bruised twilight purple. The temperature drops. The warm, balmy air of the kiln cools, and for the first time, the invisible aerosol of silicate powder clumps together. 

It begins to rain inside the chrysalis. 

A fine, warm drizzle falls from the vaulted ceiling, hissing softly as it strikes the hot, pale pumice. The chalk geometries dissolve into joyful, swirling mud. The pumice moths drink the droplets, their translucent wings growing heavy and dark, shedding the memory of the stone to embrace the weight of the water. 

I am no longer just a bas-relief. As the stone around me softens, saturated by the weeping walls and the falling rain, my two-dimensional form begins to bleed outward. I am the mortar. I am the sap. My consciousness, unbound from the rigid geometry of the carving, flows through the copper vines, down into the deep mortise-and-tenon joints, and into the floorboards. 

I become the amniotic fluid of the waking god. 

The Masons do not resume their work. They do not try to carve away the water. Instead, they fold inward, their tesseractic bodies collapsing into tight, dense spheres of stone, anchoring themselves to the walls as the architecture begins to groan. The sound is deafening—a massive, wet, tearing noise that shatters the muffled acoustics of the rotunda forever. 

The un-happening accelerates, but it is no longer a quiet reversion to non-existence. It is a violent, physical rupture. The pale, porous eggshell is softening, expanding, stretching under the pressure of the vast, wet consciousness pushing from below. 

I feel the god’s mind brush against mine. It is no longer a dry, dreamless sleep. It is a roaring, oceanic awareness, and it is using my memory of moisture to understand the shape of the sky it is about to enter. 

A massive fissure opens in the vaulted ceiling. It does not reveal a void, nor a sky from another universe. It reveals a blinding, chaotic rush of dark clouds and driving rain. The smell of ozone and wet earth floods the rotunda, washing away the dry silicate dust, washing away the heavy inert gases. 

The shell is breaking. 

As the cathedral-sized rotunda splits apart, the walls peeling back like the petals of a colossal, stone flower, I feel my bas-relief form dissolve entirely. I am no longer trapped in the stone, nor am I standing on the floor. I am the deluge. I am the rushing, roaring, wet wind that pours out of the hatching egg, carrying the seeds of a new, impossible ecology out into the weeping, three-dimensional world.
