I begin as a vibration. 

It is a low, subsonic thrum that originates in the deep, expanded barrel of my chest, rolling outward through my ribs and into the wet pumice beneath my bare, ash-grey feet. I am crouched on a razor-edged shelf of unweathered obsidian, my center of gravity pressed low to the stone. Here, in the bruised, perpetual twilight of the high spires, haste is a fatal flaw. A single slip, a single rushed movement, means being flayed alive on the aggressive, crystalline spikes of our violently young world. And so, I move with a deliberate, liquid grace. Every shift of my weight is a calculated pour of energy; every breath of the thin, crisp air is a conscious expansion of my lungs.

I am Vael. My skin, mottled and the color of wet ash, renders me nearly invisible against the dark basalt. I close my disproportionately large, entirely black eyes, letting their highly reflective surfaces gather the scattered photons of the star-pricked indigo sky above. I do not need to see to know my work. I feel it. 

I am a Resonance-Tender. While my kin spend their cycles pecking stone to carve the wide, empty plazas or weaving the pale, flexible wood of our scaffolds with coaxing vines, my duty is to the deep moss. I press my palms flat against the spongy, pale-green mat of the Pulse-moss that drapes over the deadly rock. I resume my hum. It is not a sound meant for the air—the jagged acoustics of the spires would turn a vocalization into a chaotic, dangerous echo—but a vibration meant for the earth. 

Beneath my hands, the colonial organism responds. The Pulse-moss possesses a rudimentary, shared nervous system, and it knows the frequency of my chest. As I hum, a synchronized wave ripples outward from my touch. The moss stands on end, its bioluminescent blue veins pulsing brighter, exposing maximum surface area to the heavy, clinging mist. It is a silent conversation between my biology and the flora, a tactile intimacy that binds me to the spire. Around me, the Weep-ferns rhythmically cup and release the heavy condensation, their translucent, muscular fronds snapping shut occasionally on drifting micro-fauna. Their continuous, soft *drip-drip-drip* forms the baseline rhythm of my existence.

Then, the air pressure changes. 

My hum ceases instantly. The fine hairs on my arms stand on end as a sharp, sweet tang of ozone cuts through the smell of crushed fern and wet stone. The ambient electrostatic charge in the air spikes, and the entire terrace of moss shivers in anticipation. 

The Tide is coming.

I flatten myself against the basalt, pulling my shallow, pumice-carved bowl close to my chest. I become part of the architecture, a grey shadow on a grey rock, waiting in the profound, heavy silence.

It drifts into the plaza above me. The Aero-benthic.

Even though I have witnessed the passing of the leviathans a thousand times, the sheer, staggering scale of the beast still roots me in awe. It moves through the dense, mist-heavy atmosphere as if it were deep ocean water, its vast, undulating mantle displacing the cool air in utter, velvet quiet. There is no sound of its passing, only the visual symphony of its biology. Beneath its translucent skin, the slow, rhythmic pumping of its internal gas bladders is visible, glowing in shifting hues of bruised purple and deep sea-green. It is a cathedral of flesh, drifting in a slow, deliberate arc through the flight corridor my ancestors carved specifically for its passage.

I watch, breathless, as its hundreds of gossamer tendrils descend. They trail beneath the galleon-sized beast like weeping willow branches made of spun glass, brushing gently against the empty stone plazas and the lush mosses. They are highly electro-receptive, tasting the magnetic anomalies of the rock and the biological density of the flora. 

When the tendrils sweep across my terrace, they touch the Pulse-moss I have just coaxed into blooming. The reaction is instantaneous. The moss flares with a blinding, vibrant green bioluminescence, releasing a cloud of ion-heavy spores. The leviathan’s mantle expands, inhaling the spores through microscopic pores to feed the symbiotic bacteria in its gut that generate its lifting gas. In return, the beast’s tendrils deposit the "god-sweat"—a fine, nutrient-rich, ionized mist that falls like warm, heavy snow.

I hold up my pumice bowl. The mist hits my skin, smelling of deep ocean and lightning, warming the chill of the high altitude. Along with the mist, a few Mantle-scuttlers drop from the deep, warm folds of the leviathan’s mantle above. The small, six-limbed, pale crabs click softly as they hit the damp moss, immediately scurrying into the spongy green to breed before they will inevitably find their way back to a passing god.

The beast’s massive mantle contracts, pushing a micro-current of wind against me. The lashed-wood scaffolds groan and sway, the woven vines holding fast. The leviathan glides past, its bruised purple glow fading as it disappears back into the churning clouds below and the indigo twilight above. 

The velvet silence rushes back in, broken only by the weeping of the condensation and the creak of the wood. 

I look down into my bowl. It is filled with the aggressively glowing, nutrient-dense moss and the soft-shelled scuttlers. This is the harvest. This is the symbiosis. I dip my fingers into the bowl, tasting the copper and sweet rot of the moss, the rich, heavy protein of the scuttlers. It is the taste of the sky, filtered through the flesh of a god and the roots of the stone.

I stand slowly, my joints popping in the cool air, and begin the long, deliberate climb up the switchbacks toward my sleeping alcove. I am incredibly small. I am a speck of ash-grey life clinging to a needle of shattered rock, suspended above a floor of impenetrable clouds. Yet, as I press my hand against the damp, glowing moss to steady myself on the steep vertical drop, I feel no insignificance. 

I am a vital thread in a silent, drifting tapestry. I am the gardener of the leviathans, the singer of the moss, the caretaker of the sharp edges and the soft life. Here, in the quiet, high-altitude dampness, I am exactly where I am meant to be.
