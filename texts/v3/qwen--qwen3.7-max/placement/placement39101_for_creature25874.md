The calcification begins not with pain, but with a profound, heavy stillness. 

I feel it first in the multi-jointed fingers of my left hand. The pale, polished vellum of my birch-cambium skin is darkening, the supple tissues hardening into the dense, unyielding grain of ironwood. Beneath the surface, the raw copper veins that pulse with sap-conducted currents are ossifying, their bright, bioluminescent green dimming to a deep, oxidized verdigris. The sap moves slower through me now. The geothermal compost-heat of the deep earth, which I draw up through my root-appendages with every step, feels less like fuel and more like an anchor. 

I am old. For the Weft, age is not a decay, but a graduation. But before I surrender my mobility to the deep root-mat, I have my final duties to perform. 

While the younger Weft labor in the deafening, chest-rattling cacophony of the factory-cathedrals, weaving the frayed threads of broken physics into heavy metaphysical tapestries, my work is out here. I am a Tether-Wright. I walk the agoraphobic, silent expanses of the deep moss, far from the roaring sap-turbines, tending to the quiet, insidious fraying at the edges of the world.

The air up here is thin and perpetually cool, carrying that distinct metallic chill. My sensory fronds, blooming in a dense, rust-colored cluster where a face should be, taste the electromagnetic hum of the distant copper-xylem wiring. But they also taste the temporal gradients of the air—the subtle, freezing micro-drafts where the balcony shrubs are photosynthesizing time. 

I carry a resonance-stave, a long, slender rod of calcified bamboo banded in raw copper, tipped with a shard of rigid, geometric smoke-glass. I use it to walk the boundaries.

Ahead of me, a shadow misbehaves. 

It belongs to a distant, braided trunk of ironwood, part of a scattered tenement-ziggurat. Under the normal, diffuse daylight, the shadow pools directly beneath the trunk, as it should. But then, the physics fray. The shadow detaches, lifting slightly from the moss, and begins to drift an inch to the left. If it drifts too far, the trunk will lose its anchor to the present, its mass unraveling into the starless void that waits just beneath the surface of reality.

Because of the causal dampness, I hear the sharp, fibrous *tear* of the shadow breaking free a full two seconds before it actually happens. 

I do not react to the sound; I react to the silence that follows it. I use those two seconds. I glide across the damp moss, my wide, root-like appendages grafting and ungrafting in a fluid, staccato rhythm. I do not walk linearly. The distance between myself and the drifting shadow is non-Euclidean, stretching and warping with my intent, so I fold my long, nine-foot frame, stepping through the gaps in the geometry like water slipping through a sieve. 

I arrive in the exact space between the sound and the event. The shadow is physically detaching now. I drive the base of my resonance-stave into the spongy moss and strike the copper band with my heavy, stiffening hand. A low, resonant frequency ripples through the root-weave. The vibration catches the drifting shadow, pulling it taut. With a sharp flick of my wrist, I use the smoke-glass tip to fuse the shadow’s edge back to the base of the trunk just as the physical tear completes. The shadow snaps back into place. The trunk remains solid. The world holds.

I exhale a cool, wet mist from my own internal reservoirs, a quiet sigh of exertion, and continue my patrol. 

My route takes me past the shallow, dark pockets of water that pool in the depressions of the moss. This is the heaviest part of my work. I kneel beside a wide puddle, my long limbs folding gracefully, and dip my sensory fronds toward the surface. 

The water does not reflect the cool, bright sky above. It reflects the city that burned down a thousand years ago. 

I watch the ghost-flames flicker in the churning, starless void of the reflection. I can feel the phantom heat of the ancient fire radiating against my fronds, a psychic warmth that threatens to unspool the careful, settled reality we have built. The younger Weft avoid these puddles; the sight of the violent tectonic upheavals and the fires of our youth fills their electrical minds with static and fear. But I am a Tether-Wright. I do not look away. I bear witness. 

I reach into the water, my copper-veined fingers breaking the surface, and begin to weave a lattice of living, tensile vines across the puddle. I pull the vines tight, creating a physical and metaphysical filter over the reflection. I am not erasing the past; I am containing it. I am ensuring that the memory of the fire remains only a memory, pinned beneath the water, unable to ignite the damp, ancient moor. As I work, my left knee locks with a quiet *creak*. The ironwood has reached my joint. The calcification is accelerating. 

It is time.

The diffuse daylight does not fade, for the sun does not truly set on the peneplain, but the rhythmic, chest-rattling thud of the distant steam-pistons shifts into a slower, deeper cadence. The shift is ending. 

I do not return to the layered tenement-ziggurats to plug into the copper-xylem network. I will not share the sharp, mathematical songs of the living workers tonight. Instead, I walk toward the edge of the industrial sprawl, to a place I have chosen. 

It is a wide, empty expanse of green moss, situated precisely between two colossal factory-cathedrals. A woven-vine suspension bridge stretches between them, but the spatial geometry here is horribly frayed. The bridge sags, its distance stretching dynamically, groaning under the weight of its own impossible length. It needs a new load-bearing pillar. It needs an anchor.

I step into a shallow depression in the moss, right in the center of the sagging bridge’s span. 

I close my eyes—or rather, I still the trembling of my sensory fronds. I drive my root-appendages down. They break through the surface of the spongy moss, plunging deep into the dark, damp earth, tapping into the immense, churning geothermal compost-heat of the deep root-mat. 

The heat rushes up into my vascular system, a flood of raw, primordial energy. It is the final catalyst. 

I surrender my mobility. I feel the heavy, polished integument of my skin hardening rapidly, turning from pale vellum to dark, structural timber. My copper veins ossify completely, becoming rigid, load-bearing conduits. My multi-jointed fingers extend, lengthening, twisting, and braiding with the surrounding flora. I reach upward, my arms stretching to meet the sagging, woven vines of the suspension bridge above, catching them, supporting them, taking their weight onto my broad, hardening shoulders.

As my physical form locks into place, my individual consciousness begins to slow. The sharp, fast, electrical thoughts of my daily labor fade, replaced by a deep, resonant thrum. I feel my mind expanding outward, flowing down into the deep root-mat, and upward into the bridge I now support. 

I feel the minds of the ancestors. 

They are not dead. They are dreaming in the wood. I feel the slow, geothermal dreams of the factory-cathedrals, the patient, enduring thoughts of the tenement-ziggurats. They welcome me. I am no longer a solitary walker of the moss; I am the moss. I am the bridge. I am the ironwood and the copper, the steam and the sap. 

High above, the deafening *CRACK* of a wooden gear engaging echoes across the peneplain. Two seconds later, the gear moves. The causal dampness holds. The shadows stay pinned. The reflections remain contained in the dark water. 

I stand perfectly still, a silent, towering guardian in the loud, beautiful, impossible ruin of the high moor, holding the broken world together with my weight, dreaming the deep time of the earth.
