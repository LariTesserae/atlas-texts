I begin with the edge. 

Before I take in the vast, terraced expanse of the caldera, before I register the blinding, high-albedo glare of the upper rims, my hands find the stone. It is cold, crisp, and entirely unforgiving. I run my thumb along the lip of a newly cut parapet. It is sharp enough to part the skin, and it does—a fine, painless line of red that quickly beads and dries in the cool air. I do not flinch. My skin, like the skin of all the First Dwellers, has thickened into a pale, calcified hide, bearing the thousands of hairline scars that map our devotion to this embryonic world.

But as I move deeper into the packed, shoulder-to-shoulder warren of the mid-terraces, slipping into the stark, black shadows cast by the un-eroded eaves, the nature of my relationship with the stone begins to shift. I am not here to haul the massive, freshly quarried blocks, nor do I carry the heavy wooden mauls that ring out from the sun-baked upper plains. My hands are not wrapped in thick, resin-stiffened hemp to absorb violent shocks. They are bare, or lightly bound in soft cloth, because my work requires the intimate, tactile feedback of the rock’s deepest secrets.

I am looking for the weep.

The water table, fractured by the violence of the city’s excavation, weeps endlessly from the raw rock. The First Dwellers fear this weeping. They see the moisture as the vanguard of time, the first agent of the softening they so desperately wish to delay. They sweep the dust, they channel the air, they try to hold the city in its frozen, pristine state. But the stone will weep. The water will always find a way. 

My task is to listen to the rock and give the water a path before it can choose its own. I am a Weep-carver. A shepherd of the moisture.

While the Cutters birth the macro-geometry of the city, striking the bedrock to create the grand, sweeping terraces and the thick, buttressed walls, I am responsible for the micro-geometry. I carve the runnels. 

I press my bare fingertips against the pale-grey limestone in a shaded alcove. The stone is razor-sharp, but beneath the surface, I can feel the faint, capillary pulse of the dampness. I can sense where the microscopic fissures lie, where the moisture is gathering, preparing to breach the surface and blur a scalloped chisel mark. 

With a soft, deliberate tap of my ironwood mallet, I drive a fine bronze burin into the rock. *Thwack. Thwack.* The sound is dull, quickly swallowed by the heavy, humid air. I carve a shallow, meticulous groove, following the natural grain of the limestone. I am not just cutting a ditch; I am sculpting a vein. I angle the floor of the runnel by a fraction of a degree, ensuring the water will flow swiftly, leaving no puddles, ensuring it will not linger long enough to soften the sharp lip of the channel I am creating. 

When the Sweepers pass in their choreographed, silent streams, their brooms whispering *shhh-shhh* against the raw floors, they do not break their rhythm. But as they pass my alcove, a Sweeper will offer a slight, deliberate tilt of the head—the silent lexicon for *integrity confirmed*. I reply with a single, sharp tap of my bronze pick against my thigh: *yield, the channel is open*. We do not speak. The heavy, sacred quiet of the caldera demands our silence. 

But in that silence, I am the one who provides the city's only continuous voice. 

When I finish a runnel, I wait in the cool, clinging mist. Sometimes it takes hours, sometimes days, for the water to finally trace the new path. But eventually, a single bead of condensation will gather at the weep-hole. It will swell, catch the bounced, fractured light of the sun, and drop into my carved channel. Then another. And another. Soon, a tiny, crystalline rivulet is racing down the terrace, singing a faint, high-pitched *hiss* against the raw stone, joining the larger streams that flow toward the deep cisterns. 

The elders, sitting by the pools below, watch the water with melancholy. They see it as the erosion of their monument. But I see it differently. By carving the runnels, I am not just preserving the sharpness of the stone by diverting the water; I am giving the water a voice. I am negotiating the terms of the city's aging. 

I know the paradox of my existence. I am a caretaker of the embryonic now, yet I am the one laying the tracks for the future. I know that long after my hands have lost their feeling, long after the First Dwellers have turned to dust, the water will still be flowing through the runnels I carved. The edges of my channels will eventually round. The pale green moss will eventually creep up the shaded sides of my grooves, their roots probing the microscopic faults I left behind. I do not fear this. I see it not as the death of the newness, but as the next layer of the city's life.

But until that day, I remain in the cool, diffused luminescence of the mid-terraces. I press my scarred fingertips to the unblunted rock. I listen for the weep. I raise my small mallet, and in the profound, heavy silence of a world holding its breath, I strike the stone. 

I am not just holding back time. I am teaching the water how to fall.
