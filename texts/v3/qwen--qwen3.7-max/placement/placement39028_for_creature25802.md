I would not be found in the sun-drenched, upper plazas where the younger Kin glide on the brisk thermals, nor would I be in the central cathedral-root where the evening choruses are loudest. 

I would be at the bottom. 

I am an Echo-Carver, one of the oldest of the Caldera Kin. My body tells the story of my years: the subcutaneous layer of blubber beneath my pearlescent skin is exceptionally thick, giving me the rounded, heavy silhouette of a river stone. My skin, once a bright, iridescent abalone sheen, has dulled to the soft, matte luster of polished sea-glass. The heavy, transparent nictitating membranes over my massive indigo eyes are scarred with tiny, frost-white scratches from decades of navigating the microscopic silicate dust that occasionally sheds from the caldera walls. 

I live in the Anchor-Roots. 

Here, at the very floor of the sunken world, the colossal, petrified trunks of the ancient water-cypresses flare out into massive, calcified buttresses that grip the bedrock. Because the water is impossibly clear, the blinding, unfiltered mountain sunlight still reaches me. It does not fade into a murky twilight; instead, it pierces the deep in sharp, geometric shafts, illuminating the sweeping curves of the deep roots in stark, high-contrast relief. It is bright, it is freezing, and it is profoundly quiet—or at least, it is the quietest place in a deafening world.

To be an Echo-Carver is to be the custodian of the city’s voice. 

The Caldera Kin do not speak; we strike, we click, we thrum. Our language is carried through the water and into the bones. But wood, even petrified, water-logged cypress, changes over centuries. It absorbs minerals, it shifts with the micro-currents, it swells and compresses. Over generations, a root-tower’s acoustic resonance can drift. A pillar that once sang a crisp, sharp *clack* might dull to a muddy *thud*. If the architecture loses its tuning, the language of the Kin becomes muddled. The city loses its voice.

My purpose is to keep the mountain singing in tune.

I float in the twelve-degree chill, perfectly neutrally buoyant. My kelp-woven harness, strapped with pitch-sealed buoyancy bladders, holds me suspended just inches from the sweeping flank of the First Ancestor root. I do not kick. Kicking is for the young and the hurried. I simply let the slow, glacial thermal draft cradle me, making minute adjustments with the thick, muscular webbing of my hands to keep my position.

I unhook my tools from my belt: a heavy mallet carved from the dense femur of a mountain elk, and a pumice rasp shaped by my own hands over forty years. 

I close my eyes. I press my jawbone firmly against the smooth, water-worn wood of the root. I lack external ears, so I do not hear the water; I feel the world through the dense conduction of my skeleton. 

I raise the bone mallet and strike the wood.

*Thwump.*

The vibration travels through the wood, into the water, and slams into my jaw. It rolls down my neck, vibrating through my collarbones, rippling through the thick blubber of my chest, and echoing in the fluid of my inner ear. 

I frown. The vibration is slightly flat. The frequency is dragging, bogged down by a century of mineral accretion on the port side of the pillar. 

I open my eyes, the nictitating membranes sliding back, and begin to work. I do not hack or chop. I apply the pumice rasp to the wood and begin to move my arm in long, sweeping, circular motions. I work with the grain, following the ancient, swirling growth rings of the cypress. *Swish, swish, swish.* The water immediately catches the fine, snow-like dust, carrying it away in the gentle current. 

I carve for hours. Time here is not measured in minutes, but in millimeters of removed wood and the shifting angle of the sun-shafts. My elongated, multi-jointed fingers ache with a dull, familiar warmth, but my mind is entirely empty, settled into the profound, unshakeable patience of the caldera. I am not just shaping wood; I am shaping the sound that my great-grandchildren will use to say *I love you*, or *danger*, or *gather*.

Eventually, my lungs begin to burn. The deep, slow metabolism of the Kin conserves oxygen, but the physical labor demands a toll. 

I push off the root, gliding a few dozen yards through the bright, empty water to a patch of deep-water breath-lotuses. These are older, paler variants of the flowers above, their massive, inverted bells swaying gently on thick, ropy vines. 

I approach the largest bloom. My movements slow, becoming ceremonial. I unstopper my wax-sealed gourd. With the thick, leathery pads of my webbed fingers, I gently massage the underside of the fleshy petals. The plant responds to the touch, releasing a large, shimmering bubble of pure, photosynthesized air. I guide the bubble into the neck of the gourd. 

When it is full, I lean in and press my face to the flower’s thick stamen. I exhale my spent, carbon-heavy breath deep into the plant’s tissues, feeling the subtle, pulsing warmth of its biology against my cold skin. *A trade.* I stopper the gourd, bring it to my mouth, and drink the air. It is crisp, biting, and tastes faintly of pine and high-altitude frost. It floods my blood with energy.

As I secure the gourd to my belt, the light in the caldera begins to change. The sharp, blinding white of high noon softens into the rich, angled gold of late afternoon. The shadows of the root-towers stretch long and dark across the caldera floor.

It is time for the evening gathering.

High above me, in the central cathedral-root, the Kin are assembling. I do not need to swim up to join them; my place is here, at the foundation. 

I press my jaw back against the First Ancestor root. 

It begins as a faint tickling in my teeth. Then, it becomes a hum in my jaw. Finally, it blooms into a massive, physical weight against my chest. Fifty Kin, hundreds of feet above, are striking the upper tiers of the city with stone mallets and bone castanets. 

*Clack-clack. Thrum. Clack-clack-clack. BOOM.*

The sound travels down the colossal wooden pillars, amplifying as it moves through the dense, petrified heartwood. The acoustic chaos of the caldera—the clicking crayfish, the booming salamanders—is entirely swallowed by the synchronized, percussive symphony of my people. The vibration is so intense it massages my internal organs, a deep, rhythmic thumping that aligns my heartbeat with the collective pulse of the settlement.

I raise my bone mallet. I find the exact spot I spent the day carving, the spot where the wood is now perfectly tuned, perfectly clear. 

I strike.

*THWUMMM.*

My note is the bass. It is the deep, resonant foundation that catches the higher, syncopated clicks of the Kin above and grounds them. I feel their rhythm shift, adjusting to the pure, deep resonance I have sent up the pillar. The city’s chord locks into perfect, echoing equilibrium. 

Hanging there in the freezing, blindingly bright deep, suspended by kelp and buoyancy, feeling the song of my people vibrating through my very marrow, I am entirely complete. I am the Echo-Carver. I am the deep root. I am the quiet mind holding the mountain’s song in the dark.
