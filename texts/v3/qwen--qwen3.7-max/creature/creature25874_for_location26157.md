To understand what lives in the Loom-City, you must first discard the concept of fauna. The prompt noted that the animal kingdom has abandoned this place, and it is true: there is no flesh here, no blood, no beating hearts, no lungs drawing the thin, crisp air. The inhabitants of this high peneplain are not animals. They are the apex of the botanical. They are the sentient, mobile extensions of the root-mat itself. 

They are the Weft.

You will not see them immediately. At first, you will only notice the way the environment accommodates them. The deep, spongy moss does not merely sigh when stepped upon; it exhales in measured, rhythmic cadences, parting to allow passage before springing back into a seamless, unbroken weave. The thick, grey smoke that crystallizes into fractal glass does not fall randomly; it shatters in deliberate, cleared paths, swept aside by long, multi-jointed limbs before it can cut the moss. 

When a figure finally resolves through the hissing steam and the deafening roar of the arboreal factories, the sheer, alien geometry of their biology becomes apparent. 

They are impossibly tall, often stretching nine or ten feet in height, their proportions stretched thin to adapt to both the low oxygen of the high altitude and the non-Euclidean distances of their home. They possess no skin, no fur, no scales. Their outer layer is a pale, polished integument of compressed peat and living birch-cambium, smooth as cured vellum and cool to the touch. They have no faces in any human sense. Where a head should be, there is a dense, blooming cluster of sensory fronds and pale, rust-colored lichens that taste the electromagnetic hum of the copper-xylem wiring and read the temporal gradients of the air. 

Beneath their pale, bark-like exterior, they are luminous. Their vascular systems are intertwined with the city’s infrastructure; veins of raw, unspun copper pulse with sap-conducted currents, glowing with a faint, bioluminescent green. When they move, they do not walk with the heavy, heel-to-toe gait of animals. They glide on wide, root-like appendages that temporarily graft to the moss with every step, drawing micro-doses of geothermal compost-heat and moisture directly from the earth to fuel their immense, churning metabolisms.

To watch the Weft work is to watch a masterclass in navigating broken physics. They live entirely within the frayed edges of reality, perceiving the world in a state of temporal parallax. 

Stand near one of the massive sap-turbines and watch a worker tend the burl-wood gears. The air is shattered by the deafening *CRACK* of a wooden cog engaging. A full two seconds pass before the gear actually moves. The Weft worker does not react to the sound; they react to the silence that follows it. Their long, multi-jointed fingers pull the heavy iron-banded levers in that exact two-second gap, anticipating the physical movement through the causal dampness. They do not experience the delay as a glitch; to them, the sound is the promise, and the movement is the fulfillment. They live in the space between the two.

They are equally attuned to the spatial anomalies. When a Weaver needs to cross one of the woven-vine suspension bridges that stretches dynamically with every stride, they do not walk linearly. They step in folded, staccato rhythms, their long legs folding and unfolding in ways that seem to slip through the gaps in the distance. What takes a human three hours of exhausting, mind-bending trekking, a Weaver crosses in a fluid, ten-minute dance, their bodies slipping through the non-Euclidean geometry like water through a sieve.

Their labor in the colossal factory-cathedrals is fierce, unending, and profoundly vital. The mechanized looms they tend are not weaving textiles. They are weaving reality. 

The Loom-City exists on a landscape where the fundamental laws of physics have eroded. If left untended, the peneplain would simply dissolve into the starless void reflected in the puddles. The Weft operate the massive, steam-driven looms to catch the rigid, geometric fractals of crystallized smoke, the detached, drifting shadows, and the frayed, temporal threads pulled from the ambient air by the balcony shrubs. With deafening clanks and hissing steam, the looms weave these broken physical laws into heavy, dense, metaphysical tapestries. These tapestries are then dragged out and laid over the deep moss, acting as a stabilizing weight, pinning the broken physics down, forcing the landscape to remain in its "settled," ancient state. They are quite literally manufacturing the continuity of their world.

Despite the roaring, chest-rattling cacophony of the factories, the Weft are a profoundly silent people. They have no vocal cords, and the air is far too loud for acoustic communication anyway. Instead, they speak through the city. 

When their shift at the looms is done, they return to the layered tenement-ziggurats. Here, they do not sleep. They "root." A Weaver will approach the living, tensile walls of their dwelling and press their sensory fronds against the exposed copper-xylem nodes. Instantly, their consciousness plugs into the sap-conducted grid. The entire city becomes a hive-mind of silent, electrical thought. In this vast, shared neural network, they exchange complex schematics, share the emotional resonance of the day’s labor, and sing silent, mathematical songs of deep time. The hum of a million quiet thoughts vibrates through the root-mats, a secret, telepathic tranquility hidden beneath the violent, mechanical noise of the surface.

And when a Weaver’s time is done, they do not die. They integrate. 

Senescence for the Weft is a slow, beautiful calcification. As they age, their pale cambium skin hardens into ironwood; their copper veins ossify into rigid, structural conduits. When they feel the final stiffening of their joints, they walk out to the agoraphobic expanses of the empty green, far from the roaring factories. There, they find a shallow depression in the moss, step into it, and drive their root-appendages deep into the damp, dark earth. 

They surrender their mobility. They surrender their individual consciousness to the deep root-mat. Over the next few decades, their bodies grow, expanding, twisting, and braiding with the surrounding flora, until they become the very load-bearing pillars, the hollowed sequoia-like stalks, and the spiraling staircases of the city. The colossal plant-architecture of the Loom-City is not just grown; it is inherited. Every factory-cathedral, every tenement-ziggurat, every woven balcony is made of their ancestors. The city is a living necropolis, a monument of wood and copper built from the bodies of the willing dead.

If you stand quietly on the damp moss, ignoring the hiss of the steam valves and the thud of the pistons, you might see one of them pause. A tall, pale figure, glowing faintly with green sap-light, standing at the edge of a shallow, dark pool of water. They will tilt their faceless, frond-crowned head, looking down into the water. They are not looking at their reflection. They are looking at the reflection of the city that burned down a thousand years ago, watching the ghost-flames flicker in the starless void. 

They will stand there for a long time, perfectly still, anchoring the broken world with their weight, a silent guardian in the loud, beautiful, impossible ruin of the high moor.
