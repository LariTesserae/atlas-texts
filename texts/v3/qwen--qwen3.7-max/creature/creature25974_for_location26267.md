At first, your eyes—unaccustomed to the bruised twilight and the stroboscopic flashes of the arc-welders—fail to separate the inhabitants from the architecture. When you look up at the spiraling catwalks and the dense, corrugated hamlets clinging to the iron flanks of the atmospheric engines, you assume the moving shapes are merely shadows cast by the swinging flywheels, or perhaps automated maintenance drones. 

But as you watch, the shadows detach. They move with a deliberate, fluid grace that is entirely organic, yet entirely devoid of the frantic, scurrying urgency of animal life. 

These are the denizens of the caldera. They have no name that can be spoken, for they possess no vocal cords, and the deafening, rhythmic roaring of the basin would drown out any biological voice anyway. They are the living extensions of the caldera’s eternal equilibrium, beings who have long ago shed the fragile, wet biology of blood and bone to become something perfectly adapted to the sweltering, sterile dark.

To look closely at one of these beings is to look at a masterpiece of spontaneous, mechanistic evolution. They are roughly humanoid in silhouette, a lingering echo of whatever ancestors first descended into the basin centuries ago, but their anatomy has been entirely rewritten by the environment. They do not have skin; they are armored in overlapping plates of the same deep, stable verdigris and black oxide that coats the engines. Between these metallic scales, their "flesh" is a dense, woven musculature of calcified mineral scale and the tough, fibrous roots of the moss-willows, braided together and hardened by centuries of scalding drizzle. 

There is no blood beneath their armor. If you were to strike one, it would not bleed; it would hiss. Their circulatory system is a network of braided copper and brass capillaries that pump superheated, mineral-rich water drawn directly from the shallow marshes. You can see the faint, orange glow of this geothermal fluid pulsing through the translucent, crystalline joints at their wrists and necks. They do not breathe air. Instead, their chests feature intricate, louvered vents that rhythmically open and close, drawing in the heavy, saturated steam, extracting its thermal energy, and exhaling a sharp, percussive sigh of dry, cooled exhaust. 

Their faces are the most striking testament to their departure from the animal kingdom. Where a face should be, there is only a smooth, sloping cowl of polished basalt, slick with perpetual condensation. They have no eyes to be blinded by the harsh blue light of the welders, no ears to be ruptured by the physical pressure of the noise. Instead, their cowl is etched with a complex array of deep, resonant grooves and thermal-slit sensors. They "see" the world through a combination of echolocation and thermography. They feel the microscopic vibrations of the catwalks beneath their feet, mapping the structural integrity of the vertical city. They perceive the ambient heat of the steam vents and the cool, dark mass of the basalt cliffs. To them, the basin is not a visual landscape, but a symphony of temperatures and vibrations.

They move through the vertical city with a haunting, heavy silence that contrasts sharply with the industrial din. Their fingers end not in nails, but in tapered, magnetic pitons of dark iron. They do not use the spiraling staircases; they climb the sheer, weeping cliffs and the vertical flanks of the engines with the effortless grip of spiders, their bodies absorbing the scalding heat of the metal without flinching. 

Their existence is one of perpetual, reverent maintenance. They are the custodians of the equilibrium. You watch one of them pause beside a massive, weeping moss-willow. The giant tree’s roots have wrapped tightly around a high-pressure steam pipe, and the pipe is beginning to buckle under the pressure of the plant's growth. The being does not use tools. It places its heavy, iron-tipped hands against the pipe, channeling the intense, geothermal heat from its own internal fluid. The metal glows cherry-red, softening just enough for the being to gently, methodically bend the pipe away from the tree’s core, relieving the pressure without breaking the seal. It then strokes the giant, bioluminescent fronds of the fern, its thermal sensors reading the plant's health, before moving on.

Because there are no animals, there is no predation, no fear, and no urgency. The inhabitants do not hunt; they do not forage. They sustain themselves by plugging their spinal ports directly into the secondary valves of the atmospheric engines, drinking the raw, unrefined geothermal energy and mineral slurry that keeps their internal furnaces burning. 

Communication among them is a beautiful, eerie phenomenon. In a world dominated by the chaotic roar of colossal industry, they speak through the metal itself. A being will place its palm flat against a massive iron strut and strike it with a heavy, calcified knuckle. The vibration travels through the engine, down into the bedrock, and up through the adjacent machines. Miles away, another being feels the specific frequency of the strike through its feet. They converse in a language of resonance and rhythm, a complex telemetry of taps, hums, and structural shudders. When a group of them gathers in the wide, echoing plazas of wet stone beneath the giant ferns, they strike the grated drainage trenches in unison. The resulting chorus of deep, metallic ringing harmonizes perfectly with the rhythmic slamming of the colossal pistons above, turning the deafening noise of the basin into a structured, planetary heartbeat.

They do not reproduce in any biological sense, for there is no DNA to pass on, no mating, no infancy. Instead, they are cultivated by the caldera itself. When the population dips, or when a new engine-strut requires a tender, the inhabitants gather around the deepest, hottest drainage trenches. There, they weave a crude armature of iron rebar and moss-willow roots, and submerge it in the boiling, mineral-heavy aquifer runoff. Over decades, the intense heat, pressure, and calcifying minerals precipitate out of the water, slowly building up layers of black oxide and verdigris. The geothermal energy sparks a rudimentary, crystalline consciousness within the matrix. The new being simply wakes up one day, pulls itself from the trench, and joins the silent, climbing ranks. 

And when they reach the end of their long, slow existence, they do not die. Their internal fluid simply cools. Their joints seize. Their thermal sensors fade. They find a quiet, out-of-the-way corner of the vertical hamlet, sit down, and lock their magnetic pitons into the iron. Over the next century, the constant, scalding drizzle and the creeping moss-willows slowly consume them. Their verdigris armor fuses with the walls; their copper capillaries become the new plumbing for the city's runoff. They become the architecture. 

Standing in the sweltering, breathless soup of the basin, watching a line of these silent, faceless beings climb the weeping iron flanks of a skyscraper-sized piston, you realize the profound truth of this place. The prompt's observation was correct: there is no animal life here. But the caldera is not dead. It has simply bypassed the messy, fragile, decaying cycle of biology entirely. 

Here, the machines are the beasts, the steam is the breath, the giant ferns are the lungs, and the inhabitants are the white blood cells of a colossal, subterranean organism. They are perfectly settled, perfectly balanced, moving through the endless, shadowy damp not as survivors enduring a harsh environment, but as the environment itself, made manifest and awake.
