To look at the swarm from a distance is to watch a living, shifting circuit board. The high-altitude lithovores do not scurry or swarm in the chaotic, organic sense of lower-elevation insects. They flow. They are hexapedal, low-slung, and heavily armored, their bodies flattened into aerodynamic, teardrop-shaped carapaces that hug the stone to escape the biting, thin wind. Their exoskeletons are a masterpiece of extreme-environment engineering: a matte, slate-gray chitin that perfectly mimics the raw basalt, layered with microscopic, vacuum-insulated chambers to trap their internal heat. Crucially, the outermost layer of their shell is secreted with a hyper-hydrophobic, waxy lipid. In a landscape that weeps supercooled water, this dry coating prevents ice from forming on their bodies, allowing them to remain perfectly friction-ready in the damp gloom.

Zooming in on a single unit, the brutality of their existence becomes apparent. Their legs end in multi-jointed tarsi tipped with micro-cleats of biomineralized iron and tungsten, allowing them to grip the razor-sharp, unweathered shards of granite without slicing their own joints. But it is their heads that are truly built for industry. Their mandibles are massively hypertrophied, asymmetrical, and lined with enamel harder than the stone they consume. One jaw acts as a chisel, shearing off precise, microscopic flakes of fresh basalt; the other acts as a mortar, crushing the shards into a coarse grit. They do not eat the rock for sustenance in the traditional sense; they eat it for raw material. 

The true marvel of the lithovores, however, is not their physical anatomy, but their collective neurology. They possess no individual brains, no central queen, and no pheromone trails. Instead, their consciousness is entirely electrical. As millions of their dry, waxy carapaces rub against one another in the freezing, low-humidity air, they generate a massive, continuous triboelectric charge. This charge manifests as a faint, bluish corona of static electricity that arcs and dances across the surface of the swarm. This electrified web is their nervous system. When a tectonic shift fractures the ground ahead, the change in the rock’s conductivity alters the static field, and a million legs adjust their trajectory in a microsecond. The bluish lightning is the physical manifestation of their collective thought—a sprawling, decentralized mind thinking in volts and amperes, coordinating the labor of millions with zero latency.

Their harvesting process is a masterclass in biological fluid dynamics. The swarm organizes itself into vast, sweeping conveyor lines. The outer edges of the swarm move slowly, acting as the "intake" valves, where the lithovores scrape the mineral-rich frost and snap off flakes of raw stone, storing the grit in specialized, expandable crop-sacs. Once loaded, they merge into the inner, faster-moving lanes, accelerating toward the geothermal fissures. From above, the movement looks like the rhythmic, pulsing veins of a colossal, slate-gray heart. The sound is a muffled, hypnotic *click-clack-click-clack*, a million mandibles and iron-tipped feet keeping perfect, metronomic time.

When the conveyor lines reach the hissing, steaming geothermal vents, the most dangerous phase of the industrial process begins. The fissures are the swarm’s external stomachs—natural blast furnaces where the earth’s mantle vents raw, subterranean heat. Here, a specialized caste of larger, heavier lithovores—the "crucible-bearers"—take over. Their underbellies are coated in a thick, ablative chitin that constantly chars, flakes away, and regenerates, protecting them from the searing updrafts. Moving with mechanical precision, they approach the lips of the hissing cracks and regurgitate their harvested stone and mineral-frost into the boiling, superheated steam and acidic volcanic gases below. 

They do not wait. They immediately step back, allowing the earth to do the work of chemical breakdown. Minutes later, as the geyser vents pulse and spit, the crucible-bearers step forward again, using specialized, heat-resistant scooping mandibles to retrieve the chemically altered, nutrient-dense slurry that bubbles up from the deep. This cooked, mineral-rich mud is then passed back down the conveyor lines, distributed across the weeping plateau, and excreted into the shallow, slushy marshes.

This is the grand, silent purpose of the swarm. They are the primordial terraformers of the embryonic world. The mountain is thrusting up raw, dead, sterilized rock, entirely devoid of the complex chemistry required for life. The lithovores are the bridge between violent geology and the future biosphere. By running the planet’s raw bones through their biological refinery, by using the earth’s own internal fire to cook the stone, they are systematically manufacturing soil. The fine, nutrient-rich sludge they wash into the freezing marshes is slowly building the foundation for the first mosses, the first deep-rooted sedges, the first true life that will one day follow them.

They build no hives and leave no ruins because they do not need to. Their legacy is the landscape itself. As the subsonic thud of the tectonic uplift continues to push the plateau higher into the bruised, twilight sky, the swarm marches on. Beneath the heavy fog, illuminated by the ghostly, arcing flashes of their own static minds, the million-legged refinery clicks and hums, endlessly chewing the new world into being.
