Life in the rotunda does not begin with birth; it begins with subtraction. 

To understand what lives here, you must first look closely at the base of the food chain—the grazers of the ontological bleed. The tiny, multi-legged geometries of carved chalk that scurry across the floor are not merely animals; they are living abacuses, biological calculators mapping the decay of the room’s physics. They do not eat in any traditional sense. Instead, they cluster around the microscopic spatial folds where the stone is un-happening, absorbing the raw, frictionless static of dissolving reality. As they feed, their chalky bodies become momentarily translucent, revealing inner structures of shifting, non-Euclidean fractals before they scurry away to excrete tiny, temporary pebbles of condensed, stabilized space.

Above them, the lapis lazuli and wrought-iron beetles serve as the heavy lifters of this dry ecology. They are the scavengers of the void. When a flake of the pale pumice detaches and begins its minutes-long, feather-slow descent into non-existence, the beetles catch it. Their jointed iron legs lock onto the fading stone, and they carry these heavy, half-erased flakes into the deep, shadowed mortise-and-tenon joints of the walls. There, they build shimmering, impossible hives—structures that exist in four dimensions, folding inward on themselves, visible only as a dizzying blur of polished blue stone and dark iron when you look at them from the corner of your eye. 

The pumice moths are the pollinators of the unmaking. With their heavy, sluggish grace, they flutter from the dissolving central pillar to the dense thickets of mica ferns. They do not carry pollen; they carry the *concept* of the pillar. When they land on the beryllium fronds, they dust the plants with the memory of the stone, allowing the flora to grow in the exact shape of the architecture that is disappearing. 

And the flora *is* growing. The copper-wire vines and basalt-rooted thickets are not just passive decorations; they are the nervous system of the rotunda. In a space where sound dies the moment it is born, the plants have evolved to feel the vibrational absence. The spun beryllium fronds of the ferns act as cilia, sensing the subtle shifts in atmospheric pressure when a spatial fold opens. When the reality of the room fractures, the copper vines do not recoil; they lean in. They plunge their fibrous basalt roots directly into the razor-sharp chisel grooves that bleed into other universes, drinking the cold, alien starlight and the raw, unformed potential leaking from the broken geometry. The heavy, coral-like flowers that bloom from them do not produce seeds; they produce small, perfectly spherical drops of liquid silence that fall to the floor and evaporate into the heavy, dry mist.

But the true inhabitants of this place—the apex of this silent, desiccated ecosystem—are not the insects, nor the plants. 

They are the walls themselves. 

If you stand perfectly still and let your eyes adjust to the golden-hour light, you will notice that the claustrophobically dense bas-reliefs choking the walls are not static. The overlapping carvings of interlocking joints, flying buttresses, and geometric patterns are shifting. It is a movement so profoundly slow, so deeply embedded in geological time, that a human heart could beat a thousand times before a single carved limb completes a single gesture. 

These are the Masons. The original architects of the rotunda never left; they simply became the architecture, and the architecture became them. 

They are towering, impossibly thin entities composed of the same pale, porous pumice as the room. They have no faces, no eyes, no mouths. Their bodies are constructed of the same impossible angles that break down the physics of the room—their limbs joint in ways that are simultaneously acute and obtuse, folding back into themselves in tesseractic loops. When they move, they do not bend or flex like biological creatures; they slide, rotate, and reconfigure like the interlocking pieces of a colossal, divine puzzle box. 

They are still working. They never stopped. 

In their long, multi-jointed hands, they wield the primitive tools of their trade: heavy stone mauls and soft-bronze chisels. But the mauls are not made of ordinary stone; they are carved from the compressed, acoustic dead-zones of the room. When a Mason swings a maul, it strikes the chisel with absolutely zero sound. The impact does not create a shockwave in the air; it creates a shockwave in the local ontology. 

You watch one of the Masons standing beside the massive, un-happening pillar in the center of the room. It raises its bone chisel and taps the pale pumice. The strike is silent, but you feel it in your teeth—a deep, resonant vibration of *erasure*. The Mason is not shaping the stone; it is mining reality. It is carefully, methodically carving away the three-dimensionality of the pillar, flake by microscopic flake. 

As you watch them, the purpose of the rotunda begins to dawn on you, shifting the image of the inhabitants from mere builders to something far more profound. 

They are not destroying the space. They are unmaking a cage. 

The sunken, cathedral-sized rotunda is not a building. It is a chrysalis. The millions of hours of labor, the dense, hoarder’s sanctuary of manual craft, the absolute lack of erosion, the aggressive dryness, the ambient golden light—it was all an incubator. The pale, porous mineral is an eggshell, and the Masons are the embryonic teeth of whatever vast, incomprehensible entity is growing inside it. 

The flora and fauna are not an accident of nature; they are the amniotic ecology of a waking god. The chalk geometries calculate the thinning of the shell. The lapis beetles reinforce the structural integrity of the remaining reality. The copper vines and mica ferns act as the umbilical cords, drawing in the ambient light and the dry silicate mist to feed the consciousness expanding within the stone. 

And the Masons are the entity’s first thoughts, given physical form, tasked with dissolving the womb from the inside out. 

The breakdown of the rotunda is accelerating, though only by fractions of a millimeter per century. The golden-hour light is growing slightly warmer, slightly more intense, as the entity beneath the floorboards stirs in its deep, dreamless sleep. The heavy, dry silence of the room is not merely an acoustic property; it is the held breath of a colossal organism waiting to be born. 

You stand in the center of the un-happening room. A pumice moth lands on your shoulder, its translucent wings dusting your skin with the memory of a vanished pillar. A Mason, thirty feet tall and folded into a shape that hurts your eyes to look at, slowly turns its featureless head toward you. It does not see you, for it has no eyes. But it senses the heavy, aqueous, three-dimensional reality of your body. 

It raises its bone chisel. It raises its maul of condensed silence. 

It is not going to hurt you. It is simply going to carve away the illusion of your depth, flattening you into a beautiful, permanent bas-relief on the wall of the hatching egg, so that you, too, might be part of the unmaking when the shell finally breaks, and the dry, warm void rushes in.
