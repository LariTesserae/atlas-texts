If you stand perfectly still on the humid, polished highway of braided iron-bamboo, and let your eyes adjust to the heavy, pearl-lit twilight, the wood itself will eventually begin to move. 

At first, you will think it is just the play of the mist and the non-Euclidean warping of the space. A shadow detaches from the sweeping curve of a massive liana. A limb, impossibly long and jointed in three places, reaches out from behind a strut that is supposedly a mile away, yet the fingers are suddenly brushing the damp moss just inches from your face. Space folds around them, and they fold with it.

These are the weavers. The tenders of the warp. The children of the High Loom.

They are bipedal, but only in the loosest, most generous sense of the word. Their bodies are elongated, stretched thin by generations of living in a world where gravity is a localized suggestion and distance is a matter of perspective. An adult weaver stands perhaps nine feet tall, but their posture is perpetually stooped, their spines curved to match the sweeping, organic arcs of the architecture. They possess no sharp angles. Every joint, every digit, every slope of their musculature is rounded and smoothed, mimicking the eroded, wind-polished grain of the ancient wood. 

Their skin is the color of wet heartwood—a deep, mottled mahogany that weeps the same warm condensation as the beams they walk upon. It is slick and hairless, textured like tightly packed bark, and completely seamless. They do not wear clothes; the heavy, greenhouse warmth of the Loom requires none, and their skin secretes a fragrant, resinous oil that waterproofs them and repels the parasitic fungi that bloom in the perpetual damp. 

But it is their faces that truly unmoor you from the familiar. Where eyes should be, there is only smooth, unbroken skin. In the perpetual gloom of the High Loom, and in a space where light bends and loops back on itself, vision is not just useless; it is a liability. To see here is to be driven mad by the broken geometry. Instead, the weavers perceive the world through tension and vibration. 

Running down the center of their long, narrow faces, and continuing down their chests, are deep, resonant hollows lined with stiff, bristly cilia. These hollows act as acoustic and tactile chambers. They "see" the Loom by feeling the subsonic hum of the continental lattice. They can feel the weight of a manta-glider landing on a branch three tiers below them; they can sense the microscopic shifting of a mortise-and-tenon joint settling under the stress of a high-altitude gale. When a weaver turns its face toward you, you feel a strange, rhythmic pressure in your own chest—the gentle, inquiring pulse of its sonar-like resonance mapping your density and your heartbeat.

Their hands are the most startling revelation of the Loom’s history. The prompt of your mind might wonder where the stone adzes and bone chisels went, the primitive tools that shaped this god-like megastructure. You are looking at them. The weavers’ fingers do not end in soft pads or flat nails. They terminate in hardened, calcified spurs of dense, ivory-like bone, shaped exactly like the scoops of adzes and the sharp edges of chisels. They do not carry tools; they *are* the tools. When a weaver needs to shape a new strut or prune a rebellious offshoot of iron-bamboo, it simply brings its hands to the wood and begins to carve, its bone-tools sliding through the fibrous cambium with a soft, wet *shhhk-shhhk* sound.

To watch a weaver move is to watch a masterclass in navigating broken physics. They do not walk so much as they pour themselves through the folds of the Loom. Because gravity pools in the knots of the wood, a weaver will step onto a steeply slanted beam and simply lean backward, letting the local gravity catch them, walking upside down along the underside of the strut with the same casual grace as a cat on a fence. When they need to cross a vast, empty gap between platforms, they do not leap. They find a "thin spot"—a fold in the spatial fabric where a hundred yards compresses into three steps. You will watch a weaver step off a ledge into the dizzying, cloudy abyss, only for their foot to seamlessly emerge from the mist and touch down on a distant platform, their body stretching and compressing like a reflection in a funhouse mirror, entirely unbothered by the visual impossibility of their own transit.

They are profoundly, deeply silent. They have no vocal cords. In an ecosystem where sound is swallowed by moss and vast emptiness, spoken language would be an evolutionary dead end. Instead, they communicate through the wood itself. 

When two weavers meet, they do not gesture or embrace. They press their long, multi-jointed palms flat against the polished floor of the beam. Through the capillary networks and the dense, interwoven fibers of the Loom, they send chemical and vibrational signals. A "conversation" is a shared tremor in the wood, a complex exchange of sap-borne pheromones and rhythmic tapping that travels for miles. If you are standing between them, you won't hear a word, but you will feel a rapid, fluttering vibration buzzing up through the soles of your feet, like the purr of a colossal, hidden engine.

Their society is one of endless, meditative maintenance. The Loom is not a finished structure; it is a living, growing entity that requires constant, generational tending. The weavers spend their long lives pruning the overgrowth, guiding the directional growth of new lianas, and tightening the ancient lashings. They work in a state of flowing trance, their movements slow and deliberate, perfectly synchronized with the slow, churning motion of the ecosystem. A weaver might spend a decade coaxing a single, massive root to bridge a gap between two thunderhead-scraping arches, singing to the wood not with sound, but by rubbing its resonant chest-hollows against the bark, transmitting the exact subsonic frequency the plant needs to accelerate its cellular growth.

They are the apex of this silent world, yet they are entirely benign. They do not hunt the velvet-furred arboreal cats or the gliding rodents. Their diet consists of the massive, snowflake-sized spores that drift upward in the thermal drafts, which they catch in wide, funnel-like membranes hidden beneath their jaws, and the nutrient-rich condensation that pools in the hollows of the wood. When a flock of bioluminescent moth-avians lands on a weaver’s shoulders to rest, the weaver does not brush them away; it simply continues its work, a walking monument to the wild.

But the most haunting truth of the weavers is found at the massive, central intersections of the Loom—the colossal, cathedral-sized knots where the thickest cables of petrified lianas cross and bear the unimaginable weight of the continent. 

Here, you will find the Elders. 

The weavers live for thousands of years, but they do not die in the traditional sense. When a weaver grows too old, when its bone-chisel fingers become dull and its resonant chest-hollows begin to calcify, it seeks out a major structural joint. There, it sits, folding its impossibly long limbs against its chest, and presses its hands deep into the heartwood. 

Over centuries, the weaver stops moving. Its resinous skin hardens. Its biology slowly merges with the flora. The weaver petrifies, its body turning into the very iron-bamboo and liana that makes up the Loom. If you look closely at the deep, weathered grain of these central, load-bearing knots, you will see the faint, ghostly scalloping in the wood. Those are not just tool marks. Those are the fossilized, overlapping fingers of a thousand ancient weavers, locked in an eternal, structural embrace, their bodies having become the literal foundations of the sky. 

They are still alive, in a slow, botanical sense. If you press your ear to the great central knots, beneath the *tink... tink...* of the dripping water and the subsonic hum of the wind, you can hear a sound like a incredibly slow, deep heartbeat. The ancestors are still dreaming, holding the continent together, while their long-limbed children walk the twilight beams above, weaving the sky, keeping the tension, tending to the endless, silent damp.
