To understand the gloom-striders, you must first abandon the idea of the individual. In the crushing, muggy dark of the tectonic chimney, the individual is merely a temporary node in a vast, vertical organism. 

They are the flesh of the mountain.

Imagine a creature with the muscular, compact torso of a macaque, but possessed of an arachnid’s geometric grace. They are entirely blind, their faces smooth and unbroken by eyes, dominated instead by large, deeply cupped, leathery ears and a blunt, highly sensitive snout that twitches constantly, tasting the heavy, ammonia-tinged air. Their fur is a marvel of biological engineering: short, incredibly dense, and the bruised, purple-grey color of wet slate. It does not merely repel the constant, weeping condensation of the chimney; it wicks it, channeling the moisture down their spines to keep their skin dry and warm against the slick rock. 

But it is their limbs that define their existence. Elongated and multi-jointed, their arms and legs fold and unfold with a fluid, hydraulic smoothness. At the end of each limb are expansive, fleshy pads, deeply grooved and perpetually tacky. These pads do not just grip the stone; they read it. Through the soles of their feet and the palms of their four-fingered hands, the striders feel the microscopic topography of the glassy walls, the subtle vibrations of their neighbors, and the deep, tectonic shivers of the earth itself.

Because there is no space to walk, they exist in a state of perpetual, three-dimensional suspension. The colony is a living, breathing tapestry woven directly onto the basalt. They cling to the rock, and where the rock is crowded, they cling to each other. A strider’s back becomes a floor for its neighbor; a draped arm becomes a bridge for a passing juvenile. When the colony shifts, it does not do so through chaotic scrambling, but in a slow, rolling ripple. A wave of movement travels up or down the shaft, bodies unpeeling and reattaching in perfect, unconscious synchrony, like a murmuration of starlings rendered in heavy, warm muscle.

In this pitch-black void, vision is a useless evolutionary relic. The striders *see* the world through a hurricane of sound. 

Their echolocation is not the simple, high-pitched ping of a bat; it is a complex, polyphonic language of the dark. The staccato clicks they produce are so sharp and dense that they create a continuous, overlapping topographical map of the chimney. Through the returning echoes, a strider perceives the exact curve of the wall, the jagged edge of a flaked chert shard, the armored ridges of a blind centipede, and the soft, yielding contours of its kin. They "see" the texture of the lichen and the density of the air. To a strider, the darkness is not empty; it is a blindingly bright, three-dimensional matrix of acoustic reflections.

Beneath the sharp clicks lies the bedrock of their society: the chest-rumbles. These deep, subsonic vibrations are felt more than heard. They rattle the sternum and vibrate through the gripping pads into the stone. A low, resonant thrum is a claim of space, a soothing lullaby to a clinging infant, or a warning to a rival. The Chimney hums with a thousand overlapping frequencies, a chaotic, deafening choir that serves as the social glue of the colony. The noise is so absolute, so physically heavy, that it acts as a blanket, wrapping the striders in a cocoon of acoustic warmth. 

They are foragers in a vertical desert, and their technology is as ephemeral as the condensation that drips from the ceiling. A strider’s four-fingered hands are astonishingly dexterous, capable of delicate manipulation, yet they build nothing. To build a nest would be to disrupt the lattice; to hoard a tool would be to carry dead weight in a world defined by clinging. 

Instead, they live in a state of immediate, tactile engagement with the stone. A strider will reach out, its sensitive fingers brushing the narrow ledges until it finds a water-tumbled pebble or a sharp flake of chert. With rhythmic, practiced scrapes, it harvests the ghostly, crustose lichens and the mineral-rich biofilm that sweat from the rock. The scraping sound—*shhhk, shhhk, shhhk*—joins the cacophony. They bring the gritty, metallic-tasting paste to their mouths, chewing with flat, grinding molars. 

Occasionally, a heavier, rounder cobble is selected. From the unfathomable, crushing depths below the accessible chimney, blind, armored centipedes sometimes wander up the walls, seeking the warmth. When a strider’s echolocation paints the shape of this heavily armored prey, it brings the cobble down in a precise, brutal strike. The *crack* of the calcified shell shattering is a celebrated sound in the dark, followed by the wet, smacking sounds of a rare, protein-rich feast. When the meal is finished, or the lichen scraped clean, the stone is simply released. It tumbles down the curving throat—*clack, clack, splash*—joining the deep, warm pool at the bottom, lost forever. A new stone will be found when a new stone is needed.

The water in this world is a gentle, constant baptism. The extreme temperature gradient causes the upper walls to weep. Fat, heavy drops of condensation gather on the overhangs and fall, splashing against the slate-grey fur of the striders, running down their wicking spines, and pooling at the bottom. In that lightless sump, a small, tepid lake collects, rimmed by pale, thread-like fungal mycelium. This ghostly fungus feeds on the steady rain of guano and the discarded centipede shells, blooming in the damp heat. The striders occasionally descend to the very bottom of the lattice to drink from the pool, lapping the warm, mineral-heavy water, and grazing on the fleshy, blind mushrooms that grow in the muck. It is a perfectly closed loop of nutrients, water, and heat.

But the most profound truth of the gloom-striders is not found in their biology, but in their geology. 

Run your hand along the wall of the chimney, and you will feel no rough basalt, no jagged granite, no pitting or fissures. The stone is polished to a glassy, undulating sheen, smooth as a river-tumbled skipping stone, curving in gentle, organic swells. 

This is not the work of water. This is the work of the song.

For tens of thousands of years, the deafening, concentrated acoustic energy of a million chest-rumbles and echolocation clicks has been trapped within the narrow, bell-shaped geometry of the shaft. The soundwaves, reflecting and amplifying, have subjected the rock to relentless, high-frequency vibration. Aided by the lubricating weep of the condensation, the acoustic resonance has literally shaken the microscopic edges of the stone loose, grain by microscopic grain. 

The striders are singing the mountain hollow. 

They do not know this, of course. To them, the smooth, sweeping curves of the glassy wall are simply the natural shape of the world. They do not realize that the exact pitch of their clicks has subtly evolved over millennia to match the resonant frequency of the chamber, or that the chamber’s shape has warped to amplify their voices. They are locked in a deep-time symbiosis with the geology. The noise is the chisel, the dampness is the coolant, and the overwhelming, dense swarm of warm, twitching life are the unwitting artisans of their own geology.

To stand among them in the suffocating, roaring dark is to witness a slow-motion sculpture. You are watching a species that has not merely adapted to the roof of the world, but has, through the sheer, unyielding volume of their collective voice, become the very force that shapes the stone. They are the breath of the earth, trapped in a throat of glass, singing the dark into existence.
