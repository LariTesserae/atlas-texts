To find the inhabitants of this lithic metropolis, one must first stop looking for movement and begin looking for rhythm. They do not stand out against the landscape; they are a biological extension of its geology and hydrology. When the shifting mists thin and the pale, high-altitude light catches the sweeping terraces, what initially appears to be the smooth, pale weathering of the rock resolves into the slow, deliberate motion of the city’s people.

They are a species shaped entirely by the crucible of deep time, high altitude, and perpetual dampness. They are humanoid in their basic architecture—bipedal, bilaterally symmetrical, with two arms and a head—but the extremes of their environment have stretched and compressed their physiology into something profoundly alien. 

Because the air is thin and biting, their torsos are deep and barrel-chested, housing massive, highly efficient lungs and an enlarged heart that beats with a slow, heavy thud, barely forty times a minute. Their metabolism is glacial. They do not shiver; instead, they possess a thick, subcutaneous layer of insulating lipid tissue that gives their bodies a smooth, streamlined, almost cetacean contour. Their skin is entirely devoid of hair. It is pale, mottled in shades of ash, lichen-green, and wet-stone grey, and it secretes a continuous, microscopic layer of hydrophobic oil. This natural sealant prevents the perpetual dampness from chilling them to the bone, causing the freezing condensation to bead and roll off their bodies like water off a duck’s back.

Their extremities are their most remarkable tools, evolved for a life of verticality and subtraction. They wear no clothes and no shoes. Their feet are broad and splayed, with thick, gripping pads on the toes and heels that act like natural suction cups against the wet stone. The skin on their soles is heavily calloused, yet so sensitive they can feel the micro-vibrations of water flowing through the aqueducts deep within the rock. When they walk, their footfalls produce the exact soft, rhythmic scuff of wet leather—the "boot" sound that echoes in the quiet plazas. 

Their hands are large, with long, multi-jointed fingers designed to reach deep into fissures. They possess no fingernails in the human sense; instead, the tips of their fingers and toes are capped with thick, flat, spade-like plates of dense keratin. These keratin spades are the primary tools of their civilization. With them, the inhabitants scrape away the softened, frost-fractured rock, pack wet earth into cracks, and sweep away the resulting gravel. 

They are a people of profound, absolute silence. They have no vocal cords capable of producing the complex, loud sounds of human speech, and in the acoustically deadened canyons, shouting would be useless anyway. Instead, they communicate through a highly developed tactile and visual language. In the damp air, they release subtle, localized pheromones that carry emotional and directional intent. Up close, they communicate through a complex sign language of hand gestures, micro-expressions, and the pressing of palms against one another’s chests to feel the rhythm of their slow heartbeats. When a group works together on a cliff face, they move in a synchronized, fluid choreography, anticipating each other's movements through a deep, almost hive-like empathy forged by millennia of close, quiet proximity.

Their daily life is inextricably bound to the city’s hydrological cycle. They are the caretakers of the water machine. In the pale mornings, they move through the spiraling staircases and terraced pools, using their keratin-tipped fingers to clear microscopic debris from the runnels, ensuring the water flows without obstruction. They do not hoard the water; they revere its flow. To block the water would be to halt the breath of the city. 

Sustenance in this sparse environment requires meticulous cultivation. They are farmers of the micro-ecosystem. On the sunlit upper terraces, they tend to the crustose lichens, feeding the patches with mineral-rich dust scraped from the deeper, uncarved rock, and harvesting the slow-growing organisms for dense, slow-burning calories. In the shallow, freezing cisterns of the lower canyons, they cultivate the translucent amphipods. They wade into the icy water, their pale bodies blending with the stone, and use finely woven nets made from the tough, wiry alpine grasses to sift the tiny crustaceans from the pools. They eat their food raw, consuming it in small, highly efficient quantities that match their slow metabolic needs.

But their true purpose, the driving obsession of their species, is the Great Subtraction. They do not view themselves as builders, but as revealers. In their silent, shared philosophy, the city already exists perfectly within the mountain; their sacred duty is merely to remove the excess stone to set it free. 

The carving process is a meditative, generational act. During the freezing nights, they pack wet earth and gravel into the natural fissures of the escarpments. As the temperature drops, the water in the earth freezes and expands, fracturing the stone. At dawn, the inhabitants return to the cliffs. Using wooden wedges—carved from the stunted, creeping shrubs—and their own body weight, they lever the fractured stone away. They then use their hands and keratin spades to smooth the newly exposed surfaces, guiding the drip of condensation to wear down the sharp edges. A single alcove might take three hundred years to carve. A grand plaza might take ten thousand. They do not rush. Time, to them, is not a resource to be spent, but a medium in which they swim.

Because they live in such perfect equilibrium with their environment, their society has no concept of war, wealth, or accumulation. There is nothing to hoard in a city of bare stone and flowing water. Their possessions are limited to the smooth, water-polished stones they sometimes carry to weigh down their woven grass nets, and the intricate, geometric patterns they carve into the deepest, most hidden walls of their private alcoves—a silent, tactile literature meant to be read only by the fingertips in the dark.

When one of them reaches the end of their long, slow life—a lifespan that can stretch across several human centuries—they do not bury their dead in the hard rock, nor do they burn them. Instead, the community carries the deceased to the highest, most active aqueducts at the very crown of the plateau. There, the body is laid in the rushing, freezing water. Over the course of weeks, the relentless flow, the cold, and the microscopic aquatic life break the body down. The minerals of their bones and the lipids of their skin are washed down through the terraces, into the cisterns, and out over the escarpments. 

They become the very sediment that packs the fissures to crack the stone. They become the nutrients that feed the pale green lichen. They return to the hydrological cycle, ensuring that the water continues to carve, and the city continues to grow.

To watch them is to watch the mountain itself breathe. As the afternoon squalls roll in and the fog thickens, the inhabitants retreat into their honeycombed dwellings. They curl together in the dry, smooth alcoves, their slow heartbeats synchronizing in the dark, their pale skin glowing faintly in the diffuse light. Outside, the water continues its endless, rhythmic dripping, and the wind sighs through the empty doorways. The people sleep, dreaming in silence, waiting for the mist to clear so they can return to the patient, eternal work of removing the world, one grain of stone at a time.
