To walk the wet, polished plazas of this high city is to share the space with beings who are not of the animal kingdom, for the plateau’s absolute lack of fauna extends to its builders. There are no lungs to draw the thick, balmy air, no hearts to beat against the heavy silence, no vocal cords to break the monastic quiet. The inhabitants of this highland world are a sentient, mobile extension of its flora—a people of lignin, cellulose, and living hyphae, as much a part of the landscape as the ferns and the weeping basalt.

They move through the wide, empty spaces between the stone pavilions with a soundless, fluid grace. They are tall, often reaching eight or nine feet in height, with willowy, tapering proportions that mimic the slow-growing trees of the lush hollows. They do not walk with the percussive, jointed strike of bone and muscle. Instead, their locomotion is driven by shifts in hydraulic turgor pressure; they glide and sway, their limbs bending with the smooth, continuous curve of a willow branch in a gentle stream. 

Their "skin" is a breathtaking mosaic of botanical textures. Their torsos and limbs are wrapped in overlapping layers of pale, fibrous bark, smooth and cool to the touch, resembling the peeled wood of the plateau’s ancient trees. Veins of translucent, sap-filled tubules run beneath this outer layer, pulsing with a slow, bioluminescent jade light that is visible only when they pass through the crisp, clean shadows of the architecture. Where a human might have a face, they possess a crown of delicate, translucent petals, much like the pale orchids that cling to the crevices. These petal-arrays do not hold eyes or mouths; instead, they are highly sensitive chemoreceptors and photoreceptors, fluttering imperceptibly as they read the subtle chemical gradients, humidity shifts, and steady, unvarying light of their world.

Their feet are bare, but not fleshy. The soles are broad, spongy pads of dense, interwoven root-mycelium. As they step across the damp, hand-chiseled stone, these root-pads temporarily graft to the wet surface. Through this direct contact, they drink. They do not forage, hunt, or consume solid matter. They draw the pure, mineral-rich weeping water directly up through their xylem, supplementing their energy by photosynthesizing beneath the steady, natural daylight that bathes the plateau. When they stand still in the wide plazas, they are quite literally rooting themselves into the city, entering a state of deep, waking contemplation.

It was these very bodies, and the biological imperatives of their kind, that shaped the city. Lacking metal, and lacking the brute, destructive force of animal muscle, they built their sprawling grid through patience, chemistry, and organic leverage. The massive, mortarless stone blocks were not merely chipped away; they were coaxed. The builders secreted mild, highly targeted organic acids from their fingertips to soften the sharp, young basalt, while utilizing wedges of ultra-dense, water-expanding wood—grown specifically for the purpose—to split the rock along its natural fault lines. Their "primitive tools" were pumice stones bound in hardened tree resin, and abrasive pads of silicified fern-roots. They smoothed the violent, jagged geology into elegant, geometric slabs not by conquering the stone, but by engaging in a slow, physical dialogue with it, wearing it down drop by drop, scrape by scrape, over uncounted centuries. 

Because there is no wind to carry voices, and no animal ears to hear them, their society communicates in a language of scent and dust. When they gather in the sweeping plazas, they engage in conversations of spores. From the porous vents along their fibrous shoulders, they release delicate, drifting clouds of microscopic dust. These clouds are not merely reproductive; they are complex chemical vocabularies. A mingling of spores between two individuals is an exchange of memories, emotions, and architectural plans. To stand among them during a gathering is to be enveloped in a shifting, invisible tapestry of aromas: the sharp, clean tang of crushed fern to express clarity; the deep, loamy scent of wet earth to convey sorrow or reverence; the sweet, heavy perfume of orchid nectar to signify joy or agreement. The air in the plazas often shimmers with these faintly tinted clouds, hanging perfectly still in the windless humidity before slowly settling onto the wet stone.

They are the caretakers of the frozen equilibrium, but they are also the agents of its eventual, distant change. The profound silence of the plateau is their sanctuary, yet they recognize the violently young, jagged nature of the bedrock. They are the reason the lush vegetation exists in such vibrant, isolated pockets. As they walk the empty, sweeping expanses of bare, black stone, they occasionally pause to press their long, tapering fingers into the deep V-shaped fissures and shallow depressions. From their fingertips, they deposit concentrated seeds and mycelial threads, tending to the spongy mosses and heavy ferns.

They know it will take millennia for the sharp cliffs to soften, for the soil to deepen, for the isolated pockets of emerald and jade to eventually bridge the vast expanses of wet black stone. They are in no rush. The temperature will not drop, the sun will not race, and the water will not flood. 

To watch them is to witness the physical embodiment of the plateau’s peace. A solitary figure might stand for days at the edge of a sheer, unweathered cliff, its translucent petal-crown turned toward the steady light, its root-feet drinking from the weeping rock, its sap glowing faintly in the shadow. It is a being entirely devoid of urgency, perfectly adapted to a world caught in a permanent present, serving as both the architect of its quiet city and the gentle, silent gardener of its distant future.
