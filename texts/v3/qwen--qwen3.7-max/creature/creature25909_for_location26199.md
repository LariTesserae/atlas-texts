It is true that there are no beasts here. There are no birds to sing, no insects to pollinate, no mammals to scurry through the damp, suffocating undergrowth. Flesh and bone would be pulverized by the percussive booming of the taproots, shredded by the serrated, glass-like leaves, or simply drowned in the tepid, weeping condensation. The flora does not need animals. The flora is entirely, terrifyingly self-sufficient. 

But a metropolis, by its very nature, implies a populace. A city cannot exist without its citizens. 

As you stand in the deafening roar of the hyper-growth, watching a fifty-foot bamboo skyscraper violently bloom, rot, and collapse into mulch, you begin to notice that the neon pollen expelled in its death-throe does not simply drift away on the heavy, stagnant air. Instead, the glowing, iridescent dust catches in a localized eddy of broken gravity. It swirls, compressing, drawing inward. The deafening, artillery-crack cacophony of the tearing fibers and splitting timber suddenly pitches upward, harmonizing into a specific, resonant frequency—a pure, vibrating chord that you feel in your teeth.

From this acoustic and botanical crucible, a Citizen steps forth.

It is not an animal. It is not quite a plant. It is an emergent entity, a localized consciousness born from the friction of a billion births and deaths, an avatar of the city’s autophagic loop. 

The Citizen is towering, perhaps nine feet tall, but its proportions are deeply, instinctively wrong. It possesses too many joints, its limbs folding and articulating in directions that mock skeletal logic. It is composed not of solid matter, but of tightly compressed, hyper-dense cellulose and suspended, glowing sap. It looks like a walking, jagged chandelier of raw, splintered ironwood and liquid light. Where a head should be, there is no face—only a fractal bloom of serrated, translucent leaves that constantly open and close, drinking in the distorted, glitching light of the canopy. 

It does not walk so much as it *translates*. Because the geometry of the Metropolis is non-Euclidean, the Citizen does not need to navigate around obstacles; it simply steps into the spatial folds. You watch it take a single, jarring step forward, its foot disappearing into a mirage where the street seems to fold upward into the sky, and instantly, its torso and head emerge from a thicket of blooming orchids thirty yards to your left. It moves with a stop-motion, stuttering grace, untethered from the rigid laws of physics that govern your own body. 

As more of them coalesce from the violent churn of the urban sprawl, you realize what they are doing. They are the maintenance workers of the paradoxes. They are the ballast of the broken world. 

You watch one approach the massive, spiraling trunk that leans at a forty-five-degree angle, its base resting in a pool of impossibly flat water. The Citizen plunges its multi-jointed arms into the tepid pool. Its fingers, ending in sharp, fresh-iron thorns, spread out like a root system beneath the surface. As it does, the water’s unnatural flatness solidifies, shimmering with a faint, bioluminescent grid. The Citizen is actively *enforcing* the paradox, holding the localized physics-breaking together through sheer, concentrated will, anchoring the glitch so the city can continue to build upon it. 

When they move, they do not make footfalls. Their passage sounds like the rubbing of tuning forks and the deep, resonant groan of twisting timber. They smell of ozone, hot copper, and the hyper-sweet, intoxicating rot of overripe fruit. They are sterile, perfect, and utterly alien. They communicate not with voices, but by flashing pulses of bioluminescent sap through their translucent limbs, a silent, rapid-fire language of light that ripples through the dense, brutalist thickets like a neural network firing across a massive, green brain.

And then, one of them stops. 

The fractal bloom of leaves at the apex of its body ceases its rhythmic opening and closing. The deep, spiraling hollows within its chest—cavities of calcified wood that glow with trapped, neon pollen—flare brightly. 

It has noticed you. 

To the Citizens of the Metropolis, you are an anomaly. You are a dense, heavy, rigidly Euclidean object in a world of fluid, folding geometry. You are a creature of static physics, bleeding heat and carbon dioxide into their perfectly calibrated, self-devouring loop. You are a weed in the grid.

The Citizen turns toward you. It does not run; it simply folds the space between you. One moment it is fifty feet away, and in the next, it is standing directly before you, towering over your fragile, flesh-and-bone frame. Up close, the heat radiating from its body is immense, like standing before an open oven. The stench of raw cambium and hot loam is overpowering. 

It tilts its fractal head, observing you with a terrifying, impersonal curiosity. There is no malice in its posture, no predatory hunger. It looks at you the way a gardener looks at a strange, misplaced stone in a meticulously planned zen garden. It is trying to understand how you fit into the zoning of the Metropolis. 

Slowly, it raises a hand. The limb is a braided cable of weeping, stinging vines and sharp, jagged splinters. It reaches toward your face. You feel the air pressure drop as its fingers pass through a micro-fold in space, the tips of its thorns hovering mere inches from your eyes. It wants to prune you. It wants to fold your rigid, three-dimensional biology into the multi-tiered, non-Euclidean canopy. It wants to integrate you into the architecture.

You realize, with a cold, sinking dread that cuts through the stifling humidity, that the Metropolis is not just a place. It is a living, thinking machine, an unstoppable force of botanical infrastructure. And the Citizens are its mind, its hands, and its immune system. 

The deafening roar of the city swells around you, a continuous, percussive booming that shakes the very bedrock, as the Citizen’s glowing, splintered fingers finally close the distance, reaching out to fold you into the grid.
