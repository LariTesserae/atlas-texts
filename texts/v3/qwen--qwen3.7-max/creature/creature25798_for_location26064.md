To look upon a Marrow-singer is to look upon a creature that has entirely surrendered to the deep time of its environment. They are towering, impossibly slender beings, standing three or four meters tall, yet they possess a liquid, heavy grace. They have six multi-jointed limbs that end in broad, splayed, gelatinous pads, distributing their mass so perfectly that they leave only the faintest, shallowest depressions in the moss—depressions that the spongy ground takes nearly a century to fully exhale and erase. Their skin is the color of wet parchment, translucent and mottled, revealing a slow-pulsing vascular system that pumps a pale, bioluminescent sap in time with the continent’s subsonic groans. 

They have no eyes; in the perpetual, bruised-peach twilight of the Swell, vision is a useless evolutionary extravagance. Instead, their heads are crowned with blooming, fractal rosettes of trembling cilia. These sensory plumes taste the thermal gradients, read the chemical whispers of the spore-fog, and feel the minute shifts in the barometric pressure of the mist. When a Marrow-singer "looks" at you, it turns its face toward your body heat and breathes in the carbon dioxide of your exhalation, mapping your physical form through the subtle disturbances you make in the heavy air.

The "faint marks" scattered across the landscape are not footprints, but the *Resonance Grooves*. Because the damp cellulose of the air swallows all high-frequency sound, the Marrow-singers communicate through the ground. Using calcified, spade-like spurs on their lower heels, they carve shallow, intricately branching fractal patterns into the living peat. These grooves act as acoustic waveguides. When a singer strikes the center of a groove with a heavy, rhythmic stomp, the subsonic vibration travels flawlessly through the miles-deep phytomass for hundreds of miles. The continent’s surface is etched with this silent, physical sheet music—a sprawling, overlapping web of geological poetry that allows them to sing to one another across the vast, rolling domes.

Their "structures" are the *Graft-cathedrals*. The Marrow-singers do not cut or break the Aero-banyans; they guide them. By secreting specific, highly concentrated enzymatic pastes from their palms, they coax the banyans' massive lateral roots to weave, braid, and fuse into vast, suspended, rib-cage-like domes. Inside these living sanctuaries, the singers gently massage the translucent, house-sized bladders of the canopy, stimulating them to pulse with a synchronized, rhythmic green light. These cathedrals are not shelters from the elements—the singers are perfectly adapted to the weeping mist—but rather acoustic chambers where the subsonic songs of the grooves amplify and harmonize, creating deep, resonant chords that vibrate through the marrow of their bones.

Their society is one of profound, unhurried mutualism. They share the canopy with the spore-rays, allowing the flat, translucent gliders to land on their broad shoulders to rest, absorbing the excess moisture the rays shed. They walk alongside the massive Velvet-striders, occasionally reaching out to gently scrape the parasitic fungi from the striders' mossy fur, consuming the scrapings as a vital source of trace minerals. There is no predation here, no violence, no urgency. The Marrow-singers live for tens of thousands of years, their heartbeats so slow that a human observer might watch them for an hour and assume they are statues carved from pale birch. 

When they gather, they do not speak. They press their cilia-crowned heads together and exchange a single, concentrated drop of enzyme-laced memory-fluid. In that brief, chemical communion, they share decades of sensory experience—the taste of a shifting thermal current, the sorrow of a rotting root-system, the joy of a newly bloomed canopy bladder. 

But it is in their death that the Marrow-singers reveal their ultimate purpose, and solve the mystery of the Great Swell’s geology. 

When a singer feels the deep, cellular fatigue of its final century, it does not seek out a hidden place to die. It walks slowly to the highest, most exposed crest of a continental dome. There, it folds its six limbs beneath it, presses its sensory crown deep into the gelatinous moss, and begins the *Calcification*. Over the course of a millennium, its body draws up heavy iron, silica, and dense minerals from the deepest layers of the peat. Its translucent flesh darkens, hardens, and compresses. Its vascular system lignifies into dense, iron-rich bark. 

The "rocks" of the Great Swell—the massive, rounded nodules that weep continuously in the mist—are not geological formations. They are the calcified elders. The landscape is literally populated by the sleeping, dreaming ancestors of the Marrow-singers. Even in their petrified state, their deep root-connections remain intact, acting as the structural pilings that hold the miles-thick sponge of the continent together. The moisture that beads and weeps from their stony hides is the final, perpetual exhalation of the ancients, watering the moss that grows over them, sustaining the equilibrium of a world that has finished changing, watched over by the slow, silent giants who walk upon their sleeping heads.
