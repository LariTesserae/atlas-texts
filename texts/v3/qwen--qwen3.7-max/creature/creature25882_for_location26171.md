To understand what lives in this submerged, lightless metropolis, one must first discard the human concept of a "citizen." There are no pedestrians here, no builders with blueprints, no merchants, no watchmen. The inhabitants of this secreted city do not merely occupy the architecture; they are the biological culmination of its fluid dynamics, the apex expression of the warm, heavy dark.

They emerge from the soft, oval hollows of the bone-stone ziggurats not by walking, but by *unfurling*. 

They are vast, pelagic gliders, creatures of immense grace and terrifying, alien beauty, perfectly evolved for a world of crushing pressure and blood-warm viscosity. To call them fish or cephalopod would be a failure of imagination. They are hydrostatic entities, their bodies composed of a dense, gelatinous musculature reinforced by a flexible, internal lattice of the same pale chitin that forms the city walls. They have no eyes. In the absolute dark, eyes are a useless vulnerability. Instead, their broad, undulating mantles are draped in a shimmering, trembling array of sensory cilia—fleshy, translucent tendrils that trail behind them like the train of a regal gown. 

Through these tendrils, they "see" the world. They perceive the microscopic chemical gradients of the sulfur vents, the minute temperature shifts of the convection currents, and the faintest displacement of water caused by a drifting copepod a hundred yards away. Their skin is the color of bruised pearls, smooth and entirely devoid of scales or粗糙 textures, secreting a thick, slippery mucus that prevents the city’s encrusting barnacles and sponges from taking root on their flesh. 

They do not swim by thrashing. The water is too thick, the energy cost too high. Instead, they sail. 

This is where their profound symbiosis with the city’s architecture becomes apparent. The inhabitants are masters of rheotaxis—they read the flow. They position their broad, wing-like pectoral fins to catch the rising thermal updrafts in the wide avenues, allowing the warm, buoyant water to lift them effortlessly toward the vaulted colonnades. When they reach the upper tiers, they fold their fins, tucking their bodies into sleek, teardrop shapes to slip into the descending, cooler downdrafts angled by the ziggurats. They move through the metropolis in a continuous, looping, three-dimensional dance, riding the very breathing cycles of the deep. To watch them—if one could see in the dark—is to watch leaves caught in a slow, majestic, underwater autumn wind.

Their society, if it can be called that, is built entirely around acoustic resonance and the maintenance of the flow. The muffled soundscape of the basin is their canvas. Lacking vocal cords, they communicate through a complex system of subsonic vibrations generated by specialized, fluid-filled chambers in their chests. They produce deep, resonant hums, rhythmic clicks, and long, mournful drones that travel for miles through the dense water. 

But they do not just project sound into the water; they use the city itself as an instrument. 

When a gathering occurs in one of the grand, circular plazas, dozens of these gliders will align themselves along the smoothed, curving walls. By emitting specific low-frequency pulses, they cause the ancient, biomineral architecture to vibrate. The rounded arches and vaulted ceilings act as massive acoustic amplifiers, focusing and bouncing the sound waves back into the center of the plaza. The resulting chorus is a physical force, a heavy, rhythmic thrumming that massages the internal organs of every creature present. It is a language of pressure and vibration, conveying complex information about thermal shifts, the locations of nutrient-dense microbial blooms, and the structural integrity of the reef. In these moments, the city literally sings, and the inhabitants are its choir.

They are also the gardeners of the metropolis. While the city was originally secreted by blind, colonial polyps over hundreds of thousands of years, those foundational organisms have long since died out, replaced by this higher order of life. The gliders actively maintain the biological boulevards. When a current shifts or a geothermal vent begins to cool, the flow of the city is disrupted. The gliders will detect this stagnation. Gathering at the site, they will extrude a fast-hardening, calcium-rich paste from glands beneath their jaws, meticulously sculpting new baffles, smoothing rough edges, and guiding the growth of the living patina of sponges to redirect the water. They prune the giant tube-worms and cull the overpopulations of hound-sized isopods, keeping the ecological niches perfectly balanced. They are the immune system and the architects of the reef, ensuring the fluid machine never clogs.

They are not alone in their grand endeavors. A complex web of commensal life clings to them. The albino, serpentine eels mentioned in the shadows of the windows are not just random scavengers; they are the attendants of the gliders. The eels weave through the trailing sensory cilia of the larger creatures, picking off parasites and cleaning the delicate chemoreceptors. In return, the gliders provide the eels with the scraps of their filter-feeding and the protective slipstream of their massive bodies. The giant isopods, too, follow in their wake, acting as the heavy scavengers that clean the plazas after the gliders have fed on the dense clouds of copepods.

But the most awe-inspiring inhabitants are those that remain unseen, the presences felt only as a heavy, displaced *swish* of water and a sudden, chilling drop in pressure. 

These are the Elders. The Leviathans. 

They are the same species as the gliders, but of an age and scale that defies comprehension. In a world without sunlight to dictate seasons, and with a slow, cold-blooded metabolism fueled by the endless geothermal warmth, these creatures do not die of old age; they simply continue to grow. The oldest among them have become so massive, their bodies so heavy and dense, that they can no longer ride the convection currents. They have become semi-sessile, anchoring themselves in the deepest, widest trenches at the very bottom of the basin. 

These leviathans are the size of submarines, their pale, melted-wax bodies draped over the lowest tiered ziggurats like slumbering gods. They are so encrusted with centuries of bioluminescent bacteria and deep-sea anemones that they look like living hills of glowing, ghostly moss. They barely move, conserving their immense energy, but they are the anchors of the ecosystem. Their slow, titanic breaths—drawing in thousands of gallons of thick, warm water and expelling it in massive, nutrient-rich plumes—act as the primary biological pumps for the lowest levels of the city. Their subsonic heartbeats are the deep, foundational thrum that vibrates through the bedrock, setting the baseline rhythm for the entire metropolis. The younger gliders frequently descend to rest against the flanks of these Elders, absorbing the ambient heat and listening to the deep, geological memories encoded in their slow, booming pulses.

To exist as an inhabitant of this place is to experience a life of profound, dark intimacy. There is no horizon, no sky, no concept of "up" or "down" other than the gradient of heat. There is only the heavy, enveloping embrace of the warm water, the smooth, sensual curves of the bone-stone walls sliding against their skin, and the eternal, rhythmic pulse of the current. They are creatures of the deep equilibrium, living in a state of suspended, twilight grace, perfectly tuned to the breathing of a world that has never known the sun, and never will.
