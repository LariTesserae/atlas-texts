To survive at the roof of the world, the people of the spine have become an expression of the mountain itself. They do not merely inhabit the violent geometry of the cliffs; they have been physically and culturally sculpted by it. 

They are a compact, dense people, built for the thin, biting air and the relentless verticality of their home. Height is a fatal liability on a sheer escarpment, so they are notably short, rarely exceeding five feet, but they possess a staggering, wiry density. Their ribcages are profoundly barrel-shaped, flaring wide to house massive, highly efficient lungs capable of extracting oxygen from the rarefied atmosphere. Their blood is thick and rich, giving their skin a deep, ruddy undertone that contrasts with the pale, frost-bitten grey of the granite. They move with a low center of gravity, their bodies acting as perfect counterweights against the dizzying drops.

Their faces are shaped by the harsh, unfiltered light and the biting winds. Heavy, prominent brow ridges and deep-set eyes shield them from the blinding, diamond-like glare of the sun hitting the rime ice. Their irises are pale—striking shades of slate, ice-blue, or pale amber—acting as a natural defense against the snow-blindness that would afflict lesser eyes. Thick epicanthic folds and dense lashes keep the freezing, intermittent fog and blowing rock-flour at bay. 

But it is their hands and feet that truly define their existence. Their fingers are short, thick, and heavily splayed, ending in pads of calloused, leathery skin that have developed a microscopic ridging over generations, allowing them to grip frost-slicked basalt with startling security. Their joints are hyper-flexible, enabling them to wedge their hands and feet into the narrowest, most unforgiving fissures of the living rock. They wear no rigid footwear; instead, their feet are wrapped in soft, supple boots crafted from the cured under-belly hides of the giant high-altitude ungulates. These boots are stuffed with dried, insulating moss and lashed tightly around the calves with sinew. The soft soles allow them to *feel* the rock, reading the micro-textures of the stone through their feet, sensing shifts in temperature, dampness, and stability before their eyes even register them.

Their clothing is a masterclass in thermal engineering, born entirely from the fauna of the crags. Base layers are woven from the incredibly fine, warm under-wool of the giant muskoxen-ibex herds. Over this, they wear quilted mid-layers stuffed with the down and body feathers of the millions of cliff-nesting birds, trapped in small, sewn pockets to prevent the insulation from shifting. Their outer shells are made of heavy, wind-proof hides, heavily oiled with rendered animal fat to shed the persistent, chilling dampness of the weeping rock. There are no buttons or metal fasteners; everything is secured with intricately carved toggles of bone and antler. Hoods are heavily lined with thick ruffs of shaggy ungulate fur, framing their faces and trapping their exhaled warmth. Nothing is loose; every strap is pulled tight, every hem secured, ensuring that the sudden, cyclonic high-altitude winds have nothing to catch.

Because the air is thin and the environment unnervingly quiet, the people have learned to conserve their breath and their noise. They do not shout. To shout is to waste precious oxygen and to risk vibrating the loose, fractured scree above. Instead, they communicate through a highly sophisticated, fluid sign language. Their hands move in tight, close-to-the-body gestures, easily read in the pitch-black shadows of the narrow alleys. When they must vocalize, they use low, resonant hums, soft clicks of the tongue, or breathy, pitched whistles that cut through the rush of the wind without echoing wildly into the vastness. The city whispers because its people whisper.

Their movement through the three-dimensional labyrinth is a mesmerizing display of unconscious proprioception. They do not walk; they flow. Navigating the sheer drops and the terrifying, swaying bridges of braided sinew, they maintain a constant, instinctual three-point contact with the stone. When crossing a chasm, they do not look down into the jagged V-valleys; their eyes track the weave of the hair-ropes, their bodies shifting in perfect, rhythmic synchronization with the sway of the bridge. It is a dance of micro-adjustments, an energy-conserving grace that makes them look as though they are gliding over the abyss rather than fighting it.

Their daily lives are dictated by the slow, patient dynamic of the mountain. A master mason might spend a week simply studying a sheer wall of basalt, looking for the natural fault lines, before applying the fire-setting technique. When the stone shatters, the resulting blocks are not hoisted by complex machinery, but moved by teams of workers using heavy timber levers, wooden wedges, and the sheer, coordinated physical strength of the community. They work in total silence, guided only by the soft *clink* of antler picks and the rhythmic, unified breathing of the crew. 

They are deeply intertwined with the sparse ecology. The moss gardens, fertilized by the carefully harvested guano of the snow-finches and raptors, are tended with reverence. The moss is scraped, washed in iron-cold meltwater, and fermented in deep stone vats to break down its tough cellulose, creating a nutrient-dense, warming paste that supplements the heavy diet of ungulate meat and rich, fatty milk. The herding of the giant, shaggy beasts is done without dogs—which would be too loud and require too much food. Instead, the herders use subtle shifts in body weight, soft whistles, and the gentle pressure of long, bone-tipped crooks to guide the massive animals along the razor-edged slopes and down into the terraced pens.

Culturally, they possess a profound, almost geological perception of time. They do not measure their lives in fleeting days or seasons, but in "thaws" and "rimes," and in the incremental expansion of their city. When a child is born, the parents and the community begin carving a new habitation pod into the cliff face. Using stone mauls and fire, they chip away at the granite, expanding the room by mere inches a month. By the time the child reaches adulthood, the room is finished, smooth, and ready. 

Death, too, is handled with this deep sense of geological patience. When an elder passes, they are not buried in the hard, unyielding rock, nor are they burned, as timber is too precious. Instead, they are carried to the highest, most remote cirques, where the great glaciers grind against the peaks. There, they are laid upon the ice. Over decades, the creeping glaciers slowly envelop them, eventually grinding their bones into the fine, pale rock-flour that washes down into the valleys, becoming the very soil that feeds the sparse wire-grass and black lichens. 

To watch them in their environment is to witness a perfect, quiet equilibrium. If you stand in the shadowed, damp crevice of a carved plaza, you might see an elder and an apprentice working a doorway. The elder pauses, wiping a streak of condensation from the weeping rock with a moss-stuffed glove. They hand a heavy, polished basalt maul to the youth. There are no words spoken. The youth takes the tool, feels its weight, finds the angle of the stone, and strikes. *Clink.* The sound is soft, muffled by the freezing air and the damp moss, instantly swallowed by the vast, silent majesty of the mountain. They breathe in the thin, sharp air, and continue their slow, unyielding work.
