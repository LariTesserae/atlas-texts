To understand what lives in the sunken basin, you must first discard the earthly definition of life. Here, biology and geology are not separate disciplines; they are a single, continuous spectrum of silent, anhydrous chemistry. Life in this caldera does not conquer its environment, nor does it seek warmth, moisture, or companionship. It merely surrenders to the geometry of the void. 

The foundation of this sparse ecosystem is the piezo-synthetic mineral-lichen. Up close, it does not look like a plant, but rather like a sprawling nervous system of fiber-optic threads. It clings to the razor-sharp corners and the sweeping, hollow arches of the resin city. Because there is no sun to speak of—only the bruised, filtered twilight of the heavy gas—the lichen feeds on the kinetic stress of the growing city. As a new balcony extrudes, the physical tension in the silicate-resin generates a micro-electric charge. The lichen absorbs this charge, and in response, it flares with a cold, bioluminescent blue. 

If you stand in a plaza and watch a spire grow, you will see the lichen pulse in a wave of azure light, tracing the exact path of the extrusion. When the spire inevitably snaps under its own weight, the sudden release of tension causes the lichen to flash blindingly bright for a fraction of a second before dimming to a dormant, ghostly gray. It propagates not by seeds, but by shedding microscopic, glass-like spores that are caught in the slow, heavy convection currents of the pooling gas, drifting until they snag on a newly formed, highly stressed edge.

Suspended within these same heavy convection currents is the basin’s micro-fauna: the dry plankton of the caldera. They are impossibly thin, nematode-like threads of pure, flexible crystal. They have no organs, no digestive tracts. They "swim" through the dense halogen vapor by undulating their bodies, riding the thermal updrafts created by the sublimating dust of collapsed towers. They are the scavengers of the microscopic, absorbing the chemical energy of the sublimating gas directly through their crystalline skin. They are entirely invisible to the naked eye, revealing themselves only when a shaft of dim, violet light catches them, turning the air itself into a swirling, silent snowglobe of glittering motes.

It is upon these micro-threads, and the heavier sublimating dust, that the basin’s most majestic inhabitants feed: the silicate mantas. 

To watch one of these creatures drift through the heavy gas is to watch a phantom. They are vast, some spanning ten feet from wingtip to wingtip, yet they weigh almost nothing. Their bodies are composed of the same pale, translucent resin as the city, but grown in impossibly thin, flexible layers that ripple like silk. They have no eyes, for there is nothing to see in the dim twilight that their pressure-senses cannot map. They have no mouths, for they do not eat in any conventional sense. 

As a manta glides, its porous underbelly acts as a chemical sieve. It sweeps through the clouds of sublimating dust left by shattered spires, absorbing the energized silicate particles directly into its shell. The creature is a living filter, purifying the air of the basin’s debris. As it feeds, its shell grows thicker, more intricate, developing the same fractal, lace-like patterns as the city’s colonnades. 

But the manta’s lifecycle is inherently tragic, bound by the brutal rules of the caldera. It cannot grow forever. As it absorbs more dust, it becomes heavier, its movements slowing, its wingtips drooping. Eventually, it becomes too dense to remain buoyant in the heavy gas. In total silence, it begins its final descent. It does not die, not in the way a biological organism dies. Instead, it calcifies. As it settles onto the floor of a hollow plaza, its flexible wings stiffen, locking into rigid, geometric angles. Its internal chemistry shifts, merging with the bedrock. Over the next few hours, the extrusion imperative of the earth takes over, and the calcified manta becomes the seed for a new structure. Its wings become the sweeping, cantilevered roof of a pavilion; its body becomes the central pillar. The creature and the architecture become one. 

While the mantas patrol the open spaces between the buildings, a far stranger, more reclusive organism inhabits the hollow interiors of the towering spires. 

If you were to peer through the translucent, needle-thin walls of a twisting skyscraper, you might spot the Tension-polyps. They look like clusters of dry, translucent grapes, or perhaps deep-sea siphonophores, tethered to the interior walls by gossamer threads of spun glass. They are soft-bodied anomalies in a world of hard edges, filled with the same heavy, anhydrous gas that pools in the basin. 

The polyps live in the dark, hollow bellies of the spires, completely isolated from the shifting streets outside. They feed on the acoustic dampening of the environment itself. Because the heavy gas swallows all sound, the kinetic energy of the city’s growth and collapse is trapped as low-frequency, sub-audible vibrations within the hollow tubes. The polyps absorb these vibrations through their gas-filled bladders, swelling and contracting in a slow, silent rhythm. 

They are the hermits of the glass city, living in total isolation. But they are intimately tied to the violent cycle of the caldera. When the spire they inhabit reaches its critical mass and begins to fracture, the polyps do not flee. As the glass walls shatter, the sudden, violent depressurization causes the polyps to explosively rupture. In that silent, microscopic fraction of a second, they release their payload: thousands of tiny, hardened resin seeds, shot outward into the heavy gas. The parent polyp is crushed into glittering dust along with its spire, but its seeds drift downward, settling into the newly opened alleyways, waiting for the next hollow tube to extrude around them.

There are no predators here. There is no competition, no territoriality, no mating dances, and no aggression. The sheer hostility of the anhydrous, razor-sharp environment has stripped away the evolutionary need for conflict. Energy is too precious, and the chemistry too rigid, to support the messy, heat-generating mechanics of a food chain. Everything here feeds on the environment, and everything here eventually becomes the environment. 

To walk among them is to realize your own profound irrelevance. If you reach out to touch a drifting manta, your warm, moist hand would likely cause the cold, dry resin of its wing to instantly fracture, shattering the creature into a cloud of silent dust. If you step on the piezo-lichen, your soft boots would fail to generate the sharp, kinetic stress it requires to feed, and it would simply wither beneath you. 

You are a ghost haunting a machine of glass. The life here does not fear you, nor does it acknowledge you. You do not displace the heavy gas correctly; you do not vibrate at the frequency of the shifting stone; you carry the alien taint of moisture and heat. To the blind, unthinking chemistry of the caldera, and to the silent, drifting creatures that call it home, you are nothing more than a brief, warm illusion, passing through their cool, eternal twilight before fading away.
