To understand what lives in this bruised, weeping expanse, you must first understand that the silence here is not an absence. It is a medium. It is a heavy, saturated fluid in which every organism is submerged, and to which every organism has perfectly, flawlessly adapted. 

The *Litho-leviathans* are the anchors of this world, but they are not its sole inhabitants. They are, in truth, mobile continents, and like any landmass, they carry an entire biosphere upon their backs, within their bellies, and in their slow, sweeping wake.

If you were to stand on the polished, aerodynamic curve of a leviathan’s carapace—a treacherous, sloping landscape of bone-smooth chitin and thick, insulating blubber—you would find that the armor is deeply grooved. These tectonic fissures in the beast’s shell are micro-climates. Because the leviathans vent warm, damp exhaust from their thermal pores, the deep canyons of their carapaces are significantly warmer than the freezing ambient air. Here, sheltered from the biting wind, thrives a lush, pale jungle of specialized flora. *Thermal-ferns* and *steam-mosses* cling to the chitin, their translucent fronds unfurling to drink the constant, humid exhalations of the beast. 

Drifting through this canopy of pale fronds are the *Vapor-jellies*. They are the aerial fauna of the carapace, buoyant, silent creatures that resemble deep-sea medusae adapted for the thin air. They possess gas-bladders filled with hydrogen, a byproduct of the leviathan’s own digestion, which they siphon from the exhaust vents. They do not fly; they drift, their long, velvet-soft tentacles trailing through the steam, snatching up microscopic spores and the tiny, blind mites that crawl upon the moss. When a leviathan vents a massive plume of steam, the jellies are carried upward in the thermal updraft, rising like silent, ghostly lanterns against the slate-grey sky before slowly, lazily sinking back down to the warm ridges.

But the true marvel of the leviathan’s biology lies beneath the armor, within the cavernous, glowing belly. The prompt mentions the dull, orange glow of their internal combustion, but this fire is not merely chemical; it is biological, mediated by a vast, symbiotic organ-forest. Lining the walls of the primary digestion chambers are the *Crucible-polyps*. These are massive, heat-resistant organisms, clustered like anemones in a boiling tide pool. They secrete the hyper-corrosive, exothermic acids required to break down the silicate-heavy topsoil and raw stone the leviathan consumes. The polyps pulse with bioluminescence, a deep, rhythmic orange that provides the ambient light of the gut. In return for their digestive labor, the polyps feed on the refined, nutrient-rich slurry, their glowing tissues acting as a secondary nervous system that regulates the beast’s core temperature. 

Down on the ground, in the spaces between the gliding giants, the ecology is defined by the leviathans’ passage. The shallow, glass-still pools of condensation are not entirely natural; many are the fossilized footprints of the leviathans, pressed into the stone millennia ago and polished to a mirror shine by their spongy, silent footpads. 

In these freezing, shallow bowls live the *Pallid skaters*. They are arachnids, but they move with the fluid grace of water-striders. Their legs are coated in a hyper-hydrophobic oil, allowing them to glide across the surface tension of the near-freezing water without breaking it. They are entirely eyeless, navigating instead by feeling the subsonic vibrations of the leviathans through the water. When the deep, rhythmic thrum of an approaching giant vibrates through the pool, the skaters instinctively freeze, their bodies flattening against the water's surface to avoid being swallowed by the displacement of the beast’s passing. They feed on the drowned spores of the frost-grass and the microscopic, silica-shelled diatoms that bloom in the cold water.

When the leviathans excrete their waste—the refined, glassy slag—it falls to the polished stone with a muffled, heavy thud. This slag is not merely waste; it is a foundational resource for the benthic creatures of the plains. The slag is excreted at a high temperature, and as it cools in the freezing mist, it fractures into intricate, geometric shards. 

Here, the *Vitreous borers* make their homes. They are beetle-like organisms, low to the ground, with carapaces that mimic the jagged, glassy texture of the slag. Their mandibles are reinforced with biologically deposited carborundum, allowing them to chew through the hardened silicate. They hollow out the cooling shards of slag, creating labyrinthine, translucent hives. When the pale, diffuse sun catches these hives, they refract the dim light, casting faint, prismatic rainbows across the weeping grey stone—the only bright colors in a world of charcoal and indigo. The borers farm the pale, wire-thin lichens that grow on the shaded sides of the slag, living out their quiet, slow lives inside their glass fortresses.

And what of the predators? In an ecosystem of such colossal, heavily armored giants and tiny, hidden scavengers, where is the violence? 

There are no apex predators in the traditional sense. The energy required to hunt a *Litho-leviathan* is biologically impossible, and the smaller creatures are too well-hidden, too perfectly adapted to the silent damp. But there is the ultimate consumer: the *Cenotaph-worm*. 

A leviathan lives for tens of thousands of years, but eventually, its metabolic furnaces cool. Its vast, spongy footpads wear thin. When a leviathan finally dies, it does not collapse in a dramatic, earth-shattering fall. It simply slows, its steps taking hours, then days, until it gently lowers its immense bulk onto the polished stone and exhales its final, silent bloom of steam.

The Cenotaph-worms, which spend their centuries dormant deep within the porous bedrock, sense the drop in the subsonic hum. They emerge from the stone—massive, pale, segmented annelids as thick as ancient tree trunks. They do not tear or rip; such actions would disrupt the sacred quiet of the plains. Instead, they secrete a slow-acting, calcifying enzyme. They glide over the cooling carapace of the fallen giant, dissolving the thick blubber and the heavy chitin, converting the biological dreadnought back into the mineral landscape. 

The process takes centuries. As the worms consume the leviathan, they excrete a fine, bone-smooth paste that hardens into the same polished, aerodynamic rock that forms the plateau. The fallen leviathan is not scavenged; it is geologically integrated. Its domed carapace becomes a new, sweeping hill of polished stone. Its deep armor grooves become new channels for the weeping moisture. Its immense, hollowed-out ribcage becomes a cavernous sanctuary for the frost-grass and the rime ice.

Nothing is wasted. Nothing is loud. 

To stand on this high, bruised plateau is to stand inside the belly of a single, continent-sized organism. The *Litho-leviathans* are its grinding teeth and its beating heart; the *Vapor-jellies* and *Crucible-polyps* are its flora and its breath; the *Pallid skaters* and *Vitreous borers* are its fleeting, darting thoughts; and the *Cenotaph-worms* are its memory, turning the dead into the very ground upon which the living glide. 

It is a world where evolution has rejected the frantic, noisy struggle for survival in favor of a deep, resonant harmony. Life here does not fight the cold, the damp, or the crushing weight of time. It absorbs them. It processes them in the glowing, silent dark, moving with the slow, terrifying grace of a glacier, leaving nothing behind but polished stone and the faint, fading warmth of a breath in the mist.
