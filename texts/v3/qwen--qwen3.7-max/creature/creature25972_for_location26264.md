To live in a world that has not yet learned how to age is to exist in a state of perpetual, hyper-vigilant tension. The people of this sunken caldera are as raw, unblunted, and sharply defined as the pale-grey limestone they carve. They are the First Dwellers, a society born entirely within the embryonic geometry of their own making, and their bodies, minds, and culture have warped to fit the extreme parameters of this pristine, unforgiving environment.

Physically, they are a people shaped by the razor-edges of their city. Because every parapet, lintel, and staircase is sharp enough to cut skin, their clothing is highly specialized. They wear thick, layered garments of tightly woven hemp and stiff, untreated leather, padded at the shoulders, hips, and shins to protect against the un-eroded corners. Yet, no amount of padding can protect them entirely. Their skin is a map of the city’s sharpness. Their hands, forearms, and faces bear thousands of fine, pale, hairline scars—the "kiss of the new stone." They do not bleed easily anymore; their epidermis has thickened, calcifying into a tough, pale hide that mirrors the very rock they excavate. Their hands are heavily calloused, the fingers wrapped in resin-stiffened cloth to grip the bronze chisels and wooden mallets without slipping on the ever-present, fine stone dust.

Their faces are striking, defined by the violent albedo of the unweathered rock. The brilliant, bouncing sunlight of the open caldera would blind a surface-dweller. To survive the glare, the inhabitants wear wide-brimmed, stiff reed hats that cast deep shadows over their faces. Their eyes are heavily lined with thick, dark paste made from crushed charcoal and damp soot, acting as a physical glare-shield. Beneath this dark paint, their irises are pale—washed out to a milky amber or soft grey by the relentless, high-contrast reflection of the white stone. They do not look directly at the sunlit upper terraces; their gaze is naturally drawn downward, tracking the shadows, the damp crevices, and the subtle shifts in the pale rock.

In a place where acoustics swallow echoes and the air is heavy with clinging humidity, their voices have adapted. They do not shout. To raise one's voice in the terraced basin is to disrupt the heavy, sacred quiet, and the damp air simply absorbs the clamor anyway. Instead, they speak in low, resonant baritones and altos, their words pushed from the chest rather than the throat, allowing the sound to carry through the mist without breaking the ambient hush. 

But more often than not, they do not speak at all. The dense, organized chaos of the packed urban fabric requires a fluid, choreographed movement that leaves no room for verbal negotiation. In the narrow, shoulder-to-shoulder alleys, they communicate through a highly developed, silent lexicon of gestures, shifts in posture, and the rhythmic clinking of their tools. A tap of a bronze chisel against a thigh means *yield*; a sweep of a dusty hand means *clear the runnel*; a slight tilt of the head indicates the structural integrity of a newly cut load-bearing wall. When thousands of them move through the tight warrens, it looks like a school of pale fish navigating a coral reef—seamless, instinctual, and entirely silent.

Their diet and biology reflect the microbial, subterranean pace of the lower terraces. Lacking established topsoil or grazing land, they subsist on what the damp, sunken environment provides. They cultivate pale, fleshy fungi and moisture-loving lichens in the shaded catchment pools. They fish for the blind, translucent cave-fish that dart through the deepest, coldest cisterns, and they roast the starchy, pale tubers that manage to push their roots through the fractured bedrock. As a result, they are lean, sinewy, and light-boned. They lack the heavy, broad-shouldered bulk of agricultural surface-dwellers; they are built to slide through low doorways, crouch in narrow runnels, and balance on steep, unpolished ramps. Their skin carries a faint, perpetual chill, acclimated to the crisp coolness pooling at the bottom of the excavation.

Psychologically, they are bound by a profound, almost religious obsession with the "newness" of their world. They are acutely aware that they are living in a frozen moment of genesis, and they view the inevitable arrival of time—weathering, erosion, softening—with a mixture of awe and deep melancholy. 

This is why they sweep. The soft *shhh-shhh* of the brooms is not merely a chore; it is a daily liturgy. They sweep the fresh stone dust from the floors and stairs to reveal the pure, unblemished, high-albedo rock beneath, trying to preserve the exact moment of its completion. They carefully channel the weeping water through the carved runnels, terrified of letting it pool against a wall and soften the sharp, scalloped chisel marks. They are the caretakers of the embryonic city, desperately trying to hold back the slow, inevitable work of aging. 

Yet, they are also the agents of its expansion. The youngest and most vigorous among them are the Cutters, working the upper, sun-baked terraces, driving the bronze mauls to push the city’s boundaries further into the plains. They are covered in a perpetual layer of white dust, looking like ghosts against the bright sky. The elders, however, retreat to the lowest, dampest terraces. They sit by the catchment pools, watching the hesitant, pale green mosses take root in the crevices. They run their thick, scarred fingers over the very first spots where the stone has begun to smooth, where the water has slightly rounded a sharp edge. 

They are a people caught in a beautiful, tragic paradox. They are building a monument to permanence using the primitive tools of the earth, yet they know that the very act of living in it—the shuffle of their leather sandals, the weeping of the water table, the breath from their lungs—will slowly wear it away. They live in the pristine, sharp-edged now, moving in their heavy, disciplined silence, serving as the fleeting, conscious heartbeat of a landscape holding its breath, waiting for time to finally arrive.
