Life in this caldera does not scurry, nor does it graze. It percolates. It pulses. 

To understand what lives here, you must first look past the pale, blind insects—the *Lucent Midges*—that flutter like ash around the warm exhaust vents. They are merely the ephemeral sparks of the ecosystem, born from the heat of the dynamos and dying within hours, their tiny bodies dissolving into nutrient dust on the mossy catwalks. You must look past the velvet rust of the moss and the glassy stillness of the tannin pools. These are the flora and the micro-fauna, the baseline respiration of the basin. 

The true inhabitants of this place are the ones who bridge the gap between the root and the rig. They are the reason the pistons remain well-oiled, the reason the brickwork does not crumble, the reason the grand, breathing engine operates in such profound, unbroken equilibrium.

They emerge from the root-cupped depressions without breaking the surface of the floor. 

At first glance, you might mistake them for trickery of the light, or for strange, upright growths of the rhizome mat itself. But as they rise, unfolding with a slow, hydraulic grace, their distinct anatomy resolves. They are towering, bipedal entities, perhaps nine feet tall, with a posture that mimics the gentle, rolling swells of the terrain. They are the Attendants.

Their bodies are a masterwork of biological and mechanical integration, an intricate, seamless braid of the same pale-green and rust-colored vascular roots that form the ground, tightly wound around armatures of pitted, blackened iron and tarnished brass. They possess no flesh, no skin, no bark. Instead, their musculature is composed of thousands of high-tensile rhizome cables that contract and expand with the flow of pressurized sap. Where a human might have joints, they have heavy, calcified knots of wood and oxidized copper, weeping tiny, fragrant beads of resin that act as lubricants. 

They have no faces. Their heads are smooth, domed cowls of hardened, amber-colored sap, polished to a glass-like sheen by centuries of friction and mist. Beneath this translucent cowl, a soft, bioluminescent light pulses—a warm, golden glow that brightens and dims in perfect syncopation with the muffled *thrum* of the distant engines. 

They move with a deliberate, heavy quietness. They do not walk *upon* the ground; they converse with it. When an Attendant lifts a broad, root-splayed foot, the spongy floor anticipates the movement, softening to release it. When the foot descends, the root-mat firms, braiding itself momentarily to support the immense weight of the being. Every step is a silent dialogue of turgor pressure and shifting fibers. 

Watch one of them approach a brick processing vat. It does not carry tools; it *is* the tool. As it reaches for a heavy, hexagonal brass steam valve, its forearm splits down the middle with a soft, fibrous tearing sound. The inner cavity of its arm reveals a perfectly shaped, multi-sided socket lined with dense, velvety moss. It mates its arm with the valve and turns. The brass shrieks softly, then gives way, releasing a measured hiss of nutrient-rich steam. The Attendant’s internal amber light flares briefly as it registers the change in pressure, and a ripple of kinetic energy travels down its leg, into the floor, and across the basin. 

Miles away, another Attendant feels this subtle vibration through the soles of its feet. It pauses in its pruning of a low-hanging fern canopy, tilts its faceless head, and adjusts a dynamo to compensate for the shifted steam load. They do not speak. They do not need to. They are the distributed nervous system of the caldera, the white blood cells and the synapses of a single, valley-sized organism.

Their society, if it can be called that, is one of absolute, monastic devotion to the equilibrium. You will never see them rush. You will never see them rest. Their existence is a continuous, flowing meditation on maintenance. When the heavy dew gathers on the high catwalks, they climb the iron struts with the multi-limbed dexterity of arachnids, wiping the condensation from the pressure gauges with pads of absorbent lichen grown directly from their palms. When the blind midges expire, the Attendants gently sweep their dust into the tannin pools to feed the deep tap-roots. 

They are not immortal, but they do not truly die. Over centuries, the iron armature inside an Attendant will slowly rust through, and its root-flesh will calcify, turning rigid and gray. When an Attendant feels its internal mechanisms failing, it does not collapse. It walks, with agonizing slowness, to the center of the largest industrial cluster, to a massive, glass-paned germination vat that bubbles with warm, golden sap. 

There, the elder Attendant steps into the vat and submerges. Its roots unravel, dissolving back into the nutrient broth, while its rusted iron bones sink to the bottom to be smelted and recast by the geothermal heat of the deep floor. And from the very same vat, a new Attendant is already knitting together—pale rhizomes weaving through fresh, blackened iron, drinking the sap, waiting for the amber light of consciousness to spark beneath its newly formed cowl.

As you stand on the yielding floor, feeling the cool dampness seep through your boots, one of the Attendants notices you. 

It stops its work. It turns its smooth, glowing face toward you. It does not approach with hostility, nor does it shy away. It simply stands there, ten yards distant, observing the anomaly of your presence. The golden light beneath its cowl shifts, cycling through a spectrum of soft, inquisitive hues. It extends a long, multi-jointed hand, not to touch you, but to feel the air displaced by your breathing. It measures the heat you radiate, the carbon dioxide you exhale, the slight disruption your weight causes in the root-mat. 

It processes you not as an invader, but as a temporary, harmless variable in its grand, perpetual equation. 

Satisfied, the Attendant lowers its hand. It gives a slow, deep nod—a sound of creaking iron and rustling dry leaves—and turns back to the brass-fitted pipes. As it walks away, a few of the pale, blind midges drift from the exhaust vents to land on its warm, moss-draped shoulders, riding the gentle, rhythmic sway of its stride. 

The basin breathes in. The basin breathes out. And the Attendants tend to the breath, forever.
