There is no green here. No grass, no shrubs, no trees, not even the simplest moss. The ground is an endless expanse of bare, wet mineral. It is a world of oxidized rust, pale chalk, slick black basalt, and veins of milky quartz. What little biological film clings to the stone is purely fungal—a pale, rubbery lichen that feeds on the damp rock and the airborne mineral dust, forming a slick, non-vegetative crust over the high plains. 

Yet, despite the barren flora, the fauna is overwhelming. It is a tide of flesh and chitin that defies the sterility of the ground. Because there are no plants to anchor the food web, life here has adapted to graze directly on the mineral-fungal crust and on each other. Rivers of pale, multi-legged crawlers, each the size of a human hand, flow continuously over the weeping rocks, scraping up the lichen with rasping mandibles. Above them, the warm sky is thick with slow-beating, leathery-winged filter-feeders—massive, manta-like creatures that swim through the humid air, straining airborne spores and moisture from the thermals. And walking among it all are the highland titans: beasts of burden and grazing giants with shells of porous, calcified stone, moving with a glacial, deliberate slowness. They lower their massive, blunt heads to the wet rock, scraping up tons of fungal crust and stone in a single, grinding bite. The sheer volume of life is deafening, a swarming, shifting carpet of biology that coats the barren geology.

Carved directly into the deeply eroded flutes and canyons of this vast plateau is the city. It is an urban sprawl of staggering density, built not *on* the landscape but *into* its ancient, weathered bones. The rock here has been shaped by millions of years of warm, wet wind and weeping water; it is smooth, rounded, and deeply fluted, lacking any sharp edges. The city mimics this established geology. Its architecture is layered and terraced, clinging to the sheer, sweeping walls of the highland gorges in dense, near-touching clusters. 

This is an industrial metropolis, humming with mechanized life. Factories and foundries are wedged into the natural amphitheaters of the stone, their smokestacks rising like artificial stalagmites. Because the environment is so wet, the technology has adapted. Massive condensation-catchers, resembling the ribbed shells of the local fauna, funnel the weeping moisture into roaring, electrified turbines. Funiculars and heavy-gauge rail lines zigzag down the smooth cliffs, their iron tracks gleaming in the normal, bright daylight. The air smells of ozone, hot brass, wet stone, and the deep, musky ammonia of the swarming beasts. Cables thick as tree trunks stretch across the ravines, vibrating with the high-voltage current generated by the plateau’s endless, slow-flowing water.

The soundscape is loud, a constant, overwhelming wall of noise, but it lacks the frantic, chaotic pitch of a lesser place. It is a deep, resonant loudness. The swarming crawlers produce a ceaseless, dry rasping, like sand pouring over glass. The airborne filter-feeders emit low, thrumming booms as they displace the heavy air. The industrial sector adds its own voice: the rhythmic, earth-shaking *thud-thud-thud* of massive drop-forges, the high-pitched shriek of electrified transit cars, and the deep, rushing roar of water churning through the turbine sluices. It is a loud world, but one that vibrates in the chest rather than piercing the ears.

Despite the noise, the density, and the sheer volume of moving life, the underlying dynamic of the landscape is profoundly slow. The highland titans take a full minute to complete a single step, their massive joints popping like distant gunfire. The industrial processes are built for endurance, not speed; the great smelters burn with a steady, unblinking heat, processing the mineral-rich shells shed by the swarms in week-long cycles. The weather itself moves with a languid, continental grace, the warm mist rolling in and out of the vast canyons over the course of days. 

Even the city feels patient. It is an established, settled thing. The weeping water has already stained the lower tiers of the urban terraces with dark, mineral deposits, blending the iron and concrete into the ancient stone. The boundary between the natural and the constructed has eroded away. The city is just another layer of the highland, a dense, loud, mechanized reef clinging to the weeping rocks, sustained by the slow, overwhelming tide of life that grazes upon the barren, beautiful stone.
