To be submerged here is to be held in the heavy, warm palm of the sky. 

This is the Zenith Caldera, an ocean sitting at the roof of the world. You are floating thousands of feet above the true crust of the earth, suspended in a vast, high-altitude basin where the air is thin and freezing, but the water is a balmy, enveloping warmth. The liquid that fills this elevated trench is not quite ordinary water; it is a hyper-dense, mineral-rich brine, heavy as warm syrup and saturated with dissolved geothermal heat. It clings to the high basin, held in place by its own immense density and a strange, convex surface tension that domes slightly above the jagged rim of the rocks.

Because the geology is violently young, the terrain has not yet learned to be smooth. There are no rolling hills or softened, sandy bottoms. The rock is fresh from the mantle, thrust upward in a recent, cataclysmic tectonic birth. It is a landscape of fractal cliffs, obsidian-black spires, and razor-sharp basalt blades that cut through the warm fluid like broken glass. Every edge is unweathered, every crevice a sharp, geometric shadow. To brush against the stone is to risk a cut; the mountains here have not yet been humbled by time.

Yet, life has already claimed the sharp edges. The vegetation is incredibly lush, thriving in the warm, heavy liquid and the bright, unfiltered alpine sun. Because there is almost no current—the dynamic of this place is profoundly slow, shifting only over seasons and centuries—the plants do not sway. They stand in rigid, majestic stillness. They are massive, fleshy things, adapted to the heavy fluid: broad, paddle-like leaves in deep magentas and saturated emeralds, thick rubbery stalks, and sprawling canopies of bulbous, gas-filled bladders that anchor them to the sharp rocks. The flora is intensely dense, layered upon itself in a high-saturation jungle. Vines thick as tree trunks weave through the sharp spires, and carpets of velvet-textured mosses smother the lower shelves, creating a multi-tiered, near-touching canopy that fills the volume of the water with vibrant, breathing color.

The light is normal, bright, and piercing. High above the surface, the sky is a deep, stratospheric violet, but down here in the fluid, the sunlight fractures into sharp, distinct shafts. Because the water is thick but remarkably clear, the light does not scatter into a hazy blue gloom. Instead, it strikes the broad leaves and black rocks in high-contrast beams, illuminating the rich pigments of the jungle with blinding clarity. Dust motes and microscopic, slow-drifting spores hang suspended in the god-rays, completely motionless.

It is a profoundly quiet world. The density of the heavy fluid swallows high-frequency sounds, leaving only a deep, muffled silence. There are no crashing waves, no rushing rivers, no chatter of wildlife. Fauna is exceedingly rare; the heavy, warm water and the sharp, young rocks have not yet invited the chaotic rush of animal life. Occasionally, you might see a single, massive, translucent bell-drifter—a slow, solitary jelly-thing the size of a house—pulsing with glacial patience through the upper canopy, its movements so slow they are imperceptible to the naked eye. Otherwise, the only sounds are the subsonic, resonant hums of the young earth settling beneath the rock, and the rare, deep *creak* of a massive plant stalk adjusting its weight over the course of a week.

The scale of the caldera is vast and open. Despite the density of the vegetation, the trenches and valleys are wide, extending for miles in every direction. You can look out across great, sweeping plains of jagged black stone and dense, colorful forests, the sightlines stretching until the heavy fluid finally turns the distant spires into sharp, dark silhouettes. 

If you were to swim upward, breaking the surface, the transition would be jarring. There is no splashing, no chaotic frothing of waves. The surface tension of the heavy water is so strong that your head would push through it like pushing through a membrane of liquid glass. You would emerge into the freezing, whisper-thin air of the high altitude, gasping in the cold, looking down at a perfectly still, mirror-like meniscus reflecting the violet sky, while beneath you, the warm, dense, and silent jungle waits in slow, eternal patience.
