To find it, you must follow the fresh scar in the bedrock. The erosion here is young, the stone splintered and sharp, smelling of crushed quartz and raw, bleeding sap. You slip sideways through the narrow, jagged fissure, descending deep below the surface of the world, and then the rock simply gives way. 

You step out onto a membrane of woven, living fiber and look into a vast, hollow void. 

This is the pocket. The chamber is immense, yet entirely subterranean, a hidden geode buried in the deep earth. But there is no stone here, no soil, no dead matter. The walls, the vaulted ceiling, the curved floor beneath your feet—all of it is pure, total vegetation. It is an ecosystem collapsed into a single, colossal organism. Thick, muscular roots braid into load-bearing pillars; vast, translucent leaves form the vaulted canopy; spongy, breathing parenchyma tissue pads the curves. The plant is the architecture, the substrate, and the atmosphere.

Yet, for all the overwhelming, suffocating mass of the walls, the center of the chamber is profoundly, startlingly empty. The density of the air in the vast middle is near-void. It is a hollow lung. The space between the walls is a sheer, unobstructed drop, an intimate room scaled up to a cathedral's volume, containing absolutely nothing but empty air. 

Directly above, the young fissure you entered through widens into a jagged, irregular skylight. Through it pours a shaft of perfectly normal, unfiltered surface daylight. It is a stark, brilliant beam that cuts through the subterranean gloom, illuminating drifting spores. The air in this beam is heavy and warm, a greenhouse breath that presses against your skin, humid and close, like the inside of a composting heart.

It is deeply damp. Condensation weeps continuously from the high, vaulted ceiling of leaves, gathering into heavy, intermittent drops. When a drop falls, it plummets through the empty void and strikes the taut, drum-like floor of woven roots. 

The sound is a deafening, booming *CRACK*. 

The acoustics of the hollow chamber amplify everything to a roar. The space is incredibly, oppressively loud. You hear the wet, tearing sound of cellular mitosis, the groaning of cellulose under tension, the sharp *snap* of fibers elongating. The plant is growing in real-time, actively expanding into the fresh fissures of the earth, and it sounds like a wooden galleon breaking apart in a hurricane. 

Amidst the deafening creak of the living walls, you spot the mechanics of the place. There are no built structures, no carved artifacts, yet the chamber operates on primitive technology. The plant itself has evolved simple machines. Thick vines loop around smooth, calcified nodules to form biological pulleys, hoisting massive stone fragments pulled from the earth to clear space for new growth. Wedge-shaped taproots act as levers, prying apart the bedrock. A rare piece of fauna—a solitary, pale, six-legged creature the size of a hound—watches you from a high ridge of bark. It holds a sharpened, discarded thorn in its mandibles, using it as a primitive pry-bar to pop open a hardened seed pod. It is a scene of raw, uncrafted ingenuity, life using the simplest tools to manipulate its world.

But as you stand in the warm, roaring hollow, the reality of the space begins to fray. 

The sheer, empty volume in the center of the room is not just air; it is a glitch in the geometry of the world. You look across the hollow to the far wall, but the distance is wrong. It stretches into a mile, then compresses into an inch, the space folding like wet paper. The shaft of normal daylight pouring from above does not travel in a straight line; it bends and spirals around unseen, localized gravity wells, casting shadows that point *toward* the light.

The ecosystem is in violent, active motion, but the growth is slipping out of time. You watch a massive root burst from the wall, but you see the visual echo of the root three seconds *before* it actually breaks the surface. The loud, booming cracks of the water drops hit the floor, but the sound arrives before the drop falls. The "empty" space in the center of the room begins to feel viscous, heavy with overlapping probabilities and fractured physics. 

The plant walls pulse, breathing in and out, and with every exhalation, the physical laws of the chamber degrade a little more. The sharp, young edges of the entrance fissure behind you begin to blur, the concept of "inside" and "outside" losing its meaning. You are standing in a hollow, subterranean seed of a broken universe, deafened by the roar of its own impossible growth, bathed in a daylight that has forgotten how to shine straight.
