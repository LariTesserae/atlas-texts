You are submerged in the roof of the world. 

This is a high-altitude ocean, a stratospheric sea pooled in the upper atmosphere of a massive planet, held aloft by gravity and thermal inversion. The medium that fills your lungs and suspends your body is a cryogenic fluid—a liquid so cold and of such low density that it feels like swimming through a heavy, freezing vapor. It parts around your limbs with ghostly resistance, yet it wets, it chills, and it entirely submerges you. 

Despite the depth, the light is perfectly, brightly normal. Because the fluid is so remarkably thin, it scatters almost no light. There is no murky blue fade, no dark abyss below. It is high noon in a boundless, luminous expanse. The light is crisp, sharp, and shadowless, illuminating the environment with the blinding clarity of a sunlit snowfield. 

And yet, for all its vast, continental scale, the world is profoundly empty. The density of solid matter approaches zero. You could drift on the slow, cold currents for weeks and encounter nothing but the bright, freezing fluid. The space between presences is a near-void, an overwhelming expanse of pale, empty luminescence. 

When the terrain does finally emerge from the bright void, it is shocking in its violence. The rock is incredibly young, freshly thrust upward by some recent, unseen tectonic heave. There are no soft curves, no eroded slopes, no gentle seabeds. The spires and ridges are composed of black volcanic glass and raw, unoxidized metal, featuring edges so sharp they could cut flesh. They jut into the fluid like shattered teeth, unweathered and absolute, their jagged geometry preserved perfectly in the freezing cold. 

Clinging to these sharp, sparse spires are the scattered remnants of heavy industry. They are small against the overwhelming scale of the ocean—blocky, brutalist outposts of iron and concrete, partial remains of factories, and lone, towering turbines. They are spaced vast distances apart, isolated needles in a bright, liquid haystack. 

These scattered engines are the source of the sound. 

The noise is absolute. It is a deafening, physical weight, a continuous, roaring shriek of heavy machinery that vibrates in your teeth and rattles your bones. It is the sound of scaled production, of massive turbines churning the low-density fluid, of pistons firing and gears grinding. It is loud enough to rupture an unprotected eardrum, a solid wall of acoustic pressure. 

Yet, impossibly, the sound never changes. 

Not a single pitch wavers. Not a single decibel drops or rises. There is no rhythm, no crescendo, no mechanical stutter. It is a perfectly flat, unvarying, eternal roar. This is a world locked in a frozen equilibrium. The engines do not wear down; the fluid does not warm; the currents do not shift. The deafening noise is not a sign of chaotic industry, but of a terrifying, time-invariant stability. It is the sound of a perfect, unchanging steady state. The dynamic forces of the world have long since cancelled each other out, leaving behind this static, roaring noon.

There is no plant life here. No kelp forests, no algae, no microscopic phytoplankton. The mineral world is entirely sterile of flora. 

But the fauna is abundant. 

Drifting through the vast, empty spaces between the sharp spires are leviathans of the cold. They are huge, translucent creatures, resembling deep-sea siphonophores crossed with rigid, geometric zeppelins. Their bodies are filled with the same low-density cryo-fluid that surrounds them, rendering them nearly invisible against the bright light, save for the dark, crystalline organs pulsing slowly within their mantles. They have no mouths, for there is nothing to eat. Instead, they graze on the kinetic energy of the deafening roar. Their internal structures are tuned to the exact, unvarying frequency of the scattered turbines, absorbing the acoustic vibrations to fuel their slow, eternal drift.

Swarming around the exhaust vents of the isolated factories are smaller, angular creatures. They look like darting shards of obsidian, their carapaces hard and sharp, mimicking the young, unweathered terrain. They feed on the mechanical turbulence, riding the permanent, unchanging slipstreams of the turbines, locked in a perpetual, swirling dance that never alters its pattern. 

To exist here is to be suspended in a paradox. You are submerged in an ocean, yet you are high in the sky. You are surrounded by a deafening, industrial roar, yet the world is perfectly, eternally still. You are bathed in bright, normal light, yet the cold bites down to the marrow. It is a landscape of extreme extremes, held in a delicate, unchanging balance—a bright, empty, roaring void at the top of the world, where time has stopped, but the machines never cease.
