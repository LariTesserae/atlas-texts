The air here is thin, crisp, and tastes faintly of ozone and wet stone. You are standing on the Zenith Peneplain, a landscape of extreme, lung-straining elevation that has been subjected to the slow, crushing grind of deep time. There are no jagged peaks or sharp alpine ridges here. The mountains were worn down to their roots eons ago, and then tectonic forces pushed those roots back into the sky. What remains is a sprawling, open expanse of massive, bulbous stone domes and sweeping, undulating valleys. The rock—pale granite and ancient gneiss—is entirely devoid of sharp edges. It has been polished by a billion years of wind and ice into smooth, sweeping curves, like the backs of sleeping leviathans resting above the clouds.

Despite the extreme altitude, the world is not barren. A perpetual, cool overcast traps moisture against the high stone, creating a lush, high-altitude cloud forest. The light is dim, a perpetual silver-gray twilight that softens all shadows and flattens perspective. The sun is rarely more than a pale, diffuse coin behind the thick mist. In this cool, damp gloom, vegetation thrives in a dense, saturated emerald palette. Thick, spongy mosses carpet the ancient stone in layers inches deep. Giant, fleshy rosette plants with pale, frosted leaves cling to the steeper curves, their broad petals designed to catch the endless condensation. Ferns uncurl from the crevices, their fronds heavy with water. 

Because the air is cool and the light is dim, evaporation is slow. Water does not flow in roaring rivers here; it accumulates, weeps, and drips. The landscape is defined by this intermittent, pervasive dampness. Condensation beads on every surface, gathering until it runs in tiny, silvery rivulets down the smooth, ancient rock, pooling in the shallow, bowl-like depressions between the domes. 

Clinging to the sides of these massive, eroded stone domes is the city of Aethelgard. It is an urban environment of striking density, built in tight, overlapping layers that follow the gentle, primordial curves of the rock. There is no empty space within the settlement zones; buildings are stacked upon buildings, terraced and interlocked. The architecture is heavily industrial, constructed from dark iron, oxidized copper, soot-stained brick, and thick, rain-streaked glass. Yet, the city does not conquer the landscape; it is swallowed by it. The lush, damp vegetation creeps over the infrastructure. Moss softens the sharp corners of brickwork; pale green vines thread through the iron lattices of catwalks and suspension bridges. 

For an industrial metropolis, Aethelgard is profoundly, unnervingly quiet. The dense, wet moss and the heavy, moisture-laden air act as massive acoustic dampeners, swallowing the sharp clatter and clank typical of mechanized production. Instead, the city operates on a subsonic, muffled frequency. Deep within the terraced factories, massive, slow-turning atmospheric moisture-harvesters and heavy steam-turbines rotate with a rhythmic, heavy grace. You feel the industry more than you hear it: a deep, rhythmic thrumming in the soles of your boots, a low *shhh-thump, shhh-thump* of low-pressure pistons, and the soft, continuous hiss of venting steam that immediately condenses into the chill air. The loudest sounds are natural—the perpetual, musical dripping of water from eaves and fern-fronds into the iron gutters, and the low sigh of the high-altitude wind slipping over the smooth, ancient domes.

The scale of the region is open, allowing you to stand on a high catwalk and look out across the misty valleys. You can see the city repeating itself on the neighboring stone domes, dense clusters of dark iron and bright, glowing incandescent windows connected by swaying, heavy-cable funiculars and arched iron bridges that vanish into the fog. The horizon is a rolling sequence of these smooth, green-and-gray monadnocks, stretching out into the dim distance.

The ecology of this high, damp world is highly active, driven by the slow but relentless cycles of condensation and thermal shifts. As the dim light shifts with the unseen sun, the mist rolls in thick, heavy waves, flushing the city’s complex network of aqueducts and water-wheels. The ecosystem is in constant, breathing motion. The strange, fleshy high-altitude flora responds to the dim light and the touch of the mist, slowly opening and closing their pale leaves in a visible, creeping rhythm. 

Fauna is rare, driven away by the thin air, the cold, and the heavy industrial presence, but what remains is distinctly strange. There are no mammals or common birds. Instead, the damp air is occasionally crossed by "mist-skimmers"—large, spindly, pale arthropods with translucent, balloon-like sacs on their backs that allow them to drift on the high-altitude thermals. They feed on the soot and the iron-rich moss, their long, delicate legs walking silently across the surface tension of the condensation pools. In the deeper, darker alleys of the lower terraces, blind, luminescent centipedes navigate the dripping pipes, drawn to the faint electrical hum of the city’s generator lines. 

Everything here is in a state of settled, ancient motion. The stone has finished its billion-year journey of erosion, resting in its final, smoothed form. The city, too, feels as though it has reached a final equilibrium with its environment. The industry does not rage; it hums. The water does not flood; it weeps. The cold, damp air holds it all in a quiet, suspended breath, a dense and thriving pocket of life and iron clinging to the smooth, eroded roof of the world.
