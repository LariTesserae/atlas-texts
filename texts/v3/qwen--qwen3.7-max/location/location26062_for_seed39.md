At this extreme elevation, the air is thin, biting, and tastes faintly of ozone and crushed ice. The sky is a bruised, perpetual twilight, filtered through a heavy, low-hanging ceiling of stratocumulus clouds that trap the highland in a dim, slate-gray gloom. This is a place of profound, aching cold, where every breath feels like swallowing glass, and the ambient temperature hovers just below the freezing point, kept from plunging further only by the deep, radiating warmth of the earth below.

The ground beneath is brand new. This is an embryonic landscape, a vast, high-altitude plateau currently being thrust into the stratosphere by slow, violent tectonic uplift. Because it is just coming into being, the geology is entirely raw and unweathered. There is no soil, no rounded river-stone, no softening of edges. The bedrock is a sprawling expanse of fresh basalt and pale granite, fractured into sharp, crystalline shards and jagged plates that could slice through leather. It is a pre-formation world, displaying the naked, uneroded bones of the planet before wind and time have had the chance to wear them down.

Despite the freezing temperatures, the landscape is intensely wet. The air is so heavily saturated with moisture that the atmosphere itself seems to condense directly onto the freezing stones. The raw rock weeps constantly, slick with a film of supercooled water. In the shallow, microscopic depressions of the uplifted stone, slushy, high-alpine marshes pool and freeze, their surfaces skimmed with delicate, fractal ice that constantly cracks and reforms as the ground subtly shifts. A heavy, freezing fog clings to the surface, rolling in slow, dense banks across the wet stone. 

It is a massively open expanse, stretching for dozens of miles across a regional highland basin, yet it feels intensely hollow. The physical density of the space is near zero. There are no boulders, no towering crags, no trees, and no large monolithic features to break the sightline. It is a vast, flat, empty void of wet stone and fog. Vegetation is incredibly sparse, limited to a few pale, crustose lichens and rare, wiry tufts of freeze-dried sedge clinging to the absolute driest ridges. For the most part, the eye travels across endless, unbroken emptiness.

Yet, this empty void is humming with a relentless, mechanized industry. 

The landscape contains absolutely no built structures—no hives, no nests, no burrows, no ruins, no artifacts of any kind. Instead, the "technology" of this place is entirely biological and geological, functioning with the ruthless, scaled efficiency of an industrial refinery. The emptiness is carpeted by an astonishing abundance of high-altitude lithovores—millions of specialized, chitinous arthropods adapted to the extreme cold and thin air. They do not build; they process. 

These creatures operate in massive, synchronized conveyor-lines, marching across the weeping rocks in endless, rhythmic waves. Their behavior is purely industrial. They harvest the mineral-rich condensation and the sharp, raw flakes of newly uplifted stone, acting as a mechanized biological assembly line. They march in perfect unison toward the natural geothermal fissures that scar the plateau—deep, hissing cracks where the earth’s own internal engines vent raw, subterranean heat. The creatures use these natural blast furnaces to catalyze their digestion, dropping their harvested raw materials into the steaming vents and retrieving the chemically broken-down slurry. 

The sheer scale of their biological operation generates its own power grid. As millions of their dry, chitinous bodies rub together in the freezing, low-humidity air, they generate a massive static electrical charge. A faint, bluish corona of static electricity arcs between the swarms, an electrified biological network that coordinates their movements and powers their collective labor. It is a living factory, engaged in the scaled, mass production of mineral processing, turning raw, emerging geology into a fine, nutrient-rich sludge that washes away into the slushy marshes. 

Because the air is so thin and the heavy fog acts as an acoustic dampener, the soundscape remains mostly quiet. There is no deafening roar of machinery, no chaotic clamor of a swarm. Instead, the industrial process is reduced to a soft, pervasive soundscape. There is the quiet, continuous hiss of condensing steam from the weeping rocks and the geothermal vents. Beneath the feet, there is the deep, subsonic, rhythmic thud of the tectonic uplift—the slow, grinding engine of the earth still pushing the plateau upward. And overlaying it all is the muffled, synchronized *click-clack* of a million mandibles working in unison, a steady, rhythmic ticking like a colossal, quiet clock.
