The light here is absolute, unfiltered, and entirely normal. It falls from a pale, high-altitude sun with the crisp, shadow-defining clarity of a perfect midday. There is no strange tint to the sky, no bruised twilight, no atmospheric filtering. It is just bright, honest daylight illuminating a world that has entirely forgotten how to obey it.

This is a high, arid plateau, elevated so far above the absent sea level that the air is thin and bites at the lungs. It is a vast, open expanse of baked silicate, oxidized iron, and porous pumice. There is no water. There has never been water. The terrain is carved not by rivers, but by eons of dry, abrasive wind and the slow, grinding passage of the locals. The erosion is deeply established; the canyons and mesas are not jagged or young, but worn into smooth, sweeping, aerodynamic curves. They look like the bones of a desert, polished by time into a state of perfect, static balance. There are no plants. Not a single blade of grass, no lichen, no fungal crust. The ground is purely mineral, a sprawling canvas of rust-reds, burnt ochres, and blinding whites.

Yet, for all its vastness, the landscape feels profoundly empty. The scale is regional, stretching out for dozens of miles in every direction, but the density of objects is incredibly low. There are huge, hollow volumes of space between the smoothed mesas, vast gaps of nothingness. 

But it is not quiet. 

The sound is a physical weight, a loud, deafening, and continuous low-frequency roar that vibrates in the marrow of your bones. It sounds like a million distant jet engines idling, or the grinding tectonic groan of planetary gears. It is overwhelming in its volume, yet entirely devoid of dynamic change. There are no sudden crashes, no rhythmic pounding, no variations in pitch. It is a patient, eternal, unvarying drone. 

The source of this sound, and the sole occupants of this empty, high-altitude desert, are the fauna. 

They are overwhelming in their dominance, yet spaced miles apart, dotting the horizon like slow-moving islands. They are biological, but their biology is indistinguishable from heavy industry. They are colossal, multi-legged silicate beasts, the size of cathedrals, encased in thick, heat-radiating carapaces of dark, slag-like chitin. They have no eyes, only massive, gaping intake maws that drag along the bedrock, and towering, fleshy exhaust stacks rising from their dorsal ridges. 

There are no built structures here. No pipes, no smokestacks, no concrete pads, no artifacts of any civilization. The industry is entirely metabolic. These creatures are walking refineries. They consume the raw, oxidized rock of the plateau, and deep within their cavernous, hyper-thermic bellies, biological blast furnaces burn at thousands of degrees. They are incredibly hot to the touch, radiating a dry, baking warmth that warps the air around them. Through a slow, agonizingly patient digestive process, they strip the impurities from the stone, smelting it internally. From their underbellies, they extrude perfectly formed, glowing ingots of pure refined metal, leaving trails of iron, copper, and titanium scattered across the empty desert floor. 

Despite the deafening roar of their internal furnaces and the overwhelming presence of their herds, their dynamic is glacial. A single step takes weeks. The processing of a single boulder takes a decade. The loud, continuous roar is simply the baseline hum of their existence, a sound that changes on a geological timescale. They are locked in a slow, gradual cycle of consumption and excretion, moving with a weak gradient of intent, entirely unhurried.

But it is their exhaust that breaks the world. 

The towering dorsal vents of the smelter-beasts do not release smoke or steam. They exhale ontological waste—the broken, un-rendered byproducts of reality that cannot be smelted into physical matter. This exhaust is a shimmering, heavy, iridescent gas that sinks and pools in the dry canyons and hollows of the plateau. 

Where this gas settles, reality breaks down. 

The physics of the landscape dissolve into paradox. In the heavy pools of exhaust, gravity loses its consensus; boulders hover inches off the ground, while dust falls upward into the sky. The normal, honest sunlight hits the gas and fractures into impossible, non-existent colors that the human eye cannot process, leaving lingering auras of "wrongness" in your vision. Space becomes non-euclidean. You can look across a seemingly empty, half-mile stretch of the plateau and see the back of your own head. Distances compress and stretch without warning. Time, too, dilates in the exhaust. The slow, patient cycles of the fauna become trapped in localized loops. You might watch a glowing ingot of pure iron drop from a beast’s belly, hit the ground, and then fall upward back into the creature, over and over, a stuttering glitch in the world’s rendering. 

There is no magic here, no malice, and no grand design. It is simply an ecosystem operating at the absolute limit of physical law. The high, hot, dry wind continues to blow across the smoothed, empty mesas. The clear, normal sunlight continues to shine. And the colossal, living factories continue their slow, deafening, eternal crawl, eating the world, refining the earth, and exhaling the broken pieces of reality back into the thin, dry air.
