To stand on the precipice of the High Tiers is to look out over a valley that was never carved by glaciers, but forged in the fires of a forgotten, planetary industry. The elevation is staggering, a dizzying altitude where the air grows thin and cool, though the ambient warmth of the lower decks radiates upward, creating a perpetual, muggy subtropical climate. You are thousands of feet in the air, looking across a vast, open expanse of jagged peaks and deep ravines. But as your eyes adjust to the blinding, high-altitude sunlight, the illusion of natural geology shatters. 

There is no bedrock here. There is no natural substrate anywhere. The sheer, thousand-foot cliffs dropping away beneath your boots are made of riveted ferrocrete and oxidized iron plating. The "boulders" scattered across the terraced slopes are petrified, colossal gears, their teeth worn smooth. This is a fully constructed world, an artificial mountain range of world-engines and atmospheric scrubbers that has reached a state of deep, established equilibrium. 

The erosion here is not the frantic crumbling of a dying structure, but the patient, settled weathering of deep time. The metal has achieved a permanent patina. Layers of rust have compacted into a hard, rust-red stone; copper runoffs have stained the great vertical bulkheads in brilliant, sweeping veins of verdigris. The sharp edges of the original architecture have been softened by centuries of wind and acidic condensation, rounding the corners of the megastructure into organic, rolling contours. It is an industrial geology, settled into its own permanent state.

Over this iron skeleton, a suffocatingly lush and dense vegetation has taken hold. Because there is no soil, the roots of massive, strangling figs and thick-corded lianas have woven themselves through the grating of catwalks, anchoring into the compacted dust of pulverized rust and bird guano. The canopy is a layered, near-touching density of broad-leafed epiphytes, dripping ferns, and vibrant, waxy orchids that cling to the fluted sides of dormant smokestacks. The air is thick, bright, and heavy with the scent of blooming jasmine and hot ozone. 

Water is a persistent, damp presence, though rarely gathered in large flows. The environment relies on the weeping of the machine. Massive, subterranean cooling pipes sweat constant, heavy condensation that drips from the undersides of suspended walkways. Intermittent, warm rain squalls blow through the high-altitude valley, caught and funneled by the dished tops of giant turbine housings, creating deep, temporary pools of lukewarm water. When the sun breaks through the mist, the light is blinding, glaring off millions of wet leaves and the polished, rain-washed brass of ancient valve wheels.

Despite the overwhelming presence of nature, the tech of the landscape remains undeniably industrial, though it operates on a deeply slow, geological timescale. The world-engine has not stopped; it has merely settled into a glacial rhythm. The dynamic of this place is one of patient, gradual cycles. Deep within the hollowed-out peaks, a piston the size of a cathedral rises and falls, emitting a chest-rattling, deafening *THOOM* that echoes across the valley once every four hours. Giant, rusted cogs at the base of the ravines turn with a grinding, metallic shriek, advancing a single tooth per day to slowly open or close the great ventilation baffles. 

Because of this slow, heavy machinery, and the sheer verticality of the terrain, the landscape is profoundly loud. The sound is a constant, roaring tapestry. The wind howls through the fluted cooling towers like a giant, wooden flute. The heavy, rhythmic dripping of condensation onto hollow metal decks creates a chaotic, percussive clatter. And when the great pistons fire, the sound is a physical weight, a low-frequency boom that shakes the pollen from the canopy and vibrates in your teeth. 

Yet, life has not just survived this loud, bright, artificial wilderness; it has thrived in abundant, hyper-adapted profusion. The fauna is everywhere, woven into the dense, layered ecology. Flocks of birds with iridescent, oil-slick feathers nest in the hollowed-out spark plugs of dormant combustion chambers. Arboreal mammals, resembling long-limbed macaques, swing not from branch to branch, but along the high-tension power cables that still hum with a low, electric current. Insects with metallic, carapace-like shells drink from the warm, shallow pools of rainwater gathered in the rusted treads of immobile, building-sized crawlers. Even the predators have adapted; large, sleek cats with padded paws stalk the shadowed, multi-level gantries, their claws finding perfect purchase in the diamond-plate steel.

It is an unusual place, though not overtly alien. The weirdness lies simply in the realization of what you are looking at: a wild, untamed ecosystem that is entirely, fundamentally synthetic. As you walk along a moss-draped suspension bridge, feeling the warm, damp air on your skin and listening to the roar of the wind and the distant, booming heartbeat of the slow-turning engines, the boundary between the manufactured and the natural completely dissolves. The machine has become the mountain, and the mountain is alive.
