There is no bedrock here, not in any sense that a geologist would recognize. The ground itself is an ocean of compressed, petrified, and living phytomass, built up over billions of years into a high-altitude continent. This is the Great Swell, a plateau elevated thousands of meters above the planetary cloud-sea, yet it possesses no peaks, no cliffs, no sharp tectonic scars. Time and the patient, weeping water have eroded the original mountains into their final, terminal state: immense, convex, continent-sized domes that roll gently toward the horizon. It is a landscape that has finished changing. It has reached its oldest, most stable equilibrium, where the rate of decay perfectly matches the rate of growth. 

To walk here is to walk on a deep, warm sponge. The substrate is a miles-thick layer of lignified roots, fossilized peat, and living, gelatinous moss. It yields underfoot with a slow, hydraulic sigh, holding the impression of a footprint for decades before the memory of the weight is gently pushed back out by the swelling fibers. 

The air is heavy, warm, and perpetually dim. Because of the elevation, the light from the sun is a bruised, diffuse peach color, filtered through a permanent, high-altitude overcast and the impossibly dense canopy above. It is a world of perpetual twilight. The temperature hovers at a persistent, breath-like warmth, the kind of humid, close heat that relaxes the muscles and makes the eyelids heavy. 

Water does not flow in rivers or streams; the landscape is too flat, the substrate too absorbent. Instead, water is a constant state of being. The atmosphere is thick with condensing mist that clings to every surface. The "rocks"—which are actually massive, rounded nodules of compressed, iron-rich bark—weep continuously. Moisture beads on the fractal filaments of the canopy, swells until it is heavy, and drops in ultra-slow motion, striking the spongy ground with a muffled, barely audible *thwump*. The entire continent is a high-altitude marsh, a weeping, breathing sponge suspended in the sky.

Vegetation is not merely present; it is absolute. It is the architecture, the ground, the sky, and the atmosphere. The canopy is formed by the *Aero-banyans*, colossal, slow-growing structures that do not possess leaves. Instead, their trunks flare out into vast, vaulted, cathedral-like hollows, supporting a secondary sky of woven, lateral roots and moisture-trapping bladders. These bladders, the size of houses, are translucent and filled with a warm, bioluminescent fluid that pulses with a faint, sickly-sweet green light, providing the only sharp illumination in the dimness. The plants do not photosynthesize in any traditional sense; they chemosynthesize the heavy, warm mist, breathing in the water and exhaling dense, rolling banks of spore-fog that settle into the deep hollows of the swells.

Because the flora fills every volume of space, the density of life is staggering, yet the world is profoundly quiet. The acoustics of the Great Swell are entirely muffled by the billions of tons of damp cellulose. Sound does not carry; it dies the moment it is born. The only ambient noise is the soft, continuous *hiss* of condensation, and, very rarely, a subsonic, deep-bass groan that vibrates through the soles of the feet—the sound of a continent-sized root system shifting a fraction of an inch over the course of a month.

Fauna is abundant, but it has evolved to match the profound stillness and slow dynamic of the environment. There are no birds, no calling insects, no rustling predators. The creatures here are soft-bodied, heavy, and deliberate. Velvet-striders, resembling massive, six-legged sloths covered in damp, mossy fur, move through the fluted colonnades of the banyans. They do not chew; they press their soft, enzyme-secreting maws against the bark-stone and slowly dissolve their meals over days. In the air, spore-rays—flat, translucent, fluid-filled gliders—drift on the weak thermal gradients, navigating by sensing the chemical trails left by the plants. They communicate not with sound, but by flashing subtle, rhythmic patterns of bioluminescence along their wing-edges, silent conversations in the twilight.
