The world here has been worn down to its final, peaceful geometry. This is a peneplain of the deepest antiquity, a low-lying basin where millions of years of wind and water have eradicated every sharp edge, every cliff, every jagged fracture. The terrain is an endless, undulating expanse of polished bedrock, rolling in gentle, frictionless swells that stretch out to a dim, mist-choked horizon. There is no soil, no dirt, no dust. There is only the ancient, exposed bone of the earth, buffed to a smooth, glass-like finish by aeons of relentless erosion. 

It is a profoundly wet place, though there are no deep rivers or crashing waves. The water table sits mere inches below the surface, and the porous, ancient stone constantly weeps. Moisture beads on the polished rock, gathering into shallow, mirror-like pools in the lowest depressions before spilling over as thin, frictionless sheets that glide silently across the swells. The air is heavy, cool, and saturated. A perpetual, slate-grey overcast hangs low, diffusing the weak light into a shadowless, twilight gloom. Breath plumes in the chill air, and the pervasive dampness settles into the lungs, tasting of cold ozone and wet mineral.

Because the light is dim and the air is thick with condensing mist, the vastness of the basin feels both open and enclosed, a sprawling amphitheater of silver and shadow. Yet, the visual serenity of this smooth, wet, cool world is a lie. 

The landscape is deafening. 

The sound is not a mere noise; it is a physical pressure, a chest-rattling vibration that hums in the teeth and shakes the fluid in the inner ear. It comes from the fauna. In the absence of plant life—there is not a single blade of grass, no moss, no lichen, no wood—the base of the ecosystem is a pale, fleshy, fungal-mineral crust that chemically digests the wet bedrock. Grazing upon this crust are the basin-striders. They are abundant beyond counting, vast herds of them moving across the polished stone. They are amphibious, pale-skinned, six-limbed creatures, low-slung and broad, with massive, barrel-like chest cavities and no visible eyes. 

To navigate the dimness and communicate across the open scale of the basin, the striders sing. Or rather, they resonate. They force air through complex, calcified internal baffles, producing booming, low-frequency calls that easily exceed a hundred and thirty decibels. When a herd moves, the collective sound is a roaring, thrumming wall of acoustic force. The ancient, bowl-like erosion of the landscape acts as a perfect acoustic reflector, catching the sound and amplifying it, bouncing the roars back and forth across the shallow valleys until the very air seems to boil with the noise. The rush of the weeping water over the stone is entirely drowned out; you can only feel the water, you cannot hear it.

The ecosystem is in constant, violent motion. Despite the deafening roar, predators move among the herds—sleek, heavily armored, semi-aquatic hunters that use the acoustic chaos to mask their approach. They strike with sudden, brutal efficiency, dragging their prey into the shallow, freezing pools. The water clouds with pale, mineral-rich blood, quickly washing away over the frictionless stone, leaving no trace but the lingering vibration of the herd’s panic-calls.

Scattered very sparsely across this roaring, wet expanse are the faint marks of habitation. There are no grand cities, no sprawling villages. The built environment consists of isolated, small structures spaced miles apart, appearing as dark, rounded blemishes against the pale, wet stone. These are acoustic baffles and survival blinds, built by a nomadic, primitive people who traverse the basin. 

Constructed without metal or complex machinery, the structures are simple domes made of carefully selected, water-smoothed cobbles, stacked by hand and mortared with a thick, clay-like paste harvested from the deep fungal mats. There are no sharp corners in their architecture, mirroring the ancient erosion of the world; every wall curves to deflect the wind and disrupt the sound waves. Inside these small, scattered huts, the noise is muffled to a bearable, albeit still loud, thrum. 

The technology of the builders is rudimentary, perfectly adapted to the constraints of the mineral world. They use knapped tools made from volcanic glass and chipped silicate, bound to handles of cured, leathery fungal-stalks with woven mineral-fibers. They fashion heavy, padded helmets stuffed with dried, spongy fungal-pith to protect their hearing when they step outside their domes. They hunt the smaller striders using atlatls and smooth-stone bolas, moving quietly in a world where quiet is impossible, relying on the visual cover of the dim mist and the chaotic noise to mask their own movements.

To stand in this landscape is to be overwhelmed by paradox. Visually, it is the picture of ultimate, ancient stillness—a cool, dim, perfectly smoothed world of weeping stone and shallow, silver water, devoid of any green or growing thing. It feels like the end of the earth, a place that has finally settled into its final, unchanging form. But aurally and dynamically, it is a place of furious, overwhelming life. The air shakes with the booming roars of a million pale beasts, the water flows in endless, active cycles from the weeping rock to the shallow pools and over the edge of the swells, and the scattered, primitive hunters move through the mist, participating in the loud, wet, ancient rhythm of the basin. It is a world that has been worn perfectly smooth, only to be filled to the brim with the deafening roar of the present.
