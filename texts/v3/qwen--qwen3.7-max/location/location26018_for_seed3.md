The air up here is thin, cold, and tastes of wet iron. You are at an extreme altitude, high above the cloud floor, where the sky is a perpetual, bruised twilight. The light is dim and heavy, struggling to penetrate the thick, high-altitude overcast that traps the landscape in a state of endless, shadowy dusk. There is no direct sunlight, only a diffuse, slate-grey illumination that flattens distances and makes the vast, open scale of the region feel both immense and claustrophobic. 

Beneath your boots, the ground is shockingly sharp. This is a geology in its embryonic stage, entirely devoid of erosion. There are no smooth river stones, no wind-carved arches, no softened slopes. The rock is raw, pre-formation basalt and pale quartz, extruding from the earth in jagged, geometric shards. It looks as though the mountains were birthed only hours ago, their edges razor-keen and unweathered. Yet, the process of their formation is agonizingly slow. If you watch a cliff face for hours, you might see it accrete a fraction of a millimeter, the stone literally knitting itself together out of the cold mist, patient and glacial in its upward push. 

Clinging to these newborn, jagged peaks is an archipelago of heavy industry. The built environment is intensely urban and densely layered within its specific nodes, but scattered sparsely across the vast, open scale of the mountain range. Huge gaps of empty, swirling fog and raw, unclimbed stone separate the settlements. Where the city exists, it is a vertical tangle of infrastructure. Layered tenements of black brick and corrugated iron are stacked atop massive stamping mills and boiler houses. Funiculars and iron catwalks bridge the dizzying drops between spires, suspending workers over abysses of grey mist. 

And it is incredibly loud. The acoustics of the un-eroded, highly reflective stone act as massive parabolic dishes. The rhythmic, deafening *thud-hiss-clatter* of mechanized drop-forges, the shrieking of steam whistles, and the deep, chest-rattling hum of electrified dynamos bounce off the sharp cliffs, multiplying and overlapping until the air itself vibrates. The noise is a physical weight, a constant industrial roar that echoes across the sparse, open valleys.

But the sound behaves strangely, because the consensus reality of this high place is slowly, patiently breaking down. 

The weirdness here is not a chaotic, flashing unraveling of physics; it is a slow, creeping decay of natural law, moving at the pace of geology. Because the rock itself is "pre-formation," it hasn't entirely committed to the rules of the universe yet. You might walk past a smokestack that is slowly, over the course of a decade, bending into a non-Euclidean fractal, its bricks extruding into a fourth spatial dimension. The workers simply build scaffolding around the anomaly and keep shoveling coal. Gravity stutters in localized, slow-shifting pockets; a dropped wrench might fall upward for three feet before remembering its mass and clattering to the iron grating. Shadows cast by the dim, diffuse light occasionally detach from their objects, drifting a few inches to the left over the course of an afternoon. The reality breakdown is an accepted, mundane fact of life, a slow fraying of the world's edges that the heavy, greasy machinery of the city simply powers through.

Amidst the iron, the noise, and the fraying physics, the environment is persistently, uncomfortably damp. Water does not flow in rivers here; it weeps. Condensation beads on every cold surface, slicking the iron catwalks and dripping from the sharp, embryonic rocks in a constant, intermittent drizzle. The air is saturated with a fine, freezing mist that coats your eyelashes and soaks through heavy wool coats. 

Where this dampness pools in the sheltered crevices of the raw stone and the warm runoff of the factories, vegetation erupts in startling, lush pockets. It is not a sprawling jungle, but highly concentrated bursts of vibrant life. Thick, spongy mosses in shades of neon green and deep violet carpet the bases of the smokestacks. Pale, translucent ferns uncurl aggressively around steam vents, their fronds glistening with condensation. Strange, bioluminescent fungi cling to the underside of iron viaducts, feeding on the damp and the ambient electrical charge of the factories, casting a faint, sickly-sweet glow in the dimness.

This lush, damp micro-ecology supports a staggering abundance of fauna. The air is thick with them. Flocks of heavy, dark-feathered avians with multiple sets of joints in their wings roost on the high-voltage lines, their deafening, metallic cries adding to the industrial cacophony. Swarms of gossamer-winged insects, thriving in the cool mist, cloud the lantern light. Strange, multi-legged grazers with pale, blind eyes herd along the narrow, un-eroded ridges, licking the mineral-rich condensation from the raw stone. They have adapted perfectly to the slow reality breakdown; you can watch a flock of birds fly into a localized gravity-anomaly, casually flapping in mid-air for minutes until the localized physics drift away and they resume their flight. 

To stand in this place is to be caught in a profound tension. You feel the biting, cool dampness on your skin and the dizzying, extreme drop of the sparse, open valleys below. Your ears ring with the loud, relentless, mechanized pounding of a city that refuses to stop producing, even as the very stones it is built upon slowly forget how to be solid. It is a landscape of heavy iron and sharp rock, of lush green moss and pale twilight, forever caught in the slow, loud, and patient process of coming into being, even as the rules of its being quietly dissolve.
