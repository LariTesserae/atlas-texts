The air up here is thin, crisp, and perpetually cool, carrying the distinct, metallic chill of high altitude. You are standing on a peneplain—a vast, high-elevation plateau where the mountains have been worn down by eons of wind and time into smooth, rounded, primordial domes. This is an ancient, settled geography, the oldest stable state of a landscape that has long since forgotten the violent tectonic upheavals of its youth. It is deeply aged, near-flattened, and stretches out in an open, regional expanse that allows you to see for dozens of miles in every direction under a canopy of normal, diffuse daylight.

But there is no dirt beneath your boots. There is no stone. The ground is entirely, totally vegetation. 

It is a deep, spongy, unbroken ocean of compacted plant matter. Pale green mosses, rust-colored lichens, and densely woven root-mats form a substrate dozens of feet thick. It is a pure-plant ecosystem acting as the very crust of the earth. The environment is damp; the thick vegetative sponge holds intermittent moisture, weeping condensation from the cool air and collecting it in shallow, dark pockets of water that pool in the depressions of the root-weave. When you step, the ground sighs, exhaling a cool, wet mist that clings to your ankles.

Across this vast, open scale, the urban structures rise. They are sparse, widely scattered across the miles of flat, damp moss, leaving huge, agoraphobic expanses of empty green between them. Yet, what they lack in density, they make up for in sheer, organized scale. This is a city, but it is a city grown, not built. Plant-as-architecture has been taken to its absolute extreme. Colossal factory-cathedrals and layered tenement-ziggurats are formed from living, braided trunks of ironwood and calcified bamboo. Staircases spiral up the insides of hollowed, ancient sequoia-like stalks; balconies are woven from living, tensile vines that tighten and reinforce themselves under weight. The urban infrastructure is a masterclass in botanical engineering, a settlement-scale organization of living mass.

And it is fiercely, unapologetically industrial. 

The air vibrates with a deafening, relentless loudness. Deep within the wooden hulls of these arboreal factories, massive mechanized processes are at work. Giant gears carved from burl-wood and banded in rusted iron grind against one another. Steam-driven pistons, powered by the immense geothermal compost-heat of the deep root-mats, slam up and down with rhythmic, chest-rattling thuds. Copper wiring is woven directly through the xylem and phloem of the structural trees, electrifying the city with sap-conducted currents. The noise is a cacophony of hissing steam valves, clanking mechanized looms, and the deep, resonant groaning of living wood being forced to act as steel. It is an active, churning ecosystem of production, where the biological pace of growth has been violently accelerated and harnessed by engines.

Yet, for all this noise and motion, the landscape is profoundly empty of life. Fauna is incredibly rare. There are no birds in the cool sky, no insects buzzing over the damp moss pools. The only movement is the mechanical and the botanical. Occasionally, you might see a single, pale, blind millipede the size of a forearm navigating a steam-pipe, or a solitary, mechanical heron ticking quietly in a puddle, but the animal kingdom has largely abandoned or been excluded from this place.

It is when you look closely at the edges of this loud, active, green industrial sprawl that the true nature of the landscape reveals itself. The weirdness here is not a flickering anomaly; it is a deeply settled, ancient reality breakdown. The world’s physics have frayed, and the landscape has simply eroded into this broken state as its final, stable form.

Watch the smoke billowing from the wooden exhaust stacks of the factories. It does not rise and dissipate. Instead, the thick, grey smoke extrudes upward in rigid, geometric fractals, crystallizing into solid, fragile glass-like shapes that eventually snap under their own weight and shatter silently onto the moss. Look at the shadows cast by the normal daylight: they do not stretch away from the sun, but pool directly beneath the objects, occasionally detaching and drifting a few inches to the left before snapping back. 

The spatial geometry of the urban layout is fundamentally non-Euclidean. A woven-vine suspension bridge connecting two distant factory-trees appears to be only a hundred yards long, but walking across it takes three hours, the distance stretching dynamically with your stride. The loud, rhythmic clanking of the sap-turbines suffers from causal dampness; you will often hear the deafening *CRACK* of a wooden gear engaging a full two seconds before you see the gear actually move. The reality here is porous. In the damp pockets of water scattered across the moss, the reflections do not show the cool, bright sky above, but rather a churning, starless void, or sometimes the reflection of a city that burned down a thousand years ago.

Even the vegetation participates in the breakdown. The leaves of the smaller, scattered shrubs that grow on the balconies of the wooden tenements do not photosynthesize light; they photosynthesize time. If you stare at a patch of them, the air around them shimmers, and the cool temperature drops to freezing for a microsecond as they pull ambient seconds out of the air to fuel their growth. 

This is the high, ancient moor of the Loom-City. It is a place of brilliant, diffuse light and chilling, damp air. It is a loud, mechanized triumph of botanical urbanism, scattered sparsely across an open, primordial plain. It is a world where the engines roar, the roots drink, and the fundamental laws of physics have long since surrendered to the slow, grinding, beautiful erosion of the impossible.
