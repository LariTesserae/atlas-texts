The first thing you feel is not the sound, but the vibration. It enters through the soles of your boots, travels up the marrow of your bones, and settles in the hollow of your chest. Only then does the noise register—a deafening, continuous roar that presses against the eardrums like deep water. 

This is the high basin of Oakhaven, though there are no oaks here, nor any haven from the noise. Situated at a high elevation where the air should be thin and biting, the atmosphere is instead unnaturally warm and thick. It is a perpetual, humid bathhouse of a climate. The normal, pale daylight filters through a ceiling of warm, condensing mist, casting a flat, shadowless illumination over a landscape that stretches for miles across a vast, sunken plateau. 

The terrain is a masterpiece of established erosion. Millions of years of water and wind have sculpted the high-altitude basalt into fluted, honeycombed formations and deep, sweeping channels. There are no sharp edges left in the geology; everything has been worn down to smooth, undulating curves and deep, polished bowls. And everywhere, the rock weeps. Water seeps from the microscopic pores of the stone, beading on the smooth surfaces before sliding down into shallow, warm marsh-edges that pool in the eroded divots. The air is so saturated that condensation forms on your eyelashes and drips from your chin. It is a wet, weeping world, glistening under the ordinary grey-white sky.

Rising from this deeply weathered stone is the city. It is an urban expanse of staggering scale, yet it feels profoundly empty. The density of the settlement is sparse; the structures are colossal, separated by vast, echoing plazas and wide, terraced chasms. There are no tight grids of row houses or cramped alleys. Instead, the city is organized into massive, layered mega-blocks. Infrastructure is dense within these blocks—tangled catwalks, stacked residential terraces, and thick bundles of insulated piping—but there is immense, breathing space between them. You can stand in the center of a grand, empty civic concourse and look a mile across the mist to the next towering district. 

The technology that defines this city is heavy, industrial, and entirely mechanized. Colossal engines and scaled production facilities dominate the skyline, but they do not look like the factories of a bustling metropolis. They look like geological features. Huge, rusted turbines the size of cathedrals are sunk directly into the weeping rock. Miles of electrified conduits and thick, calcified iron pipes route the endless supply of warm, weeping water into the bellies of these machines. The architecture is heavily layered, with centuries of industrial expansion built directly over and into older, fossilized layers of infrastructure. The city is a sedimentary record of its own mechanical history.

Yet, for all the deafening noise, the dynamic of the city is agonizingly slow. This is the great, strange paradox of the basin. The sound—a relentless, bone-rattling boom and hiss—is generated by the friction of millions of gallons of water being forced through narrow, eroded stone valves and ancient iron turbines. But the machines themselves move with glacial patience. A single, house-sized gear in the central milling district takes three full days to complete one rotation. The massive pistons rise and fall with the slow, rhythmic breathing of a sleeping leviathan. The industry here is not producing goods for a fast-paced market; it is engaged in some patient, eternal process. Perhaps it is slowly grinding the mountain down to regulate the high-altitude water table; perhaps it is weaving the mineral-rich mist into the bedrock. The purpose is obscured by time, leaving only the deafening execution of a very slow cycle.

Because of the overwhelming acoustic pressure and the warm, mineral-heavy runoff, ecology is nearly non-existent. Fauna is exceptionally rare; no birds fly through the mist, and no insects buzz in the warm air. The noise would drive them mad, and the heavy, vibrating stone offers no quiet refuge. Vegetation is equally sparse, clinging to life only in the most stubborn, strange forms. In the quietest, most sheltered crevices of the weeping rocks, you might find patches of pale, rubbery lichen or translucent, glassy ferns that do not photosynthesize, but instead feed on the acoustic vibrations and the warm, copper-tasting condensation. They are ghostly, low-saturation specks of life in a world of wet black stone and rusted iron.

To walk through this city is to be immersed in a sensory contradiction. The air is warm and soft against your skin, the light is a gentle, normal daylight, and the visual sweeping scale of the open valley invites a long, peaceful gaze. But the sound forbids peace. It is a physical weight, a deafening wall of industrial roar and rushing water that isolates you in your own skull. You cannot speak; you cannot hear your own footsteps on the wet stone. 

You can only watch the warm mist curl around the fluted, weeping rocks, and observe the colossal, rusted gears turning their imperceptible, eternal fractions of an inch. The city exists in a state of perfect, settled balance—a loud, wet, warm monument to slow time, roaring endlessly into the high, thin air.
