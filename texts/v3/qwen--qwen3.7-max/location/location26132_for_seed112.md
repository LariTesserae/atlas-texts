There is no water here. Not a drop, not a molecule of H₂O in the soil, the air, or the sap. 

Instead, the world is an endless, high-altitude expanse of cool, thin air and blinding, shadow-casting light. You are standing on a continent of wood. The ground itself is a planetary substrate of braided, calcified, and living root-mass, rising thousands of feet above a lower atmospheric stratum of dense, dry clouds. Because there is no water to rot it, and no rain to wash it, the surface of this wooden world has been eroded by millennia of abrasive, high-altitude winds into a smooth, undulating plain of polished, bone-white lignin. It feels like walking on a glacier of pale hardwood, cool to the touch and slick beneath your feet.

This is an ecosystem of total vegetation, yet it is profoundly sparse. The "ground" is 100% plant matter, but the vertical elements—the towering flora that rise from this wooden floor—are spaced miles apart. Between these colossal structures, there is nothing but the glaring, sun-baked expanse of the root-plain, stretching unbroken to the curved horizon. The emptiness is vast, a low-saturation canvas of pale wood and bright sky, making the isolated giants that do exist feel impossibly monumental.

And they are not merely trees; they are biological factories. 

Though there are no built structures, no ruins, and no artifacts of any intelligent maker, the flora has evolved to harness the principles of heavy industry. The sparse, mile-spaced spires are arbor-engines. Their trunks are hundreds of feet thick, wrapped in overlapping plates of iron-dense bark that shift and grind like tectonic gears. Deep within their fluted columns, biological combustion chambers burn volatile, anhydrous resins to generate immense thermal energy. You can see the heat shimmering around their crowns. Thick, pneumatic vines pulse with pressurized liquid ammonia and methane—their anhydrous "sap"—hissing violently from relief valves of porous bark. 

The air tastes sharply of ozone, turpentine, and hot dust. Vast coils of metallic, copper-rich xylem wrap around the trunks, generating massive electrical potentials. With every shift of the wind, the trees discharge dry, blue lightning into the cool, thin air. The light from the star above is already blinding, but the strobing flashes of bio-electricity make the shadows dance and fracture.

It is deafening. The sound is not just heard; it is a physical weight that rattles your ribs and vibrates through the soles of your feet. The wind howling across the miles-wide gaps between the spires creates a continuous, roaring baseline. But the trees themselves are the true source of the din. They groan with the sound of stressing timber, deep and subsonic. Their internal pistons of sap-wood slam in rhythmic, thunderous beats. High-pressure gas vents shriek like tearing metal. The grinding of their bark-plates sounds like a mountain range being crushed. To speak is impossible; to think requires pushing through the sheer, oppressive wall of mechanical noise. 

Yet, life thrives in the turbulence. The fauna is abundant, perfectly adapted to the hyperactive, industrial biome. Swarms of iridescent, chitin-armored gliders ride the thermal updrafts generated by the trees' exhaust vents, their wings snapping taut in the gale. On the polished wooden plains below, massive herds of six-legged, resonant-chambered grazers move in flowing, river-like formations. They do not eat the wood; they feed on the electrical discharge and the nutrient-rich, carbon-heavy ash that falls from the combustion-canopies. When they call to one another, their voices are low, brass-horn honks that cut through the mechanical roar, adding a mournful, organic counterpoint to the industrial clamor.

The environment is in a state of constant, chaotic flux. It is a turbulent world, driven by the hyperactive metabolism of the arbor-engines. Because the trees vent such enormous amounts of heat, ammonia vapor, and electrical charge, they create their own localized weather systems. Dry lightning storms ignite and extinguish in minutes. localized tornadoes of hot gas and ash spin across the polished wood. 

The ground itself is unstable. The "erosion" here is not just wind-swept polishing; it is the slow, massive respiration of the substrate. Every few hours, a section of the root-plain the size of a city block will suddenly heave upward with a sound like a cannon shot, settling into a new topological ridge as the deep roots shift to accommodate the turbulent growth above. Fissures snap open in the white wood, venting blindingly bright, anhydrous gas, before sealing shut again as the living lignin rapidly knits itself back together. 

It is a world of violent creation and destruction, an anthill-energy scaled up to planetary physics. There is no quiet, no stillness, and no water to soothe the friction. There is only the cool, thin air, the blinding light, the miles of polished white wood, and the roaring, grinding, electric heartbeat of a forest that builds itself anew every single day.
