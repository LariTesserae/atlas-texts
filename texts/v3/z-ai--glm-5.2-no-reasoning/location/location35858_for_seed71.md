# The Vast Quiet

## I. Approach

You come up into it gradually. That is the only way. There are no sudden edges here — no cliff line, no dramatic threshold. The ground tilts and keeps tilting, and the air changes by degrees so slow you cannot mark where one atmosphere ended and another began. You walk for hours. The world behind you becomes a memory that you cannot quite locate in any direction.

You notice the silence first. Not absence — silence. The distinction matters. Absence is when something is missing. Silence is when something has learned to stop.

## II. The Land Itself

This is a plateau so large it has weather of its own. Not metaphor — literal weather. Clouds form and dissolve within its borders. Rain falls in one valley and never touches the next. The scale is difficult to hold in mind. You can walk for two days and still see the same horizon on every side, faintly blue, slightly shimmering, never closer.

The ground is stone. Not bedrock — something older. Something that has been stone so long it has forgotten what it was before stone. It is the color of ash and the color of rust and the color of nothing in particular. Flat in most places. Grooved in others, the grooves so old they have become geological features in their own right. Water moves through some of them. Not rivers — threads. The water is cold and moves slowly and sometimes does not move at all, simply rests in the groove until some invisible tilt encourages it onward. You could follow one for a day and never hear it make a sound.

The stone is not flat the way a floor is flat. It is flat the way a face is flat after the bones beneath it have settled into their final arrangement. There are undulations. Shallow basins the size of towns. Long swells like the backs of animals sleeping underground. If you lie on your stomach and press your cheek to the surface, you can feel warmth from somewhere deep — not heat, warmth, the memory of heat, the way a stone wall holds the afternoon sun long after dark.

In the basins, water collects. Not pools — dampness. A sheen. You can see your reflection in some of them if the light is right, but the reflection is always slightly wrong. The angle is off. The face in the water is a half-second behind where it should be. You learn not to look.

## III. The Green

Here is what lives: plants. Only plants. Nothing moves that is not moved by wind or water.

The vegetation is impossible. Not because it shouldn't be here — the elevation and the cold and the damp should make it sparse, stunted, alpine. Instead it is dense and layered and continuous, and yet somehow also scattered, as if each plant has decided to grow where it wants and the wanting is spread thin across the enormous stone.

What grows is not familiar. There are things like trees — vertical, woody, branching — but they grow from cracks in the stone, and their trunks are translucent at the base, darkening to opacity as they rise. You can see water moving inside them if you look closely. The water is not the water from the grooves. It is darker. It moves with a rhythm that does not match any wind.

There are things like moss but they are not moss. They cover the stone in patches, and the patches breathe. Not metaphor. You can watch a patch expand and contract over the course of a minute. Very slightly. The edge of the growth moves out, then retreats, then moves out. If you sit beside one long enough, your breathing will sync with it. This takes about twenty minutes. After that, you feel calm in a way you cannot account for.

There are things like grass — thin, pale, growing in tufts from the basins where water collects — but they are cold to the touch, colder than the air, and when the wind blows across them they make a sound that is almost a word. Almost. Never quite.

The largest growths are the things like trees. They cluster in the deeper basins and along the wider grooves. Some are thirty feet tall. Their canopies are sparse but interlock, and beneath them the light is green and indirect and the temperature drops. Walking among them is like walking through a cathedral that has no floor — the stone is continuous, the roots are exposed, gripping the rock with fingers that look deliberate. The roots have grown around objects. You can see shapes in the tangles. You choose not to identify them.

New growth is happening. You can see it. Small pale shoots emerging from cracks. Spores settling on damp stone and becoming green. The pace is slow but visible if you watch. The ecosystem is not dying. It is not in decline. It is active and reproducing and spreading. It is simply doing all of this in total silence, with no animals to eat it or pollinate it or carry its seeds. It does not need them. It has not needed them for a long time.

## IV. The Damp

The moisture here is strange. It does not rain the way rain rains elsewhere. Instead, the air thickens. You feel it on your skin before you see it. The visibility decreases — not to fog, to something less than fog, a softening of distance. The stone gets darker. The moss-things brighten. Then, after an hour or two of this thickening, water begins to fall. Not in drops. In a fine, slow descent, like the air itself has become wet and is settling.

It lasts for an hour. It stops. The air thins. The sun, which has been behind nothing, reappears as if it was always there.

In the grooves, the water rises slightly. In the basins, the sheen becomes a film. The roots of the tree-things darken and you can see the dark water moving through their translucent bases a little faster.

Condensation is the other source. At night — and there is night here, the light is normal, the sky cycles — the temperature drops further and every surface collects a thin layer of frost that melts into water as the light returns. The moss-things seem to drink it directly. Their breathing quickens. You can see the moisture disappearing into them.

Nothing here is dry. Nothing here is flooded. Everything is in the middle state, the patient state, the state of being damp and staying damp. The stone does not dry. The roots do not dry. The air does not dry. It is a landscape that has chosen its level of wetness and committed to it absolutely.

## V. The City

There are buildings. Or there were. Or there are structures that are still, technically, buildings, though they have been incorporated into the landscape so thoroughly that calling them buildings feels like calling a skeleton a person.

They are made of the same stone as the plateau. Not cut from it — grown from it, or perhaps *returned* to it. The walls rise in geometric patterns that are still visible — straight lines, right angles, arches — but the surfaces have eroded to smoothness, and the green has grown over them, and the roots have grown through them, and in many places the wall and the stone beneath it are indistinguishable.

There are streets. You can walk them. They are narrow and they connect in a grid that suggests planning, intention, a civic order. But the grid is incomplete. It starts and stops. Some streets lead to walls. Some lead to basins of water. Some lead off the edge of the built area into bare stone and then just continue as grooves in the rock, as if the street always wanted to be a groove and eventually got its wish.

The buildings are not empty. They contain things. Not people — there are no people here, have not been for a long time — but *things*. Tools. Objects. You find them in the rooms, sitting where they were left. Stone vessels. Cordage made from the grass-things, still intact, still flexible. Implements of bone or wood — hard to tell which, they have been here so long. Nothing metal. Nothing glass. Nothing that requires fire or forge.

The technology is hand-tools. Everything is made by hand from what the plateau provides. The stone vessels are carved with patterns that match the natural grooves in the rock. The cordage is woven in patterns that match the branching of the tree-things. There is a continuity between the made world and the given world that is so complete it becomes difficult to tell which came first.

One building — larger than the others, central — contains a series of basins carved into the floor. Water from one of the grooves flows through them. The water is clear and cold and moves with the same slowness as everything else. The basins are arranged in a line, and the water flows from one to the next, and in each basin something grows. The moss-things, but different — cultivated, shaped, each basin holding a different variant. Someone was growing things here. Someone was selecting and breeding. The moss-things in the basins breathe at a different rhythm than the ones outside. Faster. More purposeful.

The city is not ruined. Ruin implies collapse, violence, a failure of structure. This is not that. The buildings are settling. They are becoming the plateau. The process is slow and continuous and nobody is fighting it. The walls will be stone again. The streets will be grooves again. The basins will be basins. The cycle is completing, and the green is the intermediary, the agent of return, the thing that bridges the gap between what was built and what was always here.

## VI. The Quiet

The sound is the most difficult part.

There is wind. It moves across the plateau in long, slow passages. You feel it before you hear it, and you hear it only as a change in the quality of silence — a lower register, a deepening. It does not howl. It does not whistle. It passes.

The water makes no sound. The threads in the grooves are too slow. You can see them moving, but the movement is so gradual that your eyes lose track. There is no splash, no trickle, no rush.

The green is silent. The tree-things do not creak. Their wood is flexible, yielding. The moss-things make a soft sound when they breathe — a faint exhalation, almost sub-audible, like a sigh from a sleeping room. When you are near a large patch, you can hear it. When you are far, you cannot.

Your own sounds are strange. Footsteps on stone echo, but the echoes come back wrong. Not delayed — *different*. A footstep returns as something that is almost a footstep but has a second sound in it, a softer sound, as if your foot landed twice. Speaking aloud is worse. Words come back with extra syllables. You learn to be quiet. The plateau prefers it.

There is a sound that has no source. It is very low and very far away and it never stops. You feel it more than hear it. It is in the stone, in the soles of your feet, in your chest when you lie down. It might be the wind. It might be the water moving deep in the rock. It might be the plateau itself, the whole vast thing, resonating at a frequency below hearing, a note that has been sounding since before the city was built, since before the green grew, since before the stone was stone.

You stop trying to identify it. It is there. It does not require your understanding.

## VII. The Wrongness

And then there is what is wrong.

It starts small. You notice that the horizon, which was blue and slightly shimmering, is not always in the same place. It moves. Not quickly — over the course of hours. You walk toward it and it does not get closer. You walk away and it follows. You realize, after a day, that the horizon is not the edge of the plateau. The plateau has no edge. Or if it does, the edge is not where the sky meets the ground.

The sky is wrong too. It is the right color. The light is normal. But the sun — if that is the sun — does not move the way a sun should. It does not arc. It drifts. It occupies different positions in the sky at different times, but the positions are not sequential. It is on the left in the morning and on the right at midday and below the horizon — briefly, a flash of light from underneath — and then on the left again. Night comes, but the stars are not fixed. They rearrange. Not randomly — in patterns that you almost recognize, that your mind tries to read as constellations, that resolve for a moment into something meaningful and then dissolve.

The stone has marks on it. Not the grooves — other marks. They appear in the bare stone, in the flat areas between basins, in the streets of the city. They are shallow and they are old. They are geometric. Circles within circles. Lines that intersect at angles that should be right angles but are slightly off — off by a degree, by two degrees, by amounts that seem intentional. The marks are not carved. They are *absences*. Places where the stone is not. Places where the stone was never. As if the stone formed around them, grew up to their edges and stopped.

The tree-things are not always in the same place. You are certain of this. You walk through a grove in the morning and count eleven trunks. You walk back in the afternoon and count thirteen. You do not know which two are new. They all look the same age. You touch each one. They are all warm. They are all real.

Time is the wrongness that contains all the other wrongnesses. You cannot tell how long you have been here. Your body tells you one thing — you are not tired, you are not hungry, you do not need to sleep — and the plateau tells you another. The light cycles are not regular. The dampness comes and goes without a pattern. The moss-things breathe at a pace that does not correspond to any clock you carry inside yourself. You walk for what feels like an hour and find that the light has changed by half a day. You sit for what feels like a few minutes and find that a pool of water has formed around you, has risen, has begun to cover your boots.

You are not afraid. This is the strangest part. The wrongness does not produce fear. It produces a kind of acceptance, a loosening. The plateau is old. It is older than the city, older than the green, older than the stone. It has reached a state where the rules it follows are not the rules you know, and it has been in this state for so long that the state is stable. It is not breaking down. It is not failing. It is simply operating at a level of age where reality has worn thin, has become flexible, has settled into a final form that does not require consistency.

The city-builders knew this. You think this, standing in the room with the water basins, looking at the cultivated moss. They did not build here despite the wrongness. They built here because of it. The plateau was already old when they arrived. They came to the place where things had stopped being strictly true and they built a city of stone and they grew things in basins and they made tools by hand and they lived in the space between the rules, in the damp, in the quiet, in the green.

And then they left, or became the green, or settled into the stone. And the plateau continued. And the moss-things breathed. And the water moved through the grooves at a pace too slow to hear. And the horizon moved, and the sun drifted, and the marks in the stone remained where they had always not been.

## VIII. What Remains

You are standing in the city. The light is normal. The air is damp. A moss-thing near your foot breathes in, breathes out. Your breathing matches it. You have been here for a long time or a short time. You cannot say which.

There is a tool in your hand. You do not remember picking it up. It is stone, carved with a pattern that matches the grooves. It fits your palm. It is warm.

Ahead of you, the streets continue into the green, and the green continues into the stone, and the stone continues into the haze that is not quite horizon and not quite sky and not quite anything you can name.

The plateau breathes. You breathe with it.

You walk on.
