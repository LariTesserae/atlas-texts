# The Weeping Expanse

The first thing is the sound. Not a sound — *the* sound. A continent-wide harmonic drone pressed from ten million sources, each too small to hear alone, all of them weeping.

Water doesn't fall here. It *emerges*.

---

The ground is not ground in any sense that holds. It is a laminate — millennia of biological deposition compressed into something between peat and stone and glass. Old. Unimaginably old. The erosion value reads 2.8 and it means this: the land has been wearing down since before the concept of land had settled on anything. Mountains that existed here were mountains once, then hills, then ridges, then wrinkles, then suggestions, then memory. Now there is only the Expanse — flat as a held breath, running to every horizon, and every square meter of it is *wet*.

Not wet with puddles. Not wet with marsh. Wet the way a sponge is wet that has been sitting in a basin for a thousand years — saturated past the point of absorption, the water no longer *in* the material but * synonymous with it*. You step and the surface gives, not collapsing but yielding, and water rises around your foot not from below but from *the compression itself* — the ground expressing its moisture the way a body expresses blood from a wound. Step off and the footprint fills. Not slowly. The water is right there. It was always right there.

The rock weeps. This is literal. Stone surfaces across the Expanse — and there are stone surfaces, tabular slabs of something like chert but softer, more organic, pushed up by processes too slow to witness — run with continuous films of water. Not rain. Not runoff. *Condensation*. The atmosphere here is so close to its dew point that the temperature differential between air and stone is enough to wring moisture from nothing. The rock doesn't collect water. It *generates* it. Every exposed face is a membrane. The water sheets down in films thinner than paper, converging into threads that converge into rivulets that converge into channels that converge into the broad, shallow, almost-stagnant rivers that vein the entire Expanse in a dendritic pattern so fine it looks like a lung viewed from inside.

The rivers don't flow. They *breathe*. The Expanse's weather — and it has its own weather, continental, self-sustaining, a closed system that has been cycling since the age of its making — pushes and pulls them. Atmospheric pressure shifts across thousands of kilometers cause the water to surge inland and then retreat, surge and retreat, a tidal respiration that has nothing to do with tides. The water moves at the speed of weather. Sometimes you can watch a river reverse — the whole broad shallow sheet of it sliding backward over its own bed, the direction of flow dictated by pressure gradients too large to see and too present to deny.

---

The vegetation is sparse. But *sparse* here doesn't mean absent. It means *chosen*.

There are no trees. Nothing vertical. Nothing that rises more than a meter from the surface, and even that is generous. What grows here grows *flat*. Growth forms that would be aberrant elsewhere are the only ones that survive — prostrate, repent, creeping, *pressed*. The dominant form is something you'd call a moss if you had to, but it isn't moss. It's older. Its lineage doesn't connect to anything in the taxonomy you know. It spreads in contiguous sheets across the wet ground, silver-green where the light reaches it, black-green where it doesn't, and it doesn't root. It *adheres*. A molecular bond with the substrate. Peel it and it comes up like a skin graft, leaving a wet wound in the surface beneath.

It isn't alone. There are other forms — hollow tubes, finger-thick, translucent, rising from the wet substrate like straw in a field. They are not plants. They are not animals. They are structures built by something — a process, an organism, a *tendency* — and they function as the Expanse's respiratory system. Every tube exhales vapor. Every tube inhales atmospheric water. The collective effect is the humidity. The tubes *make the air wet*. They are the reason the rock weeps. They are the reason the ground is saturated. They are the reason the sky is so close.

The sky. Dim. Light reads 1.4 and it manifests as a permanent state of diffuse, sourceless illumination — not a sun behind clouds, not a moon glow, but a *general radiance* that seems to come from the atmosphere itself. The water vapor in the air is so dense that it scatters light isotropically. There is no shadow on the Expanse. Nothing casts one. The light comes from every direction simultaneously, a flattening wash that makes distance impossible to judge. You see something on the horizon — and you can't tell if it's a kilometer away or fifty. The haze thickens with distance until it becomes a wall of pearl-grey nothing. The horizon isn't a line. It's a *dissolution*.

And the sound. The sound from the tubes.

---

Each tube produces a tone. A single, sustained, un varying tone determined by its length, its diameter, the thickness of its wall, the volume of vapor passing through it. One tube alone would be below the threshold of hearing. But there are not one tube. There are *millions*. Tens of millions. Hundreds of millions, across the full continental span of the Expanse, each one singing its note into the wet air, and the air — dense, saturated, acoustically *thick* — carries every frequency. The sound doesn't travel. It *accumulates*.

The drone is not uniform. It shifts. Pressure changes alter the flow through the tubes, raising some pitches, lowering others, and the harmonic structure of the Expanse's voice changes with the weather. When the atmospheric pressure drops — the rivers surge inland, the ground weeps harder, the stone films thicken — the drone deepens, and you feel it in your chest before you hear it with your ears. It sits on you like weight. When the pressure rises, the pitch climbs, and the sound becomes something almost like a voice, almost like a word, almost like something trying to be said by a mouth that doesn't exist.

The sound is deafening. Not in volume — though it is loud, conversation-ending, thought-scattering loud. But in *presence*. The sound is so constant, so total, so inescapable that it stops being sound and becomes *environment*. You stop hearing it the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. It becomes the texture of being here. You swim in it. You breathe it. It enters the spaces between your thoughts and fills them. After an hour, silence would feel like a wound.

---

The weirdness is in the light.

No — the weirdness is in what the light *does*. And what the light does is *not stay consistent*.

The Expanse has weather of its own, and its weather is not the weather of a world with a sun and seasons and predictable fronts. Its weather is *turbulent* in the mathematical sense — a chaotic system, sensitive to initial conditions, capable of producing structures that appear designed but are not. The atmospheric humidity oscillates. The temperature — warm, steady, unremarkable in its averages — *flickers*. Not by much. A degree. Half a degree. But the Expanse's atmospheric system is balanced so precisely on the edge of condensation that half a degree is enough to tip the entire sky from *almost-raining* to *raining everywhere simultaneously and then back again in the time it takes to notice.

And when the light shifts — when the diffuse radiance changes character, which it does without warning, without pattern, without any visible cause — the Expanse *responds*. The tube-fields change pitch. The moss-skin changes color. The water on the stone surfaces refracts differently. And you see things.

Not hallucinations. Not visions. *Refractions*. The water layering the stone acts as a lens when the light hits it at certain angles, and certain angles are produced by certain atmospheric states, and certain atmospheric states are produced by the turbulence, and the turbulence is *chaotic*, which means it produces states that have occurred before and states that have not and states that *cannot occur again*. Through the water-film lenses, you see the ground beneath you — not as it is but as it *was*. Or as it *will be*. Or as it *might have been*. The images are not clear. They are not informative. They are wet, smeared, dreamlike — the Expanse looking at itself through its own condensation, seeing its own past, its own potential, its own可能性, and showing it to anyone standing on its surface at the moment the light aligns.

You look down. Through the water film on a stone slab, you see the mountains that were here. They are enormous. They are black. They are already eroding. You blink and the image is gone. You are looking at flat wet stone. The water sheets down. The drone continues.

You look again, elsewhere, and see something else — a structure. Small, low, built. Not imagined. *Real*, or once real. The built parameter reads 0.8 and it means this: the Expanse has been walked on before. Something has been here. Something has made marks. The structures are few and far between and they are so old they have been absorbed by the Expanse's surface — incorporated into the laminate, fossilized in the wet compression of the substrate. They surface sometimes. A wall-edge, flush with the ground. A circle of stones, each one weeping. A pit, perfectly round, filled with still water that reflects the dim sky with a clarity nothing else here possesses. The structures are not ruins. Ruins imply a fall. These are *sediments*. The structures have been laid down by time, the way silt is laid down, and they remain in the strata the way fossils remain — impression, trace, the ghost of a shape in a medium that no longer remembers what the shape was for.

But in the water-film refractions, you see them *occupied*. You see movement. You see — not clearly, never clearly — forms going about the business of a life you cannot reconstruct. The turbulence-driven lenses show you time-slips, each one unique, each one governed by the chaos of the atmosphere, and you cannot predict what you will see or when. You can only look. And the Expanse looks back.

---

The fauna is rare. 1.4. This is not because the Expanse is hostile — it is, but it's hostile the way a river is hostile, by being *monolithic*. The fauna that exists here exists in the cracks, in the margins, in the few places where the system's totalizing grip falters. There are organisms in the rivers — flat, eyeless, slow, moving with the tidal breath of the water, feeding on the particulate matter the rivers carry. There are organisms in the moss-skin — too small to see, but you can feel them. The moss is warmer than the substrate beneath it. The warmth is metabolic. Something is *digesting* in there. And there are organisms in the tubes.

The tubes are not empty. Something lives in them. You can hear it — not in the drone, which is purely physical, purely acoustic, purely the resonance of vapor through hollow columns — but in the *interruptions* to the drone. A catch. A stutter. A momentary shift in pitch that doesn't correspond to any atmospheric change. Something inside the tube is altering its flow. Something is *inside*. You can't see in. The tubes are too narrow, their walls too opaque. But if you put your hand over one — and you do, because the alternative is not knowing — you feel exhalation, warm and wet and rhythmic, and against your palm, something soft. Something that presses back.

You pull your hand away.

The tube resumes its note.

---

The dynamic is 3.0 — turbulent, chaotic, a system in permanent energetic flux — and it manifests as *restlessness*. The Expanse never stops. The rivers breathe. The tubes sing. The light shifts. The ground yields and compresses and expresses and absorbs. The water condenses and sheets and pools and evaporates and condenses again. The moss adheres and grows and dies and is subsumed and grows again on top of its own remains. The turbulence drives all of it — an engine with no fuel, a cycle with no beginning, a process that has been running since the Expanse was young and will run until the substrate finally wears away to nothing and the tubes fall and the rivers spread into the sea that was always underneath everything, the sea that the mountains rose from and returned to, the sea that is the Expanse's end-state, its final form, its 2.8 erosion made literal — everything flat, everything dissolved, everything returned to water.

But that end is not soon. The Expanse has been here for longer than the structures have been ruins. It will be here after you leave. It was here before whatever made the structures arrived. It does not care about you. It is not indifferent — indifference implies a perspective. The Expanse has no perspective. It is a *process*. It does what it does. The water emerges. The tubes sing. The light shifts and the stone shows you what it remembers and the ground takes your weight and gives it back and the drone — the drone, the continent-wide, deafening, harmonic, unvarying, ever-changing drone — fills every space, every silence, every gap between one moment and the next, and the Expanse continues, and continues, and continues.

You walk. There is no path. There is no destination. There is the flat wet ground in every direction and the dim sky above it and the sound, the sound, the sound, and the water rising around your feet with every step, and the certainty — growing, unavoidable — that the Expanse knows exactly what it is doing, and what it is doing is *eroding*, and you are here, and you are also eroding, and the water is happy to help.

The drone shifts.

You look down.

The water film on the stone shows you your own face.

You are not sure it's your face *now*.

---

The Expanse is a wet, old, flat, vast, packed place. It is almost nothing — almost no structures, almost no tools, almost no animals, almost no light. It is almost empty. And the "almost" is what fills it. The almost-rain. The almost-shadow. The almost-silence. The almost-life in the tubes. The almost-image in the water. The Expanse is a landscape made of *almost*, and the almost is deafening, and the almost is *everything*, and the water rises around your feet, and you walk, and there is no end to it.
