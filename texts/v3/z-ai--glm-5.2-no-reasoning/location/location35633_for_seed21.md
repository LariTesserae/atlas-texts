# The Weeping Platform

## I.

You come upon it from below, which is the only way anyone comes. A climb up through broken stone — basalt still sharp-edged, unrounded by time because there hasn't been enough time. The stone under your hands is warm. Not sun-warm. Metabolically warm, the way a living body is warm, and when you press your palm flat against a clean fracture face you can feel a rhythm so slow it barely qualifies as rhythm. One pulse. A long wait. Another pulse. The mountain is not a mountain. It is also not *not* a mountain. It is a thing that is doing a job, and the job has something to do with water.

The Platform — locals don't have a word for it because locals don't exist, but the survey markers, five of them, each a small threaded rod of synthetic sapphire driven into the stone at the cardinal points and one at center, all carry the same engraved designation: **P-7, WEEPING, ACTIVE** — the Platform sits at roughly 2,400 meters where the stone shoulders open into a shallow bowl, and the bowl is the place.

It is not large. Maybe eighty meters across at its widest. You could walk its circumference in four minutes. You could know it entirely in an afternoon, and that intimacy is part of what makes it strange. It has the scale of a cathedral but the shape of a wound — an oval depression, three meters deep at center, where the rock has been removed rather than carved. The cuts are precise. Not machined-precise. *Tissue*-precise. The angles of removal follow grain lines in the basalt that you cannot see but can sense in the way the remaining surfaces weep.

Because everything here weeps.

## II.

The rock faces cry. Not metaphorically. Water emerges from the stone at every vertical surface — from the bowl's inner walls, from the exposed faces of the climb, from the low outcrops that ring the Platform's rim. It does not rush. It does not drip with any urgency. It films the stone in a layer so thin it is nearly optical, catching light and releasing it, and then it collects into threads that descend with a patience that makes waiting feel like a verb. You can watch a single thread travel two meters down a rock face over the course of a minute. It will join another thread. The combined thread will move faster. This is the only place where the physics of surface tension seems to be performing — not broken, but *demonstrated*, as if the stone were a teaching surface and the water a medium for showing what adhesion and cohesion look like when they are given time and silence.

The water is warm. 38°C, approximately. You know this because when you kneel and touch a collecting pool at the bowl's base — a pool no larger than a bath, clear, mineral-tasting, faintly alkaline — the warmth is immediate and animal. Not hot-spring sulfurous. Clean. Like touching the inside of a wrist.

The air above the Platform holds this warmth and this water. It is not fog. It is not mist. It is a denseness — a visible thickening that does not obscure but saturates, so that distances read differently than they should. The far rim of the bowl, forty meters away, looks as detailed as something ten meters away. The near stone, one meter away, looks as detailed as something *inside* you. Light passes through the wet air and arrives carrying more information than light should carry. You can see the grain of the rock. You can see the individual filaments of moss. You can see, or you believe you can see, the pores in your own knuckles when you hold your hand up.

It is hot. The heat is the constant. Not desert heat, not solar heat — a heat that comes from below, from the stone, from whatever the stone is doing with the water. It is the kind of heat that makes you sweat within minutes and then forget you are sweating because the air is so wet that the sweat cannot evaporate. It sits on your skin like the water sits on the stone. You become part of the weeping.

## III.

The vegetation is what tells you this place is young.

Not sparse, exactly. But particular. There are no trees. There are no shrubs. There is moss — a single species, or what appears to be a single species — that grows in the seams where water flows. It is a pale moss, almost white, almost translucent, and it grows in a pattern that does not follow the patterns of any moss you have seen. It does not spread in circular colonies. It grows in lines. It follows the water threads down the rock faces, tracing the same paths the water traces, and where two water threads join, the moss threads join too, so that the rock is mapped in living filaments that exactly overlay its hydrology. You get the sense that the moss is not growing *near* the water. It is growing *as* the water. Or the water is growing as the moss. The distinction is not available.

In the collecting pool at the bowl's center, there is a single plant. It has no leaves. It has a stem, translucent, the width of a finger, and it rises thirty centimeters above the water surface and terminates in a structure you cannot quite call a flower. It is a node. A small, dense, geometric node — like a tetrahedron folded from wet paper — and it is warm to the touch. Warmer than the water, warmer than the air. When you hold your hand near it, your palm tingles. When you pull your hand away, the tingling stops. The node is doing something with heat. Concentrating it. Holding it. You don't know what for.

There are no other plants. No grass. No lichen on the higher rocks. The bare stone is bare not because it is hostile but because it is new, and what grows here grows only where the water has already written the instructions.

## IV.

The built elements are minor. Five survey markers. A single low platform of a material you cannot identify — not metal, not ceramic, not polymer — set into the stone at the bowl's southern edge, roughly a meter square, raised two centimeters, with a surface texture that feels like skin under a microscope. You find this by almost stepping on it. When you do step on it, it gives, very slightly, like the pad of a thumb. You step off. It recovers. You step on it again. It gives again. It is warm.

Near the western rim, someone has left — or something has deposited — a collection of small objects on a flat stone. Seven of them. Each is the size of a grape seed. Each is a different color, but none of the colors are colors you can name. Not in the ordinary spectrum. They read as colors only in certain angles of light, and the light here, filtered through the wet air, is the kind of light that produces those angles often. You pick one up. It is weightless. Not light — weightless. You put it back. You do not pick up another.

The survey markers are the clearest sign of prior human attention. The sapphire rods are embedded to a depth that suggests drilling, and the engraving on each is precise to a tolerance that implies tools you do not own. But the markers are not old. The sapphire is clean. The threads are sharp. Whoever placed them was here recently — recently in the geological sense, which might mean last year, which might mean three hundred years ago. The stone does not weather here. Things placed on it stay placed on it.

## V.

It is quiet. The quietness is the first thing you notice after the heat and the water, and it is the thing that stays with you.

The water does not make sound. This is wrong. You know it is wrong. Water moving down rock should make sound — should tap, should plink, should whisper. This water moves in silence. The collecting pool is silent. The air is silent. Your footsteps on the stone make a sound — a flat, dry, unremarkable sound — but the water that your footsteps displace makes no sound, and the air that your footsteps disturb makes no sound, and within a minute of arriving you begin to feel that your own sounds are intrusive. You walk more quietly. You breathe more quietly. The place is not asking you to be quiet. The place is simply quiet, and your noise becomes inappropriate in the way that shouting in a library is inappropriate — not forbidden, just wrong.

There is one sound. It comes from the central node, the translucent plant in the pool, and it is not a sound you hear with your ears. You hear it in your sternum. A low, periodic thrum. Once every forty seconds. It corresponds to nothing visible — not the water, not the air, not the stone's pulse. It is its own rhythm. When you lean close to the node, the thrum becomes a tone. Not a pitch. A tone — the way a bell has a tone, the way a struck glass has a tone, the way a held note has a tone. It is the only music this place makes, and it is barely music, and it is enough.

## VI.

The animals, if they are animals, are rare. You see one thing living here that is not moss, not the node-plant, not you. It moves along the bowl's rim at dusk — the light here at dusk is ordinary, golden, unremarkable, and this ordinariness is almost a relief after the hyper-clarity of the daytime wet air. The thing is the size of a hand. It has too many legs. Not a centipede's too-many — a different kind of too-many, legs that are placed in a way that suggests the number was *chosen* rather than evolved. It moves with a smoothness that the leg count makes possible but that still looks impossible. It is drinking from a water thread on the rim stone. It drinks for a long time. It does not notice you, or it notices you and does not care, or it notices you and considers you the same category of thing as the stone and the water and the moss — present, relevant only insofar as you are also part of the system. It finishes drinking. It goes. You do not see where it goes. The rim stone is bare. You touch the place where it drank. The water there is fractionally warmer.

## VII.

What you come to understand, over hours, is that this place is a *component*. Not a habitat. Not a monument. A working part of something larger, and it is doing its work right now. The water emerges from the stone warm and mineralized and flows down the bowl's inner walls in threads that the moss traces, and it collects in the pool where the node-plant sits, and the node-plant concentrates heat — and perhaps something else, something in the water, something the water has picked up from whatever is below the stone — and the concentrated whatever-it-is enters the air. The wet air carries it. The air moves. You can feel it move — a slow exhalation from the bowl's center outward, up and over the rim, dispersing into the larger atmosphere. The Platform is breathing out. It has been breathing out since before you arrived. It will breathe out after you leave. The rhythm of the node is the rhythm of the breath, and the breath is the work, and the work is the point.

You do not know what the breath carries. You do not know where it goes. You do not know what the system is, or what the system is for, or who designed it, or whether "designed" is even the right word for something that grows basalt and weeps warm water and grows moss in the shape of its own hydrology and produces a heat-concentrating node that breathes minerals into the air on a forty-second cycle. You know only that it is functioning. The survey markers say ACTIVE. The stone says ACTIVE. The water says ACTIVE. The moss says ACTIVE. The node says ACTIVE in a tone you feel in your sternum.

## VIII.

You leave. You go back down the warm broken stone. Behind you, the Platform weeps and breathes and the moss traces the water and the node hums and the multilegged thing drinks and the air carries whatever it carries to wherever it goes. You go. The Platform stays. It does not need you. It was working before you came. It will work after you leave. It is not for you.

But your hands smell like the water when you reach the bottom — faintly alkaline, faintly warm, faintly mineral. The smell lasts three days. You do not wash it off. You could. You don't.
