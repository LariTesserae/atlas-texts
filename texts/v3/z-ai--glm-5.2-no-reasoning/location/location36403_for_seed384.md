# The Drowned Switchyard

The water moves at the pace of a slow exhale.

There is no rush here. Nothing has rushed in a very long time.

---

You enter through a hatch that no longer seals — the gasket gave up its rubber soul decades ago, became something closer to chalk, then powder, then memory of a seal. The hatch itself is green-steel diamond plate, the kind with the raised pattern that once kept boots from slipping on oil-slicked surfaces. Now it keeps nothing. It hangs open at a forty-degree angle, half-buried in silt that has accumulated on the landing below like a soft, gray-brown snowfall.

The landing is a platform. Maybe three meters by four. A maintenance alcove at the bottom of an access shaft. The shaft rises above you — you can feel it more than see it, a column of slightly less-opaque water extending upward into a darkness that does not end so much as lose interest in describing itself. Below the platform, the shaft continues down. You have no way of knowing how far. The silt gives no indication of a floor beneath it. It is just a softness that begins where the platform ends.

The water is cold. Not the sharp, immediate cold of something that has recently been chilled — not ice water, not snowmelt. This is a settled cold. A patient cold. It has been this temperature for longer than most things have been anything, and it has no intention of changing. It sits at roughly four degrees Celsius, the density maximum, the temperature water finds when it has been left alone long enough to decide for itself. You feel it in your teeth first, then your joints, then everywhere, and then you stop feeling it because the body gives up on complaint when it becomes clear that no one is listening.

---

The switchyard is a room. Or it was a room. The distinction matters less than you'd think.

Before the water — and there was a before, you can feel it in the geometry, in the way the space was organized for human bodies moving on horizontal surfaces — this was an electrical distribution chamber. Not a power plant. A node. A place where current was received, divided, redirected, sent on its way to places that needed it more. You can see the ghost of this function in what remains.

The switchgear cabinets line the walls in a horseshoe. Eight of them. Maybe nine — the far corner is lost in a haze of particulate that the water holds in suspension, a permanent fog of mineral and rust and something organic, something that might be bacterial, might be something else entirely, something that has found a niche in the chemistry of this place and filled it without asking permission. The cabinets are steel, painted gray, and the paint has done something strange. It has bubbled. Not uniformly — not the predictable blistering of moisture intrusion. The bubbles follow patterns. Lines that almost trace the outlines of the buswork behind the paint, as though the electrical pathways imprinted themselves on the surface coating over years of current passing through, leaving a kind of shadow-graph in the paint's memory. The largest cabinet — the one directly ahead, the one that would have been the main breaker — has a pattern across its face that looks, from a distance, like a city seen from above at night. Streets and blocks and little clusters of light. Up close, the bubbles are just bubbles. But from the landing, with what little light reaches this deep, the illusion is convincing enough to make you pause.

The cabinets are not empty. This is important. The buswork is still inside — copper bars, flat and thick, bolted to porcelain insulators that have darkened to the color of old teeth. The copper itself has oxidized to a green so deep it reads as black in this light, but when you move your light across the seams of the cabinet doors, you can see the green catching, brightening for a moment, a flash of color that exists nowhere else in this space. The connections are still sound. Or rather, they are still physically connected — the bolts are in place, the bars touch where they are meant to touch, and where they are not meant to touch, they are separated by insulators or by air gaps that are now, of course, water gaps. Whether the connections are electrically sound is a different question and one that, in this environment, has a complicated answer.

---

There is current down here. Not much. Not the way the engineers intended. But something.

The water itself carries a charge — faint, intermittent, a ghost of conductivity that comes from the dissolved minerals in the water rather than from any functioning generator. But there are also batteries. Or what were batteries. In the second cabinet from the left, a bank of industrial lead-acid cells sits in a rack, and while most of them have cracked and leaked their sulfuric acid into the surrounding water — which is how the water got its mineral load, its taste of metal and sharp and old — two of the cells have done something unexpected. They have resealed. The cases, which were a hard rubber compound, have swelled and softened and then, under the pressure of the water at this depth, compressed back against the cracks. Not perfectly. But enough. And the lead plates inside have been interacting with the seawater that has replaced the acid electrolyte, producing a faint, unreliable, deeply strange voltage. Not twelve volts. Not six volts. Something that varies between a few hundred millivolts and, occasionally, a brief spike of two or three volts, when some internal equilibrium shifts and the chemistry decides, for a moment, to try.

This voltage does not power anything. It is not enough. But it travels along the buswork, because the buswork is still connected, and it reaches the other cabinets, and it reaches the transformers, and the transformers — which are oil-filled, and whose oil has not fully escaped, and whose copper windings are still mostly intact — do what transformers do. They change the voltage. They step it up or step it down based on their winding ratios, and the faint, stuttering signal from the two surviving batteries gets transformed and split and sent along pathways that were designed for sixty-hertz alternating current but now carry something else entirely.

If you touch the cabinet faces — and you should not, but if you do — you can feel it. A tingling. Not steady. Not rhythmic. Something more like a pulse that has forgotten its tempo. A stutter in the electromagnetic field, long, long, long, short, long, that almost resolves into a pattern but never does.

---

The room is not large. Cathedral scale, perhaps, if the cathedral were a chapel. The ceiling is a low curve of concrete, the kind poured in sections with steel forms, and you can see the seams between the pours, the lines where one batch of concrete met another and decided, grudgingly, to bond. Stalactites have formed at some of these seams — not the grand limestone formations of cave systems, but small, thin, translucent fingers of mineral deposit, maybe ten centimeters long at most, pale against the dark concrete. They are not still. The water moves through this space — not fast, not visibly, but it moves, and the stalactites flex slightly in the current, bending and recovering, bending and recovering, like the fingers of a sleeping hand.

The floor is buried. Under the silt, there are cable trays, conduit runs, junction boxes, the entire nervous system of an industrial space that has been laid out with the care and precision of a time when electricity was dangerous and respected and routed with intention. The silt has covered all of it to a depth of perhaps thirty centimeters. It is soft. It is light. When you step off the platform — and you will, eventually, because there is nowhere else to go — your feet sink to the ankle, and the silt curls up around your boots like a cat pressing against a leg. It does not want to let go. It is not malicious. It is just that nothing has touched it in a long time, and it has forgotten the difference between holding and clinging.

There is a cable, though, that emerges from the silt in the center of the room. It rises maybe a meter and a half before curving over and drifting, suspended, in the current. It is armored cable — steel braid over rubber insulation over copper — and it has been here long enough that the armor has split in places, and the rubber has bloated, and the copper has turned green, and the whole thing has become a kind of vertical element in a space that otherwise has none. It sways. Not much. A centimeter of movement, maybe two, in each direction, the current pushing it one way and its own weight and buoyancy pulling it back. It has been doing this for years. Decades. It will do it for decades more. It is the closest thing in this room to a living motion.

---

The light — what light there is — comes from two sources.

The first is the access shaft above. A faint, blue-gray cylinder of dimness that descends from the open hatch and reaches, on a good day, the platform and the first meter of the room beyond. It is not enough to see by. It is enough to orient by. It tells you which way is up, and in a space this enclosed, that is not nothing.

The second source is stranger.

The second cabinet from the left — the one with the surviving batteries — has a window. A small window, a rectangular pane of thick glass set into the cabinet door so that an operator could look inside and check the cells without opening the enclosure. The glass has held. It is clouded, filmed with mineral deposits and biological growth, but it has held. And behind the glass, something has happened to the electrolyte.

There is a light. It is very faint. It is not a glow, exactly. It is more like what you would see if you took a bioluminescent organism — a jellyfish, a dinoflagellate, something that makes its own cold light — and diluted it across a liter of water. A pale, blue-white something that exists at the edge of visibility. It pulses with the stutter of the voltage. When the batteries surge to three volts, the light brightens, and you can see the interior of the cabinet: the rack, the cells, the connections, the water inside the water, which is slightly clearer than the water outside. When the voltage drops, the light dims to nothing, and the window becomes just a rectangle of dark, and you are alone again in the dark.

The source of the light is not the batteries. It is not the copper. It is something that has colonized the electrolyte — a microorganism, or a chemical precipitate, or something that does not have a name yet because no one has been here to name it. It responds to current. When current passes through the water in the cabinet, it lights. When the current stops, it stops. It is the only light source in the room that is not the shaft, and it is not reliable, and it is not enough, but it is the only thing in this space that produces something rather than merely persisting.

---

The sound is almost nothing. Almost.

There is the sound of your own movement — the displacement of water, the soft compression of silt, the occasional click of a boot against a buried conduit. There is the sound of your breathing, which in an environment like this, in a helmet or a mask or whatever you are wearing, becomes the loudest thing in the room. You hear yourself. You hear your apparatus. You hear the small, mechanical sounds of your own survival.

Beyond that, there is the sound of the water. Not the sound of water moving — this is not a river, not a tide, not a current with energy behind it. This is the sound of water existing. A low, omnidirectional something that sits below the threshold of hearing and above the threshold of feeling. You sense it in your chest. A pressure that is not the depth, not the cold, but the vibration of a volume of water that is so much larger than you that its internal motion — thermal convection, molecular diffusion, the slow settlement of particulate — registers as a physical presence.

And there is one more sound. It comes from the cabinet with the batteries. When the voltage spikes, when the chemistry decides to try, there is a click. Not a loud click. Not even a quiet click. A click that exists at the boundary between mechanical and electrical, the sound of a contact somewhere in the switchgear engaging for a fraction of a second before the voltage drops and it releases. The click echoes in the cabinet, in the water, in the room, and then it is gone. And then, after a long time — a minute, five minutes, sometimes twenty — it happens again.

Click.

Silence.

Click.

Silence.

Click.

Silence.

The rhythm is not a rhythm. It does not repeat. It does not signal. It is not a message. It is a mechanical reflex, a ghost action, the last surviving habit of a system that was designed to do things and has not yet fully accepted that it no longer does them. But standing in this room, in this water, in this dark, with the silt at your ankles and the cable swaying above you and the blue-white light pulsing and failing and pulsing again behind the clouded glass — standing here, you will find it very difficult not to interpret.

---

There is almost nothing alive here. Almost.

A single organism occupies this space, and it is not the thing in the cabinet. The thing in the cabinet is something else — a chemical curiosity, an edge case, a phenomenon. What lives here, what is actually alive, is smaller and more widespread and less interesting to look at.

A biofilm. A thin, almost transparent layer of biological material that coats every surface — the cabinets, the cable, the concrete, the stalactites, the silt itself. It is not slime. It is not algae. It is a community of microorganisms that have found, in the mineral-rich water and the faint electrical current and the cold and the dark, a niche they can occupy, and they have occupied it completely. It covers everything. It is the reason the silt clings to your boots — the biofilm binds the silt particles together, gives them a slight cohesion, a slight elasticity. It is the reason the stalactites flex rather than break — the biofilm has grown into the mineral matrix and reinforced it, the way rebar reinforces concrete. It is the reason the cable sways so slowly — the biofilm on its surface has changed its hydrodynamic profile, adding a slight resistance to the water that dampens its movement.

The biofilm is gray. It is translucent. It is almost invisible. If you touch a surface — the cabinet face, the cable, the platform railing — you will feel it as a slight slickness, a texture that is not quite wet and not quite dry and not quite anything else. It is the skin of this place. It is the only living thing here, and it is everywhere, and it does not care about you at all.

---

There is a second room. Or rather, there was. A doorway in the south wall — a steel frame, double-wide, designed to admit equipment on pallets or carts — leads to what was once an adjacent space. The door is open. It has been open for a long time. The frame has corroded in such a way that the door, if you tried to close it, would not fit; the rust has built up on the hinges and the threshold until the gap between door and frame is no longer a gap but a warp, and the door rests at an angle against the wall, held there by nothing but the water's buoyancy and its own deformed weight.

Beyond the doorway, the corridor extends for perhaps four meters before it ends. Not in a wall. In a collapse. The concrete ceiling has given way — not recently, not dramatically, but in the slow, inevitable way that concrete gives way when its rebar rusts and expands and the force of expansion exceeds the tensile strength of the surrounding material. The rebar is visible in the collapse, sticking out of the broken concrete like the ribs of something that was once alive, each one a finger's thickness, each one bright orange with corrosion, each one bending downward where the concrete released it and gravity remembered it. Beyond the collapse, there is water. Just water. The same water, the same temperature, the same darkness, but no longer contained, no longer in a room. It extends outward, upward, downward, into the space beyond the collapsed wall, which might be another room, might be open water, might be the interior of a structure that has been here so long it has forgotten its own outlines.

You cannot go through the collapse. The rebar is too dense, the concrete too sharp. You can look through it, though, and what you see is — nothing. Not darkness. Nothing. The absence of reflected light, of particulate, of surface. The water beyond the collapse is clearer than the water in the room, and this clarity is what makes it dark — there is nothing in it to catch the light, nothing to scatter it, nothing to tell you that there is water there rather than a hole in the world.

---

The switchyard is in equilibrium. This is the thing that defines it more than the water, more than the cold, more than the dark.

Nothing here is changing. Not in the way that things change above water — the rust, the decay, the biological succession, the erosion. Those processes happened. They have happened. The rust has formed. The paint has bubbled. The concrete has cracked. The batteries have failed and partially un-failed. The biofilm has colonized. The silt has settled. All of this happened, and now it is done, and what is left is a system in a state of dynamic equilibrium — not static, not dead, but balanced. The water moves through at the rate it moves through. The biofilm grows and dies at the rate it grows and dies. The batteries surge and fail at the rate they surge and fail. The cable sways at the rate the current pushes it. The stalactites flex at the rate the water moves. Everything is in motion. Nothing is going anywhere.

This is what the place feels like. Not abandoned — abandonment implies that someone left, that someone might come back, that the place is waiting. This place is not waiting. It has settled into itself. It has become what it is going to be, and it will be this until something changes — a stronger current, a deeper collapse, a shift in the temperature, a human hand reaching in through the hatch and trying to make it what it was. Until then, it is what it is: a room underwater, cold and dark and quiet, with a switchgear that almost works and a light that almost glows and a sound that almost means something, and a cable that sways, and a floor that holds, and a skin of life so thin you could miss it, and a door that leads nowhere, and a silence that is not silence but is the closest thing to silence that a place with water in it can be.

It is a cathedral for no one. It has no congregation. It has no faith. It has only its own persistence, which is enough, which has always been enough, which will be enough long after the hatch above has sealed itself with silt and the shaft has filled and the last faint click from the last surviving battery has finally, quietly, stopped.
