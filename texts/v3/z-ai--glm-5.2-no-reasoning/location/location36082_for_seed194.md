# The Hollow Beneath the Rootward Quarter

## First impression is sound before place

You know it by the sound before you know it by anything else. A low, arrhythmic thrum — not a single note but a chord made of dozens of sources, each slightly detuned from the others, each breathing at its own pace. The sound enters through bone before it registers as air vibration. It is loud enough that you feel it in your jaw, in the plates of your skull, in the cartilage of your sternum. Not painful. Not aggressive. Simply *present*, the way tides are present. It has been present for longer than anything you have ever encountered has been present at anything.

You are somewhere small. You know this not because you can see the dimensions — you cannot see at all — but because the sound behaves as though it has walls. It reflects. It accumulates in corners. There is a ceiling close enough above that the thrum gains a half-second resonance, a phantom tail. The space is perhaps the size of a modest room. Perhaps smaller. The dark is so total that your eyes produce their own static, their own invented grain, and you cannot distinguish open air from surface six inches from your face.

The floor is warm wood. Not wood as you know it from forests — not the dead, seasoned timber of felled trees — but living wood, still engaged in whatever slow chemistry living wood performs. You can feel it give very slightly under weight. Not spongy. Not rotten. *Resilient*. The way a green branch bends before it breaks. The surface is not smooth. It has grain, ridges, shallow grooves that run in long curves, the growth patterns of something ancient.

## What grows here is everything

The vegetation parameter reads *total*, and this is not a metaphor. There is no substrate that is not plant. The floor is root-wood. The walls — you find them by reaching — are bark, thick and laminated, layered like sh parchment peeled and re-formed over millennia. The ceiling is a mat of root-fiber so dense it feels compressed, felted, the individual threads invisible to touch. Everything you contact is plant material in some stage of its very long life or its very long death.

And the place is *built*. This is the strangeness. The walls are not merely present — they are *constructed*. The bark has been shaped, trimmed, fitted with the care of masonry. The corners are close to square. The ceiling meets the walls at a deliberate angle, not a natural curve. There are joints. There are pegs — wooden pegs, driven through wooden sockets, holding wooden panels to wooden frames. The craftsmanship is patient and imprecise. The pegs are slightly irregular, hand-shaped. The panels do not sit perfectly flush. But the engineering is sound. This room was made, by hands or by hand-analogues, out of the material of the world it sits within, and it was made to last, and it has lasted.

It is a room inside a root mass. A room hollowed from the interior of a root system so vast that its full extent is unimaginable from within. The root belongs to something that grew here when the geography was different — when there was water, perhaps, or when the climate ran warmer — and it has continued its slow metabolism for so long that the surrounding landscape has eroded down around it, flattened by time, reduced to mineral dust and salt, while the root endured. The root *is* the landscape now. The room is embedded within it the way a cyst is embedded within tissue, or the way a heart chamber sits within a body.

## The sound has a source

You crawl. There is no room to stand. The ceiling drops lower as you move toward what you assume is one end of the space, and you realize the chamber is not rectangular but *tapered*, narrowing like the interior of a great pod. The floor's grain runs along the long axis. You follow it.

The sound changes as you move. It does not grow louder or softer — it *shifts*, the way a chord shifts when you walk through a room with multiple speakers. Some frequencies strengthen. Others fade. You are moving through the acoustic field of the space, and the field is complex, shaped by the geometry of the walls and the placement of the sources.

The sources. You find them by touch. Along one wall, set into the bark at intervals of perhaps a hand-span, there are pores. Not knots, not scars. *Pores* — open, smooth-edged, each the width of a finger, each exhaling a column of air that you can feel against your skin. Cool air. And each column carries its own tone, produced by the air moving through a resonant cavity behind the wall. The pores are organ pipes. The root itself is the organ. Air moves through interior channels — hollowed by decay, perhaps, or grown hollow by design — and exits through these pores at pressures and velocities determined by the diameter of the channel and the size of the opening. Each pore sings a different note. The walls of the room are pierced by dozens of them. Together they produce the thrum.

The air is moving because the root is breathing.

Not metaphorically. The root system, vast and ancient, still conducts respiration — not the rapid gas exchange of a living leaf but the slow, deep diffusion of oxygen through woody tissue across concentration gradients so weak they take hours to reverse. The root inhales for half a day. It exhales for half a day. The pores drive their columns of air outward during exhalation. The sound shifts, very gradually, as the pressure changes. A note that is strong now will be barely audible in six hours, and another will have taken its place. The chord *breathes*.

This is the only sound. There is nothing else. No wind. No animals. No water. No creak of settling structures. Just the root, exhaling its long, slow chord into a room that was built to contain and shape that sound.

## The room was built to listen

You find the second feature by collision — your hand strikes something that is not wall. It is a structure. In the dark, you map it by touch: a low bench, or shelf, or *altar*, running along the wall opposite the pores. It is made of the same root-wood, but finished differently — sanded smooth, oiled perhaps, with a faintly waxy residue that coats your fingertips. The top surface is carved.

The carving is regular. Grooves cut into the wood in a pattern — not decorative, or not *merely* decorative. You trace them with your fingers and realize they are *maps*. Not of terrain, but of *sound*. The grooves describe the acoustic shape of the room — the locations of the pores, the angles of reflection off the walls, the nodes and antinodes of the standing waves. Someone mapped the sound. Someone sat here, in this dark, in this cold, and spent what must have been years of patient touch-work carving a representation of what they heard into the surface of a bench.

There are multiple layers of carving. Older grooves, nearly worn smooth, beneath newer ones. The map has been revised many times. This makes sense: the root grows. The pores shift. The channels widen or narrow as the wood expands and contracts over decades. The acoustic geometry is not static. It changes at a pace visible only across years, but it changes. And someone — or several someones, across generations — returned to this bench again and again to update the map.

## Who lived here

The people who built this room are gone. Not recently. The pegs in the walls have the dull polish of extreme age. The carvings on the bench show wear patterns consistent with centuries of handling. The oil on the bench surface is not residue from human hands — it is the root's own sap, weeping slowly through the wood and coating surfaces that once were maintained by their makers. No one has oiled this bench in a very long time.

But they were here. They built with hand tools — no metal, or very little. The pegs show the marks of stone shaping, of grinding and sanding rather than cutting. They were a people who lived *inside* the root. Who found this hollow — or hollowed it — and shaped it into a chamber. Who fitted walls and ceiling from bark stripped from the root's own outer layers. Who built a bench opposite a wall of singing pores and spent long hours — days, years, lives — listening to the sound of the root's respiration and mapping its slow changes.

They left no other trace. No tools. No refuse. No remains. Either the root has absorbed everything organic left behind — and over this timescale, it would have — or they took what they carried with them when they left. The room is clean. The room is *empty*. It contains only itself.

## The cold

The temperature is cool. Not cold enough to harm, but cold enough to notice — the kind of cool that settles into joints after an hour, that makes you aware of your own warmth as a finite resource. The air coming through the pores is cooler than the ambient air in the room, which means the root's interior channels extend deep into cooler strata, perhaps into mineral earth below the salt-flat, into regions where the temperature is stable and low. The room is cooled by the root's own breath. This may have been desirable. In a hotter age, a cool chamber inside a vast root might have been a refuge.

Now it is simply cold. The coolth has no urgency. It will not kill you. It will simply persist, the way everything here persists, at a pace too slow to feel.

## The emptiness has weight

The density parameter reads *empty*, and this is the parameter that matters most. The room is nearly devoid of contents. The bench. The walls. The floor. The pores. That is everything. No clutter. No accumulation. No objects whose purpose might be guessed at. The emptiness is not the emptiness of a place that has been cleared — it is the emptiness of a place that was designed to contain very little. The builders wanted *space*. They wanted a volume of air shaped by wood, filled with sound, and nothing else.

Why build a room for sound and emptiness? The question sits in the dark with you. It is not uncomfortable. The room does not feel like a tomb. It does not feel abandoned in the way that ruins feel abandoned — haunted by the echo of former purpose. It feels *complete*. It feels like it is doing what it was made to do. The sound fills it. The emptiness holds the sound. The bench holds a listener. The listener is not here. The room waits for the listener to return, or for a new one to arrive, with the patience of wood, which is to say with the patience of something that measures time in growth rings.

## The world outside

You cannot see it. You can only infer it. Above the ceiling of this chamber — above the felted root-fiber matting — there is more root. Tons of it. The root system extends for what must be miles in every direction, a flat, spreading network that has survived the erosion of everything around it. Above the root: mineral dust. Salt. The desiccated remnants of a landscape that was once something else and is now, after incomprehensible time, nearly nothing. A flat, cool, dry world with no water, no animals, no visible life except the root, which does not need to be visible because it is *underneath*. The root is the landscape the way a foundation is a building. Everything above it has been worn away. Only it remains.

And somewhere in the root — perhaps many places, perhaps only here — there are rooms. Hollows shaped by hands that have been gone for longer than the rooms have been empty, fitted with bark and pegs and benches, tuned to the respiration of the root that surrounds them. Rooms that are speakers. Rooms that are ears. Rooms that exist only to hold a sound that has been playing since before the builders were born and will continue playing after the root itself finally dies, which it may never do, because nothing is left to kill it.

## What it is like to be here

You sit on the bench. The wood is cold. The grooves of the acoustic map press into your thighs through whatever you are wearing. The dark is absolute. You cannot see your hands. You cannot see the pores in the opposite wall. You know they are there because you can feel the columns of cool air against your face, your chest, and because each one is a voice in the chord that fills the room.

The sound is loud. Loud enough that you stop trying to parse it as music. It is not music. It is the sound of a living process occurring at a scale and pace that dwarfs you. The root is breathing. The root has been breathing for longer than your species has existed. The room was built to let you hear that. The emptiness was built to let the sound exist without interference. The darkness is not an absence of light but a *condition* — the condition under which the sound becomes the entire sensory world.

You sit. The chord shifts, very slightly, over the time you spend there. One frequency strengthens. Another recedes. The root is exhaling. In six hours it will inhale, and the columns will reverse, and the tones will change, and the map on the bench will be, in some minute degree, inaccurate, and someone — not you, probably not anyone for a very long time — will need to come back and carve a new groove.

The room waits. The root breathes. The dark holds them both.
