# The Congregation of Still Water

## First Approach

You hear it before you see it. Not a sound — a pressure. A single sustained tone that has no beginning and apparently no end, vibrating at a frequency that lives in the jaw and the sternum before it reaches the ears. It is not loud in the way a crash is loud. It is loud in the way the ocean is loud: total, undivided, so constant it becomes a kind of silence of its own. The body adapts within minutes. Conversation would be impossible here. Not difficult — impossible. The air itself hums with it.

The tone comes from below.

---

## The Terrace of Weeping Stone

The city — if that word still applies — descends in vast, curving terraces cut into the face of an escarpment so tall its upper reaches vanish into a permanent white haze of moisture and heat. The stone is not ordinary stone. It weeps. From every horizontal surface, from every seam between blocks, from the crowns of carved arches and the lips of drainage channels, water emerges. Not in flows or streams — in a constant, slow, patient secretion, as though the rock itself were a sponge compressed just below its saturation point. The water appears, gathers, beads, trails, joins other trails, finds the grooves cut for it centuries ago, and descends to the next terrace, where the process repeats. The entire city is a cascade. Not a dramatic cascade. A slow one. The slowest imaginable. Water moving with the patience of geology.

The stone is pale. Not white — pale. The color of old bone, of cream left too long in sun, of salt flats seen through humid air. It darkens where the water touches it, deepening to a grey-green that is almost black in the crevices. Lichen — or something that behaves exactly like lichen — maps the wet surfaces in intricate, maplike patterns: coastlines and archipelagos of pale cyan and mineral blue, spreading in fractal edges where the water's flow is most consistent. The patterns never change. They have looked this way for a very long time.

The terraces are broad. Each one is wide enough to hold a neighborhood, and many of them do. The architecture is dense, layered, constructed with an obsessive understanding of how water moves and how stone endures. Buildings are built into the cliff face as much as on top of it. Their walls are the same pale stone, cut with channels, their roofs angled not to shed rain — there is no rain, not exactly — but to catch the condensation that drips from the overhanging rock above and redirect it into cisterns that are built into the very bones of the structures. Windows are narrow. Doorways are deep. The interiors are cool and dim and constantly, gently wet, their walls slick with a sheen of moisture that never fully evaporates because the air is always so close to its dew point.

It is hot. The heat sits on everything like a weight. Not the dry heat of a desert — a wet heat, a heat that carries its own humidity, that makes the air feel substantial, almost drinkable. Breathing it is like breathing warm cloth. Sweat does not evaporate. It joins the general moisture, runs down skin the way water runs down the stone, following the same laws. The heat is not punishing. It is simply present. Total. It has been this temperature for as long as anyone can remember, which is longer than anyone expects.

## The Absence of Animals

There are no animals. This is not something you notice at first, because the place is so full — so dense with water, with growth, with sound, with the architecture itself — that the absence seems to leave no gap. But it does, once you look. No birds cross the haze above. No insects crawl on the wet stone. No fish move in the channels where the water gathers before it falls to the next level. The cisterns are clear and still and contain nothing but water and the pale reflection of the sky.

The ecology is entirely botanical. And it is, in its way, complete.

---

## The Garden That Is a City That Is a Garden

The vegetation is extraordinary. It has to be, because there are no animals to carry seeds, no pollinators, no grazers to shape it. What grows here grows by water alone, and what reproduces does so by the mechanisms available to it: fragmentation, water-dispersal, vegetative spread, the patient physics of roots finding new ground.

Every terrace is a garden. The original builders — whoever they were, whenever they were — designed the city to hold plants as much as people, and the plants have taken this invitation and exceeded it. The retaining walls of each terrace are planted with hanging growths: long, trailing curtains of a plant that has no common name, something between a fern and a moss, with leaves like dark green glass, translucent, heavy with the water that clings to them. They hang in perfectly still vertical lines because there is no wind. Not here. Not ever. The air moves only with heat, and even that motion is slow, convectional, a gradual rising and falling that stirs nothing.

Larger plants grow in the channels where water flows most steadily. They are not trees, exactly. They have the scale of trees — tall, broad, their trunks as thick as the columns of the buildings — but they are not woody. Their trunks are fibrous, swollen with water, pale green where the bark should be, and they are warm to the touch. Warm the way a living body is warm. They do not photosynthesize in any way that would be recognizable. Their broad, flat leaves are dark and matte, absorbing not just light but something else, something that the air carries in its humidity, some quality of the place that they metabolize and convert into the dense, sweet-smelling tissue that makes up their interior.

Between these columnar plants and the trailing curtains and the lichen on the stone, every surface is occupied. Every wall has something growing on it. Every ledge holds moss. Every crack hosts a root. The density is total. Not chaotic — there is nothing random about any of it — but total. Layer upon layer: stone, lichen, moss, vine, root, trunk, canopy, and above the canopy, the permanent haze of moisture that softens the light that filters down from the invisible summit of the escarpment.

The light is normal. That is the strange thing. For all the haze, the light that reaches the terraces is clear and even, a pale gold that does not flicker or shift. It does not move because the source does not move. There is no visible sun. The sky above the escarpment — what you can see of it through the haze — is a uniform luminous white, neither bright nor dim, casting no shadows that move. The light simply is, the way the temperature simply is, the way the water simply is. Everything here simply is.

## The Inhabitants

There are people here. Or there were. Or there are, in a way that requires the word to be used differently.

They are the still ones.

On the third terrace — the broadest, the one where the water gathers into shallow pools before descending to the fourth — there are figures. Dozens of them. Perhaps hundreds, if you count across all the terraces, all the levels, all the chambers and passages and cistern-halls. They stand. They sit. They lean against walls. They lie in the shallow pools with their faces above the water. They are not dead. They are not sleeping. They are not meditating, because meditation implies a return. They are simply present, the way the stone is present, the way the water is present.

Their skin is the color of the stone. Not because they are made of stone — they are flesh, warm to the touch, soft — but because they have been here so long that they have become part of the place's palette. They are naked or wrapped in garments that have rotted and been replaced by the slow growth of moss, so that some of them appear to be clothed in green velvet. Their eyes are open. They do not blink. They do not need to, because the air is so humid that their eyes never dry.

They are not paralyzed. They can move. You can see it in the stillness: it is the stillness of something that has chosen not to move, or has forgotten that movement was an option, or has moved so far beyond the need for movement that the question no longer applies. Occasionally — and here occasionally means perhaps once in a period of time you cannot measure, because time has become strange — a hand will shift. A finger will extend. A head will turn, very slowly, as though tracking something that is not visible. And then the stillness resumes, and it is as though the motion never happened.

They do not speak. They do not need to. The sound that fills the air — that single, total, bone-deep tone — is their sound. It comes from them the way the water comes from the stone: not as an action but as a condition. They are producing it simply by existing. Their bodies vibrate at the frequency of the place. They have become instruments. They have become part of the mechanism.

---

## The Mechanism

Because this is a mechanism. The city, the terraces, the water, the plants, the still ones — they are all parts of something. You can feel this in the way the elements fit together, in the way the water always finds the channels, in the way the plants grow exactly where the flow is most consistent, in the way the sound never wavers. It is not a machine in any sense that involves tools or technology — there are no tools here, no devices, no evidence that the concept of a tool ever existed in this place. It is a machine in the sense that a body is a machine: integrated, self-regulating, each part serving a function whether or not it understands the function.

The water comes from the stone. The stone comes from the cliff. The cliff is the source. The water flows down through the terraces, through the channels, through the buildings, through the roots of the plants, through the shallow pools where the still ones lie, and at the very bottom — far below, where the terraces end and the landscape opens out — it gathers into a lake. The lake is warm. The lake is still. The lake has no shore, because the ground around it is as wet as the lake itself, a gradual transition from solid to liquid that makes the boundary meaningless. The lake is the reservoir. The lake is where the water waits before it begins its ascent again.

Because the water ascends. This is the part that makes no sense, the part that breaks the world's rules. The water that seeps from the stone on the highest terrace cannot be coming from rain, because there is no rain. It cannot be coming from snowmelt, because the climate is uniformly hot. It comes from the stone itself, and the stone draws it up from below, through some process that is not capillary action and not osmosis and not any physics that has a name. The cliff is saturated. The cliff is a column of water held in mineral form, and it sweats the way a living thing sweats, and the sweat is the source of the river that flows down through the city, and the river that flows down is the same water that gathers in the lake, and the lake feeds the cliff, and the cliff feeds the city, and the city feeds the still ones, and the still ones sustain the sound, and the sound sustains... what?

This is where understanding fails. The sound must do something. The sound must be the purpose, or part of the purpose, because nothing here is without purpose. The tone that fills the air is too constant, too total, too carefully maintained to be a byproduct. It is a product. It is the product. The city exists to produce the sound. The water exists to sustain the city. The cliff exists to lift the water. The still ones exist to... translate? amplify? embody? The sound is not coming from a source. The sound is coming from everywhere. The still ones are everywhere. The still ones are the source.

## The Sound

It is deafening. That word is usually a metaphor for pain, for damage, for the body's alarm response to acoustic assault. Here it is literal. The sound deafens. Not by damaging the ears — though after enough time, it might — but by filling the acoustic space so completely that nothing else can enter. There is no room for another sound. The tone occupies every frequency the ear can perceive and several it cannot. It is not white noise. It is not a drone. It has a quality that language struggles with: it is a *note*, a single, specific, deliberate note, held with the precision of a voice that has been singing for uncounted years, and it is so loud that it presses against the skin like wind.

You cannot tell where it is loudest. You cannot tell where it is quietest. You can walk from the highest terrace to the lowest, through the passages and the cistern-halls and the gardens and the shallow pools, and the sound does not change. Not in volume, not in pitch, not in texture. It is the same on the cliffs as it is in the chambers. It is the same next to a still one as it is between two still ones. This should be impossible. Sound should attenuate. Sound should reflect. The acoustics of this place — the stone walls, the water surfaces, the open terraces, the enclosed passages — should produce variation, echo, dead spots, resonant zones. They do not. The sound is uniform. The sound is a field. The sound does not behave like sound.

## The Breakdown

This is where the place begins to come apart at the edges. Not visibly — nothing here is decaying, nothing is in disorder — but conceptually. The rules stop working when you try to apply them.

The water flows downhill. Yes. But it also flows uphill, inside the stone, through a process that is not a process because it has no mechanism. The plants grow. Yes. But they do not grow *toward* the light. They grow toward the sound. You can see it in the way they lean: the trailing curtains on the retaining walls hang straight down, but the columnar plants in the channels tilt, very slightly, in the direction of the nearest still one. They are listening. They are growing toward the source of the sound the way other plants grow toward the sun.

The still ones are alive. Yes. But they do not breathe. Their chests do not move. Their pulses, if they have pulses, are so slow that no amount of patience would let you detect them. And yet they are warm. And yet they produce sound. And yet, when you stand near one — and you can stand near one, because they do not react to your presence, do not flinch when you touch them, do not open their mouths to speak — when you stand near one, you can feel that the sound is coming from *inside* them, from somewhere deep in the torso, from a place that is not the throat and not the chest and not any anatomy you can name.

Time does not move here. This is the hardest part to accept, and the most important. The water moves. The water flows down through the terraces, drops from level to level, gathers in the pools, descends to the lake. But the water that is flowing is always the same water. It has always been flowing. It will always be flowing. It is not that the water is recycled — that would imply a temporal sequence, a journey, a before and after. The water is not on a journey. The water is in a state. The state is: flowing. The flowing is eternal. The water does not remember a time before it was flowing. The water does not anticipate a time after.

The still ones are the same. They have always been here. They will always be here. They did not arrive. They did not settle. They did not choose. They are part of the mechanism the way the stone is part of the cliff. They were not built. They were not born. They emerged, perhaps, the way the water emerges from the stone: inevitably, from saturation, because the place could not hold any more of whatever it is that makes them, and they precipitated out of the air and the water and the sound the way minerals precipitate out of a saturated solution.

And you — the visitor, the one who walks through this place with a body that still moves and a mind that still asks questions — you are the anomaly. You are the thing that does not belong. Not because you are unwelcome — nothing here reacts to you at all, not the water, not the plants, not the still ones — but because you are *temporal*. You have a before. You will have an after. You are moving through time, and nothing else here is. The place is not frozen. The place is not paused. The place is *complete*. It has reached a state that requires no further input, no further change, no further becoming. It is what it is. It has been what it is forever. It will be what it is forever. The water will flow. The plants will lean. The still ones will stand and sit and lie in the shallow pools, and the sound will fill the air, and the heat will sit on everything, and the light will be clear and even and will not move.

You are the only thing in this place that is changing. You are the only thing that has a direction. When you leave — and you will leave, because you are temporal, and temporality implies departure — the place will not notice. The place will not remember. The place will not adjust. The water will not alter its course. The plants will not turn to watch you go. The still ones will not close their eyes.

The sound will not skip a beat, because there are no beats. There is only the tone. Sustained. Total. Older than the stone.
