# The Condensaries of Vash Hollow

## I.

You come down into it through a cleft in the ridge — a crack really, narrow enough that your shoulders nearly brush stone on both sides — and the temperature drops as if you've stepped through a curtain. Not gradually. Like a door closing behind you. The stone under your feet changes too: dry granite giving way to something darker, finer-grained, almost vitreous, and suddenly wet. Not flowing-wet. *Seeping*. The rock has been seeping for a long time. Centuries maybe. The surface gleams.

Then the cleft opens and you're inside Vash Hollow, and the light is everywhere.

---

## II.

The hollow is not large. You can see its boundaries from anywhere within it — a rough oval perhaps three hundred paces along its long axis, walled on all sides by pale stone cliffs that rise to irregular heights, some forty meters, some sixty, their tops ragged against a sky that is almost white. Not overcast. *Luminous*. The sky here has a quality of pressed brightness, as if the light is not coming *from* the sky but has been compressed *into* it, and what leaks downward is the excess. It is not warm light. It carries no heat. It is the brightness of snow, of ice, of magnesium burning underwater — a cold, total, shadowless illumination that makes every surface in the hollow appear etched, every edge knife-sharp.

There are shadows, technically. The cliffs cast them. But the light bounces off wet stone and off the air itself with such persistence that the shadows are pale gray things, barely darker than their surroundings. You can see through them. You can see everything through them.

---

## III.

The settlement occupies the southern half of the hollow, built against the cliff wall where a broad bench of rock provides a foundation. Sixty structures, perhaps seventy — difficult to count because they are not separate in the way buildings are separate. They share walls. They overlap. They have been built on top of each other, under each other, into each other, in a process that is clearly not finished and just as clearly never *was* finished. The oldest walls are the same dark wet stone as the hollow floor, fitted without mortar, the joints so tight that water sheets down them in continuous films. The newer walls — and "newer" is relative, the newest I can see still looks decades old — use the same stone but rougher-cut, and here and there a lintel or a doorframe has been shaped from a pale, chalky substance that might be bone or might be a soft mineral dug from somewhere nearby.

Mud-brick appears too, in upper courses, in patched rooftops, in the precarious extra rooms that residents have stacked atop older structures. The brick is not fired. It has set in the cold and the wet, and it has a sickly gray-green color, and it *weeps*. Everything here weeps.

The passages between buildings are narrow. Some are roofed with hide or with rough plank, and these have become interior spaces by accretion — alleys that turned into halls that turned into rooms that turned into alleys again as the settlement grew and shrank and grew. You could navigate the whole place in five minutes if you walked straight, but you cannot walk straight. The passages fork, dead-end, loop back on themselves, drop into shallow stepped descents where the floorstone has been carved away to create headroom, rise onto slick ramps where the natural bedrock tilts. The density is extreme. The settlement has the internal complexity of a lung or a sponge — maximum surface area compressed into minimum volume.

---

## IV.

Water is the first thing you understand about Vash Hollow and the last thing you stop understanding.

It comes from the walls. All of them. Every cliff face that rings the hollow is streaked with water-trace, the vertical channels carved by centuries of flow, and from each channel — from dozens of them, from hundreds of smaller seeps and pores and cracks — water emerges and runs down. Not in streams. In sheets. In films. In individual rivulets no thicker than a finger that trace slow, branching paths down the stone and gather at the base into a shallow, moving film that covers the entire hollow floor to a depth of one or two centimeters. You are walking in water from the moment you enter. The stone beneath it is smooth and treacherously slick — not slimy, not algae-covered, just *polished* by the long passage of water and the long absence of anything else.

But the floor is not the only place water goes.

The atmosphere in the hollow is saturated. Not merely humid — *saturated*, at the edge of condensation, at the point where air and water lose their distinction. Every surface that is not actively flowing is beaded with condensation. The walls of the buildings. The hide roofs. The posts and lintels. Your own skin, your clothing, the lenses of your eyes if you stand still long enough. The cold makes the condensation visible: you can see the air, or nearly. It has a substance, a thickness, a faint luminous density that catches the extraordinary light and softens it, so that the hollow appears to exist inside a sphere of pale, bright, aqueous jelly.

The locals — there are perhaps two hundred people living here — move through this atmosphere with the indifference of long habit. Their clothing is dark, heavy, soaked and re-soaked and never truly dry. It is animal hide, mostly, untreated except by the water itself, shaped by wear rather than by tool. Some of it has been stitched with sinew in rough, uneven seams. Some of it has been *grown* — shaped while still on the animal and worn after, with the hide cured by nothing more than time and the particular chemistry of the hollow's water. The people are pale in the way that people are pale when they live in light without warmth. They move carefully, with the flat-footed gait of those who have learned never to trust a surface. They do not speak loudly. They do not speak *often*.

---

## V.

The quiet is the thing that gets into you.

The hollow is not silent — water is never silent, and the steady sheeting flow from the walls, the trickle across the floor, the drip from every overhang, all of it produces a continuous low sound that is not so much heard as *inhabited*. It fills the acoustic space the way the light fills the visual space: totally, evenly, without source or direction. It is not loud. It is not soft. It is *present*, in the way that air pressure is present, and it creates a kind of acoustic ceiling — a threshold below which all other sounds are absorbed.

The people of Vash Hollow live below that threshold. Their footsteps are inaudible. Their voices, when they use them, are low and close and expire quickly. I watched two women negotiate the passage of a narrow alley — one carrying a woven basket, the other leading a child by the hand — and they communicated without sound, with glances and shifts of weight and a kind of sign language that was not formal but *habitual*, the gestural shorthand of people who have learned that speech is uneconomical in a place where the air eats words.

Even the animals are quiet. And there are *many* animals.

---

## VI.

This is the contradiction of Vash Hollow, the thing that does not resolve: the abundance of life in a place so cold, so wet, so spare of vegetation.

There is almost no plant life. A few crustose lichens on the upper cliff walls, pale gray and orange, spreading in circles no wider than a hand. A film of something algae-like on the floor stones where the water pools — not green but a deep, translucent brown, like diluted ink. On the northern wall, in a sheltered crevice, a single growth that might be a moss or might be something else: dark red, almost black, wet as a wound, no larger than a man's fist. That is all. The hollow is stone and water and the products of human hands.

And yet.

The air is full of birds. Not many species — two, I think, perhaps three — but many *individuals*. Hundreds. They nest in the cliff faces, in the fissures and pockets and eroded hollows of the pale stone, and they are the color of the stone, which is why you do not see them at first. You see movement. A flicker. A shape detaching from the wall and becoming itself, becoming distinct, and then you see how many there are. The cliff faces are *textured* with birds. They roost in the seeps, in the wet. Their nests are built of nothing — of small stones fitted together, of mud scraped from the floor and carried up, of bits of hide and sinew and the chalky pale mineral substance. The nests are wet. The birds are wet. They appear to be waterproof in the way that seals are waterproof — not feathered but *furred*, or something between, a pelt of dense short fibers that sheds the constant water.

They make almost no sound. A clicking sometimes. A low, resonant hum that I think is territorial or perhaps navigational. It blends into the water-sound and disappears.

On the floor, the water is alive. Small things move in it — not fish, not exactly. Something between a salamander and a leech, dark, sleek, five or six centimeters long, moving in the shallow film with a serpentine motion that leaves momentary trails on the polished stone. They are everywhere. Every square meter of floor water contains a dozen or more. They do not avoid the human traffic. They move around feet, under them, between them, with an indifference that suggests they either cannot perceive the people or do not consider them relevant.

The people eat them. I saw a child catch one with a quick bare-hand grab, bite the head off, and swallow the body whole, and then go back to whatever she had been doing, which was watching a bird descend the cliff face in a stuttering, near-vertical glide.

---

## VII.

The turbulence is not visible at first. Not to the visitor. It becomes visible over *time*, and the time it takes is part of the turbulence itself — the way change here is fast and slow simultaneously, the way the hollow is stable and inconstant in the same breath.

The water shifts. That is the first sign. The seeps in the cliff walls do not flow from fixed points. Over the course of an hour, a seep that was producing a steady sheet of water will slow and stop, and another will open three meters to the left, and the water will begin to sheet from there instead. The floor-film adjusts — new channels form, old ones drain. The shallow topography of the floor, invisible under the water, reveals itself in the pattern of flow. The settlement does not move, but the water moves *around* it, and the people adjust — shifting a basket from one alcove to another, rerouting a passage by leaning a stone slab against a wall to redirect the flow.

Then the buildings shift. Not all at once. Not dramatically. But over hours, the *use* of buildings changes. A room that was a dwelling in the morning is a passage by afternoon — the residents have removed their few belongings and left the space open, and the people moving through the settlement now take a different route, and by evening the room has accumulated other things: baskets of the small water-creatures, coils of sinew, the pale chalky mineral in rough-cut blocks. A storehouse. By tomorrow it may be something else.

The people move within the settlement the way the water moves within the stone — finding paths, making paths, abandoning paths. No one appears to own a specific structure. Families — I think they are families, groups that share physical resemblance and proximity — occupy clusters of rooms that shift and reorganize continuously, the boundaries of home as fluid as the water on the floor. I asked, through gesture, a man with deep-set eyes and a hide coat stained dark by years of water, whether he lived in a particular room. He looked at me with genuine confusion and then pointed at the floor, at the water, at the cliff walls, at the sky. *Here*, he meant. *I live here.*

---

## VIII.

The birds are the engine of the turbulence.

I spent long enough in the hollow to begin to see it — the cycle that drives the place, that makes it what it is.

The birds eat something. I could not see what. They launch from the cliffs and climb, in the strange bright air, and they disappear above the rim of the hollow, and they return — some of them, most of them — with their bellies distended, and they go to their nests and they feed their young and they excrete, and the excrement runs down the cliff faces in the water, and it feeds the brown algae-film on the floor and the lichens on the walls and the single dark red growth in the crevice, and it feeds the small water-creatures, and the people eat the water-creatures and the birds and the eggs and the lichens and the red growth, and the people excrete and the waste goes into the floor water and feeds the same cycle.

But the birds also *bring* things. They bring mud from outside the hollow — from where, I do not know, perhaps from lower elevations, perhaps from a place that is not so cold. They bring fibers — grass, or something like grass, pale and dry and impossible in this wet. They bring the chalky pale mineral in fragments, and the people collect it from the nests and from beneath the nesting sites and use it for construction. The birds are the settlement's supply line. They are the only connection between the hollow and a world that is not stone and water and cold.

And the birds are inconstant. Their numbers fluctuate. Some days the cliffs are thick with them, and the floor water is dark with excrement, and the people feast. Other days — not days of departure, not exactly, but days of *thinning* — the birds are sparse, and the nesting sites are quiet, and the people eat less and move less and sit in their wet garments in the narrow passages and watch the water flow and wait. The turbulence is this: the cycle turns, but it turns unevenly, and the people have organized their entire existence around an irregular pulse they cannot predict and cannot control.

There is no storehouse. No granary. No preservation. The water-creatures live in the floor and are caught as needed. The birds are caught as needed. Nothing is kept, because nothing *can* be kept — the wet and the cold rot everything, dissolve everything, return everything to the water and the stone. The people live in an eternal present, punctuated by the rhythm of a cycle they did not create and cannot influence.

---

## IX.

The light does not change.

That is the final strangeness. The sky above the hollow is the same at what I think was dawn as it is at what I think was midday as it is at what I think was night. The light does not dim. The light does not warm. It is the same pressed, luminous, cold brightness at all hours, and the people do not sleep on a schedule. They sleep when they sleep, in wet garments, in alcoves and passages, alone and in groups, and they wake and continue. The birds sleep in their nests — some of them, at odd times, their small wet bodies tucked into the stone and mud cups. The water-creatures do not appear to sleep at all.

I lost time in Vash Hollow. Not a little. I entered the hollow and the light was bright and the air was saturated and the water was flowing, and I walked and I watched and I listened to the water-sound and the near-silence, and at some point I realized I did not know how long I had been there. A day? Two? The people around me had shifted their locations, their rooms, their tasks — the settlement had reorganized itself several times over — but I could not say whether this had taken hours or days.

I think the people do not experience time the way I do. I think the hollow has a temporal logic of its own, governed by the cycle of birds and water and the slow reorganization of stone, and the people have slipped into that logic the way they have slipped into the water and the cold and the wet, and they are not *counting*. They are not *waiting*. They are simply *here*, in the bright wet quiet, doing what the hollow does, which is to continue.

---

## X.

I left the way I came, through the narrow cleft in the ridge, and the cold eased as I climbed, and the light remained bright but became warmer, became sunlight, became ordinary. I looked back once. The cleft showed me nothing — a slot of dark wet stone. The hollow was already gone, already sealed behind its walls, already continuing without me.

The water is still on my clothes. It has not dried. I do not think it will.
