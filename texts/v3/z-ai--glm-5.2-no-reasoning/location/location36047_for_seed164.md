# The Condensate Plateau

## I.

The first thing is the sound. Before the eye settles on any single feature — before the mind catalogues the towers or the terraces or the sheerness of the cliff-lines — the sound arrives, and it arrives all at once, as though it had been waiting.

It is not one sound. It is many, but they have spent so long together that they have become a single texture. A low harmonic hum, industrial in origin, rises from the grates and vents set into the ground at regular intervals. Over this, a wetter sound: the constant tap and trickle of water finding its way down stone faces, pooling, dripping from one surface to the next. Over that, a third layer — a kind of tonal exhalation, deep and slow, like the breathing of something large and patient that has never once considered stopping. The air itself vibrates with it. You feel the sound in your sternum before you hear it with your ears.

This is the nature of the place. Everything is audible. Nothing is hidden.

## II.

The plateau sits high — so high that the sky above it is not blue but a pale, dilute grey, as though the atmosphere itself has thinned to a membrane. You are above most of the world's weather. Below the rim of the plateau, far below, clouds move like slow herds across a lower country, but up here the air is still. It does not move. It has not moved, perhaps, in a very long time.

The ground underfoot is bare rock — a dark, fine-grained stone, something like basalt but not quite, shot through with veins of a paler mineral that catches the flat light and holds it. The surface is young. That is the strange thing to say about stone, but it is true: you can see the freshness in the fractures. The edges are sharp. The steps and terraces that have been cut into the rock look as though they were carved recently, the chisel-marks still crisp, the corners unworn. Nothing has rounded them. Time has not yet had its way with this place. The plateau was here, and then the city was built on it, and neither has eroded. It is all still new, in geological terms. In the terms that matter here, it is all still *now*.

The scale is difficult to hold in the mind. The plateau stretches in every direction, and the city stretches with it, and neither has an end that you can see from any single vantage point. The towers do not cluster; they are spaced at intervals, generous and deliberate, each one given a territory of empty ground around it. The result is a landscape that feels both built and empty — a city that has the population density of a desert. You can walk for what feels like an hour between structures, across bare stone, past the grates and the vents and the low humming stations, and arrive at another tower and find it identical in its solitude.

## III.

The water comes from the air itself.

This is the governing fact of the plateau, the rule from which all other rules descend. The air here is cold — not bitter, not biting, but a steady, damp chill that sits between 2 and 5 degrees, always, and never varies. It is the kind of cold that does not assault but accumulates. It enters the fabric of clothing. It enters the joints. It enters the stone.

The air holds a great deal of moisture, and because the air is still and because the temperature sits exactly at the point where holding and releasing are in perfect tension, the water condenses. Constantly. On every surface. The stone is wet. The towers are wet. The railings and the grates and the walkways and the windows are wet. Water beads on every horizontal surface, gathers into film, finds the edges, runs down the vertical faces in thin, unceasing threads. The plateau weeps.

The water does not rain. It does not fall. It *emerges*. It appears on surfaces as though the material itself is sweating, gathers into droplets that grow until gravity claims them, and then traces its path downward. Every vertical surface on the plateau is streaked. The towers are striped with the memory of water — darker where the flow is steady, lighter where the stone has dried, which is nowhere, because nothing dries. The water runs down the towers, crosses the ground, follows the shallow channels that have been cut into the stone for this purpose, and eventually reaches the edge of the plateau, where it spills over into the void below. From the rim, if you lean out — and no one leans out, because there is no one here to lean — you would see the waterfall that surrounds the plateau on all sides, a continuous curtain of water falling into cloud, falling into nothing, falling forever.

The sound of this water is constant. It is the second layer of the plateau's voice. The first is the hum. The second is the water. The third is the exhalation. Together they make a sound that is not noise but *presence* — the sound of a place that is full of its own operation and nothing else.

## IV.

The vegetation grows in the channels.

This is the surprise of the plateau, if anything here can be called a surprise: the water-channels, which are cut into the stone at regular intervals and carry the condensate from the towers to the rim, are lined with growth. Not the growth of a forest, not the riotous green of a wet lowland, but a particular, constrained, almost cultivated kind of lushness. Moss, first — a deep blue-green moss that is almost black when wet, which it always is. It coats the channel floors, climbs the channel walls, spills over the edges onto the surrounding stone in slow, deliberate tongues. Then a kind of fern, low and wide-fronded, with fronds that are thick and waxy and dark, almost translucent at the tips where the light passes through them. These grow in the wider channels, where the water flow is stronger, their roots anchored in the moss, their fronds lying flat against the current.

In the channels nearest the towers, where the water is richest and most constant, the vegetation deepens. A kind of reed grows here — tall, straight, grey-green, with stems like bamboo but softer, almost fibrous, and these reach upward, two, three meters, leaning toward whatever light reaches the channel. And there is a vine, or something like a vine: a climbing plant with small, round leaves and a stem that is dark and shiny, like wet wire, that wraps around the base of the towers and climbs, in places, as high as the third or fourth tier. The vine does not flower. None of the vegetation flowers. There are no blossoms. There is no pollen. There is no scent of growing things. The vegetation grows because the water is here and the temperature is stable and the conditions are exactly right for growth, and so growth occurs, relentless and directionless, without the logic of season or the punctuation of reproduction.

The green of the vegetation is the only color on the plateau that is not grey or dark or pale. It is a deep, saturated green, almost luminous against the wet stone. It follows the water, and the water follows the stone, and the stone follows the hand that cut it, and the hand is gone, and so the green traces the pattern of the absent hand, filling in the channels like ink filling in the lines of a drawing.

## V.

The towers are the city.

There are perhaps hundreds of them. Perhaps thousands. It is impossible to know because the plateau does not end, or seems not to, and the towers continue at their generous intervals into the grey distance until the air itself obscures them. They are not tall by the standard of cities elsewhere — six stories, perhaps eight, some of them, and none of them slender. They are *solid*. They are built from the same dark stone that the plateau is made of, and they are built in a style that suggests function without revealing what that function is. Flat roofs. Narrow windows, set in vertical rows. Doors at the ground level, heavy, sealed. No ornamentation. No signage. No indication of purpose.

Inside — and the interiors are accessible, the doors are not locked, they simply are, heavy and open — the towers are divided into rooms of varying size, and the rooms contain *machinery*. This is the third fact of the plateau, after the water and the sound: the towers are full of engines.

Not engines in the familiar sense. There is no combustion here. No fuel. No smoke. The engines are *electrical*, or they are something that occupies the same conceptual space as electricity. They are large, dark, cylindrical objects, anchored to the floors of the rooms, connected by bundles of cable that run through the walls and down into the stone of the plateau itself. They hum. This is the first layer of the sound — the low harmonic hum — and it comes from these engines, from all of them, from every tower, simultaneously, in a chord that never changes because the engines never stop and never vary and never falter.

What the engines *do* is unclear. They are connected to the grates in the ground, the vents, the stations. They are connected to each other through the cable-network that runs beneath the stone. They are connected to the air, in some way that is not visible but is *audible* — the third layer of sound, the deep exhalation, seems to come from the interaction between the engines and the atmosphere, as though the engines are drawing something from the air or pushing something into it, and the air responds with this low, rhythmic breath.

The temperature does not change. The humidity does not change. The water condenses and the engines hum and the air exhales, and this has been happening for as long as the plateau has been here, which is not long, in geological terms, but which is long enough.

## VI.

There is no animal life.

This requires emphasis, because the absence is not merely an absence — it is a *shape*. The plateau is built for habitation. The towers have doors and rooms. The channels carry water. The terraces provide level ground for walking. The infrastructure is intact, functioning, maintained by its own momentum. And there is no one here. No people. No animals. No insects. No birds. The sky above the plateau is empty of movement. The ground has no tracks. The water in the channels flows undisturbed. The moss grows without being eaten. The ferns spread without being trampled. The reeds stand without being cut.

The silence of life is not the same as the silence of absence, and the plateau has both. It is loud — loud with engines, loud with water, loud with the exhalation of the air — and it is silent. It is the loudest silence you have ever been inside. The sound fills the space that life would otherwise fill, and the sound is so complete, so constant, so unvarying, that it becomes a kind of quiet. A deafening quiet. The quiet of a machine that is running perfectly and has no reason to stop.

You call out. Your voice is absorbed. The towers do not echo. The stone is too wet, too dense, too soft with moisture to return sound. Your voice enters the air and is taken by it, swallowed by the humidity, and what is left is the hum and the water and the breath.

## VII.

The light is ordinary, and this is the strangest thing about the plateau.

In a place so strange — so high, so empty, so full of engines and water and the green tracery of channel-gardens — you expect the light to be strange too. It is not. The sky is grey, a flat, even grey, and the light that comes through it is the light of an overcast day in a temperate climate. It is not dim. It is not bright. It is the kind of light by which you can see everything clearly and nothing vividly. It is light without direction, without source, without shadow. The towers cast no shadows. The channels cast no shadows. You cast no shadow. The light is *everywhere* and *from nowhere*, and it does not change.

It does not change because the sky does not change. The sky does not change because the weather does not change. The weather does not change because the plateau is above the weather, in a zone of perfect atmospheric equilibrium, where the temperature and the humidity and the pressure have found their balance and hold it, indefinitely, without the disturbance of sun or storm or wind or season. The light is the same in every direction. The light is the same at every hour. The light has been the same since the plateau emerged, and it will be the same until something fundamental changes, and nothing is changing. Nothing is changing at all.

## VIII.

Walk outward from any tower and the landscape repeats. This is not a metaphor. The pattern is *literal*. The towers are spaced at intervals of approximately two hundred meters. The channels run between them in a grid. The grates are set into the ground at the midpoints between towers. The vents are adjacent to the grates. The stations — small, waist-high structures of dark metal, each one humming, each one warm to the touch — are at the intersections of the grid. The vegetation grows in the channels in the same proportions, the same sequences: moss, then fern, then reed, then vine, in bands of increasing density as you approach the tower bases.

The repetition is perfect enough to be disorienting. You walk for twenty minutes and you arrive at a tower that is identical to the one you left. You walk for an hour and you arrive at another. The landscape does not offer landmarks because every landmark is the same as every other landmark. You navigate by counting, if you navigate at all. You navigate by the logic of the grid, which is infallible and useless, because the grid has no center and no edge, or rather, its edge is the rim of the plateau, and the rim is so far away in every direction that it might as well be infinite.

The emptiness is not the absence of things. The emptiness is the *distance between things*. The plateau is full — full of towers and channels and engines and water and green — but the distances between these things are so large, so deliberate, so carefully maintained, that the overwhelming sensation is of void. You are always in the space *between*. You are never *at*. The towers are presences, but they are presences separated by absences, and the absences are larger than the presences, and so the plateau is more absence than presence, more empty than full, more void than city.

## IX.

There is something beneath the stone.

You feel it before you know it. It is in the third layer of sound — the exhalation. The exhalation is not the sound of the engines. The engines are the first layer, the hum. The water is the second. The third, the exhalation, is deeper. It comes from below. It comes from *under* the stone, from under the engines, from under the plateau itself. It is the sound of something very large breathing, or the sound of something very large being breathed, and it is the weirdest thing about this place — the one element that does not resolve into engineering or geology or the simple logic of condensation.

The exhalation is slow. Each cycle lasts — it is difficult to say. Minutes. Perhaps an hour. The sound drops below the threshold of hearing and then rises again, and in the rising, you feel it in the stone, a subsonic pulse, a vibration that is not mechanical but *geological*, as though the plateau itself is a membrane and something beneath it is pushing, gently, rhythmically, with a patience that is not human and is not animal and is not mechanical but is, in some way that is difficult to define, *alive*.

Or perhaps not alive. Perhaps simply *active*. Perhaps the plateau sits above a system of caves, or a system of pipes, or a system of something that was built before the towers were built, before the channels were cut, before the grid was laid out — something older, something that the city was built on top of, something that the engines are connected to and draw from and are, in some sense, *serving*.

You cannot know this. There is no one to ask. The grates are sealed. The vents emit air that is the same temperature as the air around them, the same humidity, and so you cannot tell what they are venting or why. The stations hum and are warm and do not open. The plateau keeps its depth to itself.

## X.

The rim of the plateau, when you finally reach it — and it takes days, or it takes hours, the light does not change and so time does not pass in any useful sense — the rim is the most dramatic feature of the landscape, and it is the one feature that the city does not touch.

The towers stop. The channels stop. The grid stops. The stone continues to the edge and then ends, and below the edge is *nothing*, or rather, cloud, which is the same thing from this vantage. The plateau is a tabletop, and below the table is a floor of cloud, and the cloud moves, slowly, in directions that are not the directions of the wind up here, because there is no wind up here, because nothing moves up here, because the plateau is in perfect stillness and the world below is not.

The water reaches the rim and falls. The channels deliver it to the edge and it spills over in thin, continuous sheets, and the sheets become streams, and the streams become falls, and the falls descend into the cloud and are lost. The sound of the falls is the fullest sound on the plateau — a low, white, continuous roar that is different from the hum and the water-on-stone and the exhalation. It is the sound of the plateau *ending*. It is the sound of everything the plateau produces — water, sound, engine-heat, condensation — being released into the void, poured back into the lower world, given away.

You stand at the rim. The stone is wet. The air is cold. The grey light is flat and even and casts no shadow. Behind you, the towers hum in their grid, the channels run with their green, the engines turn in their rooms, and the plateau breathes its deep, slow breath. Ahead of you, the waterfall falls forever into cloud. The sound is enormous. The sound is the sound of a place that has been doing exactly this, exactly like this, since it began, and will continue doing this, exactly like this, until something stops it, and nothing will stop it, because nothing changes here. Nothing changes.

The plateau is perfect. The plateau is terrible. The plateau is a machine that runs on water and silence and its own unvarying breath, and it has no purpose that anyone can discern, and it does not need one. It simply is. It simply runs. It simply breathes, and weeps, and hums, and pours its water into the void, and waits, without waiting, in the grey light, in the cold, in the loud, loud silence, for nothing.

For nothing.
