# The Weir at Lumen Step

You come up to it through a crack in the ridge — not a pass, exactly, more like a fracture that never finished breaking. The stone on either side is dark basalt, still sharp-edged, the kind that hasn't had time to round off. Fresh geology. You can feel it under your hand when you brace against the walls: this rock was deep underground not long ago. Some of the faces still show the slick sheen of pressure-formed surfaces, mineral streaks that look almost welded.

Then the crack opens, and you step into a room.

---

It's not a cave. It's a *room* — an irregular one, maybe thirty meters across at its widest, shaped like a lopsided kidney, walled on three sides by sheer basalt that rises straight up another forty meters before crumbling into open sky. The fourth side is the crack you came through. Above, the ceiling is open — not fully, but mostly. A shelf of overhanging rock covers about a third of the space, and the rest is open to a sky that always looks overcast here, white-gray and flat, though you can't tell if that's weather or just the elevation. You're high. High enough that the air has a thin, stretched quality, like it's been pulled tight over the space. Your breath doesn't quite shorten — it's warm enough for that — but there's a sense that the atmosphere is thin and tired, that it arrived here recently and hasn't settled in.

The floor is uneven. Shelves of basalt step down in shallow terraces toward the center, where a shallow pool — maybe four meters across — sits in a depression that looks hand-dug but isn't. Water fills it to the brim and spills over the lowest edge in a thin, continuous sheet that runs down a natural channel in the rock and disappears into another crack at the base of the far wall. The water is clear and faintly blue-green. It tastes mineral when you cup it. Cold, despite the warm air. It's coming from somewhere deeper, pressurized, forced up through the basalt itself — you can see the points where it seeps in: three or four fissures at the bottom of the pool, each bubbling gently, sending up small, steady streams of air bubbles that break the surface with a regular, rhythmic *blup* that echoes slightly off the walls.

This is the first sound you notice. The second is the spillway — that thin sheet of overflow water running down the channel. It's not dramatic, but in this enclosed space, the trickling carries and amplifies. The basalt reflects it. The overhang above catches it and pushes it back down. The result is a constant, layered water-sound — not roar, not hush, but a complex, mid-range murk of running and dripping and bubbling that fills the room the way ambient noise fills a workshop. It's not quiet here. It's *aqueous-loud.* You feel it in your sternum.

---

The vegetation is the thing that stops you, though.

Everything above the waterline is green. Not just green — *saturated.* The basalt walls, despite being young and sharp, are already colonized. Dense mats of moss — three or four species, some fluffy and pale, some dark and pressed flat like velvet — coat the rock in patches that overlap and compete. Where water seeps down the walls in thin films, the moss thickens into cushions that drip when squeezed. Ferns grow from the cracks: small ones, leathery, their fronds unfurling in tight spirals that never fully open in this enclosed air. Liverworts spread across the wet stone in glossy, translucent sheets that look almost like skin. And everywhere — everywhere — there are the *grasses.*

They're not true grasses, technically. They're some kind of reed or sedge, knee-high, with round stems and tiny flower-heads that look like brownish brushes. They grow from every horizontal surface: the terraced floor, the moss-covered shelves, the edges of the pool, even from the thin soil that's accumulated in the crevices of the walls up to head height. They're dense enough that you have to push through them. They wave constantly — not from wind (there's almost none here, walled in as it is), but from the water. Every surface runs with moisture. Condensation forms on the grass stems themselves, runs down, drops off. The air is so humid that the plants are *sweating.* You can see it in the right light: tiny beads forming on every surface, growing heavy, falling. The ground is never dry. The stone is never dry. Your clothes won't dry here. The moss won't dry. Nothing dries. The atmosphere condenses on everything, and the everything grows.

There's a faint smell — vegetal, mineral, slightly metallic. Iron in the water. Chlorophyll in the air. Wet stone. Something underneath that's almost sulfurous but not quite — more like the smell of a hot spring, though the water here isn't hot. It's the minerals. The rock is young enough that it's still leaching.

---

In the back corner — the corner farthest from the entrance crack, tucked under the overhang where the light is weakest — there are structures.

Not ruins. Not buildings, exactly. *Installations.*

Someone has built here, and built recently, and built with purpose.

The largest is a low shed — chest-high, maybe two by three meters, made from corrugated metal panels bolted to a frame of angle iron. The panels are rusting at the edges, but the bolts are tight and the structure is sound. It has a flat roof that slopes slightly toward the back wall. The door is a sheet of the same corrugated metal, hinged with heavy hardware, currently closed. A padlock — brass, tarnished green — hangs from the latch. There's something painted on the side panel: a number, or a code. White stencil on dark metal. You can't quite read it in this light.

Beside the shed, a pipe comes out of the wall. Iron pipe, maybe four inches in diameter, extending half a meter from the basalt, threaded at the end. A valve — a big wheel-valve, red paint mostly flaked off — sits a foot back from the end. The pipe is sweating. Condensation runs down it in steady rivulets. From inside the pipe, you hear a low, resonant hum — the sound of water under pressure moving through a confined space. The valve is closed. Water seeps from the joint where the valve meets the pipe, a slow drip that hits a stone placed deliberately beneath it to prevent erosion of the floor.

There's a cable, too. A heavy, rubber-sheathed electrical cable, black, running from somewhere up the wall — it disappears into a drilled hole about three meters up, neatly caulked around the entry point — and into the shed. It doesn't power anything visible. But when you put your hand on the shed wall, you feel a faint vibration. Something inside is running. A pump, maybe. A heater. Something that hums at a frequency just below hearing, felt more than heard.

A second cable — smaller, paired with the first — runs from the same wall-hole to a small metal box mounted on a post near the pool's edge. The box has a glass front, fogged with condensation. You wipe it. Inside: a gauge. Analog, with a red needle. The needle sits at about sixty percent of full scale. There are no units marked. Below the gauge, a strip of paper — a recorder chart — slowly turns on a drum, a clockwork mechanism barely visible behind the glass. The chart is annotated by a pen arm that twitches every few seconds, recording something. Water pressure, maybe. Flow rate. Something about the pool. The chart moves so slowly you can't see it moving. But the pen twitches. That's the only sign that time is passing here.

There are other marks of human presence, less functional. A name carved into the basalt near the spillway — initials and a date, maybe four years ago. A metal ladder, lightweight aluminum, leaning against the wall where the cable enters, left by whoever services the installation. A plastic container — the kind that holds electrical tape and zip ties — sitting open on a flat rock, half full of water from the condensation, its contents floating.

---

The fauna is sparse. You notice it by absence first — no birdsong, no insects buzzing, no movement in the reeds. Then, slowly, by presence.

There are flies. Tiny ones, black, almost invisible, that hover over the moss in dense columns where the sun — when it briefly breaks through the overcast — hits the wet stone. They don't land on you. They don't seem interested in anything but the moss.

In the pool, if you hold still, you can see something moving. Small, pale, almost translucent. Shrimp, maybe, or some kind of amphipod — freshwater, blind, adapted to the mineral darkness. They drift along the bottom, picking at whatever grows on the submerged rock. When the bubbling from the fissures intensifies — which it does in cycles, every few minutes, the flow increasing for thirty seconds and then ebbing — they scatter to the edges and then slowly return. You count maybe a dozen. That's the whole visible animal life of the pool.

And on the walls, in the moss, you find snails. Two of them, in a square meter of stone. Small, dark-shelled, grazing on the algae that grows on the wet rock. Their trails are visible — silvery lines that wander and double back. They move so slowly that even watching one for a full minute, you can't be sure it's moved. But the trails prove they do.

---

The light here is strange. Not weird, exactly — but particular. The overcast sky provides flat, even illumination from above, but the walls reflect and absorb it unevenly. The dark basalt swallows most of it. The moss reflects green. The water in the pool catches light and throws it in wobbling patterns onto the overhang above — ripples of reflected light that move in slow, wandering arcs. Under the overhang, where the shed and the pipe and the gauge-box sit, the light is dimmer, browner, filtered through wet rock and corroded metal. The contrast between the open, sky-lit center and the shadowed, enclosed back corner is strong — you can see both clearly, but they feel like different rooms. The open part is a garden. The covered part is a workshop.

When the sun does break through — briefly, for ten or twenty seconds, before the overcast closes again — the effect is dramatic. The green of the vegetation intensifies. The water in the pool lights up, and you can see straight to the bottom, which is smooth, dark stone, fissured, with the pale shapes of the amphipods drifting like motes. The metal of the shed blazes where the rust hasn't taken hold, and the brass padlock flashes. The ferns on the walls cast sharp little shadows. Then it's gone, and the room returns to its default state: lit, but muted. Like a greenhouse on an overcast day. Warm, and dim-bright, and very, very wet.

---

The world's rules, as you can read them here:

This place is new and will stay new for a long time. The basalt was pushed up fast — tectonically, volcanically, something — and it emerged raw, and the water found it immediately, and the water will be here forever. The water comes from below, pressurized, mineral, cold. It fills the pool, overflows, drains away through channels in the rock that it's still carving. The erosion here is at the very beginning: you can see the first grooves being cut. In a thousand years, the spillway will be a gorge. In ten thousand, the pool will have widened and the walls will have retreated. But for now, it's a room. Bounded. Contained.

Someone has claimed this room. Not to live in — there's no evidence of habitation, no bedding, no food, no fire ring. To *use.* The pipe, the shed, the gauge, the cable — they're monitoring something. Or extracting something. The water is the resource. The minerals in it, maybe. Or the pressure itself. Or something you can't identify — some quality of this young, wet, high-up place that requires measurement. Whoever they are, they come and go. They have a key to the padlock. They change the chart paper on the recorder. They leave the ladder. They don't stay.

The vegetation doesn't care about any of this. It grows over the pipe. It grows under the shed. It colonizes the ladder's feet. Given time — and here, time is slow, and patience is the dominant mode — it will bury the installations. The moss will eat the metal. The reeds will fill the floor until the shed is invisible. The water will keep coming up, and the water will keep spilling, and the sound — that layered, constant, aqueous noise — will be the last thing to change. It will be the same sound in a thousand years. Louder, maybe, as the channels deepen. But the same.

---

You stand in the room and the room happens around you. The bubbling. The trickle. The hum from the shed, just below hearing. The pen on the chart, twitching. The amphipods, drifting. The snails, impossibly slow. The grasses, sweating. The light, flat, occasionally brilliant, always returning to flat. The air, warm and wet and thin. The stone, sharp and dark and young. Everything moving at a pace that makes your presence here feel brief and inconsequential — not in a hostile way, but in the way that geology makes all human things feel brief. You are here, and the pool is filling, and the chart is recording, and the moss is growing, and the water is coming up from somewhere deep, and none of these things need you, and none of them will stop when you leave.
