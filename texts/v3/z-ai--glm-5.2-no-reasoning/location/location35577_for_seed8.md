# The Vargan Flats

It takes three days to cross on foot, and you hear it before you see it.

---

The sound begins as a low, arrhythmic drumming — not one source but dozens, hundreds, layered and overlapping like heartbeats of different sizes. As the land slopes down toward the basin, the ground underfoot grows soft, then wet, then somewhere between the two. Not mud. Not quite. A giving, spongy turf that releases a dark water when compressed and slowly, slowly reinflates when you lift your boot. You learn to step lightly not because the ground is fragile but because the sound of your walking joins the others, and the others are already so many that adding to them feels like a small violence.

What you hear is water moving through stone. Not over stone — *through* it.

The Flats are underlaid by a vast formation of vesicular basalt, a foam of ancient lava that cooled 40 million years ago into a sponge of caverns, pockets, and interconnected channels. The formation extends further than anyone has mapped. Water enters it from highland streams dozens of kilometers north, percolates downward through the porous rock, and emerges here, at the lowest edge of the basin, through thousands of natural openings in the stone — fissures, blowholes, seeps, and what the few people who know this place call *weepers*: vertical tubes, some no wider than a finger, some wide enough to put your hand inside, that press water upward in slow pulses.

Each weeper has its own rhythm. Its own pitch. The smaller tubes produce a higher, thinner sound, a tonal whistling as air and water share the channel. The wider ones drum and thud. The largest — and there are perhaps a dozen of these across the whole basin — exhale in long, low sighs that you feel in your sternum before you hear them with your ears.

Together, across the full extent of the Flats, they produce a continuous, shifting, never-repeating resonance. Not music. Not noise. Something older than either. The sound of a world breathing through its pores.

---

The landscape itself is almost aggressively flat. Not the flat of a plain, where the horizon stretches in a clean line and the sky opens wide. The flat of a surface that was once higher and has been pulled down. The surrounding hills are low and rounded, eroded to soft profiles, their slopes gentled by inconceivable time. They rise perhaps fifteen, twenty meters at most before they too flatten off, as if the whole region has been slowly, slowly pressed toward the level of the water table, everything settling toward a single shared plane. The oldest hills are barely hills at all — just long, low swells in the ground, their crests so gentle you might walk over one without noticing.

This is a landscape at the end of its story. Not dying — settled. The violence of its formation is unmeasurably distant. The basalt was born in fire; now it is saturated with water and softened to the consistency of stale bread. You can crumble it with your fingers. The surface of the exposed stone is pitted, fluted, scalloped — erosion has worked it so thoroughly that it has become almost organic in texture, the rock mimicking the surfaces of skin or coral or brain tissue. Round-backed humps of basalt emerge from the wet ground at intervals, smooth and dark and organic-looking, like the backs of animals resting in shallows.

Between these stone humps, the ground is covered by a thin, discontinuous layer of living things. Sparse, but present. Rust-colored mats of cyanobacteria spread over the wet rock, darkening to near-black where the water runs heaviest. Singular, hardy plants — fleshy-leaved succulents with thick, waxy skins, salt-tolerant grasses that grow in isolated tussocks rather than continuous cover, occasional mats of moss that cling to the sheltered sides of larger stones. No trees. No shrubs. Nothing that rises above knee height. The plants here grow outward, not upward. They hug the surface, spread flat against the stone, hold their moisture in thick leaves and shallow roots. They have learned that the water comes to them; they do not need to reach for it.

The openness is not emptiness. It is space. The distance between each plant, each stone, each cluster of life — it feels intentional, like objects arranged in a vast room. You can see far across the Flats because nothing impedes your sight, and the seeing itself becomes a kind of presence. The air is visible. Thick with moisture, it carries a faint tint — not fog, not mist, but a hydration so complete that distant objects seem slightly submerged, as if you are looking through the surface of a very clear, very shallow sea.

---

The warmth is the thing that surprises. Not heat — warmth. A pervasive, ambient, unaggressive warmth that seems to rise from the ground as much as it descends from the sky. The water emerging from the weeper channels is tepid. Not hot. Tepid. Bathwater that has sat and cooled to body temperature. It spreads across the stone in thin sheets, barely a centimeter deep, warming the air immediately above it, and the warm wet air sits low over the Flats like a held breath. On still days — and most days are still here — the moisture forms a visible layer at the surface, a vague brightening of the air that thickens in the low ground between stone humps and pools in the shallow depressions.

The sky is usually pale. The moisture in the air diffuses the sunlight so that it arrives without sharp shadows, without contrast. Objects are illuminated from all directions at once. You lose the habit of glancing up to estimate the time of day; the light is roughly the same at mid-morning and mid-afternoon, a flat, even, featureless glow that makes the landscape feel as though it exists outside of time, or inside a very long single moment that has not yet finished.

---

The animals make up for what the plants decline to provide.

They are everywhere, and they are loud, and they have organized their entire lives around the weepers.

The most visible are the lizards. Small, quick, dark-skinned, with bellies that flush pink when they have been resting on warm stone. They live in the crevices of the basalt, in the narrow gaps between humps, in the tiny caves that open and close with the slow erosion of the rock. They bask on the rounded tops of the stone humps in the morning, and at midday they retreat to the shaded crevices where water trickles, lapping the moisture from the rock faces with quick, pale tongues. They are not shy. They watch you approach with flat, golden eyes, and they do not flee until you are close enough to touch them, and even then they sometimes do not flee, but simply sidestep, maintaining their distance without panic.

They are not the loud ones.

The loud ones are the birds.

There is a species here — some kind of rail, some long-legged, sharp-billed, rust-and-grey bird that has no English name — that nests in the sparse vegetation at the edges of the wetter stone. It is territorial, and it is vocal, and it is numerous. Each pair claims a territory of perhaps a few hundred square meters, centered on a cluster of weepers that provide reliable water and the insects the water attracts. The territories are small but fiercely maintained. The birds call constantly — a low, croaking, guttural series of notes that carries far across the flat ground. The males call. The females call. The young call, in higher, less certain voices. When two birds dispute a boundary, the calling intensifies into a rapid, overlapping exchange that sounds like an argument between old acquaintances who know each other so well they no longer need words.

Across the full extent of the Flats, there are perhaps two thousand of these birds. Each one calling. Each one responding. The combined sound is a continuous, rolling, textured roar of organic voices, rising and falling with the cycles of territorial assertion and passing interest and the slow, daily rhythm of activity and rest.

And beneath the birds, beneath the lizards, in the thin film of water that covers the stone, there are smaller lives. Tadpoles — several species, some that mature quickly and some that live as tadpoles for years, even decades, delaying their adulthood in a strategy that makes sense only in a place this stable. Insects — water striders, backswimmers, diving beetles — that hunt and are hunted in the centimeter-deep sheet of warm water. Worms and larvae in the soft organic sediment that accumulates in the lowest pockets. Crabs — small, translucent, long-armed — that live in the wider weeper channels, retreating into the rock when disturbed, emerging cautiously to scavenge whatever washes out of the stone's interior.

The food web here is compressed. Sun feeds the bacterial mats. Bacterial mats feed the grazers. Grazers feed the predators. Predators feed the scavengers. It happens in a narrow, horizontal band — the top of the stone to the surface of the water, perhaps twenty centimeters of vertical space. Everything lives in that band. Everything hunts in it. Everything dies in it. And when things die, the water carries the remains to the nearest low point, where the sediment collects and the bacteria begin again.

---

Time moves differently here.

Not slowly — that implies it moves, just at reduced speed. It is more that time has realized there is nowhere it needs to be. The processes that shape this place operate on timescales that make human patience irrelevant. The weepers pulse on cycles tied to rainfall in distant highlands, which arrives months after the rain falls, traveling through the rock at a pace set by porosity and pressure. A heavy storm in the north might increase the flow through the weepers half a year later. A dry season there might reduce it a year after that. The landscape responds to events that are already over, reacting to history that is still traveling through the stone.

The erosion of the basalt continues, of course, but at a pace that would require a dedicated observer to notice within a human lifetime. A weeper channel widens by perhaps a millimeter per decade. A stone hump lowers by a centimeter per century. The sediment accumulates in the low places at a rate of a few millimeters per year, so slowly that the depressions are filling in, the ground is leveling, the landscape is approaching perfect flatness on a schedule that even geology finds leisurely.

The animals live faster, of course. The lizards live five, six, maybe eight years. The birds live perhaps ten or twelve. The insects live weeks. But their lives cycle within the larger cycle, and the larger cycle barely moves, and so the whole system feels settled into a rhythm that has been running so long that it has worn its own channel, like a river that has carved its bed so deeply it can no longer shift course.

You can stand on one of the stone humps at the center of the Flats and look outward in every direction. The ground stretches flat to the low hills. The birds call from their territories. The weepers drum and whistle and sigh. The warm air sits still. The light is even and shadowless and arrives without urgency. The water moves at speeds that are more felt than seen — a slow, continuous, patient exhalation from the rock, spreading outward, finding the lowest ground, evaporating or seeping back down or simply sitting in thin films on the surface of the stone, growing warm and supporting the thin layer of life that has made its home here.

Nothing is happening. Everything is happening. The two are indistinguishable, and that is perhaps the defining quality of this place — that it has reached a state where the difference between happening and not-happening has been eroded away, along with everything else, until what remains is simply continuation. The Flats do not exist in a moment. They exist in a duration. And the duration is so long, and so steady, that standing in the middle of it feels less like being somewhere and more like being somewhere *for a while* — a pause in a larger process that was here before you arrived and will be here long after you leave and does not, in any meaningful sense, notice you at all.

The weepers sigh. The birds argue. The lizards watch.

You walk on, and your footsteps are absorbed into the sound, and the sound continues, and the flat warm wet ancient world goes on being exactly itself.
