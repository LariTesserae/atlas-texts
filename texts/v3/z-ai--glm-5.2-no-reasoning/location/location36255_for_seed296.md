# The Terraces of Ashinghal

## I. Approach from the Upper Switchback

You smell it before you see it. A mineral dampness — not the clean tang of open water but something older, fetched up from stone that has been wet for longer than stone should remember. The mist comes in thin veils, never quite committing to rain, and it coats the rail of the switchback path with a film that catches what little light reaches this elevation. The path itself is old. The old kind of old. Cut steps worn to shallow concavities by feet that stopped walking here centuries ago, yet the grooves remain, polished smooth as if the stone itself chose to keep them.

The switchback descends through a band of cloud forest that clings to the mountainside like moss to a north-facing boulder. Trees here grow horizontal. Not bent by wind — adapted. Their trunks angle out over the void, and their branches platform upward, creating a architecture of layered shelves, each one catching the moisture that drifts up from below and the leaf-fall that drifts down from above. Everything is draped in a moss so dark a green it reads as black in the dim light. Long filaments of lichen hang from every horizontal surface, swaying in the updraft with a slowness that makes the air itself seem viscous.

The light is the color of pewter. Not the dramatic gloom of evening or storm, but a flat, pervasive dimness — the sky here is a permanent overcast, a high haze that softens every edge and refuses to cast shadows. You can see clearly, but nothing is sharp. Distance blurs into haze within a few hundred meters, and the world feels enclosed in a dome of pale grey cotton. It is not dark enough for artificial light, but it is dark enough that the greens deepen, the greys multiply, and every surface looks wet.

You hear almost nothing. A breeze passes through the horizontal forest and produces a sound like someone drawing a cloth slowly across a table — a whisper of texture, not pitch. Occasionally, a drop of condensed water falls from a lichen strand and strikes a leaf below with a flat, exact *tock*. That is the loudest sound for minutes at a time.

## II. The First Terrace

The switchback ends at a wall.

The wall is thirty meters high and built of a stone so dark it might be basalt, though the geology here is wrong for basalt. It is mortared with something that has the color and texture of rust — a deep ochre powder bound into a matrix that has survived, intact, for an incomprehensible length of time. The wall is not decorative. It is structural. It holds back the mountainside, and it holds up what lies behind it: the first terrace of Ashinghal.

There are forty-three terraces. You know this because someone, at some point, carved the number into the stone at the gate — a simple numeral system, dots and bars, that you can read without translation. The gate itself is open. It has been open for so long that the hinges have become part of the wall, mineralized into a single continuous mass of stone and corroded iron. It will never close again.

You step through.

The first terrace is a street. A wide street, cobbled with flat stones laid in a herringbone pattern that has not shifted in what must be millennia. Buildings rise on both sides — three stories, four, built from the same dark stone as the wall, with roofs of slate so thin it has become translucent at the edges, letting a brownish glow through when the mist catches the light. Every building is occupied. Not by people. By moss, by the horizontal forest that has crept up from the mountainside, by ferns that have colonized the gutters and the windowsills and the doorframes. Root systems have worked into the masonry and become structural — the buildings would not stand without them now. The wood of the window frames was replaced long ago by living wood, branches that have grown into the shape of frames, leaves that form shutters in summer and drop away in winter.

It is not summer. It is not winter. It is the season that exists only here — the permanent cool damp of high altitude under permanent cloud — and the leaves are half-present, buds forming and opening and falling in a slow continuous cycle that gives every tree the appearance of being mid-thought.

## III. The Water System

What makes Ashinghal viable — what has made it viable for the length of time implied by the erosion of those steps, the mineralization of those hinges, the integration of those root systems — is the water.

It is not a river. There is no river here. There is, instead, a system.

Cut into the dark stone of each terrace wall, at intervals of approximately four meters, are channels. They are precise — geometrically exact, semi-circular, the width of a hand. They run along the interior face of each retaining wall, catching the moisture that weeps from the stone itself. Because the stone *weeps*. The mountainside behind Ashinghal is saturated — not with an aquifer or a reservoir, but with a diffuse capillary network of moisture that has been working its way through the rock for so long that the rock has become a sponge, mineralogically altered at the microscopic level, its pores widened by centuries of slow dissolution. The water emerges from the stone as a constant sheen, not flowing but *exuding*, and the channels catch it and direct it along gravity's path to the next terrace down.

At the corner of each terrace, where two walls meet, the channels converge into a small basin — a square stone basin the size of a kitchen sink, perfectly level, with an overflow spout that drops the water to the terrace below. The basins are full. They are always full. The water is cold and clear and has a taste of iron and calcium and something faintly alkaline, like licking a clean chalkboard. It is drinkable. The few animals that live here drink from it.

The system is old. The channels have been worn slightly wider at their bottoms, where the constant flow has dissolved the stone over the centuries, so that each channel has developed a subtle groove — a second channel within the channel, carved by water alone. The basins have developed a rim of mineral deposit, a pale travertine ring about the thickness of a finger, slightly warmer in color than the dark stone beneath. These deposits have become habitats for a particular kind of moss that grows nowhere else — a pale, almost white moss that forms a furred ring around each basin and seems to derive its nutrients from the dissolved minerals in the splash zone.

The system was built. Someone designed this — the angles, the intervals, the precision of the channels. But the system has outlasted its designers by so long that the distinction between the built and the natural has dissolved. The channels are stone. The water is water. The moss is moss. The system runs itself.

## IV. The Town

Ashinghal is a town. It was a town. It is a town in the way that a skeleton is a body — the structure remains, the purpose has transformed.

Forty-three terraces descend the mountainside in a staggered cascade, each one perhaps twenty meters wide and extending laterally for two hundred to four hundred meters, following the contour of the slope. The terraces are not evenly spaced — the upper ones are closer together, more densely built, while the lower ones are wider, with more open space between buildings. The upper terraces were the dense core: residential, stacked, the buildings shouldering each other for warmth in the way that high-altitude settlements do. The lower terraces were the working edge: larger structures, wider doorways, yards and lots that once held activities requiring space.

On the seventh terrace, you find a forge. Or the remains of one. The chimney still stands — a square stone column rising six meters above the roofline of its building, topped with a ceramic cap that has cracked into four pieces but has not fallen. Inside, the forge itself is a brick-lined pit, collapsed inward, filled with a black material that is neither ash nor charcoal but something between — a dense, vitreous slag that has fused over centuries of slow chemical decomposition into a substance that looks like dark glass. Tools hang on the wall, so corroded that they are more oxide than metal. But someone has replaced the wooden handles with living branches that have rooted into the wall's masonry, and the tools hang at exactly the angles they were hung at, held in place by the slow growth of wood around rust.

On the twelfth terrace, there is a building that was clearly a factory. It is large — occupying most of the terrace's width — and its interior is divided by stone partitions into rooms of varying size, each one with a stone floor that has been worn into shallow bowls by the placement of heavy equipment that is no longer there. Bolt holes remain, arranged in patterns that suggest machinery of considerable weight and complexity. Iron pipes run along the ceiling, following the partitions, connecting room to room. The pipes are corroded to the point of being more hole than metal, and water drips from them in a steady rhythm that has been steady for so long that the stone floor beneath each drip has developed a small, perfectly circular basin — a cup-sized hollow worn by an infinity of identical drops. The rooms echo. The sound of dripping is the heartbeat of this building, and it has been beating for longer than any human memory.

What was produced here? The bolt patterns suggest something with rotating parts. The pipe arrangement suggests something that required cooling, or heating, or the movement of fluid. The wear patterns on the floor suggest that the equipment was heavy enough to compress stone over time. But there is no product, no waste material, no record. The factory produced something for someone, and both the something and the someone are gone, and the factory remains because stone outlasts purpose.

## V. The Engine

On the nineteenth terrace, the water system does something different.

Up to this point, the channels have been passive — gravity-fed, simple, elegant in their simplicity. But on the nineteenth terrace, the channels enter a structure. It is built into the retaining wall of the terrace above, embedded in the stone, and it is made of a different material than everything else in Ashinghal — a dark grey metal, dense and uncorroded, that has a sheen unlike iron or steel. The structure is a wheel. Not a water wheel in the conventional sense — nothing paddled or open. It is a closed drum, approximately three meters in diameter, mounted on a horizontal axis, with the water entering through a pipe at the top and exiting through a pipe at the bottom. The water flows through the drum in a path that is not straight — it spirals, entering off-center and exiting off-center on the opposite side, and the flow causes the drum to rotate.

The drum is rotating. It has been rotating, without maintenance, without intervention, for a length of time that can only be described in geological terms. The bearings are not metal — they are stone, a type of granite that has been polished to a tolerance that would be remarkable in a machined surface, and the constant flow of water has kept them lubricated and free of debris. The drum turns at approximately four revolutions per minute. It is geared — stone gears, massive, with teeth the size of a hand — to a shaft that runs horizontally through the terrace wall and emerges on the other side.

On the other side, the shaft connects to a mechanism whose purpose is unclear. It consists of a series of levers and cams, also stone, that convert the rotation into a slow, regular oscillation — a rocking motion, back and forth, with a period of about six seconds. The motion is transferred to a long stone bar that extends across the terrace, and the bar has attachments — sockets, evenly spaced, each one sized to hold something. The somethings are gone. Whatever was held in those sockets is gone. But the bar rocks, and the sockets wait, and the drum turns, and the water flows, and the engine runs.

The sound it makes is the loudest thing in Ashinghal. Not loud by any absolute measure — a low, rhythmic *grinding-sigh, grinding-sigh*, the sound of stone moving against stone with the barest film of water as lubricant — but loud in context, because the rest of the town is so quiet. You hear it from three terraces away. It becomes a kind of clock. You find yourself timing your steps to it. *Grinding-sigh*. Step. Step. *Grinding-sigh*. Step. Step. It does not stop. It will not stop. The water will not stop, because the mountain behind the town is saturated to a depth that would take millennia to drain, and the stone bearings will not fail, because stone does not fatigue, and the gears will not break, because the load is small and the motion is slow.

The engine is the heart of Ashinghal, and it is still beating.

## VI. The Vegetation

The horizontal forest that holds the town is not a single ecosystem. It is a succession — a palimpsest of biological communities laid one over the other, each one building on the substrate provided by its predecessor, and the whole thing is still in motion.

The base layer is the moss — the dark, almost-black moss that covers every stone surface. It is a pioneer species, or it was, once. Now it is the dominant ground cover, and it has been so for long enough that it has developed its own micro-stratification. The bottom millimeter is dead, decomposed into a thin soil that clings to the stone. The next millimeter is living but dormant, a kind of moss-paste that reactivates only when water is present. The top millimeter is the photosynthetic layer, and it is the part that looks black — not because it lacks chlorophyll, but because the chlorophyll is so dense and the light is so dim that the moss has evolved to absorb every photon, and a surface that absorbs all light looks black. This moss is the deepest green you will ever see, so green it has circled back to darkness.

Above the moss: the ferns. They are the structural plants of the town, the ones that have colonized the buildings and the walls and the gutters. They are not large — fronds of thirty, forty centimeters, deeply divided, with a waxy cuticle that sheds the constant moisture. They grow from every joint and crack and seam in the masonry, and their roots have become part of the mortar, their rhizomes threading through the rust-colored bonding agent like a second circulatory system. In autumn — if this place has an autumn, which it might not — some of the ferns produce spore cases on the undersides of their fronds, and these release a pale dust that drifts in the still air and settles on every surface, giving the town a faintly frosted appearance that lasts for days.

Above the ferns: the trees. The horizontal forest. They are the framework. Their trunks emerge from the terrace walls at forty-five degrees, angled out over the drop, and their canopies platform upward, catching light that the terrace surfaces do not. They are not tall — four meters, perhaps five — but they are dense, and their interlocking branches create a continuous ceiling over the town, a green roof that moves slowly in the updraft. The species is unfamiliar. The bark is pale, almost white, and it peels in long strips that curl and hang from the trunks like scrolls. The leaves are small, oval, and arranged in opposite pairs, and they have a peculiar habit: they track the light. Not in the rapid, obvious way of a sunflower, but in a slow, incremental way — each leaf adjusting its angle over the course of hours, maintaining the optimal orientation to the dim overcast sky. The result is that the canopy appears to shiver, a continuous micro-adjustment of ten thousand leaves, each one seeking the maximum possible light from a sky that offers very little.

And threading through all of it: the vines. They are the connective tissue. They are a species of climbing plant that has lost the ability to climb — the vines are too heavy, too thick, too old to ascend — but they have not lost the habit. They sprawl across the ground, over the walls, through the buildings, connecting terrace to terrace, structure to structure, in a network so extensive that pulling one vine would, in theory, disturb the entire town. They are the color of old rope. They are the texture of old rope. When you walk on them, they give slightly, like walking on a coil of cable, and you can feel them transmit the vibration of your step outward through the network, a faint tremor that the town absorbs and dissipates.

## VII. The Animals

There are few. This is a place of slow process and persistent structure, not of fast movement and quick metabolism.

There is a bird. You see it on the twenty-third terrace, perched on the mineralized hinge of a gate that has not closed in centuries. It is the size of a large thrush, dark grey, with a pale band across the chest and a beak that is unusually long and thin, curved downward at the tip. It does not sing. It makes a single sound: a low, resonant *chook*, like a wooden block struck with a felt mallet. It makes this sound at irregular intervals — perhaps once every ten minutes — and it seems to serve no purpose other than to confirm its own existence. It is watching you. It tilts its head to look at you with one eye, then the other, and it does not fly away when you approach. It has no predators here. It has no competitors. It is the only bird you will see in Ashinghal, and you suspect it is the only bird in Ashinghal.

There is an insect. Or something that functions as an insect — a small, many-legged creature with a dark, segmented carapace, about two centimeters long, that lives in the moss and feeds on the decomposing lower layer. It is visible only when you peel back the moss, which you do on the twenty-fifth terrace, and the sight is startling: a seething mass of these creatures, thousands of them, filling the space between the moss and the stone like a living fluid. They do not like light. They burrow downward, away from the exposure, and within seconds the surface is clear again, the moss settling back into place as if nothing had disturbed it. You replace the moss carefully. You feel as though you have interrupted something private.

There is something in the water. You see it in one of the basins on the thirty-first terrace — a movement, a flicker, a pale shape that is gone before you can focus on it. You wait. The basin is full, the water still, the overflow spout trickling steadily. And there it is again: a small, eel-like creature, perhaps ten centimeters long, translucent, with a visible thread of a digestive tract running the length of its body. It lives in the basin. It has always lived in the basin, or its ancestors have, for long enough that the species has adapted to the specific mineral content and temperature of this particular water source. It feeds on the pale moss that rings the basin, and on the microscopic organisms that live in the water, and it has no predators, because nothing else is small enough and aquatic enough to prey on it. It is, as far as you can tell, endemic to this basin. There may be others — each basin may hold its own variant, its own subspecies, adapted to its specific chemical environment. The water system of Ashinghal may be, in addition to everything else, an archipelago of tiny, isolated aquatic ecosystems, each one a few liters of water and a few centuries of evolution.

## VIII. The Lower Terraces

Below the thirtieth terrace, the town changes. The buildings become larger, the spaces between them wider, and the vegetation thins — not because it is drier, but because the stone here is different. The lower terraces are built on a darker substrate, almost black, with a glassy texture that the moss has difficulty colonizing. The buildings are warehouses, or they were. Their interiors are vast, columned, with ceilings high enough that the mist drifts through them in slow, discrete currents. The floors are stained with substances that have left permanent dark marks — circles, streaks, splash patterns — and the marks are arranged in a way that suggests systematic activity: production lines, or processing stations, or the movement of materials in a fixed pattern from one end of the building to the other.

The water system here is different too. The channels are wider, deeper, and they do not spiral. They run straight, and they feed into larger basins — rectangular cisterns the size of rooms, sunk into the terrace floors, their walls coated with the same pale travertine deposit that rings the upper basins, but thicker here — centimeters thick, layered, each layer representing a period of deposition, and the layers are so numerous and so thin that the deposit has the banded appearance of sedimentary rock. The cisterns are full. The water in them is darker than the water above — stained by the dark substrate, tinted with a deep brown that is not opacity but color. It is still. It reflects the dim ceiling like a dark mirror.

On the thirty-ninth terrace, there is a structure that does not fit the pattern. It is a tower. Not tall — four meters, perhaps — but circular, and built of the dark glassy stone that characterizes the lower terraces, and it is not a building in any recognizable sense. It has no entrance. No windows. No door. Its walls are solid, and its top is a shallow dome, and the dome has a pattern carved into it — a spiral, beginning at the apex and winding outward to the edge, where it terminates in a small channel that connects to the water system. The spiral is not decorative. It is functional. The mist condenses on the dome, runs along the spiral groove, and feeds into the water system as a supplementary input. It is a condenser. It harvests water from the air itself, and it has been doing so, silently, for as long as the water system has existed.

There are four of these towers on the lower terraces, arranged in a pattern that you cannot quite discern from ground level but that suggests, from the configuration, a regular geometry — perhaps a square, perhaps a pentagon, with the fifth point missing or unlocated. Each tower produces water. Each tower feeds the system. The system feeds the town. The town feeds the vegetation. The vegetation holds the stone. The stone holds the mountain. The mountain holds the water.

The cycle is complete. The cycle has been complete for a very long time.

## IX. The Edge

The forty-third terrace is the last. Below it, the mountain drops away — not vertically, but steeply, a slope of scree and low scrub that descends into a cloud layer that is permanent and impenetrable. You stand at the edge and look out. You can see nothing below the cloud. You can see nothing across, because the haze reduces visibility to a few hundred meters. You can see up — the terraces rising above you, each one a dark line edged with green, stepping up the mountainside until they disappear into the cloud forest from which you entered. The town is above you and below you and on every side, and it is quiet, and it is running, and it does not need you.

The engine turns. The water flows. The moss grows. The ferns unfurl. The trees adjust their leaves. The bird says *chook*. The insect-things writhe in their dark layer beneath the moss. The eel-like creature flickers through its basin. The condensers harvest moisture from the air. The channels carry it downward. The basins hold it. The overflow spills it to the next level. The system was built by hands that are gone, for purposes that are forgotten, and it has outlasted its builders and its purposes and it has become, in its old age, something that its builders might not recognize: a living thing. Not alive, but functioning with the autonomy and persistence of life. A machine that has become an organism. An organism that was once a machine.

The mist touches your face. It is cold. It tastes of iron and calcium and something faintly alkaline. It has been tasting the same for longer than the word *long* can meaningfully describe. You are the first thing to taste it in a very long time, and when you leave, it will go back to being tasted by nothing, and that will be fine. The system does not require an audience. The system requires only water, and stone, and the slow, patient geometry of gravity.

You turn and begin the long climb back up through the terraces. The engine marks your passage. *Grinding-sigh.* Step. Step. *Grinding-sigh.* Step. Step. The town continues around you, its processes unaltered by your presence, its cycles turning at the speed of stone and water and the growth of moss. It will be here tomorrow. It will be here when tomorrow is a meaningless concept. It was here before you were born, and it will be here after, and the difference between those two states, from the perspective of Ashinghal, is nothing at all.

The bird says *chook*.

The water flows.

The wheel turns.
