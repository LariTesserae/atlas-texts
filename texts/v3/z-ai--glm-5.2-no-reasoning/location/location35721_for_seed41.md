# The Wombgrot

You descend through a fissure in basalt — not a cave mouth, not a proper entrance, just a crack where the rock split long ago and never healed. The stone is warm under your palms. Not sun-warm. Body-warm. The kind of warmth that suggests the ground itself runs a low fever.

The passage squeezes down to shoulder-width, then opens without warning into something like a cathedral's sacristy — a side chamber of the underground, modest in scale, perhaps thirty meters across at its widest, though the ceiling vanishes into a darkness that suggests more height than the floor space quite warrants. You are below the surface. Not far — far enough. The air changes. Thickens. Carries weight.

---

The first thing you notice is the moisture. Not dripping, not flowing — *present*. The walls sweat. A film of water slides down the basalt in slow, patient sheets, collecting in channels worn so smoothly into the rock that they seem designed, though they are not. They are old. Erosion old. The kind of old that means the water has been doing this since before anything aboveground remembered it was alive. The channels intersect, branch, pool. In the lowest depressions of the uneven floor, dark water collects in basins the size of bathtubs, clear enough to see the stone bottoms, their surfaces trembling with vibrations too faint to hear.

It rains here, but not from clouds. The ceiling — you realize as your eyes adjust — is hung with condensation. When the warm air from deeper in the rock meets the cooler strata above, water gathers on the underside of the stone like breath on a window. The drops fall in irregular rhythms — plink, pause, plink plink, long pause — and each one lands somewhere below in a pool or on moss or on the broad flat leaves of things that have evolved to catch them.

Not rain. Not mist. Something between. An intermittent, indecisive precipitation, as though the cave itself is thinking about crying and hasn't committed.

---

The second thing you notice is how alive it is.

This should not surprise you, but it does. The dimness tricks the mind into expecting stone and stillness. Instead, every surface that can hold life does. Moss — no, moss is insufficient — thick velvety carpets of bryophyte cover the walls in greens so deep they read as black until your eye adjusts, and then they read as green again, and then as something that is not quite a color but a *texture of light*. Liverworts spread in rosettes. Ferns uncurl from fissures where the water-channels run, their fronds pale and broad, almost translucent, designed to catch whatever photons filter down through the crack you entered by and through two other fissures you haven't found yet. They are the pale green of lettuce held against a window. The green of skin seen through a flashlight. Sickly and gorgeous.

Orchids grow from the rock. Actual orchids — lithophytic species with aerial roots that grip the basalt like pale fingers, their flowers small and white and nodding on thin stems. They bloom in the dimness with a kind of quiet defiance, as though color itself is a mild embarrassment here and they are apologizing for it. There are bromeliads too, their tank-leaves holding small reservoirs of water that is not the same water as the wall-water — it is warmer, slightly yellow, alive with microorganisms. Tiny ecosystems the size of teacups.

And everywhere, everywhere, fungi. Bracket fungi shelf out from the wet wood of buried roots that have penetrated the rock over centuries, their surfaces slick and dark. Thin fungal filaments — white, threadlike — web across the moss, connecting organisms in networks you can actually see if you crouch and squint. In places the fungal threads glow. Not bioluminescence exactly — more a kind of phosphorescent stain, a residual light, as though they've absorbed the dimness and are holding it, rendering it slightly, barely visible. It does not illuminate. It merely marks. A map drawn in almost-dark.

This is the light: dim, secondhand, filtered. The three fissures that admit air also admit thin blades of gray daylight that fall across the chamber like scars. They illuminate columns of mist and land on specific spots — a patch of moss, a pool, a single orchid — leaving the rest to the imagination. Your eyes adapt. The dark becomes navigable. But it never becomes bright. You exist here in a permanent crepuscular state, an animal just risen from sleep, blinking.

---

The fauna is where the grotto reveals its true density.

Insects first. Not swarms — that would be too dramatic, too horror. But a constant presence. Moths the color of wet bark rest on the moss-walls, their wings flattened, almost invisible until one shifts and the pattern breaks. Springtails hop across the water surfaces, leaving dimple-trails. Beetles — small, dark, glossy — patrol the fungal shelves like sentries, and you watch one climb a bracket fungus, pause at its edge, and groom its antennae with a foreleg in a gesture so domestic it makes the place feel inhabited.

Then the amphibians. Small frogs — the size of your thumbnail — live in the bromeliad tanks, their bodies translucent, their organs visible through pale skin. They call, but the calls are almost subliminal. A thin cricket-like peep, repeated at long intervals. One calls. Another answers from across the chamber. Then silence. Then the first calls again. The sound is so small it barely qualifies as sound — it is more like a thought about sound, an intention to vocalize that barely materializes. This is the volume of the grotto: the dripping, the frogs, the occasional rustle of a gecko shifting position on stone. Nothing louder. The acoustic space feels held, as though the walls absorb anything above a whisper.

Geckos. Pale, almost white, with translucent skin that shows the dark networks of their veins. They cling to the vertical basalt, to the underside of rock overhangs, to root-surfaces. They are numerous. You count seven on one wall alone, motionless, waiting. They eat the moths. You watch one drop from an overhang, land on a moth with mechanical precision, fold it into its mouth, and return to its position on the wall. The whole action takes four seconds and produces no sound you can hear.

Snakes too. Thin ones. A species of small, rear-fanged colubrid that lives in the moss carpets and emerges to hunt the geckos. You see one — a brown ribbon of muscle — sliding through the bryophyte surface, its body barely disturbing the moss, moving with a patience that suggests it has all the time in the world. It disappears behind a fern and does not reappear. You hear nothing.

There are bats — you can smell them before you see them, that sharp musk of guano and concentrated mammal — roosting in the ceiling's crevices, sleeping in their inverted clusters. Occasionally one shifts, and a chittering so high-pitched it sits at the edge of perception flickers through the air. They will emerge at nightfall, which is every night here, the darkness above and the darkness below becoming briefly identical. You do not see them clearly. You see their shapes — small clenched fists of fur — and you see the guano below their roosts, accumulated on ledges, feeding the fungi and the moss and the roots, a nutrient delivery system from sky to stone.

And spiders. Web-builders that string silk between fern-fronds, their webs catching the falling water-drops and holding them like jewelry. The webs are nearly invisible until the water weight makes them sag, and then they appear — geometric, precise, beaded with droplets that refract the dim light into tiny prisms, each one a lens the size of a pinhead showing you a miniature version of the grotto, reversed. The spiders sit at the edges, legs splayed, waiting. Small. Dark. Patient.

---

The warmth is constant. It comes from below, from the rock itself, as though you are standing inside the slow exhalation of something deep. Not volcanic — not that dramatic — just the ordinary geothermal patience of stone that has been warm for longer than anything has been alive. The temperature sits in the range of a comfortable bath, a summer afternoon, a body at rest. You are sweating within minutes. The moisture in the air — and there is much of it, the humidity must be near ninety percent — means the sweat does not evaporate. It sits on your skin. You become part of the dampness. Another wet surface. Another sweating wall.

This is the kind of heat that makes time slow. Nothing here is in a hurry. The erosion that shaped the channels happened over millennia. The moss has been here for centuries. The frogs have been here for generations of frogs, each one so small and so brief, but the lineage deep. The orchids bloom and fade and bloom again on schedules measured in weeks. The bats leave and return. The water falls and falls and falls. Everything is in motion and nothing is rushing. The grotto is an engine running at idle — no, not idle. At pace. At its own pace, which is the pace of geology and biology braided together, slow and fast simultaneously, the moss growing in real time while the stone reshapes itself in time that cannot be perceived.

---

There are built things here. Not many. Not obvious. But if you look — and the dimness makes you look closely, makes you earn every observation — you find them. A low wall, knee-height, made of stacked basalt, running along one edge of the chamber where the floor drops into a deeper pool. The wall is old. The moss has colonized it. The fungi have softened its edges. But the stacking is too regular, too intentional, to be natural. Someone built it. Someone came down here, or lived down here, and stacked stone to keep something out or keep something in. The wall is in balance with its environment — it has not been maintained, but it has not been removed. It has simply been absorbed, becoming part of the grotto's vocabulary, another surface for life to grip.

Near the wall, in a depression that might have been a firepit — though you cannot imagine a fire here, in this wetness — there are stones that show charring. Faint. Old. The carbon has been partly colonized by fungi, but it is there. Someone sat here. Someone sat in this cathedral-sacristy and felt the warmth and listened to the dripping and watched the frogs and was here.

No tools. No devices. No technology. Whatever these people had, they brought nothing of it that survived, or they had nothing to bring. The grotto is pure nature with one old gesture in it — a wall, a firepit — and the nature has forgiven the gesture by overgrowing it.

---

Sound returns you to the present. A drop falls. A frog peeps. A gecko's foot clicks once against stone. The bats shift and chitter. And beneath all of it, below the threshold of hearing but present as vibration in the warm stone under your feet, there is the sound of water moving through rock. Not dripping. Not falling. Moving. The hydrology of the place — a whole system of seepage and percolation, of water finding its way through the basalt's micro-fissures, of the land above feeding this chamber from above and below — this makes a sound that you feel more than hear. A subsonic hum. A purr. The grotto vibrates at the frequency of patience.

You sit. The stone is warm. The moss is soft. An orchid nods near your face, its roots gripping the rock beside your shoulder. A beetle walks across your boot and does not know you are anything other than another surface. The dimness holds you. The heat opens your pores. You are in a room the earth made for itself, and you are briefly permitted.

You could stay here. The thought arrives unbidden. The grotto permits it. There is water. There is warmth. There are frogs and orchids and the slow patient company of stone. You could sit against this wall and let the moss find you and let the centuries be kind to your bones and someone might find them, centuries from now, and not know they were ever anything other than another mineral deposit.

You don't stay. But you remember. The dripping. The frog. The almost-light. The wall someone built and the fire someone lit and the long silence since.

The grotto exhales. You climb back up into the bright, dry world, and the crack closes behind you, and you are not sure, afterward, whether you dreamed it.
