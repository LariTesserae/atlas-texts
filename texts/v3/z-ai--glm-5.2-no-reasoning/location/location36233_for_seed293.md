# The Concrescence at Wjell

You arrive at Wjell through a rent in the living mat — a vertical seam that parts like a wound healing in reverse, exhaling air that tastes of iron and chlorophyll and something sweetly chemical underneath, like the ghost of a solvent. The seam does not stay open behind you. It seals with a soft, wet sound, a lip pressing to a lip, and you are inside.

Inside what, exactly, is the first question.

## What Wjell Is

Wjell is a settlement. More precisely, it is a *concrescence* — an old word repurposed by the people who came here, meaning a growing-together, a thickening, an accumulation that becomes its own substance. It occupies a shallow basin perhaps four hundred meters across, set into a floodplain that has been a floodplain for longer than anything has been anything. The basin is old. Not old like a building is old. Old like a bruise is old — the land has been hurt here, pressed down by something that no longer exists, and the depression has remained because nothing has had the energy to fill it. The ground is half a meter below the surrounding plain. Water does not drain from Wjell. Water comes *to* Wjell. This is the first and most important fact of the place, and everything else follows from it.

The settlement is built from the settlement. That is not a riddle. The substrate on which structures rise, the walls that enclose spaces, the pathways that connect them, the membranes that keep out the constant damp — all of these are vegetable tissue, living or recently living, grown from a handful of engineered plant lineages that were seeded here in whatever era produced them and have been doing their work ever since. The technology that made them is beyond what the current inhabitants fully understand. The inhabitants use it. They tend it. They prune it. They have theories about it. But the deepest grammars of the system — the molecular negotiation between root and mineral, the way a wall knows to thicken where load-bearing is needed, the signaling that tells a corridor to hollow itself — these are not things anyone here has designed. They are things that were designed, once, by minds or systems that operated at scales and in languages that the current era cannot reconstruct. The technology is not lost. It is *inherited*, which is different. You can live inside a working mechanism without knowing how to build it from nothing.

The people of Wjell — and there are perhaps a hundred and forty of them, though counting is complicated by how often they come and go and by how the settlement itself sometimes seems to produce the impression of more bodies than are present — live in the spaces between growths. They did not build the growths. They cut into them. They hollow them. They keep them pruned. The relationship is architectural but also agricultural but also something closer to veterinary. They are keeping a structure alive, and the structure is keeping them sheltered, and neither party entirely controls the other.

## The Water

The ground in Wjell is wet in the way that a sponge is wet — not because water sits on it but because the substrate *is* water, held in a matrix. You can walk on it. Your foot presses and sinks two centimeters and stops, held by the fiber, and when you lift your foot the impression fills with water so quickly it seems the ground was waiting for the opportunity. In the lower areas — the center of the basin, where the depression is deepest — the water table is at the surface, and in places above it. Shallow pools sit in the gaps between root masses, never more than ankle-deep, never less than a few centimeters. They do not reflect the sky because there is too much growth overhead for a clear reflection. They reflect the green. They reflect the structural elements — the pale boles of load-trunks, the dark strands of tension-vines, the translucent panels of membrane that serve as windows. The water is clean. It moves, slowly, through the substrate, pulled by gradients too subtle to see, and it emerges in seeps and weeps along the edges of every surface. The rocks weep. The walls weep. Even the air weeps — condensation forming on every cool surface, gathering, falling, returning to the ground, re-entering the cycle. The humidity is not oppressive. It is simply *present*, a fact of the medium, like salinity in the ocean. You stop noticing it after an hour. Your skin does not. Your skin becomes part of the system. Your clothes become part of the system. Everything becomes slightly damp and stays slightly damp and this is not a problem to be solved but a condition to be inhabited.

The water does something here that water does not do in other places. It *organizes*. The engineered plant lineages use water not just as a medium but as a signaling system — chemical messengers move through the water table faster than through the roots, and the entire settlement's plant network communicates partly through the slow movement of water-borne information. When something changes — a new load on a wall, a pathogen in a root cluster, a shift in the mineral content of the inflow — the water carries the news. The plants respond. Sometimes the response is visible within hours. A wall thickens. A corridor narrows. A new shoot emerges from the floor of a room that has been a room for decades. The inhabitants have learned to read the water — not chemically, not with instruments, but by watching what grows where and how fast.

## The Growth

Everything here is plant. This requires emphasis because the word *plant* may not prepare you for what plant looks like when it has been engineered to be everything.

The ground is a mat of living fiber — root and rhizome and a type of modified stem that the inhabitants call *weg*, which runs horizontally just below the surface, connecting every growth to every other growth in a network so dense that you cannot drive a spike into the ground without hitting something alive. The weg is the substrate. It is what you walk on. It is springy and fibrous and dark, the color of wet bark, and it smells like a forest floor concentrated to the point of becoming its own atmosphere.

The structures grow from the weg. They are not built. They are *encouraged* — pruned toward shapes that the inhabitants need, fed by channels they cut into the mat, supported by scaffolds of living tissue that the plants produce in response to mechanical stress. A wall in Wjell is not a wall like you or I would build a wall. It is a thickened growth — layers of woody tissue laid down over years or decades, hollow chambers inside for insulation, a smooth outer bark that sheds water, root-holds at the base that grip the weg and anchor the structure against wind and flood. The walls breathe. Not metaphorically. They exchange gas with the atmosphere. They take in carbon dioxide at night and release oxygen, and during the day they photosynthesize through a modified cuticle that is transparent enough to let light through to the chlorophyll-bearing cells beneath. This means the walls are slightly warm. This means the walls are slightly lighter at midday and slightly darker at midnight. This means that if you press your ear to a wall you can hear the slow tick of fluid moving through xylem, the very slow heartbeat of a very slow body.

The rooms are hollow spaces inside growths. Some were deliberately hollowed — the inhabitants cut into a mature trunk, removed the inner tissue, and kept the shell alive by maintaining a thin layer of cambium on the interior surface, which they coax into producing a smooth, waxy lining that is waterproof and faintly luminous, giving off a dim glow that is not light exactly but a kind of dark-visible luminescence, like the light inside a closed eyelid. Other rooms were *self-hollowed* — the plants produce them on their own, in response to signals the inhabitants have learned to send. A cut here, a chemical applied there, a particular pattern of pruning, and the plant begins to grow a chamber. It takes months. The inhabitants know when a chamber is nearly done because the walls begin to thin and the interior begins to dry. They enter when the plant signals readiness by opening a pore — a circular hole that appears in the outer bark, perfectly formed, exactly large enough for a person to pass through.

Between the rooms, corridors. These are not planned. They are *found*. The growths leave gaps, and the gaps connect, and the inhabitants widen the gaps by pruning and by walking through them repeatedly, which compresses the weg and kills the surface growth and creates a path. The paths are always slightly wet. They are always slightly green. They are always changing — a corridor that was wide enough for two people last month may have narrowed because the growth on one side thickened, or because a new shoot emerged from the floor and has not yet been cut back. The inhabitants do not fight this. They move with it. A corridor narrows; they use it as a single-file passage. A corridor closes; they cut a new one. The settlement breathes in this way, expanding and contracting, and the people inside it breathe with it.

Above, the canopy. It is not a canopy like a forest canopy. It is a *roof* — engineered growths that spread horizontally, supported by the load-trunks, overlapping like shingles, shedding water down their surfaces into channels that run along the upper surfaces of the walls and down to the ground. The roof is alive. It photosynthesizes. It produces the oxygen the inhabitants breathe. It also produces, from its underside, a fine mist of aerosolized compounds — sugars, volatile organics, signaling molecules — that fall continuously into the rooms and corridors below like a warm, invisible rain. This is the atmosphere of Wjell. It is not air. It is air *plus*. It tastes green. It deposits a faint film on every surface, which the inhabitants wipe from their faces and their tools but do not try to eliminate, because the film is part of the system — it inhibits microbial growth, it moisturizes the skin, it carries chemical signals from the roof to the floor. You are inside an organism. You are inside an organism that is also a town. You are inside a town that is also an organism. The distinction between these two descriptions is one of the things that does not hold up well in Wjell.

## The Noise

The first thing you notice is the sound. Not the wetness, not the green, not the strangeness. The sound.

Wjell is loud. Not loud like machinery. Loud like *process*. Everything here is doing something, and everything that is doing something is making noise about it.

The water makes the most constant sound — a multi-layered drip and trickle and seep that comes from every surface and every direction, not rhythmic but not random, a complex patter that your ear tries to organize into patterns and fails and tries again. The water moves through the weg with a sound like a slow pour from a great height, a thick liquid sound, continuous and low. The water moves through the walls with a sound like a heartbeat heard through a wall — a slow, regular thump of pressure, xylem pushing fluid upward, and this sound is synchronized across the entire settlement, all the walls beating at the same slow pace, because they are all connected, because they are all one system. This is the bass note of Wjell. It is always there. You feel it more than hear it.

The plants make noise as they grow. This is not the groan of wood. It is a sound the inhabitants call *skryll* — a high, thin creaking that comes from fibers sliding past one another as the tissue expands. It is most audible in the early morning, when the growth rate is highest, when the chemical signals in the water have reset overnight and the plants receive their dawn instructions. The skryll rises across the settlement like a flock of small birds — a scattered, stuttering chorus of tiny sounds that converge into a texture, a fabric of creaking that fills the air for minutes and then subsides. But it does not stop. At any hour, somewhere in Wjell, something is growing fast enough to skryll, and the sound carries through the dense medium of the walls and the wet air.

The inhabitants make noise. They are few, but they are busy. They prune with tools that are not blades — they are inhibitors, chemical applicators that stop growth in a line and cause the tissue to die and dry and fall away. The sound of pruning is a sound like tape being pulled from a surface, a ripping that is also a peeling, and it carries. They move through the corridors with a purposeful rapidity that suggests urgency even when there is no urgency, because in Wjell there is always something that needs attention — a corridor closing, a wall thickening into a space that needs to remain open, a water channel blocking, a root mass expanding into a room. They call to one another. Not words, mostly. Tonal fragments, musical phrases that carry meaning through pitch and rhythm and duration, a language optimized for distance and wet air. The calls bounce off the walls and come back slightly altered, slightly softened, and the echo becomes part of the call, part of the meaning, so that a shout and its echo together say something that neither alone could say. The noise of the inhabitants is the noise of a crew on a ship — coordinated, purposeful, continuous, a working noise.

And underneath all of it, from below, from the weg and the water and the root network, a sound that is harder to name. A hum. Not mechanical. Not electrical. *Biological*. The sound of a trillion cells doing chemistry simultaneously, the sound of metabolism at scale, the sound of life processing matter and energy in a space small enough that you can hear it. The inhabitants do not mention this sound. They have stopped hearing it, or they have never heard it, or they consider it to be the sound of Wjell itself and therefore not a sound at all but the medium of hearing. You hear it. It is the floor of your auditory world here. Everything else is above it.

## The Light

The light in Wjell is the light of a place that is inside a plant. It is green. It comes from above, filtered through the roof-growth, and it arrives in the rooms and corridors as a diffuse, even illumination that has no source and casts no shadows. It is bright enough to see by. It is not bright enough to read by unless you are near a membrane panel — one of the translucent sections of the wall that the plants produce where photosynthesis is most active, which are thinner and lighter and which admit a clearer, though still green-tinted, light. The overall effect is of being underwater, or inside a cloud, or in the interior of a large body. The light does not change dramatically through the day. It brightens at midday and dims at dusk and brightens again slightly at dawn, but the range is narrow. The settlement exists in a kind of perpetual green twilight, and this twilight is warm, and the warmth and the greenness and the wetness together produce a state in the body that the inhabitants call *slenk* — a drowsy alertness, a hypnosis of the skin, a feeling of being held.

There is no night in Wjell, exactly. The light dims but does not vanish. The walls continue to glow with their faint dark-visible luminescence. The water continues to move. The plants continue to grow. The settlement does not sleep. Some of the inhabitants sleep, on pads of dried weg-fiber in their rooms, but others move through the corridors at all hours, tending, pruning, watching, because the growth does not stop and therefore the maintenance does not stop and therefore the community does not stop. Wjell runs continuously. This is part of what makes it loud.

## The Fauna

The animals here are not separate from the plant system. They are part of it. This is the strangeness that takes the longest to feel.

The most visible are the *mota* — small, six-legged animals the size of a mouse, with smooth dark shells and elongated bodies, that move across every surface in Wjell — walls, floor, ceiling, water — with a fluid, climbing gait that seems to ignore orientation. They are not insects. They are not vertebrates. They are something engineered, like the plants, and they fill a niche that the plants cannot fill alone: they are the mobile immune system. They move across the living surfaces and they eat. What they eat is anything that should not be there — fungal spores that land on the outer bark, microbial films that form in the water channels, dead tissue that the plants are shedding, foreign organisms that arrive on the wind or on the clothing of visitors. The mota patrol. They do not sleep. They do not stop. If you sit still in a room for long enough, mota will cross your skin — not biting, not hurting, simply moving, investigating, tasting you with their small feet and moving on, having determined that you are not a threat and not food and therefore not interesting.

Larger animals move through the water channels — eel-like things, pale, eyeless, that the inhabitants call *feryll*. They swim through the water table, through the channels in the weg, through the root network, and they eat the sediment that accumulates in the flow, keeping the water channels open. Without them, the system would clog. The inhabitants know this and protect them. You see a feryll perhaps once a day — a flash of white in a shallow pool, a ripple in a channel, a movement in the water that is not the water's own. They are the size of a forearm. They move fast. They are gone before you can focus on them.

In the canopy, things fly. Not birds. Not insects. Something between — small, warm, covered in a fine fiber that might be feather or might be modified leaf, with wings that are not quite wings but broad flattened appendages that they use to glide between the roof-growth panels. They make a sound — a high, clicking chirp — that adds another layer to the noise. They eat the aerosolized sugars that the canopy produces, filtering them from the air with specialized mouthparts, and they excrete a nitrogen-rich waste that falls into the rooms and corridors below and is collected by the inhabitants and fed back into the system through the water channels. Nothing is wasted. The word *waste* does not quite apply here. Everything is input for something else. The animals are not living *in* the system. They are *part of* the system, as much as the plants, as much as the water.

There are other things — things that the inhabitants do not name and do not discuss, that you see in the periphery of your vision and cannot find when you look directly at them. Shapes in the water. Movements in the walls that are not the walls' own growth. The inhabitants will say, if you ask, that the system produces *artifacts* — emergent forms that were not designed and are not controlled, that arise from the interaction of the engineered lineages with the wild ecosystem and with each other, that appear and disappear without pattern. These artifacts are not dangerous. They are not helpful. They simply *are*, and the inhabitants have learned to coexist with them the way you coexist with weather — acknowledging, adapting, not trying to impose control. You will see one, perhaps. A shape in a wall that looks like a face. A pattern in the water that looks like writing. A sound that is not the sound of any known process but is nonetheless *produced* by the system, a word that the system says and does not know it is saying. The inhabitants call these *fenn*. They do not explain them. They do not explain them because they cannot, and they have stopped trying, and the stopping is part of what it means to live here.

## The Dynamic

Wjell is not stable. This needs to be said clearly, because the word *ancient* may suggest stillness, and *primordial* may suggest finality, and the deep age of the basin and the system may suggest a place that has arrived at its resting state. It has not. It is deeply aged and deeply *active*. These are not contradictions. They are the same condition.

The system has been running for longer than anyone can count. The plant lineages have been growing and dying and growing again in this basin for so long that the peat beneath the weg is meters deep — compressed remains of generations of growth that lived and processed and died and were absorbed into the substrate and became the ground that the next generation grew from. This is the ancient part. The system has settled into its final *form* but not into stillness. It is like a river that has found its bed — the bed is ancient, the course is stable, but the water is always moving, always new, always doing what water does. Wjell is its own riverbed. The processes are the water.

And the processes are *fast*. The turbulence is visible at every scale. A wall that was thin yesterday is thicker today. A corridor that was open this morning is narrowing by afternoon. A room that was dry an hour ago has a pool forming in its floor because a water channel shifted and the flow found a new path. The inhabitants move through this turbulence like sailors in a storm — not panicking, not fighting, reading the changes, adjusting, pruning, redirecting, opening a channel here, closing one there, cutting a growth that is encroaching, encouraging one that is needed. The settlement changes shape on the timescale of days. Not dramatically — not walls collapsing, not rooms disappearing — but continuously, like a conversation, like a negotiation, and the negotiation is never finished because the system is never finished because the system is alive and aliveness is not a state but a process.

There are faster changes too. The water can shift suddenly — a channel that has been flowing north for months will redirect to the east in an hour, and the rooms along the old channel will begin to dry, and the rooms along the new channel will begin to flood, and the inhabitants will scramble. This happens often enough that it is not an emergency. It is a *Tuesday*. The word they use for it is *vrenn* — a turning, a change of direction, and the word carries no anxiety. It carries a kind of exasperated affection, the way you might speak about a difficult relative who is nevertheless family.

The most dramatic changes are the ones the system produces without warning — the *fenn* again, but not the small ones, not the faces and the patterns. The large ones. A new growth that appears overnight in the middle of a room — a structure that was not there yesterday, that has no function that anyone can determine, that is nonetheless *constructed* by the system, a form that the engineered lineages have decided to produce for reasons that are not accessible to the inhabitants. These emergent structures are the *Solaris* of Wjell — the system creating something that reflects its own internal processes, its own computations, its own whatever-it-is, and the creation is not for the inhabitants, is not *about* the inhabitants, is not *despite* the inhabitants. It is simply what the system does, sometimes, and the inhabitants have learned to live around it, to incorporate it, to prune it if it blocks a corridor and to leave it if it does not, and to wonder about it quietly and not too insistently, because the wondering is part of living here but the answering is not.

## The Inhabitants

They are few. They are enough. They move through Wjell with the ease of people who have become part of their own infrastructure — not ruling it, not serving it, but *participating* in it at a level that dissolves the distinction between inhabitant and inhabited. They prune. They redirect water. They harvest the growth — taking material from the structures that are being pruned, drying it, using it for tools and pads and the few objects they need. They eat from the system — not the structural plants, which are toxic to humans, but from a separate lineage that the system produces in dedicated growth-chambers, a starchy tuber that grows in the water channels and is the staple food. They drink the water, after it has passed through a filtering growth that the system produces at the edge of every pool. They breathe the air that the system makes. They are, in a real sense, metabolically embedded in the system — they take in what it outputs and they output what it takes in, and the loop is closed, or nearly closed, and the closing of the loop is the deepest technology here, the one that no one fully understands but everyone participates in.

They have built almost nothing. The built score is for the settlement, not for them. The settlement is built — organized, structured, infrastructural. But the builders are the plants, and the technology is the plants' own, and the inhabitants are the tenants who have learned the rules of the lease by living inside it for generations. They have authority here, but it is the authority of a gardener, not an architect. They shape. They do not design.

## What It Is Like to Be Here

It is like being inside a body. Not a metaphor. The experience of Wjell is the experience of being inside a large, warm, wet, living body that is also a town that is also a machine that is also older than you can imagine and more active than you can keep up with. The walls are warm. The floor is soft. The air is thick and green and carries information you cannot read. The sounds are the sounds of process — circulation, respiration, growth, communication. The light is the light that passes through tissue. The water is the water that carries the body's messages. The animals are the body's immune cells and cleaners and the things that the body produces for reasons it does not explain.

You are inside the body, and the body does not know you are here, or does not care, or knows and cares in a way that is not the way you would know and care, and the difference between these possibilities is one of the things that you cannot resolve and that the inhabitants have stopped trying to resolve and that is, perhaps, the deepest fact of living in Wjell: you are inside something that is alive, and it is not alive like you, and you will never fully understand what it is like to be it, and it will never fully understand what it is like to be you, and you are together anyway, metabolically coupled, architecturally entangled, and the entanglement is the home.

The green light does not change. The water does not stop. The walls do not stop. You acclimate, or you do not. The inhabitants have. They have been shaped by the shaping they do, and they have the quality of people who have lived for a long time in a place that is alive — a quality of patience that is not calm but *practiced*, a quality of attention that is not anxious but *continuous*, a way of moving that is not careful but *attuned*, the way a surfer moves on a wave, not fighting it, not passive to it, reading it, riding it, staying upright by means of a relationship that is older than any single ride.

The water moves. The walls breathe. The mota cross your wrist. Somewhere, a wall is skrylling. Somewhere, a channel is vrenning. Somewhere, the system is producing a form that no one asked for, and the inhabitants will find it in the morning, and they will look at it, and they will wonder, and they will prune around it, and they will go on.
