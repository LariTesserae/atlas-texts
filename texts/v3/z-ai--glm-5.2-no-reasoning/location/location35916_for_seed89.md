# The Coil

There is a throat in the mountain that breathes.

You reach it through a chimney — a vertical fissure in basalt, slick with condensation, barely wide enough for shoulders. The climb takes forty minutes. Handholds are not carved. They are *eaten* — shallow concavities where something acidic, decades or centuries ago, dissolved the stone in regular intervals. You climb a digestive tract.

The fissure opens without warning into a chamber.

---

It is not large, as such places are measured. Perhaps forty meters at its widest. A cathedral would dwarf it. But the Coil does not deal in width. It deals in depth — the floor falls away in a spiral, a terraced descent cut into the rock by a process no geologist would sign their name to. The steps are uniform. The steps are wrong. They are too even for water, too irregular for hands. Each one bears a shallow groove along its leading edge, as though something dragged itself in a continuous downward spiral for a span of time that the mind prefers not to calculate.

The walls are basalt. The walls are *alive*.

---

Here is what you notice first, after the drop in temperature — because the Coil is warmer than the fissure, not cooler, and this is the first wrong thing — and after the darkness, which is not absolute but near enough:

The sound.

It is not one sound. It is a *layering*. From below, a low thrumming, felt in the sternum before it is heard, like a bass string vibrating against a wooden body. From the walls, a clicking — arrhythmic, widespread, the sound of ten thousand small impacts happening in overlapping waves. And from everywhere at once, a wet chittering, high and fine and constant, the way a kettle is constant just before it screams. Except the Coil never reaches that pitch. It holds. It sustains. It has been sustaining for a very long time.

The acoustic reflection makes the chamber feel smaller than it is. Sound bounces from the curved walls and returns compressed, layered over itself, so the air itself seems thick with vibration. You feel pressure in your ears. You feel it in your teeth.

---

The second thing: the light.

There is none that is not biological.

Along the lower walls — the first two terraces of the spiral — a faint bioluminescence leaks from cracks in the basalt. Blue-green. Cold in hue, though the air is warm and damp and close. It pulses, but not in synchrony. Each crack has its own rhythm, offset from its neighbors, so the glow travels across the wall in slow waves that never quite repeat. You can see by it, barely. Your hands, if you hold them up, are pale shapes. Your fingers cast no shadows because the light comes from everywhere and nowhere.

Below the second terrace, the light thickens. More cracks. More glow. The rock itself seems to thin — the basalt becomes almost membranous, translucent in places, and behind it, the blue-green pulses are stronger. Something lives *inside* the rock. Or the rock has grown around something. The distinction is not clear.

---

The air is wet. Not raining — not quite — but the humidity is total. Moisture condenses on every surface, including your skin, and it has a taste — mineral, faintly alkaline, with an undertone of something organic and sweet, like overripe fruit left in a sealed room. Your clothes darken within minutes. Your hair lies flat.

Water collects in the grooves of the spiral steps. Thin films. They catch the bioluminescence and hold it, so the descent appears to be lit from below by a thread of cold fire winding down and down and down into a darkness that the blue-green light does not penetrate.

If you kneel and touch the water — and you will, because curiosity is a disease the Coil rewards — it is warm. Body temperature. You cannot feel it on your skin. You can only see the disturbance your finger makes.

---

And then: the fauna.

This is what the Coil is *for*.

The steps are not steps. The walls are not walls. The grooves are not erosion.

The Coil is a nest.

---

They are insects, though the word is inadequate. Not beetles — their bodies are too elongated, too segmented, too flexible. Not centipedes — too few legs, and the legs are jointed wrong, bending backward at the knee-equivalent. They are pale, almost translucent — you can see the dark threads of their digestive tracts through the cuticle. They are the size of a human hand. They are the size of a human forearm. A few, deeper down, are the size of a human thigh, and these are not translucent at all but the deep mottled brown of old blood.

They cover the walls.

Not in clusters. Not in colonies. In *continuum*. The surface of the basalt, from the first terrace to the visible bottom of the spiral, is a moving pelt of pale chitin and slow, grasping legs. They do not move quickly. They move *constantly*. Small adjustments. A leg here. A mandible there. Antennae sweeping in long, patient arcs across the backs of their neighbors. The clicking is their feet. The chittering is their mandibles. The thrumming — the low, felt-not-heard thrumming — is their bodies, tensing and releasing against the stone in some collective rhythmic process that has no name in any human science.

They are not aggressive. They do not react to your presence. You are not their concern. You are not large enough, warm enough, or wet enough to be their concern.

But the ones on the lower terraces are larger, and you cannot see the bottom.

---

The spiral descends. The light increases as you go down — more bioluminescence, thicker walls of living rock, more and larger creatures. The air grows warmer. The moisture increases. The sound does not get louder — it was already at its full volume at the top — but it gets *deeper*, as though the thrumming is slowing, dropping in register, approaching a frequency that you feel in your gut rather than hear.

The grooves in the steps grow deeper. In places, they are worn through the stone entirely, and you can see that the spiral is not solid — it is hollow, and inside the hollow, pale shapes move in a slow helical procession downward, leg over leg over leg, and the inside of the structure is as covered with life as the outside.

You realize, gradually, that the chamber is not a chamber. It is a shell. The spiral is not carved. It is *secreted* — built up layer by layer over centuries by the creatures themselves, a structure of processed mineral and chitin and dried fluid, and the basalt you thought you were climbing through is the mountain's original stone *infected* by the structure, the boundary between the two blurred and gradual.

The Coil has been here for a long time. It is in no hurry. It grows by millimeters per year. The creatures inside it live and die and are consumed by the next generation and their shells become wall and their fluids become mortar and the spiral descends one fraction of a millimeter further into the earth.

---

There are no artifacts. No tool marks. No evidence that any human has ever stood where you are standing. The only technology here is the technology of the body — mandible and gland and chitin and patience. It is more sophisticated than anything you have carried in. It has solved problems of architecture, climate regulation, acoustic engineering, and collective logistics without a single thought. It does not think. It does not need to.

If you sit on the first terrace — and you can, if you are willing to let the creatures walk across your boots, your hands, your knees, which they will do without interest, their feet tiny and cool and precise on your skin — and you listen, you begin to hear structure in the sound. The thrumming is not random. It has a period — long, maybe four minutes, maybe five — and the clicking rises and falls with it, and the chittering rises and falls with the clicking, and the whole vast acoustic edifice is *breathing*. The Coil is breathing. The creatures are its lungs, its diaphragm, its alveoli. The chamber is an organism, or the organism is a chamber, and the distinction between architecture and biology was always a fiction you brought with you and the Coil does not share it.

---

The moisture increases as you descend. The air near the bottom of the visible spiral is almost fog — a warm mist that clings to the skin, that beads on the chitin of the creatures, that runs in tiny rivers down the spiral grooves and into the dark below. The light is brightest here, a cold blue glow that makes the mist luminous, that turns the moving walls into something like a living aurora seen from inside.

There are plants. A few. Sparse. Ferns — no, not ferns. Something convergent. Fronds with no vascular structure, growing directly from the moist cracks in the wall, pale and flat and translucent, feeding on the bioluminescence and the moisture and nothing else. They are part of the system. The creatures do not eat them. The creatures eat each other, and the stone, and the slow mineral drip from the mountain above. The plants eat the light. The light comes from the organisms in the walls. The organisms in the walls feed on the byproducts of the creatures' collective metabolism. The circle is closed. The circle has been closed for a very long time.

---

The bottom of the Coil is not visible. The spiral continues past the limit of the bioluminescence, down into a warm dark that smells of wet stone and chitin and something else — something sweet, something chemical, something that makes the back of the tongue tingle. The sound comes from there. The thrumming. The breathing. Whatever is at the center of the Coil, whatever the spiral is *for* — it is below.

The creatures on the lowest visible terrace are the largest. They do not move. They are fixed to the stone, their legs sunk into the surface, their mandibles working slowly in the air. Their backs are crusted with a pale deposit — mineral, crystalline, and where the moisture touches it, it glows brighter than anything else in the chamber. They are the oldest. They are becoming the wall. In another year, or ten, or a hundred, they will be part of the structure, their bodies integrated into the spiral, and new ones will have taken their place, and the Coil will be one millimeter deeper, and the mountain will be one millimeter less.

---

If you stay too long, the moisture settles into your lungs. The sound settles into your bones. The chittering becomes a texture, then a silence, then a language — not one you understand, but one you recognize. The rhythm of the Coil is the rhythm of a heartbeat, or a tide, or a season. It is patient. It is complete. It does not need you.

It is not hostile. It is not welcoming. It is *indifferent* in the way that a glacier is indifferent, or a star — vast processes that include you only as a temporary adjacency, a warm shape that will be gone soon and will not be remembered.

The Coil was here before you. The Coil will be here after you. The Coil is breathing now, in the dark beneath the mountain, and the sound it makes is the sound of something very old continuing to do the only thing it knows how to do.

You can leave. The chimney is behind you. The climb is hard but possible. The creatures will not stop you. They will not notice you. You are not part of the pattern. You are a gap in the pattern, and the pattern flows around you and continues.

You leave. The fissure closes behind you. The mountain breathes out, and the air is cold, and the sky is dark, and the sound of the Coil fades to a memory of pressure in the teeth and a tingling at the back of the tongue, and you do not go back.

You think about it.

You do not go back.
