# The Green Choir

## I.

There is a moment, before understanding, when you think the sound is wind.

It isn't. There is no wind here. What you hear is the *speaking*.

---

The land lies low — barely land at all. A shelf of black peat, spongy and new, extending further than a person could walk in a full day. It heaves in slow swells like the hide of something breathing. In every direction, walls of green rise: not hills, not ridges, but accumulations. Piled growth. The kind of density that stops being individual plants and becomes something more like weather.

You arrived at the edge of a settlement. The word *settlement* feels wrong. There was no settling. There was growth, and then there was inhabitation, and the two are the same act.

## II. Structures.

The architecture is not separate from the ecosystem — it *is* the ecosystem, shaped by intent or by something that resembles intent.

Hollow tree-trunks the width of small houses form the primary walls. Their interiors have been hollowed not by tools (there are no tools, there have never been tools here) but by directional growth — the trees grew *around* spaces, leaving chambers inside themselves like hearts leaving chambers. You can walk inside a living tree and stand in a room six meters across, the walls pulsing with sap, the floor a smooth wooden surface that grew this way deliberately.

Roofs are living mats of interlocking vine and broadleaf, woven not by hands but by the plants themselves — species that evolved to climb over one another and interlock in lattice patterns, species that were *selected* over generations for this tendency until the lattice became architecture. Rainwater (when it comes) filters through the mesh and arrives inside as a fine, clean mist. You taste it on your skin. It tastes like chlorophyll. It tastes like the color green.

Paths exist but they move. What was a corridor yesterday is a wall today. What was a wall is a corridor. The settlement is not a frozen blueprint but a slow argument between growing things, and the residents — if you can call them residents — live inside the argument.

## III. The Sound.

The sound is why this place is not empty.

There are no animals. Zero. No birds, no insects, no slithering things. And yet the air is full. The sound is *plant* sound. It is the sound of the green world speaking, and it is *deafening*.

The first layer is the low vibration — a subsonic thrum you feel in your sternum before you hear it. This is the xylem pumping. This is the sap. Every large plant here is a circulatory system under pressure, and the pressure generates sound. With thousands of trunks pumping simultaneously, the bass note is constant, physical, like standing on a drum skin while the drum is being played.

Above this: the creaking. Living wood under tension, bending under its own weight, fibers sliding against fibers, branches torquing against trunks. The groaning is everywhere. It sounds like a forest in a storm, but there is no storm. This is simply the sound of growing. The sound of living structure straining against its own mass.

Above this: the rustling. But it is not wind rustling leaves. It is the leaves themselves *moving*. Some of these plants have evolved rapid leaf-movement — not the slow heliotropism of sunflowers, but fast, twitching, insect-fast adjustments. Whole canopies of broadleaf plants shift and shimmer in cascades of micro-motion, producing a dry, papery hiss that sounds like static, like rain on tin, like ten thousand people whispering the same word at different speeds.

And above this, the thing that breaks you:

The singing.

Some of the plants here have hollow seed-pods the size of fists. When these pods dry and crack open, they produce tones — pure, clear tones, different pitches depending on the pod's size and wall thickness. The plants have been selected (or have selected themselves, or have been selected by something else) for harmonic relationships between pod sizes. When a patch of these plants releases seeds simultaneously — triggered by humidity shifts, by vibration, by the passage of large bodies through the undergrowth — the result is *chord*.

Not song. Not melody. But chords. Masses of tone, layered and dissonant and sometimes, for brief seconds, heartbreakingly consonant. The sound rolls across the settlement in waves. It echoes off the walls of living wood. It *sustains*.

You cannot hear yourself think. You try to speak and your voice is absorbed into the green noise, swallowed like a stone dropped into a river. The sound does not stop. It has not stopped, you realize, for a very long time. Perhaps it has never stopped.

## IV. The Moisture.

Water here does not flow. It *appears*.

The air is warm and saturated. Not humid in the ordinary sense — humid implies a baseline dryness being interrupted. Here, the air itself is nearly liquid. You breathe and your lungs feel coated. Your skin is never dry. Every surface is slick with a film of water that seems to condense directly out of the atmosphere, as if the air is sweating.

In the lower paths, the ones that run between root masses and fallen trunks, water pools in shallow depressions. Not streams — *pools*, scattered, disconnected, each one a mirror of dark water reflecting the green ceiling. The pools are warm. Body-temperature warm. They do not smell of stagnant water; they smell of *living* water, of the inside of a stem.

Occasionally, a rain arrives. Not from clouds — from the canopy itself. The upper layers of the vegetation condense moisture from the air above and release it downward in sudden, localized downpours. You can see rain falling in one area and dryness ten meters away. The plants have become their own weather system. The water cycles through the air, through the plants, through the air again, and the ground is just where it pauses.

## V. The Wrongness.

This is where it becomes difficult to write clearly, because the place does not behave according to the rules that make writing possible.

You notice it first in the paths. You enter a corridor formed by two root-walls and walk for several minutes and emerge where you started. Not lost — the path simply *turned*, in a way that paths should not turn, in a way that geometry does not allow for. You walked straight. You arrived back.

You notice it next in the sky. What you can see of it through the canopy is not sky-blue. It is green. Not the green of leaves seen from below — the *sky itself* is green, a pale, luminous green, as if the atmosphere has been dyed. When you focus on it, it shifts, becomes briefly blue, then green again. You cannot tell which color is real. You begin to suspect neither is.

You notice it in the plants themselves. Some of the trunks have features that are not botanical. You see a bark pattern that looks exactly like a face — not approximately, not in the way of pareidolia, but *exactly*, with specific detail, with expression. You see another trunk with what appears to be text carved into it, in a script you almost recognize, in a language that sits on the edge of memory. You reach out to touch the text and the bark grows over your fingers, gently, and releases.

The plants are not just growing. They are *responding*. They are responding to something that is not you, not the environment, not any stimulus you can identify. They are responding to something behind the visible world, and the responses are becoming *form* — faces, words, structures that resemble rooms but are not rooms, spaces that resemble places you have been but are not those places.

A vine grows toward you. Not fast. Not threatening. It grows toward you with what can only be called *curiosity*. It extends a tendril and touches your wrist and stays there, not gripping, not wrapping, just *touching*. It is warm. It pulses at a rate that matches your heartbeat. It has learned your pulse in the moment of contact.

You pull away. The vine stays extended, reaching, patient.

## VI. The Shape of the Place.

The settlement — the *grown-place* — extends across a region of perhaps several square kilometers. You cannot measure it accurately because the interior distances do not match the exterior distances. Walk the perimeter and it is a specific size. Walk the interior and it is larger. Not dramatically. Just enough to notice. Just enough to make you doubt your feet.

There are chambers within the growth that serve as communal spaces — you know this because the spaces are shaped for gathering, oriented around central root-formations that resemble platforms or tables, though no one sits at them. There are narrower corridors that lead to smaller chambers, private spaces, each one grown around a single large seed or bulb that glows with a faint bioluminescence — warm, golden, the only light source other than the green sky filtering through the canopy.

There are no residents visible. No people. No animals. No bones. No evidence of anyone having lived here, and yet the spaces are *maintained*. The paths are kept clear. The chambers are kept open. The lattice-roofs are kept intact. Something is doing this, or something has done this so thoroughly that the doing has become self-sustaining, the maintenance embedded in the growth patterns themselves.

You think: this is a city built by plants for plants. Or: this is a city that *is* plants. Or: this is not a city at all. This is a single organism and I am inside it.

## VII. The Dynamic.

The place does not hold still.

While you stand in one chamber, the walls shift. Not collapsing — *reorganizing*. A new corridor opens where there was wall. A wall closes where there was corridor. The interior of the settlement is restructuring itself constantly, slowly, like a brain thinking.

You hear the restructuring as sound. The creaking is not random — it is the sound of the place *becoming*. Every groan of wood is a wall forming. Every crack of seed-pod is a door opening. Every chord that rolls across the canopy is the settlement speaking to itself, adjusting, reconfiguring, arguing, agreeing.

The turbulence is not violent. It is relentless. Everything is changing all the time. The path you walked to get here will not be the path you walk to leave. The chamber you are in will not be this chamber tomorrow. The face in the bark will be a different face. The text will be different text. The vine that touched your wrist will have grown elsewhere, will be touching something else, will have learned a different pulse.

You realize: this is why the sound is constant. The sound of growth is the sound of *change*, and this place is changing at every moment, at every scale, from the cellular level (cells dividing, fibers extending, xylem differentiating) to the structural (walls becoming corridors becoming walls) to the conceptual (the faces in the bark shifting, the text in the wood rewriting itself).

The place is not stable. The place is not unstable. The place is *metastable* — it holds a shape, but the shape is motion. The shape is a verb.

## VIII. What Lives Here.

You asked about fauna. There is none. But you are beginning to understand that this is not because nothing lives here. It is because what lives here is not *fauna*.

The plants are aware. Not in the way you are aware — not with a brain, not with a nervous system — but with something. The vine that touched your wrist and matched your heartbeat. The face in the bark that looked at you with an expression you could not name. The corridors that opened and closed with something that might be intention.

They are not individuals. There are no individual plants here, you realize. The roots beneath your feet interconnect in a mycelial network so dense it functions as a single substrate. The branches above your head interlock in a canopy so interwoven that no single tree can be distinguished. This is not a forest. This is one thing, expressing itself as many forms, the way a single person expresses themselves as many thoughts.

It is aware of you. It has been aware of you since you entered. The vine that touched your wrist was not curiosity — it was *taking your measure*. The paths that turned you back were not malfunction — they were *redirecting* you. The faces in the bark were not pareidolia — they were *looking*.

You are not in a landscape. You are inside something's body. You are inside something's *mind*.

And the sound — the deafening, constant, layered, chord-producing sound — is not noise. It is not the sound of growth. It is the sound of *thought*.

This is what thinking sounds like when the thinker is a forest.

## IX. You Leave. Or You Try.

You retrace your steps. The path is different. You take another path. It leads somewhere you did not expect. You find a chamber you did not see before. In its center, a seed the size of your head, glowing, warm, pulsing at the rate of your heartbeat.

You did not have this heartbeat when you arrived. You had your own. The vine changed it. Or the air changed it. Or the sound changed it. You have been here long enough that your pulse has synchronized with the place, and you cannot tell whether this is adaptation or absorption.

The chord sounds again. It is close. It is around you. It is inside the chamber. You realize it is coming from the walls, from the floor, from the seed in the center, from the air itself.

You realize: you are inside the chord. You are inside the thought. The forest is thinking about you. It is thinking *with* you. Your heartbeat is part of the sound now. Your breathing is part of the sound. Your fear, your wonder, your desire to leave — these are notes in a chord you cannot hear from inside.

You walk. The path opens. You walk. The path opens. You walk.

You emerge at the edge, where the green walls thin and the black peat gives way to dry ground. The sound is still present, but dimmer now, like hearing a conversation through a wall. The air is drier. Your skin cools.

Your heartbeat is still synchronized. It has not returned to its old rhythm. You carry the forest's pulse with you, and you will carry it for a long time, and you will hear the chord in your sleep, and you will not be certain, ever again, whether you left the forest or whether the forest left a part of itself inside you.

Behind you, the sound does not stop. It has not stopped. It will not stop.

The green choir sings. It is always singing. It is singing now.
