# The Reedworks

The rain comes from below.

Not upward — not quite. But the condensation forms on the undersides of the great reed canopy forty feet overhead, gathers in slow, heavy beads along the parallel grooves of each stalk, and runs down by gravity to the lowest points of the arched ceiling, where it accumulates until the weight overcomes surface tension and a drop falls. Always falling. The air under the canopy tastes mineral and green, and the ground — if it can be called ground — is wet in the way a forest floor is wet after long rain, dark and yielding, though no rain has fallen from any sky in the memory of this place.

There is no sky here. Only reed.

---

The Reedworks occupy a hollow about two hundred meters across, bounded on all sides by the root-walls of the reed forest that extends — as far as anyone has determined — without end. The hollow is not natural. It was cut, or rather *hollowed*, generations ago, the dense packing of stalks removed down to the root layer and the roots trained to form a perimeter wall that has since calcified into something like wattle, something like bone. The root-wall is the architectural boundary. Beyond it, the reed forest stands close and dark, a vertical fibrous wilderness of stalks as thick as a man's torso rising without branching until they flower far above in the dim upper reaches. Within the hollow, the canopy still covers everything — the reeds at the perimeter lean inward, their upper portions arching over the clearing like the ribs of a long boat, so that the Reedworks exists in a permanent green twilight beneath living architecture.

It is cool here. The mass of living reed above and around holds temperature like a thatched roof, like a cave. The hollow breathes with a slow, large respiration that is not wind but transpiration — the collective exhale of thousands of stalks pumping moisture from the root mat into the air. It moves the air enough to stir the hanging lanterns.

---

The built structures within the hollow are sparse. This is the nature of the place: space is the resource, and space is what has been made, carefully, and not much of it. A few platforms raised on stilts cut from dead reed — kiln-dried, the stalks split and lashed into a material that is lighter than timber and nearly as strong. A processing shed, open-walled, where the harvest is worked. A drying frame. Two living shelters whose walls are living reed trained to grow in tight coils, forming domes about three meters across, their interiors dry and warm from the plants' own metabolic heat. The floor of each dome is root-mat, swept clean.

There is a pump. This is the industrial heart of the Reedworks, and it is modest: a mechanized press driven by a small steam engine that burns dried reed — the waste stalks, the fibrous residue — in a firebox that must be fed constantly. The press flattens and cures the reed stems into broad sheets, a material somewhere between paper and wood-panel, which is the primary product of this place. The sheets are taken out through the root-wall through a canal-lock that admits a narrow flat-bottomed barge. The barge moves along a waterway cut through the reed forest — a channel maintained by constant labor, since the reeds grow inward at the rate of about a centimeter per day. The channel is the road. The reed forest is the wilderness. The barge is the only vehicle.

The engine makes the only mechanical sound: a low, rhythmic chuffing that carries through the hollow like a heartbeat. It is not loud. It is insistent. When it stops — for maintenance, for lack of fuel, during the night — the silence is enormous and physical, a pressure, and the other sounds that were hidden beneath it become audible: the creak of living reed swelling with moisture, the faint ticking of stalks expanding, the far-off groan of a large reed somewhere in the forest succumbing to its own weight and folding down through the canopy in a slow, tearing collapse. And the water. The constant water. Drip and trickle and the soft gurgle of the channel where it passes through the lock.

---

Light is green. Not filtered — *generated*. The reed itself is faintly bioluminescent at its joints, the nodes producing a cold phosphorescence that is barely visible in daytime but becomes the primary light source after dark. It is enough to see by, not enough to read by. The workers hang kerosene lanterns from hooks driven into the stalks for tasks requiring detail — the press operation, the lashing of sheets, the maintenance of the lock gates. The lantern light is warm and small and seems precious against the living green glow, a human light in a vegetable world.

In the daytime — if it is day, which can only be known by the faint brightening of the canopy far above, where some light penetrates from whatever lies over the reed forest — the hollow is lit by a diffused, underwater-seeming luminance. Shadows are soft and multiple. Nothing casts a single hard shadow. The light seems to come from the air itself, from the moisture in it, from the pale undersides of reed leaves high overhead.

---

The root-mat that forms the ground is spongy, dark, and threaded with water. It is not soil. Nothing decomposes here in the usual way — there are no microbes, no fungi visible, no earthworms. The reed forest consumes its own dead. Fallen stalks are colonized by the root-mat and broken down over months into a fibrous pulp that is re-absorbed. The ground is, in this sense, alive. It is not advisable to stand in one place for very long without moving. Workers wear broad-soled clogs to distribute their weight.

There are pools. Small ones, irregular, scattered across the root-mat where the surface has subsided or where a channel has been cut and abandoned. The water in them is clear and faintly astringent, tannin-stained, drawn up from below by the reed's root pressure and expressed at the surface. It is drinkable. It tastes of cellulose and iron.

The moisture economy of the Reedworks is entirely dependent on the reed itself. The plants draw water from a deep saturated layer — the reed forest sits over what must be an enormous aquifer, though no one has reached its surface — and pump it upward through their vascular systems. The condensation, the drips, the pools, the channel water — all of it is reed-water, cycled through living tissue. Cut the reed and the water would stop. The hollow would dry. The channel would empty. The Reedworks exists only because the reed exists, and the reed is alive, and it is growing.

---

The workers — there are perhaps fifteen, perhaps twenty, the number fluctuates — are quiet people. They speak in low voices not from custom but because sound carries strangely in the hollow, the curved canopy focusing it in unpredictable ways. A conversation held at normal volume on the processing platform can be heard clearly at the far root-wall but not at all ten meters away. The acoustic space is folded, uneven, full of dead spots and bright spots. The workers know the sound map of the hollow the way a sailor knows shoals, and they navigate it instinctively, moving their conversations to the right spots, falling silent in the wrong ones.

The work is slow and continuous. Harvest: select mature stalks at the perimeter, cut them at the base with a long saw — the reed is dense and silica-laced, difficult to cut, and the saws dull quickly. Drag the stalk to the processing shed. Strip the leaves (they are fed back to the root-mat). Split the stem. Feed the sections into the press. Stack the sheets. Load the barge. Send the barge out through the lock. Maintain the channel. Maintain the engine. Maintain the root-wall, which means training new growth to replace the sections that have begun to ossify and crack. Maintain the domes. Maintain the channel. The channel. The channel is always narrowing. The reed grows inward always.

This is the fundamental labor: keeping the space open against the pressure of the living substrate. Everything here is temporary. The hollow was made by cutting reed and it is maintained by cutting reed, and if the cutting stops, the reed will close in at a centimeter a day, then faster as the channel narrows and the light decreases, and within a season the hollow would be indistinguishable from the surrounding forest. The platforms would be swallowed. The domes would be absorbed. The engine would be colonized and fed to the root-mat.

The workers know this. It is not a fear. It is a condition.

---

There are no animals here. No birds, no insects, no fish in the channel. The reed forest is a pure-plant ecosystem — it supports itself through closed nutrient cycling, the dead feeding the living, the living producing the dead, water drawn from below and returned through transpiration and drip. The only non-plant presence is the workers, and they are recent, a thin crust of metabolism on the surface of an ancient system.

If you stand at the center of the hollow at night, when the engine is off and the workers are sleeping in their domes, you can hear the reed growing. Not a crack, not a pop — a sustained, almost sub-audible hum, the sound of cellulose being laid down at the cellular level multiplied by a hundred thousand stalks, the sound of a living organism the size of a country quietly and continuously becoming more of itself. The ground vibrates with it. The pools ripple without wind.

It is quiet. It is active. It is alive, and it does not need you.
