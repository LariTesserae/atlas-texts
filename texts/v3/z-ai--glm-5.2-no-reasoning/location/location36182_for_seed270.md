# The Aggregate

It was not built. It *accreted*.

---

You find it through a fault in plaster — the kind of gap that opens in the wall of an old building when the foundation shifts one millimeter too far. Not a door. Not a passage. A rupture barely wide enough to admit your shoulders sideways, exhaling cold air that smells of copper and chalk. You have to turn your head to fit. Then you are inside, and the world behind you becomes irrelevant, because the space here is so full there is almost no room for you.

They call it the Aggregate, though *who* calls it that is unclear. The name was scratched into a brass plate screwed to the inner wall of the entry fissure, in a hand that lettered each character with mechanical precision. The font is industrial. The screws are flathead. The brass has oxidized to a toxic green-black. That plate is the last familiar thing.

---

## The Space

The chamber — if *chamber* is even the right word for something that was never carved but only ever *filled* — extends perhaps forty meters in its longest dimension, perhaps fifteen at its widest, perhaps eight at its highest where the ceiling vaults into a darkness packed solid with machinery. But these measurements mean little, because the interior has no clean geometry. It has the geometry of accumulation. Of things pressed against other things until they deformed, and then more things pressed against those, and so on, for what must have been an extraordinary length of time.

You are in a pocket. A bubble of absence inside a solid mass of material and mechanism, like the last pocket of air in a jar of sand. The mass around you is not random. It is *settled* — settled the way sediment settles, the way landslides settle, the way ruin settles when it has been falling for so long that falling has become indistinguishable from lying still. This is the terminal geometry. The final arrangement. The Aggregate has been finding its shape for so long that it has found it, and the shape it found is: full.

The floor under your boots is not a floor. It is the topmost layer of compressed material — a stratigraphic cross-section exposed by accident or design. You can read the layers. The lowest visible band is a dark volcanic glass, fractured into cleavage planes that catch light like oil on water. Above that, a layer of corroded iron filings fused into a brittle scoria. Above that, paper — or something like paper, compressed to the density of shale, its edges fused, its original content reduced to faint repeating patterns that might have been text. Above that, a biological mat, desiccated and mineralized, pebbled with the casts of things that once lived in it. And above that, the surface you stand on: a granular, almost sandy composite of all of the above, ground fine by the constant motion of the things that live here.

The ceiling is low enough to touch. In places you must crouch. The ceiling is not a ceiling but the underside of the next layer of mass — ductwork, cable runs, corroded tank-bodies, the collapsed frames of machines whose purpose is no longer determinable. Some of it is very old. Some of it is merely old. The distinction matters only to the things that live here, and they do not care about your distinctions.

There are no walls. There are *edges* — places where the pocket of space narrows and the mass closes in, where you must press your body between cold metal and colder stone to move from one sub-chamber to the next. The mass is not hostile. It is merely *present*. It was here first.

## The Atmosphere

The air is cool. Not cold — not the cold of absence, of space, of vacuum — but the specific cool of a basement, of a cave, of a room that has never seen the sun and has no intention of ever seeing it. The temperature sits at perhaps twelve degrees Celsius and does not fluctuate. It is the temperature of deep geology, of thermal mass so enormous that seasonal variation cannot penetrate it. The Aggregate has been at this temperature for longer than the building above it has existed.

The air is dense. Not with moisture — there is no moisture here, none worth speaking of; the water parameter is functionally zero, and what little dampness exists is locked in mineral structures, in the crystal lattices of gypsum and the hydration shells of ancient salts. The air is dense with *particulate*. With dust so fine it behaves like a gas. With the aerosolized remnants of ten thousand processes — metallic, mineral, organic-in-the-distant-past. You taste it immediately: copper, chalk, the ferric tang of old blood that has long since dried to powder and been re-aerosolized by the motion of bodies through the space. Your lips go chalky. Your tongue coats. You breathe through fabric and the fabric turns gray.

There is light. Not much, but enough. It comes from the mass itself — from the bioluminescence of the things that live here, which is constant and pale, a cold blue-white that renders everything in the manner of a surgical theater, and from a few functioning industrial fixtures embedded in the ceiling mass: glass-and-iron housings containing filaments that still glow, drawing power from sources you cannot identify. The filaments flicker at a frequency just below perception, creating a stroboscopic effect that makes motion in the Aggregate appear to stutter, to jump, to proceed by frames. The combination — bioluminescent blue-white and flickering amber electric — produces a light that is normal in quantity but deeply wrong in quality. Things cast two shadows. Sometimes three.

## The Inhabitants

This is the part that stops you.

The Aggregate is *full of life*. Not plant life — there is no photosynthesis here, no chlorophyll, no green. Not life as you would recognize it from a walk in a forest. But life in the way a reef is life, a tidepool is life, a carcass is life: the dense, seething, layered, interdependent explosion of organisms into every available niche, and here the niches are narrow, and the organisms are narrow, and there are *so many of them*.

They are arthropodal. That is the closest taxonomic handle you can apply, though the taxonomy is wrong, or you are wrong, or both. They have segmented bodies and jointed appendages and chitinous exoskeletons, but the segmentation follows rules that are not quite insect, not quite crustacean, not quite myriapod. They are like nothing so much as the creatures of the Burgess Shale — Opabinia, Anomalocaris, Hallucigenia — the experimental arthropods of the Cambrian, before life had settled on its final forms. Except these have had *far* longer than the Cambrian to evolve, and they have evolved not in an ocean but in the interstices of an industrial ruin, and it shows.

The first thing you see is a carpet.

The floor of the chamber — every surface of the chamber that is not vertical — is covered in a living carpet of small, flat, overlapping organisms like pillbugs but with twenty-four legs instead of fourteen and a segmented carapace that is translucent and faintly luminous, the blue-white light filtering up through their bodies from organs within. They are the size of a thumbnail. There are millions of them. They move in constant, slow, grinding motion — not in any direction, but in a *procession*, a circulation, a circular migration that covers the entire floor like a conveyor belt of chitin. They are eating. What they are eating is the surface layer of the floor itself — the granular composite — grinding it between specialized mouthparts, extracting something from it, excreting something back. The surface they produce is smoother than the surface they consume. They are *polishing*. They have been polishing for a very long time.

When you step, they do not scatter. They do not react. You are not a predator to them. You are not anything to them. You are a large object that compresses some of them and does not compress others, and the ones that are compressed die and are eaten within seconds by their neighbors, and the gap fills, and the procession continues. You hear the sound of them: a dry, continuous, rasping whisper, like fine sandpaper drawn across sandpaper, and it is the loudest sound in the Aggregate, and it never stops.

Above the carpet, in the middle layer of the space — the layer between your knees and your head — the larger organisms move.

There are species here. Dozens. Perhaps hundreds. The density is so high that you cannot look in any direction without seeing motion. The space is *saturated* with life, the way a rainforest canopy is saturated, the way a coral reef is saturated, every cubic centimeter claimed, occupied, utilized. They crawl on the mass.
