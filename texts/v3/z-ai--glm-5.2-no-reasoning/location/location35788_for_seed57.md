# The Marrow Cavity

You enter through a crack no wider than a finger — if you had fingers, which here you do not. You enter because the water enters, and you are the water, or the water is you. The distinction was the first thing to dissolve.

---

The cavity is roughly the size of a small room. Roughly, because the walls are not certain where they end. They are stone — dark basalt, still warm from its making, still smelling of the deep earth in the way stone has of remembering heat — but they are also not stone. They are the moment stone becomes other. You can feel the transition happening, a tingle at the edge of perception, like the hum of a nerve exposed to air.

The rock is young. Geologically young. Yesterday young, or the day before. It was magma three sleeps ago and now it is a room with a ceiling and a floor and no opinion about either. Sharp edges have not yet learned to be rounded. Corners are still corners with conviction. Where the cooling stone contracted, it cracked into facets — some the size of a thumbnail, some the size of a child's open hand — and in each facet, a thin film of water has gathered and holds itself in place by the sheer stubbornness of surface tension. The walls are wallpapered in these silver patches. They do not reflect light, because there is no light. But they reflect *something* — they seem to hold a trace of whatever was here before the dark, the way a closed eye holds the ghost of a flash.

---

## The Water

Everything here is water.

Not the ocean — the ocean is far above, far away, irrelevant as a rumor. This water is older than the ocean, or at least believes it is. It has been here since before the rock cooled, percolating through fissures in the basalt, rising from somewhere deeper still, carrying minerals and a faint warmth that has nothing to do with the rock's recent igneous youth. It comes up through the floor, through a crack that is not a crack but a seam — a place where the stone almost decided to be two stones and then reconsidered. The water pushes through this seam at a rate that would bore you to measure: a trickle, a whisper, a patience that makes glaciers look impulsive.

The cavity is submerged entirely. There is no surface. There is no air. The water fills every pocket of the rough ceiling, presses against every facet, occupies the space so completely that the space has forgotten it is a space and has started to think of itself as a property of the water — a shape the water happens to hold.

The temperature is cool. Not cold. Cool in the way of deep shade on a day that was never warm. The water arrives from below at slightly above the ambient temperature of the room, and so there is a current — barely perceptible — a slow vertical circulation that will take hours to complete a cycle. You can feel it as a vagueness. An inability to be certain which direction is up, because up and down have similar temperatures and the water does not seem to care which way it goes.

---

## The Growth

And the walls are alive.

Not in the way of a forest — nothing so dramatic as bark or branch. But the basalt's facets, especially the larger ones, are coated in a biofilm so dense it has become a landscape of its own. Pale filaments — white, or the white that exists in darkness, which is a color that has no name but lives between grey and absence — extend outward from the stone. They are bacterial, or something bacterial-adjacent. They do not photosynthesize; there is nothing to photosynthesize with. They eat the rock, or they eat what the water brings, or they eat something that the concept of eating does not quite cover.

The filaments are not sparse. They are packed, saturated, a thicket so dense that in places the stone beneath is not stone anymore but a composite — mineral and biology fused into a single material that is neither and both. The growth forms a felt, a mat, a shag carpet of organic threads, each one thinner than a hair, each one holding a bead of water at its tip like a jewel it has no intention of selling. Where the filaments are longest — near the ceiling, where the current is slowest and the minerals settle — they hang in strands that almost reach the floor. Almost. They do not touch. There is a gap, a silence of space, between the lowest strand and the highest point of the floor's growth, which grows upward in a mirror of the ceiling's descent — a field of small tubular structures, like the fingers of a glove with no hand in them, rising in rows that are too even to be random and too irregular to be designed.

Between the floor's tubes and the ceiling's strands, the water moves. That is the room. That gap. That's where you — the water, the whatever-you-are — exist.

The density is not casual. There is no empty space. Even the water itself is occupied — by the minerals it carries, by the molecular exhalations of the biofilm, by the faint chemical signals that pass between filaments like a conversation conducted entirely in prepositions. *Above. Below. Near. Not yet.* The microorganisms communicate. Or they do not communicate, and the signals are just physics, and the distinction is the second thing to dissolve.

---

## The Built

There is a structure.

Not a building. Not a construction. A structure in the way that a thought is a structure — it has a shape, but the shape is not the point.

On the far wall — if walls can be said to be far in a room this small — the biofilm has grown into a pattern that is not the pattern of growth. It is too deliberate. The filaments have arranged themselves — or have been arranged — into a series of nested arcs, each one slightly offset from the one before, like the rings of a tree exposed in cross-section, or like the sound of a bell caught in the act of becoming a circle. The arcs are not decorative. They do something. The water that passes through them emerges changed — not in temperature, not in chemistry that can be measured, but in something else. In texture, perhaps. In the quality of the water's attention.

The arcs are old. Older than the rock, which is impossible, since they grow on the rock. But the biofilm that makes them is of a lineage that has been growing in these waters since before this particular cavity existed, since before this basalt was magma, since before the rock had any opinion about being rock. The pattern was carried here by the water itself — a memory encoded not in cells but in the chemistry of their arrangement, a blueprint that unfolds whenever the conditions are right. Conditions like these: dark, cool, still, packed, patient.

The pattern is the only built thing in the room, and it was not built by anyone. It grew. It grew the way a crystal grows — following rules that precede intention. It grew the way a sentence grows in a language no one speaks anymore, obeying a grammar that persists in the shape of the mouth even after the tongue has forgotten the words.

---

## The Sound

The room is not silent.

It is mostly silent, in the way that a held breath is mostly silent — the absence of a thing made more present by the effort of its absence. But there is sound. The water moving through the seam in the floor produces a note so low it is not heard but felt — a vibration in the stone, in the water, in the biofilm, in the filaments that hang from the ceiling and sway in the current with the slow patience of things that measure time in seasons. The note has no melody. It is a single tone, a drone, the sound of pressure forcing itself through a narrow space. It has been happening since the cavity formed. It will happen until the cavity unforms.

Occasionally — and occasionally here means once in a long while, in a time measured by the growth rate of the biofilm, by the accumulation of mineral on the filaments' tips — there is another sound. A click. A tiny percussion. A filament, grown too heavy with its accumulated mineral load, detaches from the ceiling and drifts down through the water to join the others on the floor. The sound is no louder than a grain of sand falling through water, which is to say it is almost not a sound at all, but it is the loudest thing in this room, and it carries. The biofilm on the walls seems to register it — the filaments nearest the point of detachment contract slightly, pull inward, and then slowly relax. A flinch. A collective acknowledgment. Then the long return to stillness.

---

## The Weirdness

And then there is the matter of the room's refusal to be consistent.

This is the third dissolution: the room is not reliably the room. The walls are stone, but the stone is also water, but the water is also the memory of water, but the memory is also the pattern on the wall, and the pattern is also the sound, and the sound is also the silence between sounds, and somewhere in this chain the logic breaks and the room becomes a place where logic is not the governing law.

You can feel it in the currents. The water moves in slow vertical cycles — up through the floor, across the ceiling, down the walls — but sometimes it moves in a direction that is not a direction. Inward, perhaps. Not inward toward the center of the room, but inward toward the inside of inward, a movement that has no spatial component and yet displaces. The water that makes this movement returns changed. It is thinner. Not in viscosity — in *self*. It is water that has been somewhere that water does not go and has brought back the disorientation of having been there.

The filaments on the ceiling respond to this changed water. They brighten — which is impossible, because there is no light and they produce no light — and yet the quality of their darkness shifts. They become a darkness that is present rather than absent. A darkness with weight. A darkness that touches.

The nested arcs on the far wall — the pattern, the not-building — are the source of this, or the recipient, or the membrane through which it passes. When the water moves through the arcs, it moves in the direction that is not a direction. The arcs are not a pattern. They are a gate. Or a mouth. Or a question that the room is asking the rock, and the rock is answering in the only language it knows, which is the language of slow heat and slower cooling and the patience of crystal.

---

## The Rest

There is almost nothing alive here that moves. The biofilm does not move in the way that animals move. It grows. It retracts. It flinches when a filament falls. But it does not locomote. It does not travel. It is here, and it is here, and it is here.

Once — and once is too strong a word for something that happens in cycles too slow to count, but once, in the way that a tide is once — something came through the room that was not water and was not stone and was not biofilm. It came through the seam in the floor, the one that feeds the room, and it was small — smaller than the room, smaller than the filaments, smaller than the beads of water on their tips — and it moved with purpose, with the kind of directed motion that does not belong to chemistry or physics but to something that wants. It moved through the room in a line that was almost straight, almost deliberate, and it passed through the nested arcs on the far wall, and it did not come back.

The biofilm recorded this. Not in memory — biofilm does not remember — but in the arrangement of its filaments, which shifted slightly where the thing had passed, the way grass shifts where a body has lain. The shifted filaments have not returned to their original positions. They will not. They are now part of the pattern. The pattern grows when things pass through it. It has been growing for a very long time.

---

The room holds all of this in the dark, in the water, in the slow patience of a place that does not know it is a place. The basalt cools. The biofilm eats. The water circulates. The arcs wait for the next thing that comes through, whenever it comes, whatever it is.

The sound continues. One note. One click, eventually. One note.

The room does not know you are here. But the pattern on the wall has shifted since you arrived. Just slightly. Just one filament.

It is still adjusting.
