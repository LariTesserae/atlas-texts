# The High City of the Unwinding

---

You come up into it through the pass, and the wind stops.

Not slows — *stops*. As if it had been a thing operating and someone somewhere ceased to operate it. You notice this because you had been leaning into it for hours, your body braced at an angle that now has no purpose, and for a moment you tilt forward before catching yourself. The silence that replaces the wind is not the silence of shelter. It is the silence of a room where something has been removed.

The city sits at roughly forty-six hundred meters, though the number means less here than it should. The air is thin. Not dangerously so — you can breathe, though each breath feels like it's missing a component, some density or moisture you're accustomed to. The cold is present but not aggressive. Maybe four degrees. The kind of cold that sits against your skin politely, waiting. It doesn't bite. It doesn't need to. Given enough time, it will simply replace you.

---

## The Terrain

The city is built on a shelf of mineral substrate that extends across a high plateau bounded on three sides by escarpments and on the fourth by a slope that falls away into haze and distance. The rock here is not like the rock below the pass. Below, there was granite, predictable, acting like rock acts. Up here the ground is a laminate — layers of something between shale and glass, compressed and heat-altered at some point in the deep past, now exposed and weathered into sheets that ring faintly underfoot. The color moves through a range: pewter, ash, a dull cyan in certain angles, occasionally a streak of something closer to amber where mineral deposits have oxidized. There are no pebbles. The erosion here is old and settled — the surface has found its form and holds it. Dust sits in shallow depressions but does not scatter. It is too fine, too compacted by time. It has the texture of pigment.

The escarpments are significant. The eastern one rises perhaps three hundred meters above the plateau in a near-vertical face. It is stratified, each layer slightly different in color and hardness, so that from a distance it looks like the cross-section of something sedimentary, which it is, but also like a page that has been written on many times. The western escarpment is lower and more broken, fractured into columns and overhangs. It catches what dim light there is and holds it longer than the eastern wall, so that as the day progresses, the western wall becomes the brighter surface, and the city turns its face toward it.

There is no water. Not in any form. No streams, no seeps, no ice, no moisture clinging to rock faces. The air is dry enough that you can feel it in your nasal passages within minutes. The substrate does not appear to have been shaped by water erosion — wind only, and time, and something else. The something else is harder to name.

---

## The City

It was built. That much is clear. It was built with intention, by hands or by processes that acted like hands, and it was built to a plan. But the plan is not entirely legible, and the building was done so long ago that the distinction between "built" and "geological" has begun to collapse in places.

The structures are mineral. Not stone in the sense of quarried blocks — the material is the same laminate substrate as the ground, cut or separated somehow into forms and assembled. The largest buildings are two and three stories, though "stories" is misleading because the interiors are not divided the way interiors are normally divided. You enter through an aperture — not a door; there is no frame, no hinge-mark, no threshold — and you find yourself in a space that extends through the full height of the structure. The walls are smooth but not finished. They have the quality of something that was *parted* rather than built. As if the mineral was split along a hidden grain and the resulting surfaces were used as-is.

There are dozens of structures. Perhaps a hundred and forty. They cluster more densely toward the plateau's center, thinning out toward the edges. Streets exist, or paths that function as streets — channels in the substrate where the surface has been worn slightly lower than the surrounding ground, suggesting long use. But the wear is old. Nothing has walked these paths in a very long time. The dust in them lies undisturbed.

The layouts are not orthogonal. The streets curve. They meet at angles that are not right angles but are also not random — they repeat. The same angles appear throughout the city, in the streets, in the shapes of the apertures, in the proportions of the structures. It is a system. You can feel the system. You cannot read it.

Some of the buildings contain things.

Not objects, exactly. In one, there are shallow basins set into the floor — six of them, arranged in a pattern that echoes the street layout outside. The basins are dry and have always been dry, you think, but their surfaces are stained, faintly, in rings, as if something once sat in them and left residue. In another building, the walls are marked with lines — incised into the mineral, shallow, regular, running floor to ceiling. They could be writing. They could be measurement. They could be decoration. There is no way to know, and the distinction may not have existed for whoever made them.

One structure near the plateau's edge is different. It is circular. Every other building is polygonal. This one is a smooth cylinder, maybe eight meters in diameter, maybe five meters tall, and its surface is not laminate but something denser, darker, with a faint sheen that the other structures lack. It has no aperture. No entrance. Whatever it is, it was not made to be entered. Or it was made to be entered by something that did not use apertures.

## The Atmosphere and Light

The light here is a problem.

It is dim, but not the dim of evening or overcast. The sky is pale, almost white, with no visible sun. You can tell roughly where the light source is — it seems to come from a broad region of the sky to the west, and it shifts over the course of the day — but there is no disc, no point. It is as if the atmosphere itself is luminous, or as if the light is arriving from somewhere else entirely and the sky is merely how it's being diffused. Shadows do not have edges. They are gradients, soft, and in some places — particularly near the cylindrical structure — they do not point the direction they should.

The air has a smell. Not organic, not mineral exactly. It is closest to the smell of cold metal, or of the air inside a very old, very dry room. It is the smell of absence — of everything that could be present and isn't.

The silence is deep but not absolute. You hear your own footsteps, your own breathing, the faint shift of your clothing. And beneath that, at the edge of perception, there are sounds. Not sounds *in* the city. Sounds *of* the city. A low, intermittent hum that seems to come from the substrate itself, felt more than heard, occurring at intervals of perhaps forty seconds. And occasionally — very occasionally — a sound like a tap, or a click, from somewhere among the structures. Not an echo. Not a rock settling. Something more deliberate than that.

---

## The Unwinding

Here is where the description becomes difficult, because the place does not hold still.

Not in a way you can point to. The structures do not move. The streets do not rearrange. But the *space between* the structures is not consistent. You walk down a street and it is a certain length, and you walk back and it is longer. Not dramatically. A few meters. Enough to notice. Enough to make you doubt.

The escarpments are too close in certain lights. You look at the eastern wall and it is three hundred meters away, and you look again and it is closer, and you cannot tell if it moved or if your sense of distance has been altered. The plateau feels, at times, smaller than it should be. As if the buildings are pressing inward, or the edges are pulling in. And then it feels vast — the buildings scattered across a space far larger than the plateau's actual dimensions could contain, each one isolated in its own region of silence, unreachable.

The cylindrical building is the center of this. Or the cause. Or the symptom. When you are near it, the difficulty intensifies. The angles of the nearby streets stop making sense. Two streets that appeared to converge when you approached now diverge. A building that was on your left is on your right. You did not turn around. You are certain you did not turn around.

Time does something. Not dramatic — you do not lose hours. But the light does not change at the pace it should. It stays at the same dim level for what feels like too long, and then shifts abruptly, as if it has been waiting for you to stop paying attention. The intervals of the hum — forty seconds, you said, but now it's thirty, or fifty. You cannot pin it down. The rhythm is alive.

---

## What Lives Here

There is something.

You see it once, on the second day. Or you believe you see it. A shape among the buildings, at the limit of the dim light's reach. It is low to the ground, and it moves with a deliberation that is not animal — too slow, too even, as if it is choosing where to place each part of itself with full attention. It is the same dark color as the cylindrical building. It has no features you can identify. It is roughly the size of a large dog, but the proportions are wrong — too long, too low, and it seems to extend as you watch, as if it has more body than you initially saw, more length, the far end of it still disappearing behind a structure where you had thought it was fully visible.

It does not react to you. It passes between two buildings and is gone.

You find traces. In the dust near the cylindrical building, there are marks. Not prints — there are no toes, no pads, no claws. They are more like pressure marks, as if something soft and heavy pressed itself against the ground and moved. The marks are not old. The dust around them is undisturbed, but the marks themselves look fresh, the edges crisp.

There may be more than one. The marks differ in size. Some are small — the width of your hand. Some are wide as a door.

Whatever they are, they are part of this place. Not visitors, not intruders. They move through the city the way the hum moves through the substrate — as a function of its operation, not as something imposed on it. They are rare. You do not see them often. But you feel them, in the way the air changes slightly when you are being paralleled through the streets by something you cannot see, something keeping its distance, matching your pace, stopping when you stop.

## The Rules

This is a place that was built for a purpose, and that purpose is still operating, and the purpose does not include you.

The city is not ruined. It is in its working state. The unwinding of space, the shifting of distance, the hum in the substrate, the movement of the dark shapes — these are not decay. They are function. The city is doing what it was made to do. You happen to be inside it while it does it.

You cannot leave by the same pass you entered. This is not a rule posted anywhere. It is not a barrier. It is that the pass, when you return to it, is not the same pass. The angle is wrong. The wind is not there. The substrate beneath it is a different color. You can leave — there are other ways down, other fractures in the escarpment, other slopes — but the way you came is no longer the way you came. It has been used. It has been processed. It is part of the city now, in some way you cannot determine.

The structures cannot be damaged. You try, idly, with a stone, against a wall. The stone strikes and makes no sound. It does not bounce. It does not mark. It simply stops, as if the wall absorbed the effort entirely. The stone is warm when you pick it up.

The cylindrical building is the locus. Everything refers to it. The streets curve around it. The angles of the structures repeat angles found in its circular form. The hum is loudest near it. The space-distortion is most intense in its vicinity. Whatever this place is, whatever it does, the cylinder is the instrument. Or the mouth.

The dark shapes do not enter the cylindrical building. You notice this because you notice their traces, and the traces never approach within perhaps fifteen meters of it. They circle it. They move around the city in patterns that, over days, you begin to map, and the patterns are not random. They are orbital. They are performing something.

---

## What It Is

I will tell you what I think, having been here, and you can take it or not.

I think this is a machine. Not a machine in the way a device is a machine — there are no parts, no mechanisms, no moving components that you can point to and say *this does that*. It is a machine in the way a landscape is a machine, a system of relationships that, when operating together, produce an effect. The mineral substrate is the material. The structures are the architecture. The cylindrical building is the core. The dark shapes are the operators. The hum is the sound of it working.

What it produces is the unwinding. The softening of space. The loosening of the relationship between distance and time, between here and there. The city takes the firm ground of reality and makes it less firm. Not chaotic — there is still order, still system, still the repeating angles and the orbital patterns. But the order is deeper than the order of physics. It is the order beneath physics, or beside it, and the city opens a passage to it.

I think the city was built by something that understood this order and could work with it. I think they are gone. I think the dark shapes are what remains — not the builders, but something the builders made, or something the builders attracted, or something the city itself produced after long enough operation. I think they maintain it. I think they are the hands now, and the city is the instrument, and the unwinding is the music.

I think it has been doing this for a very long time.

I think it does not care whether you are here. I think that is the most frightening thing about it. Not the distortion, not the shapes, not the silence. The indifference. A machine this old, this vast, this certain of its own function, does not need to acknowledge you. You are a feature of the substrate to it. A passing density. A small warm thing that will leave or will not leave, and either way the hum will continue, and the angles will repeat, and the cylinder will stand, and the shapes will circle, and the ground will ring faintly underfoot, and the light will shift when you are not looking, and the wind will not return, and the city will unwind the space around it with the patience of geology, with the precision of something that has always been true, and the pass you came through will close behind you, and the others will open, and you will go down into the lower world carrying the feeling of this place in your bones — the feeling of a reality that is not as solid as you thought it was, that has never been as solid as you thought it was, that can be parted like mineral along a hidden grain by anything patient enough to find the line.

The city knows where the line is.

It has always known.
