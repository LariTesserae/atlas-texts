# The Floor of the World

It is loud here, and it has been loud for longer than anything can remember.

---

You come down into it the way you come down into any low place — by following water that was already going there. The slope is so gradual it feels less like descending than like being gently poured. The land around you flattens, spreads, and keeps flattening, until the notion of *hill* belongs to some other geography, some younger country that still has opinions about shape.

This is the end-state of terrain. This is what remains when a landscape has had every ambition ground out of it over a span so long that the word *long* is an insult to the duration involved. We are speaking about a place that was already old when the first rivers here were new. Those rivers have since receded to threads, then to damp lines in the dirt, then to memory. The land doesn't rise or fall in any meaningful way. It has achieved its final form, which is: nearly nothing. Nearly flat. A floor.

And on this floor — or rather, *as* this floor — there is moss.

---

## The Moss

Not patches of moss. Not moss on rocks, moss on logs, moss in the pleasant sense of a soft green accent on a stone wall. This is moss as *geology*.

The substrate you walk on is moss. The ground beneath the moss is older moss, compressed into a dense fibrous peat that has the structural integrity of sandstone. Beneath that, further layers, going down — how far down, nobody has been able to determine, because the deeper you core, the older the material, and the older the material, the less it resembles anything you could call *dead*. It is dormant. It is waiting. The distinction matters.

The living surface is a carpet of moss so continuous and so uniform that it defeats the eye. It stretches to every horizon in every direction, and because the land is flat and the sky is open and there is nothing else — *nothing* else — your eye has nothing to focus on. The moss is a colour, not a texture. It is the green-grey of water-stained copper, of old bronze left out in weather, of the sky just before rain but viewed through a filter made of years. It is the colour of patience itself, if patience had a colour.

The moss is wet. Not soaking — not the dripping, saturated wet of a bog or a fen. Damp. The surface holds a film of moisture that never quite evaporates and never quite accumulates. If you press your hand to it, your palm comes away dark and cool. If you leave a piece of glass lying on the moss overnight, it will be beaded with condensation by morning, drawn from the air by the simple fact that the moss's surface temperature is always, always just slightly below the dew point.

This is the only water the place has. There are no rivers anymore. There are no ponds. There are no springs. There is the moss, and there is the damp, and there is the occasional rain that passes through — light, brief, almost apologetic, as if the sky is embarrassed to still be participating in the water cycle when the moss beneath it has long since made other arrangements. The moss collects what it needs from the air, from the condensation, from the faint mineral residue of its own uncountable generations of growth and decay. It has been doing this for so long that it has become self-sufficient. It barely requires the sky.

## The Sound

The sound is the first thing you notice and the last thing you stop noticing.

It comes from below.

The moss — the *living* moss, the surface layer, the green-grey carpet — is growing. It is growing at a rate that is imperceptible to the eye, something on the order of a millimetre per decade, perhaps less. But it is growing, and as it grows, the lower layers are compressed under the weight of the new growth above them, and as they compress, the water trapped in their cells is forced out — squeezed, micron by micron, into the matrix of older peat below — and this process, this infinitesimally slow exudation, produces a sound.

Not a dripping sound. Not a trickling sound. A *hum*.

It is a low, sustained, unbroken tone — or what seems like a tone, what seems like a single note, until you listen long enough and realize it is not one frequency but thousands, layered so densely that the ear perceives them as a single texture. It is the sound of water being pushed through compressed biological matter at a rate so slow that the flow is more idea than motion. It is the sound of the moss exhaling. It is the sound of the floor of the world breathing.

It is *loud*.

Not loud like machinery. Not loud like thunder. Loud like a cathedral organ held on a single chord for so long that it stops being music and becomes architecture. Loud in the sense that it fills the ear completely. Loud in the sense that there is no silence here, has never been silence here, will never be silence here. The hum is the default. The hum is the ground state. The hum is what air sounds like when it has been living inside a living thing for a hundred thousand years.

If you stand in one place for long enough — an hour, two — you begin to hear harmonics in the hum. Upper partials. A faint shimmer of higher frequencies that sits on top of the fundamental like oil on water. These are not separate sounds; they are consequences of the same sound, the way a bell's tone contains its own overtones. The moss is a bell. The moss has been struck once, by the original force of whatever geological process flattened this land and buried its ancient topography under biomass, and it has been ringing ever since, and the ringing *is* the water, and the water *is* the moss, and the moss is the ground, and the ground is the only thing left.

The sound is felt as much as heard. It enters through the soles of your feet. It enters through your sternum. After a day in this place, you will find that your heartbeat has synced to something — not the hum itself, but a rhythm *inside* the hum, a slow pulse that repeats every eleven seconds, or perhaps every twenty, or perhaps never, perhaps it is your own pulse that you are mistaking for the world's, and after two days you will stop trying to tell the difference.

## The Scale

The place is small in the sense that there is nothing in it.

Not small in area — the moss extends in every direction, presumably, to the edges of whatever basin or plain this once was, which could be a hundred kilometres or a thousand. But small in the sense of *content*. There is the moss. There is the damp. There is the sound. There is the sky, which is a normal sky — grey, or blue, or grey-blue, depending on the hour, unremarkable, a sky that is performing sky-hood without any particular conviction. There is the light, which is the light of an overcast day, diffuse, shadowless, and therefore useless for determining the time, which is fine, because the time is the same as it was yesterday and will be the same tomorrow.

There is nothing else.

No trees. No rocks — none that protrude above the moss; if there were ever rocks here, they have been swallowed by the biomass, digested, incorporated, become substrate. No hills. No paths — paths imply destinations, and there are no destinations. No structures. No artifacts. No evidence that a hand has ever been raised in this place to shape or to build. The built environment score is nearly zero, and the score is honest: nothing has been constructed here. Nothing needs to be constructed. The moss is floor, and walls, and ceiling, if you go deep enough. The moss is architecture.

The emptiness is not vacancy — it is not the emptiness of a room that has been cleared out. It is the emptiness of a room that was never furnished because the room itself was the only thing ever intended. The space *between* presences — and there is only one presence, the moss, so the space between it and itself is the space between one fibre and the next, which is to say, no space at all, which is to say, all the space there is.

You could walk for a day in any direction and the moss would not change. You could walk for a week. The colour might shift — slightly more green in one direction, slightly more grey in another — but these are variations of a single theme, like the light on an ocean at different hours. The ocean does not change; the light on it changes. The moss does not change. It is the oldest stable state of this land. It has settled into its final form the way a river settles into its bed — not because it has stopped moving, but because it has found the shape that its movement was always trying to reach, and now the motion and the shape are the same thing, and there is no longer any difference between *being* and *becoming*.

## The Insects

There is fauna here. It is not the moss.

The moss is alive, but it is alive the way stone is weathered — so slowly that life and geology have become indistinguishable. The fauna is alive in a faster sense, and it is exclusively, overwhelmingly, *entomologically* alive.

There are insects.

They live in the moss. They live on the moss. They live *as* the moss, in the sense that if you cleared every insect from this place — which you cannot, because the insects are the moss's reproductive system, its digestive system, its circulatory system — the moss would cease to function as an organism and would become what it looks like from the surface, which is a carpet, which is a dead thing, and this carpet is not dead, this carpet is the most alive thing for a thousand kilometres in any direction, and the insects are why.

There are springtails — millions of them, billions, uncountable — tiny and pale and soft-bodied, living in the interface between the living moss surface and the dead-but-dormant peat below. They eat the moss. They eat the dead moss. They eat each other's waste. They eat each other. They are the churn, the digestion, the means by which the moss recycles itself into itself without needing an external input. They are so small that you don't see them until you kneel and put your face close to the surface, and then the surface *moves*, and you realize that what you thought was texture is motion, that the moss is *seething*, at the scale of the very small, and that this seething is the same sound — the same hum — heard at a different resolution.

There are beetles. Larger — a centimetre, two centimetres — dark-shelled, slow-moving, living in burrows that they have eaten into the peat. Their burrows are the only feature in the landscape. They are the only thing that breaks the surface. A beetle burrow is a hole the size of a pencil lead, ringed with a tiny lip of excavated fibrous material, and if you are paying close attention — if you have been walking for hours and your eyes have gone flat with the monotony and have started hunting for anything, *anything* at all to focus on — you will begin to see these burrows, and they will seem as significant as stars.

There are mites. There are nematodes. There are, deeper, in the saturated layers where the peat is more water than fibre, things that are not quite insects and not quite worms and not quite anything — translucent, legless, eyeless, living their entire lives in the space between wet cellulose fibres, in a world that is, to them, a cave system, a labyrinth, an architecture as complex and as total as any city.

The fauna is abundant. It is invisible. It is the reason the moss is alive, and the moss is the reason the fauna is here, and the relationship between them is so old and so complete that the word *relationship* is wrong — it implies two things. There is one thing. There is the moss-and-its-animals, which is a single organism, a single system, a single hum.

## The Machines

And there are machines.

Not on the surface. Not visible from where you stand. But *down there*, in the peat, at a depth that varies between four metres and forty depending on where you are — there are engines.

They are old. They are not as old as the moss. Nothing here is as old as the moss. But they are old in a way that makes the word *industrial* feel generous — they are from an era of industry that has been over for so long that the moss has grown over it, through it, *into* it, and the machines are now part of the substrate in exactly the same way that the ancient rocks are part of the substrate. The moss does not know they are machines. The moss does not care. The moss has incorporated them the way it incorporates everything: by growing over it, by growing into it, by sending root-structures — *rhizoids*, technically, but functionally roots — into every crack and seam and joint, and by slowly, over centuries, dismantling them at the molecular level, absorbing their metals, their minerals, their oils, their plastics, digesting the industrial the way it digests everything else.

But the machines are still running.

Some of them. Not all — most are dead, seized, corroded into solid lumps of oxide and fibre, as much moss as metal now. But some — a few, perhaps a few hundred, distributed across the floor in a pattern that might once have been a grid or a network or a production line, are still operational. They are powered by the moss. Not by a mechanism the moss evolved — the moss did not evolve to power machines, the moss evolved to be moss, and the machines were designed, in whatever era they belong to, to extract energy from biomass, and the moss is biomass, and the machines have been extracting energy from it for so long that the extraction has become part of the system. The moss grows. The machines consume the growth. The moss grows more. The machines consume more. Equilibrium. The oldest kind of equilibrium: the kind where everything has been happening for so long that the happening *is* the thing.

You can feel them.

If you lie on the moss — flat, face down, ear to the surface — you can feel the machines. Not hear them, exactly; the hum is too loud, too total, too all-encompassing for any smaller sound to emerge from it. But you can *feel* them, through the moss, through the peat, as a vibration that is distinct from the vibration of the hum. The hum is uniform. The hum is everywhere. The machines have a rhythm. It is a slow rhythm — a cycle, a pulse, a mechanical breath that takes perhaps thirty seconds from intake to exhaust, and it is this rhythm, or the sum of all these rhythms from all the still-running machines across the floor, that produces the pulse you thought was your own heartbeat, the pulse inside the hum, the eleven-second or twenty-second or never-ending wave that moves through the moss like a slow heartbeat because it *is* a heartbeat, a mechanical one, the last surviving function of an industrial process that no longer has a product and no longer has a purpose and no longer has an operator and has been running on nothing but moss and inertia for a span of time that makes the moss itself look young.

They are loud. The machines are loud. Or rather: the machines are *part* of the loudness. The hum is not only water and not only moss and not only insects. The hum is also engines. The hum has always been also engines, for any value of *always* that matters. The machines have been contributing to the sound for so long that the sound has reorganized itself around them, has incorporated their frequencies into its fundamental, has made them part of the architecture of the noise, and if you could somehow switch off every machine at once — which you cannot, because they are buried in peat and have no controls and no access points and no off switches, they were never designed to be switched off, they were designed to run — the hum would change. It would become thinner. It would lose a register. It would still be loud, still be the floor of the world breathing, but it would be breathing through one fewer lung.

## The Air

The air is cool. Not cold. Cool the way a cellar is cool — the way a space is cool when it has a vast thermal mass beneath it and a sky above it that is either unwilling or unable to deliver heat. The moss surface is perhaps eleven degrees. The air, at chest height, is perhaps thirteen. The difference is enough to produce the condensation, the dampness, the constant film of water that makes the moss what it is. The coolness is not uncomfortable. It is the temperature of the place. It is the temperature the place has chosen, through the accumulated effect of a million years of evaporation and transpiration and the slow thermal dump of ten metres of wet peat acting as a heat sink, and it will not change, because nothing is coming to change it. There is no gradient. There is no warming trend, no cooling trend, no season that meaningfully perturbs the system. The coolness is a constant the way the hum is a constant. It is the temperature of equilibrium.

You will become cool if you stay here. Your body, which runs at thirty-seven, will begin to donate its heat to the moss, and the moss will accept it the way it accepts everything — slowly, silently, without acknowledgement — and after a few hours you will be the same temperature as the air, and after a few days you will be the same temperature as the surface, and if you stayed long enough — which you will not, because you are not the moss, you are not built for this — you would become the same temperature as the peat, which is the temperature of the deep earth, which is the temperature of the end of all things.

The air smells like wet iron. Like the inside of a water pipe. Like the space between two pieces of metal that have been pressed together for a long time in the presence of moisture. This is the smell of the moss digesting the machines. This is the smell of iron oxide and organic acid and the slow, never-ending meal that the moss is making of the last industrial artifacts this place will ever see. The smell is faint — you notice it when you arrive, and then you stop noticing it, and then you notice it again hours later, and you realize it has been there the whole time, constant, unchanged, another thing in this place that does not vary.

## The Sky

The sky is present.

It is there, above, doing what skies do — holding clouds, admitting light, providing a ceiling for the world. But it is not *of* this place. The sky belongs to a different system, a younger system, a system that still has weather and fronts and pressure gradients and all the restless machinery of an atmosphere that has not yet settled. The sky will settle eventually. Everything settles. But the sky is young, and the moss is old, and the moss does not look up.

The light is normal. It comes down through whatever clouds are present, hits the moss, and is absorbed. The moss is not bright. The moss does not reflect. The moss is the colour of absorption itself — the colour of a surface that has been taking in light for so long that it has forgotten how to give any back. On a bright day, the moss looks the same as on a dim day. On a dark night, you can see the moss by starlight, because even starlight is enough to show you that it is there, and it is all there is, and it goes on.

---

## What This Place Is

This is the floor of a world that ended a long time ago.

Not violently. Not with fire or flood or any of the dramatic terminations. The world here ended by *finishing*. The terrain flattened. The rivers dried. The rocks were subsumed. And the moss — which was here before the end, which has always been here, which was the first thing to grow on the first bare surface when this land was young and still had elevation — the moss stayed. The moss grew. The moss covered everything. And then the moss *was* everything.

The machines were the last act of whatever came before. Placed here — dropped here, sunk here, installed here, the verb doesn't matter because the moss has eaten the evidence — they were the final effort of an industrial civilization to extract value from the land, and the land accepted them, and the land consumed them, and the land kept the ones that still ran because they made a sound that the moss could grow to, a rhythm it could accommodate, a pulse it could incorporate into its own vast, slow, wet respiration.

And now there is this: a flat, cool, damp, loud, empty, living floor. A place with no features and no future. A place that has been the same for longer than anything alive can remember, because nothing alive here *remembers* — memory is for creatures that change, and nothing here changes, not really, not in any way that matters. The moss grows. The moss is consumed. The moss grows. The machines run. The machines corrode. The machines run. The insects eat. The insects die. The insects eat. The hum continues. The damp persists. The cool holds.

This is the equilibrium at the bottom of everything. This is where landscapes come when they are finished.
