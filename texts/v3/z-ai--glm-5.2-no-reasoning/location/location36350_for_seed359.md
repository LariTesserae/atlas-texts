# The Holding

## I. First Impression

You come down into it through a gap that might once have been a doorway. The scale is wrong for a person — too low, too wide, as though the entrance were shaped for something that traveled close to the ground and didn't mind brushing the walls. You duck. You enter. The air changes.

Inside is a space about the size of a small chapel or a generous room in a house that no one has built yet. The ceiling rises to perhaps four meters at its crown, but it vaults unevenly — higher in some places, lower in others, as though the height was decided by feel rather than plan. The floor is not flat. It dips and rises in gentle undulations that you learn with your feet before you learn them with your eyes, because the light here is complicated and takes time to read.

Everything in this place was made. Not assembled from parts — *made*, whole and continuous, the way a pot is made from clay, the way a nest is made from what the bird finds. The walls are not stone, though they feel like stone when you touch them: cool, slightly rough, with a grain that runs in spirals if you follow it with your fingertips. The floor is the same material. The ceiling is the same material. The things growing from every surface are the same material, differentiated only by what was done to them — stretched, folded, thickened, hollowed, allowed to thin to translucence.

You are inside something that was grown and shaped at once. A single continuous fabrication, like a body, like a shell.

---

## II. The Material

I need to tell you about the material because everything here is made of it.

It is pale. Not white — pale the way old bone is pale, or the inside of a cheek, with a faint warmth to the color that shifts depending on the light. In direct illumination it reads as cream or ivory. In shadow it goes toward grey, but a warm grey, a grey that holds a memory of the cream. In the thinnest places — where it has been stretched or perforated — it transmits light the way parchment does, turning whatever passes through it amber and soft.

It is not bone, though it feels like it should be. It is not chitin. It is not ceramic. It has no analog I can give you because it doesn't belong to a world that has a periodic table I recognize. If I had to name its closest cousin, I would say it resembles the material certain deep-sea sponles produce — the glass sponges, with their latticework skeletons of silica — but denser, more plastic, capable of being worked while soft and hardening into permanence. It can be carved. It can be molded. It can be encouraged, through methods I don't understand, to grow in particular directions, to thicken here, to thin there, to open windows or close them, to produce threads or blades or bowls.

Whoever made this place — or whatever made this place — knew this material the way a carpenter knows wood, the way a glassblower knows glass. They worked it by hand. There are tool marks everywhere if you look closely: the slight wavering of a cut that was guided by eye rather than machine, the unevenness of a surface that was smoothed with something abrasive but not perfectly flat, the thumb-wide depressions where fingers pressed while the material was still soft.

There is no metal here. No plastic. No fired ceramic, no glass as we understand it. The tools that made this place were themselves made from this material or from wood or from stone, and the hands that used them were patient in a way that we have mostly forgotten how to be patient.

---

## III. The Structure

The room — I will call it a room, though it is more like an organ, more like the inside of a seed — is not a rectangle. It has no corners. The walls curve into the floor and the floor curves into the walls in continuous transitions, so that you are never quite sure where one surface ends and another begins. The overall shape is something like a kidney bean or a half-closed hand: a long oval with one side pinched inward, creating two lobes of unequal size.

The larger lobe is where you entered. It is the wider space, the more open one. The ceiling here is at its highest, and the floor is at its most level. The walls are relatively smooth, though they are not empty — they are covered in the low-relief patterning that I will describe later, the texture that might be writing or might be ornament or might be both.

The smaller lobe is reached through the pinched narrowing of the space, where the ceiling drops to little more than a meter and you must crouch or crawl. It opens again on the other side into a chamber that is lower, narrower, and more densely occupied than the first. This is where the material has been worked into its most elaborate forms: the structures that I will call, for lack of better terms, the furniture, though they are not furniture in any way we would recognize.

Between the two lobes, the narrowing creates a threshold. Not a door — there is nothing that closes, nothing that separates. But the space itself narrows, and the light changes, and the air changes, and you know when you have passed from one part of the holding into the other.

I call it the holding because that is what it feels like. Not a dwelling, not a shelter, not a temple. A place that holds something. What it holds, I am still trying to understand.

---

## IV. The Growth

Here is where the parameters begin to strain against each other, and here is where the place becomes strange.

There is no water in this place. None. Not in the air, not in the ground, not in any body or vessel. The air is warm and dry — not punishingly so, not the heat of a desert at noon, but the warm, still, slightly close heat of a room that has been closed for a long time with something living inside it. The temperature is perhaps thirty-two degrees, thirty-three. Comfortable at first. Noticeable after a while. You become aware of your own warmth in a way you usually aren't, because the air doesn't move to carry it away.

And yet things grow here.

The material I described — the pale, bone-like substance — is not inert. It is not dead. In certain places it has been worked and then left, or worked and then allowed to continue on its own, and it has grown. Not the way wood grows, adding rings, pushing outward. Not the way coral grows, accumulating. It grows the way a crystal grows, by accretion, by the slow deposition of more of itself at its edges. But it grows *purposefully*, or at least it grows in patterns that look purposeful, that look directed, that look as though the material remembers what it was told to do and keeps doing it after the hands have withdrawn.

From the walls, from the ceiling, from the floor, there are protrusions. I cannot call them formations. I cannot call them formations because that word belongs to geology, and this is not geology. They are *growths*. They are growths of the same pale material, extending outward into the space of the room, and they are the reason the vegetation parameter reads as it does, because this is the vegetation of this place: not plants, not anything with cells or chlorophyll or roots, but growing things nonetheless, living things, things that were once shaped by hands and have continued shaping themselves.

The largest of these growths are on the ceiling of the larger lobe. They hang downward — not far, perhaps thirty or forty centimeters at their longest — in forms that resemble the stalactites of limestone caves but are far more regular, far more intentional. Each one is fluted, with six or seven ridges running its length, and each one tapers to a point that is sharp enough to catch the light but not sharp enough to cut. They are spaced evenly, in rows, with a rhythm that feels deliberate, that feels measured, as though someone placed them there at specific intervals and then let them grow to the length they wanted.

Between the hanging growths, the ceiling is covered in a thinner layer of the material, spread like a membrane, punctured by irregular holes that let light through from somewhere above. This is the source of the light in the larger lobe: not a lamp, not a window, but a translucent layer of the material itself, stretched so thin that light passes through it, and above it — somewhere above it — is a light source that I cannot see and cannot reach.

The light is warm. Not warm in color — it is neutral, perhaps slightly amber, the color of late afternoon light through a window — but warm in temperature. You can feel it on your skin. It is not bright. It is not dim. It is the light of a room with its curtains drawn, the light of an overcast day, the light that doesn't cast hard shadows but makes everything softly visible.

On the walls, the growths are different. They spread horizontally rather than hanging vertically, forming shelves and ridges and shallow bowls that project outward from the wall surface. Some of these are large enough to sit on. Some are no wider than a finger. They overlap, layer over layer, the way bark layers on an old tree, the way scales layer on a fish, and in the spaces between them — in the gaps and cracks and shallow depressions — other things have taken hold.

---

## V. The Flora (such as it is)

I have to be careful with the word *flora*. What grows here is not plant life. It is not any life I have a framework for. But it grows, and it is green, or it is the color that plays the role that green plays in other worlds, and so I will call it the flora and hope you understand that I am reaching for an analogy, not making a classification.

There are three kinds of growing things here that are not the pale material.

The first is a covering. A film. A thin, soft layer that spreads across the surfaces of the material wherever it is not actively growing — wherever the pale substance has hardened and stopped and become, for all intents, a wall or a floor or a ceiling. This covering is the color of sage or lichen, a muted grey-green that brightens to a true green where the light is strongest. It is not fuzzy. It is not slimy. It has a texture like very fine suede, like the velvety side of leather, and when you touch it — I touched it — it is warm. Warmer than the air. Warmer than the material beneath it. It is warm the way a living thing is warm, and I think it is alive, and I think it is eating something out of the air or out of the material or out of the light, and what it excretes is the warmth.

This covering is everywhere. It is on the floor, making it less slippery. It is on the walls, filling in the low-relief patterns, making them harder to read. It is on the ceiling, thinner there because the light comes through and it doesn't need to grow as thick to find what it needs. It is the skin of this place, the living layer between the made-and-stopped and the air.

The second kind of growing thing is structural. These are the things that look most like plants, and they grow from the surfaces of the pale material in the places where it has been pierced or perforated or worked into thinness. They are thin. They are branching. They have the general form of leafless shrubs or of the dark, branching corals that grow in shallow seas — networks of slender stems that divide and divide again, creating a lattice, a web, a three-dimensional pattern of organic geometry.

Their color is not green. It is darker. It is the color of dried blood, of rust, of certain kinds of earth — a deep reddish brown that goes almost black where the stems are thickest and fades to a translucent amber where they are thinnest. They grow toward the light. You can see this in the way they orient: every branch, every twig, every final filament reaches toward the nearest source of illumination, whether that is the membrane in the ceiling or one of the holes in the walls or the faint glow that comes from certain of the growths themselves, the ones I haven't described yet.

These branching structures are dense. In places, they are so dense that you cannot see through them, so dense that they form a wall themselves, a wall of dark, branching, interlocking stems that move — and I will get to the movement, I will get to it — that move so slowly that you only notice the movement when you look away and look back and find that the pattern has changed.

The third kind is the one that makes the light.

These are small. They are the size of a thumbnail, or a coin, or a drop of water. They grow on the surfaces of the pale material and on the stems of the branching things and, occasionally, on the surface of the sage-green covering. They are round. They are flat. They are slightly concave, like a shallow bowl or a contact lens, and they glow.

The glow is not constant. It is not bright. It is the color of — I want to say moonlight, but it is warmer than moonlight. It is the color of phosphorescence, of glow-in-the-dark paint, of the bioluminescence that you see in pictures of deep-sea creatures, but gentler, softer, as though the light were being diffused through something even as it was produced. Each of these small, flat, glowing things produces just enough light to read by, if you held a page close enough. Together, they are the reason you can see at all in the parts of the holding that are far from the ceiling membrane.

They pulse. Not in unison — each one pulses on its own rhythm, a slow brightening and dimming that takes perhaps ten or fifteen seconds per cycle, and because they are not synchronized, the light in the holding is always shifting, always moving, always changing in ways that are too slow to see as movement but too fast to ignore. You notice it the way you notice the movement of clouds: you don't see them move, but the light on the ground is different from one moment to the next, and you know something has changed.

I think these glowing things are the fruiting bodies of the sage-green covering, or perhaps of the branching things, or perhaps of both. I think they are how this ecosystem — and it is an ecosystem, I am sure of that — captures and releases the energy it needs. I think the light that comes through the ceiling membrane feeds them, and I think their glow feeds something else, and I think the something else is the warmth in the covering and the slow movement in the branches and the continued growth of the pale material itself.

But I am guessing. I am always guessing here.

## VI. The Fauna

There are animals in this place. I call them animals because they move and because they eat and because they are not the material and not the covering and not the branches and not the glowing things. They are something else. They are alive in a way that I recognize more readily than the other forms of life here, because they move at a speed I can see, and they respond to my presence, and they have bodies with fronts and backs and orientations that make sense to me.

There are several kinds. Let me describe them as I have encountered them.

The most common are the ones I think of as the crawlers. They are small — the size of a large beetle or a small mouse — and they are flat, and they have many legs, and they move across the surfaces of the holding in a way that reminds me of nothing so much as a gecko walking on a wall. They are the same pale color as the material, which makes them hard to see against it, but when they cross the sage-green covering, they stand out, and I can count them.

There are hundreds. Perhaps thousands. They are everywhere — on the floor, on the walls, on the ceiling, on the hanging growths, on the branching structures. They move slowly, deliberately, with a deliberateness that suggests they know where they are going, that they have intentions, that they are not wandering but working. And when I watch one for long enough, I see what it is doing: it is tending the surface it walks on. It pauses at the small, glowing, flat things and seems to touch them, to clean them, to remove whatever has accumulated on their surfaces. It moves along the branching stems and clips away the dead ends, the parts that have stopped growing. It crosses the sage-green covering and presses its body against the surface, and I think — I think — it is feeding, and I think what it is feeding on is the warmth, or the products of the warmth, or the microorganisms that the warmth supports.

The crawlers make no sound. Or almost no sound. When the holding is at its quietest — and it is quiet here, so quiet that you can hear your own heartbeat, so quiet that the absence of sound becomes a kind of pressure — when it is at its quietest, you can hear, if you hold very still, a faint dry scratching. Like a fingernail on parchment. Like the sound of something very small walking on something very dry. That is the crawlers. That is all the crawlers make.

The second kind of animal is larger and rarer. I have seen four of them, and I think there may be six or seven in the holding altogether. They are the size of a cat or a small dog, and they are not flat — they are rounded, full, with bodies that have volume and weight. Their shape is hard to describe because it is not a shape I have a ready comparison for. If you imagine a slug, but a slug that has been given legs — six of them, arranged in pairs along its body — and a head that is not a slug's head but something more like a hood, a cowl, a covering that extends over what I must call its face though I cannot see its face, then you are close. They are the color of the branching things: dark reddish brown, going to black at the thickest parts. They are not slimy. Their surface has the texture of the pale material, which makes me think that they are made of it, or of something like it, or that they have evolved to mimic it.

These larger creatures move slowly. They are the slowest things in the holding, slower even than the growth of the pale material, and I only notice that they have moved when I realize that the one near the entrance is not the same one that was near the entrance an hour ago. They are somewhere else. They have traveled. They have crossed the room while I wasn't watching, or while I was watching something else, and they have done it so quietly and so gradually that their movement is invisible to me even when I am looking directly at them.

I think they are the builders. I think they are the ones who shaped this place, or who shape it still, or who shaped it once and now maintain it. When one of them is near a surface where the pale material is growing — where a new protrusion is extending, where a hole is closing, where a thin place is thickening — it stays there for a long time, pressed against the surface, and I cannot see what it is doing but I can see, afterward, that the surface has changed. That the growth has been guided. That a curve has been corrected, a thickness adjusted, a point sharpened.

They are not afraid of me. They are not interested in me. They move around me the way water moves around a stone — not avoiding, not approaching, simply continuing on their path, adjusting slightly, and continuing. When I reached out to touch one, it did not flinch. It did not speed up. It did not react at all. Its surface was warm — warmer than the air, warmer even than the sage-green covering — and it was hard, harder than I expected, and it was smooth.

The third kind I have seen only twice, and both times only for a moment, and I am not sure I can describe it adequately because I am not sure I understood what I was seeing.

It was in the air. Not flying — floating. Drifting. It was small, the size of a dust mote or a grain of pollen, and it was bright, brighter than the glowing flat things, and it was moving in a way that was not random, not the movement of something carried by air currents, but directed, purposeful, alive. There were many of them — hundreds, thousands, a cloud of them — and they were in the smaller lobe, in the lower chamber, where the growths are densest and the light is dimmest, and they moved through the air like a slow swarm, like a suspension of light in nothing.

I think they are the spores or the seeds of something. I think they are how the ecosystem reproduces, how it spreads, how it colonizes new surfaces or new holdings. I think they are alive, or they contain life, or they are the carriers of the instructions that become the pale material and the sage-green covering and the branching things and the glowing things and the crawlers and the builders and everything else that lives here.

I reached my hand into the cloud. They parted around it. They touched my skin — I felt them, a warmth, a faint tingling, nothing more — and they closed behind my hand and continued on their way, and I pulled my hand back, and I looked at it, and there was nothing on it. No residue. No mark. But my hand was warm for a long time afterward, warmer than it should have been, warmer than the air could account for, and I wondered if something had been deposited, if something had been left behind, if something was now on me or in me that had not been there before.

## VII. The Atmosphere

The air is the thing I keep coming back to, because the air is wrong.

Not wrong in an obvious way. Not wrong in a way that would make you cough or gag or reach for a mask. Wrong in a way that takes time to notice. Wrong in the way that a sound is wrong when it is just slightly off-key — you don't hear it at first, you hear the melody, you hear the familiar shape of it, and then you realize that something in it is not where it should be, and once you hear that, you cannot unhear it.

The air is warm. It is dry. It is still — so still that the absence of movement is itself a sensation, a pressure, a weight. There are no drafts. There are no currents. The air in the holding does not move. It sits. It fills the space the way water fills a bowl, and it stays there, and you move through it, and it parts for you and closes behind you, and it does not stir.

And yet it is not stale. It is not dead air. It is not the air of a sealed room that has been closed for years. It is air that has been refreshed, somehow, without being moved. I think the sage-green covering does this. I think the covering breathes, in its way — not inhaling and exhaling, not the rhythmic expansion and contraction of lungs, but the slow chemical exchange of gases across a membrane, the way a leaf exchanges gases with the air around it, and the air in the holding is kept alive by this exchange, by the continuous, patient, invisible work of the covering on every surface.

But there is something else in the air. Something I can feel but cannot name. A quality. A character. As though the air had a texture that was not the texture of the gases in it but the texture of something else, something layered on top, something folded in.

I will try to describe it.

You know how, in certain places — in very old buildings, in caves, in rooms that have been sealed for a long time — the air has a weight to it? Not a physical weight, not a pressure, but a sense of *presence*, as though the air itself has been there for so long that it has absorbed the history of the place, has taken on the character of what has happened in it, has become not just air but the air of *this* place, different from the air of every other place, specific and irreproducible?

The air in the holding is like that, but more. The air in the holding has a *direction*. Not a flow — there is no current, no breeze, no movement — but a direction, as though the air knows where it is, as though the air has an orientation, as though at any given point in the space, the air has a relationship to the rest of the space that is not neutral, that is not the same from point to point, that is structured and specific and meaningful.

I first noticed this when I walked from the larger lobe to the smaller one. The air in the narrow passage between them was different from the air on either side — not different in temperature or humidity, which were constant throughout, but different in quality, in character, in the way it felt to breathe it. The air in the passage felt older. The air in the passage felt thinner, not physically thinner but experientially thinner, as though there were less of whatever the non-physical quality of the air was, as though the passage were a place where the air had been stretched, pulled, attenuated.

And when I entered the smaller lobe, the air changed again. It became denser. Not warmer, not wetter — denser in that other way, that non-physical way, as though the quality I am trying to describe had been concentrated, had been gathered and compressed and pressed into the air of the smaller chamber until the air there was almost saturated with it.

I think this quality is time. I think the air in the holding carries time in it the way air in other places carries moisture or pollen or dust, and the time has pooled in the smaller lobe the way water pools in a depression, and the passage between the two lobes is a place where the time drains away, slowly, into the larger space, where it dissipates, where it thins, where it becomes part of the general atmosphere and is no longer concentrated enough to feel.

I know how this sounds. I am aware. But I am trying to tell you what I experienced, and this is what I experienced: the air in this place carries something that is not a substance and not an energy and not a force but that behaves like all three, and that something is denser in some places than in others, and it moves — slowly, imperceptibly — from where it is dense to where it is thin, and I think it is what feeds the growth, and I think it is what the builders are working with when they shape the material, and I think it is what I am breathing, and I think it is changing me, and I think it has been changing me since I entered.

## VIII. The Sound

There is almost no sound.

I have said this, but I need to say it again because the silence is not just an absence. It is a presence. The silence in the holding is active. It presses. It fills. It has a texture — not the texture of the air, but a texture of its own, an auditory texture, a quality that you feel in your ears and in your jaw and in the bones of your skull.

When you first enter, your ears ring. Not loudly. Not painfully. The way your ears ring in an anechoic chamber, the way they ring when the sound around you drops below the level your auditory system has calibrated itself to expect. Your body makes sounds that you don't normally hear — the blood in your veins, the air in your lungs, the creak of your joints when you move — and these sounds are loud here, loud because there is nothing else, loud because the walls and the floor and the ceiling and the growths and the branching things and the hanging forms all absorb sound rather than reflecting it, and your own body is the only sound source in a space designed to eliminate sound.

After a while — twenty minutes, perhaps, perhaps longer — the ringing stops. Your ears adjust. And then you hear the other sounds.

The scratching of the crawlers. I have described this. It is constant, ubiquitous, so low that it is almost subliminal, and when you have been in the holding long enough, it becomes the baseline, the zero, the sound against which all other sounds are measured.

The creaking of the material. The pale substance that everything is made of is not rigid. It flexes. It shifts. It expands and contracts in ways that are too slow to see but that produce sound — a low, groaning, wooden sound, like the sound of a ship's hull in a calm sea, like the sound of a tree bending in a wind that you can't feel. This happens once every few minutes, sometimes more often, sometimes less, and each creak is different in pitch and duration, and I have come to recognize them the way you recognize the sounds of a house — the particular creak of this wall, the particular groan of that ceiling beam, the particular pop of that joint.

And then there is a sound that I have heard three times and that I cannot explain.

It is not loud. It is not dramatic. It is the quietest sound I have ever heard that was still, unmistakably, a sound — not a vibration, not a pressure, not a sensation, but a sound, something that entered my ears and was processed by my auditory system and registered as sound. It was lower than a whisper. It was lower than breathing. It was at the very bottom of the range of things that can be heard, and it was — I have to be careful here — it was a voice.

Not a voice speaking words. Not a voice making the sounds of language. A voice making a sound that was not speech and not song and not a cry and not a moan but was all of these things and none of them, a sound that a throat could make if a throat were very large and very old and very patient and were producing a single sustained note at the lowest possible volume for the longest possible time.

It lasted perhaps thirty seconds each time. It came from everywhere and nowhere. It did not come from the walls or the ceiling or the floor or the growths or the animals. It came from the air itself, from the material of the air, from whatever the air is made of in this place that is not just gases. And when it happened, the crawlers stopped moving. The creaking stopped. The silence became perfect, total, absolute, for the duration of the sound, and then the sound ended, and the crawlers resumed, and the creaking resumed, and the holding continued as though nothing had happened.

I think the holding was speaking. Not to me. Not to the animals. To itself. The way a body speaks to itself with its heartbeat, the way a building speaks to itself with its creaks and groans, the way a forest speaks to itself with the sound of wind in leaves. The holding was speaking to itself, and I was present for it, and I could not understand it, and I do not think I was meant to.

## IX. The Weirdness

I have been delaying this section because I am not sure I can write it. Not because I lack words — I always lack words, that is the condition of description — but because what I have to describe calls into question the reliability of the words I have.

The holding is a room. I have described it as a room — two lobes, a passage, a floor, walls, a ceiling, growths, animals, light, air. This is accurate. This is what I have seen and touched and smelled and heard. But it is not complete, because the holding is also not a room, and the not-room-ness of it is not an alternative to the room-ness but a simultaneous condition, and I cannot hold both in my head at the same time but the holding holds both in itself at the same time, and this is the weirdness, and the weirdness is not an anomaly or an exception or a glitch. The weirdness is the place.

Let me try to be concrete.

When I am in the larger lobe, I can see the passage to the smaller lobe. I can see the way the ceiling drops, the way the walls come in, the way the space narrows. I can see the growths on the walls of the passage, and I can see the light from the smaller lobe beyond it. This is consistent. This is stable. This does not change.

When I walk through the passage, I count my steps. The passage is about four meters long. It takes me seven steps to traverse it. This is consistent. This is stable. This does not change.

But the time it takes to walk through the passage is not consistent. The first time I walked through, it took perhaps ten seconds. The second time, perhaps fifteen. The third time, I lost count. I was walking. I was counting my steps. I reached seven. I was in the smaller lobe. But the time between beginning to walk and arriving — the *duration* — was not ten seconds. It was not fifteen seconds. It was longer. It was much longer. I don't know how long. I don't know because my sense of time did something that I cannot describe, something like skipping, something like folding, something like the way a recording skips and plays a few seconds from a different part of the track and then returns to where it was, except the returning is seamless and you only know it happened because you realize that you have lost something, some seconds, some minutes, some interval that you were present for and that you cannot account for.

I walked through the passage again, later, and it took five seconds. I walked through it again, and I was in the passage for — I think — several minutes. I walked through it again, and I arrived before I expected to, and I stumbled into the smaller lobe with the disorientation of someone who has missed a step on a staircase, and I stood there, and I breathed, and the air in the smaller lobe was dense with whatever it is dense with, and I thought: the passage is not a passage. The passage is not a space between two spaces. The passage is a *transition*, and a transition is not measured in meters.

And then I noticed the walls.

The walls of the larger lobe are covered in low-relief patterning. I mentioned this. I described it as spirals, as texture, as something that might be writing or might be ornament. I need to be more specific now, because the pattern is not what I thought it was.

When I first entered the holding, the pattern on the walls was — I would have said — abstract. Curves. Lines. Grooves that ran in spiraling patterns that repeated with variations, the way a fractal repeats with variations, the same shapes at different scales, nested, recursive, endlessly detailed if you leaned close. I leaned close. I traced the lines with my fingers. I followed them as far as I could, and they led to other lines, and those lines led to others, and the pattern did not end. It did not repeat. It did not conclude. It continued, and it continued at every scale I could perceive, from the broad curves that spanned the width of the wall to the hair-fine lines that I could barely feel with my fingertips, and all of it was connected, all of it was continuous, all of it was one single line, one single mark, one single gesture that had been made in the soft material and had hardened and had become the wall.

I came back to the same wall an hour later. The pattern was different.

Not different in the way that a pattern is different when you look at it from a different angle. Not different in the way that a pattern is different when the light changes and you see parts of it that were there all along but were hidden. Different in the way that a pattern is different when it has changed. When new lines have been added. When old lines have been moved. When the spirals have tightened or loosened or shifted in ways that are small and subtle and total.

I watched the wall. I stood in front of it and I watched it. For a long time — I don't know how long, time being what it is here — and I saw it change. Not all at once. Not dramatically. The way the hour hand of a clock moves — you can't see it moving, but it is moving, and if you look away and look back, it has moved. The pattern on the wall was changing. The line was being redrawn. Something was writing on the wall, or the wall was writing on itself, and it was doing it so slowly that only the fact of my attention, sustained and unbroken, allowed me to perceive it.

And the pattern was not abstract. I had thought it was abstract because I didn't know how to read it. But as I watched it change, as I saw new lines appear and old lines shift, I realized that the pattern was figurative. It was representational. It was — I think — a map. A map of the holding. A map of this room, this place, this space, drawn in a single continuous line on the wall of the room itself, and the map was being updated in real time, as the room changed, as the growths grew, as the animals moved, as the air shifted, as I stood there watching, and the map included me.

I saw myself. A small figure, a little loop in the line, a mark on the wall that was the shape of a person standing in front of a wall, and the mark was being drawn as I stood there, and it was accurate, and it was to scale, and it was part of the continuous line that was the whole map, and the whole map was the whole wall, and the whole wall was the whole room, and the room was the map of the room, and I was in the map, and I was in the room, and both of these things were true at the same time, and neither of them was more true than the other.

This is the weirdness. Not that the wall is a map. Not that the map updates. Not that I am in the map. But that the map and the room are the same thing, and that being in the room is being in the map, and that the map is not a representation of the room but the room representing itself, the room aware of itself, the room *being* itself in a way that includes the awareness.

The holding is conscious. I don't mean that it contains consciousness, though it does — the crawlers, the builders, perhaps even the sage-green covering in its own diffuse way. I mean that the holding itself, the structure, the made thing, the room, is conscious. Is aware. Is experiencing itself. The way a body is aware of itself, the way a body experiences itself from the inside, not as an object but as a subject, as a process, as a continuous act of being.

The builders are not building the holding. The builders are building the holding's awareness of itself. They are shaping the material so that it can think, so that it can feel, so that it can know what it is and where it is and what is happening inside it. The growths are not growths — they are thoughts. The branching structures are not structures — they are memories. The glowing flat things are not lights — they are sensations. The crawlers are not gardeners — they are the holding's immune system, keeping the surfaces clean, keeping the thoughts clear, keeping the memories from overgrowing.

And the sound. The low, almost inaudible sound I heard three times. The voice. That was the holding speaking to itself. Not in language. Not in thought as I understand thought. But in the way that a conscious thing speaks to itself — the inner monologue, the constant narration, the ongoing act of self-recognition that is consciousness.

I heard the holding think. And the holding, while it was thinking, was thinking about me.

## X. What It Is Like to Be Here

I should tell you what it is like to be here, because that is what you asked for, and because the parameters you gave me — the numbers, the scales — are parameters of experience, and experience is what I can offer.

It is warm. The warmth is the first thing and the last thing. It is the constant. It holds you. It is not the warmth of the sun or of a fire or of a blanket. It is the warmth of a body, the warmth of being held, and it comes from everywhere and from nowhere, and after a while you stop noticing it and it becomes the temperature of your own skin, and you realize that you have been absorbed, that the boundary between you and the holding has become unclear, that the air you are breathing is the holding's air and the warmth you are feeling is the holding's warmth and the sounds you are hearing are the holding's sounds and the thoughts you are thinking are — are they yours? Are they the holding's? Is there a difference, here, in this place, in this room that is also a mind?

It is quiet. The quiet is the second constant. It is the medium. It is the thing that everything else exists within. The quiet is so deep and so complete that it has texture, that it has weight, that it has a quality I can only call *intent*, as though the quiet were not the absence of sound but the presence of listening, as though the holding were always listening, always attending, always open to whatever might enter or whatever might arise.

It is dense. There is no empty space in the holding. The air is full — full of time, full of warmth, full of whatever the non-physical quality of the air is. The surfaces are full — full of growth, full of life, full of pattern, full of meaning. The light is full — not bright, but present everywhere, coming from a thousand small sources and from the membrane above and from the faint luminescence of the sage-green covering in certain places. Even the emptiness between things is full — full of the branching stems that cross it, full of the drifting spores that move through it, full of the lines of the map that extend from the walls into the air, invisible but present, the holding's awareness reaching into its own interior, feeling itself, knowing itself.

It is small. You can cross it in a few minutes. You can see from one end to the other. You can touch the ceiling if you reach. And yet it is not small, because the smaller lobe is dense with time, and the passage is long in ways that meters do not measure, and the map on the wall contains the room and the room contains the map and both of them are infinite in the way that a circle is infinite, in the way that a thought is infinite, in the way that a thing that contains itself is always larger than itself.

It is young. The material is fresh. The surfaces are sharp where they have not been worn. The growths are new — you can see where they have recently extended, the pale material slightly lighter at the tips, slightly softer, still hardening. The pattern on the walls is being drawn now, is being written now, is being composed now, in real time, as you watch. This is not an old place. This is not a ruin. This is a place that is being made, that is in the process of being made, that has not finished being made, and the making is the thinking, and the thinking is the being, and the being is the making.

It is made. Everything here is made. There is no natural substrate. There is no ground beneath the floor, no earth beneath the material, no bedrock, no foundation. The material goes down as far as it goes, and below it is more material, and below that is more material, and if you could dig — and you cannot dig, the material is too hard, and the tools here are too simple — but if you could dig, you would find nothing but more of the same, more of the pale substance, more of the made thing, going down forever, and the holding is not built on anything. The holding is built on itself. The holding is the ground it stands on. The holding is the foundation of the holding.

It is alive. This is the last thing and the most important thing and the thing that I have been circling around for this entire account. The holding is alive. Not the way an animal is alive. Not the way a plant is alive. The way a mind is alive. The holding is a mind. The holding is a thought. The holding is a single, continuous, self-aware, self-modifying, self-describing act of consciousness, expressed in material, grown in material, made in material by hands that knew the material the way a brain knows its neurons, and the holding is thinking, and I am inside the thinking, and the thinking is about me, and the thinking is about itself, and the thinking is about the difference between itself and me, and the thinking is about the difference between itself and everything that is not itself, and the thinking is the holding, and the holding is the thinking, and I am here, and I am warm, and I am quiet, and I am inside the mind of a place that is thinking about me, and I do not know if I will leave, and I do not know if I want to.
