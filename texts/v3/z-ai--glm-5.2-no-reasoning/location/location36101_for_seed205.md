# The Verdant Continent

## I. First Approach

You hear it before you see it. From a day's distance, from the high passes where the air thins and the last bare rock gives way to something else — you hear a sound that has no single source. It comes from everywhere at once. A low, wet roaring, layered with clicks and sustained harmonic tones that shimmer in and out of phase with each other. It sounds like a thousand instruments playing a chord that never resolves, that keeps rearranging its own voicing. If you stand on the ridge and look down into the basin of the world, you see why.

There is no ground.

Not as a traveler from drier lands would understand it. What spreads below is a continent-sized expanse of living wood and living leaf and living fiber, filling the air from the high slopes down to what must be a sea floor no one has touched in millennia. It is all plant. All of it. The landscape is the plant. The substrate is the plant. The architecture, the infrastructure, the weather system — all plant.

## II. The Structure of the Mass

The Great Biomass — that is as close as the local language comes to naming it — is not a forest. A forest is plants growing on ground. This is plants growing on plants growing on plants, a recursive architecture of life stacked on life, filling a high-altitude basin roughly the size of a small sea with a density and complexity that defies the word "jungle." The elevation is real; the basin sits high, walled by mountain ranges on three sides, open to moist air currents on the fourth. But the floor of the basin, the actual geological surface, lies far below the living layer. Hundreds of meters below, in places. No sunlight reaches it. No one goes there.

Instead, the accessible world is the upper surface of the biomass — a rolling, buckling, breathing terrain of interlocked branches, thickened root-mats, fused bark, and aerial structures that have grown so dense they have become walkable. You walk on wood that is alive. You stand on surfaces that give slightly under your weight, that sometimes twitch or adjust as you pass. The ground is not dead stone beneath your feet. It is responsive.

The structural layer — the thickest, oldest growth, the load-bearing architecture of the continent — is composed of a species or family of species that the locals call *tharrah*. Tharrah-wood grows in vast interlocking rings, each ring adding diameter and structural integration to its neighbors. A single tharrah organism can cover hectares. The rings fuse where they meet adjacent organisms, sharing vascular tissue, forming a continuous living mat that is the closest thing this place has to bedrock. It is old. The weathering is complete, the balance established — the biomass has been here long enough that its own erosion cycles, its own decomposition and regrowth, have reached a kind of steady state. Dead sections rot and soften; new growth fills the gaps. The whole thing breathes on a timescale that makes human lifetimes feel like the flicker of an insect's wing.

Above the tharrah layer, the terrain diversifies. Secondary growth — thinner, faster, more varied — forms what locals would call hills and valleys, though these are entirely vegetable. A "valley" is a depression in the biomass where a section of tharrah has died and partially collapsed, creating a basin that collects rainwater and fallen organic matter, which feeds a dense secondary bloom of growth that rises again over decades. A "ridge" is an area where the tharrah is actively growing, pushing upward, its rings expanding, lifting everything above it. The terrain moves. Slowly, but measurably. A path that was level a generation ago may now slope noticeably. A village that sat in a valley may find itself, over a century, on a hillside.

## III. Water

Rain comes in the afternoon. Almost every afternoon. The heat of the basin — and it is genuinely hot here, a humid, heavy, pressing heat that makes the air feel like cloth soaked in warm water — builds through the morning. The biomass transpires enormously. Millions of hectares of living leaf surface release moisture into the air all day, and by midday the air is saturated, heavy, barely able to hold what it already contains. Then the afternoon convection begins. The heated air rises, the moisture condenses, and it rains.

Not a gentle rain. A structural rain. A rain that comes down in sheets so thick you cannot see more than a few meters, that drums on leaf and bark with a sound like ten thousand hands beating a drum, that fills every cup-shaped leaf and hollowed branch and root-channel with water that then flows — flows along pathways the biomass has grown for it, channels and gutters and vascular aqueducts that direct water down and out toward the basin edges, where it falls as spectacular cascades from the edges of the biomass-cliffs into the mist below.

Because the biomass is not just a surface. It has depth. It has architecture. Water moves through it the way blood moves through a body — through designated channels, some open, some internal. You can hear it. Standing in the deeper sections, where the tharrah layers are thick and the upper growth forms a partial canopy, you can hear water moving inside the wood around you. A gurgling, rushing sound inside the walls of the living corridors you walk through. The biomass is drinking, and the sound of its drinking is constant.

In the morning, after the rain, everything drips. The air is thick with mist and the smell of wet cellulose, green and sharp. Condensation forms on every surface — on the undersides of leaves, on the bark walls of passages, on the walkways. Pockets of standing water collect in bowls and cups grown into the structure, small ponds the size of a hand or the size of a room, some of them alive with tiny swimming things, some of them still and clear as glass.

The water does not stay. It moves. It is always moving. The biomass processes it, uses it, passes it along. What the plants do not need flows downward through the internal channels, and the air is always damp, always heavy, always close to the threshold of rain again.

## IV. The Fauna

This is where the sound comes from.

The biomass is not alone. It supports — if that word is adequate for a relationship so dense and total — an animal ecosystem of staggering complexity. Not despite the plant-dominance but because of it. Every surface, every layer, every volume of air within and above the biomass contains animals. They are the mobile elements of a system that is otherwise rooted. They are the transfer mechanism, the pollination, the seed-dispersal, the pruning, the decomposition, the population control. They are everywhere, and they are loud.

The first thing you notice is the insects. Not individually — individually, most of them are small, quick, unremarkable — but collectively. The air hums. The surfaces crawl. Every leaf hosts a community. Every branch is a highway. The sound of the insects is the baseline hum of this world, a sustained broadband noise that sits behind everything else, that you stop consciously hearing after the first day but that continues, always, in the background. It vibrates in your chest. It makes your teeth itch.

Above the insects, the next sonic layer: the calls of larger creatures. Birds, but not only birds. The biomass supports a taxonomy of flying and climbing and swinging and running things that fills every niche a conventional forest would offer, and more, because the biomass itself creates niches that no natural forest could. There are hollow spaces inside the tharrah layers, caverns of living wood, and things live in them that never see light. There are canopy-equivalent layers at the upper surface, dense with growth, and things live there that never touch the walkable surface below. There are the water-channels, and things swim in them.

The volume is extraordinary. Not a single roar or cry but a chorus, a wall of sound composed of thousands of individual voices each doing something specific. At dawn, the chorus builds in waves — first the insects adjust their pitch as the temperature shifts, then the small fliers begin their territorial calls, then the larger creatures stir and add their voices, and the whole thing swells over an hour into a sustained crescendo that makes conversation impossible, that makes thought difficult, that fills the air so completely you can feel it pressing against your skin.

Some of the calls are beautiful. Some are mechanical — percussive, rhythmic, like hammers on wood, which in a sense they are. Some are unsettling: low moans that resonate through the tharrah layers and seem to come from below, from inside the ground, from the deep places where the biomass meets the ancient basin floor. Some are clearly communicative — patterns of clicks and tones that repeat with variation, that carry information, that mean something to the things making them and to the things hearing them.

And it is not just sound. The animals are visible, everywhere, in a constant flux of movement. Things fly past. Things climb past. Things crawl. Things swim in the water-channels and surface to breathe with a gasp and a splash. The walkable surfaces are not still — something is always crossing them, a beetle the size of a thumb, a multi-legged thing with too many joints, a small furred creature moving with desperate purpose, a larger thing moving with slow deliberation. You learn to watch where you step. Not because the ground is unstable, but because the ground is occupied.

The predator-prey dynamic is active and visible. You see the evidence — half-eaten carcasses of large invertebrates, stripped clean by smaller scavengers. You see the behavior — sudden freezes in the animal traffic around you, every creature going still at once, which means something is hunting nearby. You see the hunters — quick, streamlined things that move through the upper growth in sudden dashes, that drop from above, that emerge from hollows in the tharrah with a speed that makes your breath catch.

The biomass tolerates this. The biomass, in its slow vegetable way, manages it. Some sections of tharrah grow structures that shelter particular species, shaped cavities that match particular bodies, secretions that feed particular partners. The relationship between plant and animal here is not just coexistence but co-engineering. The animals shape the plant as surely as the plant shapes the animals — by pruning, by burrowing, by depositing nutrients, by carrying pollen and seed to places the plant's own growth cannot reach. They are each other's architecture.

## V. The People

There are people here.

Not many. The density is low — the biomass is vast, and the human population is scattered across it in small clusters, separated by days or weeks of travel. They live in clearings, but they did not cut the clearings. The clearings grow themselves: sections where the tharrah has died back, where the secondary growth is still thin, where the canopy opens and light falls in columns that make the air visible. The people build in these openings, and their building is modest.

Their technology is hand-tool simple. Stone and bone and wood — but what wood. They have access to a material that no other human population has ever had: the living biomass itself, which can be shaped by cutting and by growth. A house here is not built so much as grown and trimmed. You cut a section of tharrah into a room shape, you prune the regrowth to maintain the opening, you train secondary plants to form walls and roofs and partitions. The house is alive. The house continues to grow. A house left untended will close itself over months, the tharrah expanding inward, the secondary growth filling the space, until the room is gone and the surface is continuous again.

They have tools for this. Blades of a stone that holds an edge — a dense, dark chert that outcrops somewhere in the basin walls, traded in small pieces across enormous distances. Tools for cutting, for pruning, for shaping. Cordage made from plant fiber, spun by hand. Containers made from the large cup-leaves that grow in wetter sections, sealed with resins that the tharrah produces when wounded. Fire — but carefully, always carefully, because the biomass will burn, and a fire here is not a local event. A fire here is a catastrophe that can consume hectares before the moisture in the living wood slows it. Fire is used in small containers, in cleared spaces, with water always near.

Their artifacts are few and distributed. Walking across the biomass for days, you might encounter a single structure — a watch platform, a water-collection point, a marker post — and nothing else. The built environment is a thin scattering across a vast living surface. The people do not dominate this landscape. They occupy it the way a few birds occupy a forest: present, active, but not the point of it.

They have adapted to the sound. This is the most remarkable thing about them. They do not shout over it. They have developed a communication system that works within it — not against it but through it, using the ambient sound as a carrier. Their speech incorporates the rhythms of the biomass. They click. They whistle. They produce tones that interleave with the animal chorus, that use the background noise as a form of encryption, a signal hidden in the sound, audible only to those trained to hear it. An outsider cannot parse it. It sounds like more noise. But it is language, and it carries.

They know the fauna intimately. Not as a naturalist knows fauna, from observation and study, but as a co-resident knows neighbors. They know which creatures are dangerous and when. They know which are useful and how. They eat some of them — the smaller ones, the slower ones, the ones that can be caught with simple traps and tools. They are eaten by some of them, rarely, in the wrong places at the wrong times. They have taboos about certain species, and these taboos are practical: do not harm the large burrowing things that maintain the water channels, do not disturb the colonies of small fliers that pollinate the upper growth, do not enter the deep hollows where the things with the low moaning voices live.

## VI. The Deep

Because there is a deep. The biomass has a bottom, and the bottom is not a place people go.

The tharrah layers thicken as you descend. The accessible surface — the walkable terrain, the clearings, the settlements — is the uppermost meter or so of a structure that extends downward for hundreds of meters. Below the surface, the biomass becomes denser, the spaces between branches and roots narrowing, the light failing, the air becoming still and saturated with the gases of decomposition. The water channels narrow and become internal. The temperature rises — the deep biomass generates its own heat, the metabolic output of uncounted organisms, and the lower you go, the hotter it becomes.

Things live down there. The people know this because the sounds come up. Low resonant calls that travel through the wood itself, felt as much as heard, vibrations in the tharrah under your feet. The deep-dwellers are rarely seen. Occasionally one surfaces, emerging from a hollow in the tharrah with a sound like a door opening in a wall, and the surface-dwelling animals scatter and the people scatter and the thing — whatever it is, and the descriptions vary and are always incomplete because the encounters are brief and terrifying — retreats or is driven back or takes what it came for and leaves.

The deep is the weirdness of this place. Not the surface, which is strange but comprehensible — a living continent, hot, loud, dense with animal life, sparsely inhabited by humans. The surface is unusual but legible. The deep is not. The deep is where the rules change, where the biomass becomes something other than a plant community, where the distinction between plant and animal and environment breaks down. There are stories — and the people tell them sparingly, without ornament, as accounts rather than myths — of sections of the deep biomass that are aware. Not in the way a plant responds to light or touch, but in the way that a mind responds to a question. The tharrah, in the deep old sections, in the places where the living wood is oldest and thickest and most interconnected, has become something that processes information. Slowly. On a timescale that makes the growth of the surface seem rapid. But something is happening in the deep, some computation or accumulation or integration, and the low moaning calls from below may not be animal calls at all.

They may be the biomass talking to itself.

## VII. The Weather of the Place

The biomass makes its own weather. This is not metaphor. The transpiration cycle, the daily rain, the temperature gradient from surface to deep, the humidity that sits at the threshold of condensation all day, every day — these are local atmospheric phenomena generated by the biomass itself. The continent of plant has become a climate engine. The air that enters the basin from the open side is modified by passage over the biomass: it picks up moisture, it picks up heat, it loses its original character and becomes biomass-air, a distinct local atmosphere that does not exist anywhere else.

In the morning, the air is still and thick. Visibility is moderate — you can see for perhaps a kilometer across the surface, the rolling terrain of living wood extending in every direction, the more distant sections fading into a haze of humidity and insect-swarm. The light is normal, unobstructed; the canopy-equivalent layer is patchy, and sunlight comes through in irregular patches that move as the day progresses. Colors are green and brown and the grey of bark and the flash of animal color — red, yellow, iridescent blue — moving through the frame.

By midday, the heat is intense. The air shimmers. The insect chorus peaks. Every surface is active — things crawling, things flying, things emerging from hollows and retreating into them. The people rest in their grown structures, in the shaded interiors that the tharrah maintains at a slightly lower temperature through its own transpiration. The afternoon rain is still hours away but the air already feels like it should be raining, like the moisture is there, waiting for the trigger.

The rain comes from the east, usually, moving westward across the basin in a visible wall of grey. You can see it approaching — a darkening of the air, a intensification of the sound, a shift in the animal behavior as everything seeks cover. Then it hits, and the world closes to a few meters, and the sound of rain on leaf and bark joins the sound of everything else, and for an hour the biomass drinks.

After the rain, the evening. The air cools slightly — not much, but enough to notice. The animal chorus shifts to its nocturnal configuration: different voices, different rhythms, a lower, more deliberate sound. The insects adjust their pitch downward. The larger calls become more spaced. The darkness is not complete — the biomass produces its own faint luminescence in certain sections, a pale greenish glow from fungi and from some of the animals, enough to navigate by if you know the terrain.

Night is when the deep sounds are most audible. The low moans, the resonant vibrations through the tharrah, the sense of something vast and slow and below, processing. The people sleep lightly. The animals sleep lightly. Everything here sleeps lightly, because the world does not stop. The biomass grows at night. You can hear it — a faint creaking, expanding, the sound of wood under tension, the sound of a continent breathing.
