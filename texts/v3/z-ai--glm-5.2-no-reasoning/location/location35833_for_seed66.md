# The Congregation at High Sump

## I.

The first thing is the sound.

Not a sound — the sound, the one that replaces what you thought silence sounded like. A low harmonic tremor that rises through the stone floor of the causeway and enters your legs at the femur. Above that, a churning wet friction, like cloth being wrung by enormous hands. Above that, clicking — a layered, arrhythmic clicking that seems to come from no single source but from the saturated air itself, as though the mist were sorting itself into syllables.

You are high. The air says so — not thin, not alpine, but expanded, stretched, as though altitude here were measured not by distance from sea level but by distance from the world's last honest ground. Below the causeway, the land drops away in shelves of wet basalt, each shelf darker and more lustrous than the one above, until the fog takes them. Above, the sky is the color of wet newspaper, and about as readable.

It has rained. It is always about to have rained here, or just finished — the atmosphere cannot quite commit to either state, so it condenses. Water beads on every surface. It beads on the carved stone balustrades of the causeway. It beads on the iron fixtures. It beads on the chitin of the things that have come to the edge of the causeway to stand and watch, and it beads on the lenses of their compound eyes, each droplet a tiny cataract over a tiny watcher.

The clicking is them.

---

## II.

The city — it is a city, though you have to walk its length to understand how — stretches along the crest of a high ridge called the Sill. The Sill runs roughly east-northeast for a distance that takes two days to walk, and its width varies — a hundred meters in places, half a kilometer in others — but its character is consistent: flat-topped, wet, ancient, and built upon in layers so deep that the original surface is theoretical.

The construction is stone and iron. Not steel — iron, worked and re-worked, bolted and re-bolted, rusted and then sealed with a tar-like compound that the rain slowly dissolves, so that everything built of iron wears a permanent weep of dark streaks down its riveted face. The stone is local basalt, cut in blocks so regular they seem machined, fitted without mortar in the lower layers — the oldest layers — and with increasingly thick bands of a pale gritty cement higher up, as though the builders' precision declined over time, or their patience, or their access to good stone.

There are structures. This word is used loosely.

Most of what is built here is built for purposes that are not architectural in any way you'd recognize. There are platforms — raised, flat, drained by channels cut into their surfaces, some large enough to hold a market, some no larger than a dining table. There are towers, but they are not towers in the sense of buildings that rise to enclose interior space; they are frameworks, open lattice-columns of iron angle-stock bolted to stone piers, rising twenty, thirty, forty meters, carrying cables and pipes and mechanisms at their tops that turn very slowly in the wind, or in something that is not wind but behaves like it. There are walls, but they do not enclose. They divide. They run across the Sill in parallel lines, spaced ten meters apart, chest-high, made of stacked basalt blocks with iron caps, and between them run the causeways — the walked paths, the arteries of whatever this place is or was.

And there are the vats.

---

## III.

The vats are the reason for the city, or the closest thing to a reason that an outsider can parse.

They are cut into the basalt of the Sill itself — enormous rectangular cisterns, each one perhaps thirty meters long, ten wide, and of unknown depth. Their rims are flagged with iron-banded stone. Their walls are lined with a dark, close-fitted masonry that is not basalt — something denser, something that holds water without weeping, without any visible porosity at all. The vats are full. They are always full.

The water in the vats is not rainwater. It is warm — visibly warm, releasing thin columns of vapor that join the general fog — and it is dark, not muddy-dark but tinted, the color of weak tea or strong urine, and it moves. Not by wind. By something in it. The surface circles very slowly, very steadily, the way water circles a drain, except there is no drain, and the water does not leave. It only turns.

The turning is slow. The dynamic of this entire place is slow. Things here happen at the speed of oxidation, of sedimentation, of geological patience. The cables on the lattice-towers turn, but it takes a full minute for the mechanism at the top to complete one rotation. The vats circle their dark water over the course of an hour. The fog moves through the city at the pace of a walking man, but only just, and sometimes seems to slow, to pool, to gather in the lower causeways and the spaces between the dividing walls, as though it, too, were subject to the general lethargy.

The vats smell of iron and warmth and something underneath both — a flat, biological sweetness, like overripe fruit in a closed room, but fainter, and more constant.

---

## IV.

The fauna are the reason for the sound.

There is no vegetation here. No grass in the cracks, no moss on the stones, no lichen on the iron. The stone is bare. The iron is bare. The cement is bare. Nothing green grows, nothing photosynthesizes, nothing puts down roots or pushes up leaves. The world here is mineral and water and manufactured things, and then, everywhere else, there are the fauna.

They are not vertebrates. They are not insects, though the word "insectoid" presses itself forward and will not leave. They are chitinous, multi-legged, and they come in a taxonomy of sizes and forms so varied that the word "species" feels inadequate — it implies boundaries, and these things seem to exist on a continuum, as though the concept of discrete types had been proposed here and rejected.

The smallest are the size of a fingernail, and they swarm. They cover the stone surfaces of the causeways in shifting carpets, especially near the vats, and they make a sound like sand being poured — a dry hiss that underlies everything else. They are dark, nearly black, and they glisten when the fog wets them. They move in patterns that look purposeful but are not readable as purpose. They are, you understand after watching, feeding — feeding on something on the stone, or in the stone, or on the fog itself.

The mid-sized ones are the watchers. They stand at the edges of the causeways, at the rims of the vats, on the ledges of the lattice-towers. They are the height of a large dog, built low to the ground, with broad flat bodies supported by many legs — six, eight, the count varies — and a head that is mostly eye. Not one eye. A clustered band of lenses that wraps the front of the head like a visor, each lens a different size, and each lens wet with condensation. They click. The clicking is the sound of their mandibles, working — not eating, just working, open-close, open-close, a rhythm that synchronizes and desynchronizes across the gathered individuals so that the sound has a tidal quality, swelling and receding.

They watch. They watch the vats. They watch the fog. They watch you, if you are there, but without urgency. Without the intensity of a predator assessing prey. More the intensity of a fixture — a thing bolted to a scene, observing because observing is what its structure does.

The large ones are rare and they are different.

---

## V.

The large ones come up from below.

The Sill drops away on both sides in shelves of basalt, and below the fog line, the rock face continues down into a landscape of terraced wet stone that you can hear but not see. The sound from below is the churning — the wet friction that underlies the clicking and the hissing. Something down there is moving, and it is moving in large numbers, and it is moving in water.

The large ones climb. They come up the basalt shelves at a pace so slow it is almost geological — you see one and think it is a feature of the rock, a protrusion, a formation. Then, over the course of an hour, it is higher. Over the course of a day, it is on the Sill.

They are the size of small boats. They are low and broad and their carapaces are ridged in a way that channels water, so that when the fog condenses on them, it runs in deliberate lines, as though the ridges were not decorative but functional — drainage for a body that expects to be wet. Their legs are thick, folded beneath them, and they move with a deliberation that makes the word "slow" feel rushed. Each step is considered. Each step takes seconds. They do not click. They make a sound like stone settling — a low, granular groan, as though their joints were made of rock, which, considering the mineral quality of this world, they may partially be.

They come to the vats. They lower their heads — eyeless heads, smooth, dark, wet — to the surface of the turning water, and they drink. They drink for a long time. The water level in the vat does not visibly drop. They drink and drink and the water circles and the fog condenses and the small ones swarm and the mid-sized ones click and the large ones drink, and this is the cycle, and it has been the cycle for longer than anything here can remember, because nothing here remembers in any way you would recognize.

---

## VI.

The city has infrastructure. This is the strangest part, and the part that takes the longest to see.

The lattice-towers are not decorative. The cables they carry run between them and down into the stone of the Sill, and where they enter the stone, there are junctions — iron boxes, riveted, seeping tar, with inspection plates bolted over their interiors. Some of the plates have been removed, by weather or by time or by the small chitinous things whose decompositional habits may include iron. Inside the junctions, there are coils. Copper wire, wound around iron cores, and the cores are warm, and the coils hum at a frequency below hearing but above feeling — you feel it in your sternum, a vibration that is not the sound but is the sound's foundation.

There is power here. Not generated — conducted. The vats are warm, and the warmth is not volcanic, not thermal in any geological sense. The warmth is induced. The coils in the junctions draw something from the vats — not electricity, or not only electricity, but something that flows in the dark turning water and can be drawn through copper and iron and made to do work.

What work? The mechanisms at the tops of the towers turn. The cables carry whatever they carry. The vats circle their water. The large ones climb and drink. The mid-sized ones watch. The small ones swarm. And somewhere in the deep masonry of the Sill, in chambers below the causeways, in spaces you can feel but not reach — a low steady vibration says that something is being done, has been being done, will continue to be done, at a pace that makes the word "process" sound frantic.

This is an industrial city. It was built to do something. The something is still being done. The workers have left, or the workers have become the fauna, or the workers were always the fauna and the city was built for them, around them, by hands that are no longer evident. The distinction between "built" and "grown" becomes difficult here. The iron was worked by tools. The stone was cut by tools. But the vats were not built — they were lined, and the lining is too close, too fitted, too organic in its precision, and the dark material of the lining is, if you touch it, if you dare, warm, and very slightly yielding, and it pulses.

---

## VII.

The fog thickens at what might be evening, or might just be a cycle of the atmosphere, unrelated to any sun that may or may not exist above the gray. The light does not change — it is the same flat, even, shadowless illumination that it has been since you arrived, as though the sky itself were a lamp left on at a single setting. The light is normal, in the sense that you can see, in the sense that colors exist and shapes resolve and distance has visual depth. But the light has no source. It comes from everywhere above, diffused through the fog, so that the city is lit the way the inside of a cloud is lit — evenly, sourcelessly, without angle or warmth.

In the flat light, the wet basalt looks blacker. The iron looks rustier. The chitin of the fauna looks oilier — a dark sheen that is not color but reflectivity, the surface of a shell catching the flat light and sending it back flatter. The vats, in the flat light, look like windows cut into the stone, and the turning water in them looks less like water and more like something thinking very slowly.

The sound does not change. The sound does not have a day cycle. The clicking continues, the hissing continues, the churning below continues, the low hum of the coils in the junctions continues, the groan of the large ones settling and drinking continues. The sound is the city's breath, and the city does not sleep.

---

## VIII.

Standing on the causeway between two dividing walls, with the small ones moving around your boots in their shifting carpet, with the mid-sized ones at the vat's edge clicking their tidal rhythm, with the fog condensing on your face and the low vibration in your sternum and the smell of iron and warm sweet water and the flat light and the vast, ancient, patient machinery of the place turning over and over beneath your feet — standing there, you understand something about this place that cannot be put into a single sentence.

It is alive, but not in any way that centers on you. It is active, but not in any way that involves you. It is aware, perhaps, but not of you in particular — of the vats, of the water, of the cycle, of the slow work below. You are a visitor to a process that was here before you arrived and will be here after you leave and does not need your attention or your interpretation. The fauna are not here for you. The city was not built for you. The power was not generated for you. The fog does not condense for you.

You are present. The place does not mind. The place does not mind in either sense — it does not care, and it does not mind, and it is not a mind. It is a system, a mineral-wet-iron-warm-chitinous-slow system, and it is in its own state, and it has been in its own state for a very long time, and the state is stable, and the state is stable because nothing here is in a hurry.

The clicking swells. The clicking recedes. The water circles. The large one at the nearest vat lowers its eyeless head and drinks, and the dark water does not diminish, and the fog does not lift, and the day, or the not-day, continues.

You could walk the length of the Sill in two days. You would see the same things. The vats would be there, and the towers would be there, and the fauna would be there in their overwhelming, unclassifiable multitudes, and the sound would be the same sound, and the smell would be the same smell, and the stone would be wet, and the iron would be streaked, and the work below would hum, and the place would continue to be itself, patient and vast and mineral and alive in its own way, which is not your way, which was never your way, which does not need to be your way.

The clicking swells. The clicking recedes.

The water circles.
