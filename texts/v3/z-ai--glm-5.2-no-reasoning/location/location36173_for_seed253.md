# The Drowned Cathedral of the Rootwater

## I. First Descent

The water is not cold. That is the first wrong expectation. It reads as cold — pale green verging to gray, scrolling with particulate like dust in an abandoned room, lit faintly from some source you cannot locate. But it touches the skin at something just below human body temperature, tepid, the warmth of bathwater left standing for an hour. Not comfortable. Not uncomfortable. The kind of temperature that erases the boundary of itself against you. After a minute, you forget you are wet.

You descend through it. There is no surface above. Or rather, there was one once, and it is so far behind you now that it has become theoretical. The water column extends downward, and the light — what there is of it — does not come from above. It comes from below, or from within the things below, and it is so dim that your eyes spend their first full minute convinced they have not adjusted yet. They have. This is what there is.

The pressure should be immense. It is not. Whatever physics governs this place has made an exception, or was never informed. You sink as if the water were only a medium, a atmosphere, and your body a slow object falling through it with no urgency.

## II. The Substrate

The first surface you reach is a leaf.

Not a leaf in the way you are accustomed to. It is perhaps forty meters across. Its edge is visible only as a faint light differential against the darker water beyond — a slightly less-dark shape curving away on both sides until it leaves your range of perception. The surface of it is smooth, waxy, dark green so deep it is nearly black, with veins running through it like roads on a map, thicker than your wrist, carrying some fluid that is not water — slower, denser, faintly luminous, a yellow-green that provides the only real light source in this place. You touch it. It is warm. Not plant-warm, not metabolic-warm, but warm the way stone is warm in a room that has always been at the same temperature. It has reached equilibrium with whatever surrounds it and it has been there long enough that the concept of "reaching" is no longer relevant. It simply is.

This leaf is not unique. It is the floor.

Below it — or rather, through it, for it is translucent enough to see the shapes beneath — are more leaves. Layers and layers of them, each at slightly different angles, each vast, overlapping like shingles on a roof that has no roofline, extending in every direction. They have been here so long that where they meet, they have grown together. The veins of one leaf merge into the veins of the next, and what you initially took for a network of roads or rivers is, in fact, a single continuous vascular system spanning — you do not know. You cannot see its edges. You cannot imagine its edges.

Everything here is plant. That statement takes time to fully absorb, because "plant" initially means something small and discrete to you — a thing in a pot, a thing in a garden, a thing you can name. Here, plant means *everything*. The floor is leaf. The columns — and there are columns, you begin to notice them as you move laterally across the leaf surface — are stems, or trunks, rising vertically through the water, each one perhaps ten meters in diameter, their surfaces rough with bark that is itself covered in smaller growths: mosses, lichens, films of algae that have evolved past algae into something that no longer needs a name. The columns are spaced far apart. Very far apart. You can see perhaps three or four from any position, and the distances between them are such that the landscape feels less like a forest and more like a plain with occasional pillars. The spaces between are not empty — they are full of water, and the water is full of floating matter, spores, fragments, slow-moving organisms — but the spaces are *spacious*. There is room here. There is more room than there is thing.

## III. The Architecture

And yet it is all made.

This is the contradiction that takes longest to resolve in the mind. The substrate is biological — leaf, stem, root, fiber — but the arrangement is not accidental. The columns are placed. Not evenly, not in a grid, but with an intentionality that becomes visible only after you have drifted among them for a time. They follow a logic that is not your logic but is recognizably a logic: they cluster more densely where the leaf-floors are thinner, as if providing structural support. They thin out where the floor is thickest, oldest, most layered. They lean, very slightly, toward the light source below, which means the entire structure is, in some sense, a roof built over a light.

It was built. Or it grew into being built, which may be the same thing given enough time. And there has been enough time. There has been more than enough time. The evidence is everywhere, in the fact that nothing here is actively growing. The leaves do not unfurl. The stems do not thicken. The vascular fluid moves, but at a pace that makes a glacier look impulsive. This is not a living system in the sense of a system that is still becoming. It is a living system in the sense of a system that has finished. It reached its final form so long ago that "long ago" is a phrase without purchase here. It settled. It locked. It became what it was going to be, and then it continued being that, and it has continued being that, and it will continue being that, for a duration that makes the word "permanent" feel flimsy.

You move through it the way you might move through a cathedral built by a civilization that has no word for "civilization" because they never needed one. The arches are stem-cross-sections that have grown into each other overhead, forming vaulted spaces that are not quite geometric, not quite organic, but something in between that makes your eyes recalibrate every time you look up. The walls of the vaults are living tissue, but they have hardened into something closer to wood, or closer to bone, and their surfaces are textured with the memory of earlier growth: rings, ridges, scars where branches once emerged and were absorbed back into the main body. The whole place is a palimpsest of its own history, every surface recording every previous state, all of them compressed into a single stable present.

## IV. The Sound

The sound is what tells you this place is alive.

Not visually. Not chemically. Aurally. The volume is the first thing that struck you, and it continues, constant, unvarying, a total acoustic environment that comes from no single direction because it comes from everywhere. It is a low thrum — not a hum, which implies a mechanical origin, but a *thrum*, a vibration with harmonic complexity, with layers. The deepest layer is so low you feel it in your sternum more than hear it: the vascular fluid moving through the giant leaves, through the stems, through the root network below. It is the sound of a circulatory system at planetary scale, or at least at cathedral scale, and it has been running at exactly this rate for so long that it has become a constant, a fundamental, something you stop hearing the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat.

Above that low layer, there are mid-range frequencies: clicking, snapping, the sound of small hard things striking larger hard things. These come from the fauna. They come from everywhere and nowhere, and they are so numerous that they merge into a textured static, a crackling layer over the deep thrum. And above that, higher still, there are tones — not musical, but tonal, sustained notes that rise and fall in volume but never in pitch, as if something is resonating at a fixed frequency and the only variation is how many of those somethings are resonating at any given moment.

The total effect is loud. Not painfully, not aggressively, but comprehensively. The sound fills the water the way the water fills the space. There is no silence here. There has never been silence here. Silence would require the cessation of processes that have not ceased since before your species had a species, and they will not cease for any reason you can bring about.

You speak and your words do not travel. The acoustic environment absorbs them. Your voice is a pebble thrown into an ocean of sound. This is not a place where speech is expected.

## V. The Fauna

They are everywhere, and they are the reason the space does not feel empty despite its sparseness.

The largest are the drifters. You see them first as shadows in the middle distance — shapes that you initially mistake for columns until you notice they are moving. They move slowly. They move *very* slowly. A drifter the size of a small house — an oblong, darkly translucent body with internal structures visible through its skin, organs or organelles that glow faintly amber — takes perhaps ten minutes to cross the gap between two columns. It does not appear to be going anywhere. It appears to be maintaining a position, adjusting for currents that are too subtle for you to perceive. Its body is not smooth; it is covered in fine filaments, like cilia, like the hairs on a bean leaf, and these filaments are in constant micro-motion, tasting the water, sorting its contents, rejecting most of it and absorbing some of it. You watch one for a long time. It does not react to you. You are not food. You are not threat. You are not anything in its categorical system.

Smaller animals — you call them animals for convenience, though several of them appear to be operating at a level of complexity that makes the animal/plant distinction feel quaint — move through the water in greater numbers. There are things like shrimp, pale and long-legged, that walk across the leaf-surfaces in groups, combing the surface for whatever film or residue has accumulated there. There are things like jellyfish, but structured, with definite axes of symmetry, their bells angular rather than round, their tentacles branching in patterns that repeat fractally down to the limit of your vision. There are things you cannot categorize at all — flat, shadow-thin creatures that drift along the surfaces of the stems like film on water, so thin they are almost a property of the surface rather than an organism separate from it, and you only notice them when one detaches from a column and swims — with a motion like a flag in very slow wind — to another column thirty meters away.

And there are the shell-bearers. These are the ones you hear most: the clicking, the snapping. They are hard-bodied, armored, the only organisms here that seem to have invested heavily in mineral rather than plant tissue. Their shells are not calcium — they are something darker, denser, almost metallic, and they grow in spirals and ridges that click against each other when the animal moves, and click against the leaf-surfaces when the animal walks, and click against each other when two shell-bearers meet and interact in ways you cannot parse. They are territorial, perhaps, or they are communicating, or they are simply colliding because the world is large but they are numerous and their paths cross. The sound of their clicking is the middle layer of the thrum. It is the texture of this place made audible.

None of them flee from you. None of them approach you. You are not in their world. You are passing through their world, and they have no more reaction to you than the columns have to the water. You are incidental. The humility of this takes time to settle, but it settles.

## VI. The Light

It comes from below.

The leaf-floors are translucent, and beneath them — how far beneath, you cannot determine — there is light. Not much. A green-gold glow that is the color of sunlight filtered through a closed eyelid, or of firelight seen through a wall of bottle glass. It does not flicker. It does not pulse. It is constant in the way that everything here is constant: not because it is maintained, but because it reached a stable output and then time stopped mattering.

The vascular fluid in the leaf-veins contributes its own light — that yellow-green luminosity, faint but everywhere, so that the floor you stand on is a map of its own illumination, a network of lit lines on a dark field. Where leaves overlap, the light intensifies at the junction. Where the floor is a single leaf with nothing beneath, the light is dimmer, and you can see down through it to the next layer, which glows more faintly still, and below that another, each layer deeper and dimmer until the light is absorbed by the sheer mass of plant matter between you and the source.

The columns cast no shadows because the light comes from below and they are pillars of opaque material, which means they are dark against the lit floor, and their darkness extends upward into the water as a column of slightly darker water — not a shadow, exactly, but a zone of reduced light, a shaft of relative darkness that extends through the water column until the general luminosity of the dissolved particulate overwhelms it. These dark shafts are, paradoxically, the most visible features of the landscape. They are the negative spaces that give the positive spaces their definition.

You have no light of your own. You do not need one. Your eyes, after sufficient time, begin to construct images from the faintest differentials — the difference between water and leaf, between leaf and stem, between the shadow of a drifter and the water around it. The world assembles itself in your vision slowly, like a photograph developing, and once it has developed, it does not change. The light does not shift. The shadows do not move. The only motion is the fauna, and the fauna move so slowly that motion itself becomes a quality you notice only over long observation, like watching a clock hand and realizing it is, in fact, moving.

## VII. The Deep Structure

You descend further, through a gap in the leaf-floor where two leaves have separated slightly, their edges curling away from each other like pages of a book opened and left. Between them, the water is warmer by a degree. You pass through and find yourself in a space between layers — a chamber, perhaps, though "chamber" implies enclosure, and this is not enclosed. It is a space between two floors, a gap perhaps three meters high, extending in every direction, its ceiling the underside of the leaf above, its floor the upper surface of the leaf below. Both surfaces glow. The space between them is lit from above and below, and the light is green, and the water is clear here, clearer than the water above, with fewer particulates, and you can see farther than you could before.

The columns pass through this space, and in this space, you can see them more clearly. Their surfaces are not merely rough; they are inhabited. The bark is a substrate for a hundred different organisms, layered on top of each other, each one occupying its niche — the fine filamentous things that catch particulate from the water, the flat things that graze on the filamentous things, the small hard-shelled things that seem to prey on the flat things, the things that look like nothing so much as tiny mouths set directly into the bark, opening and closing slowly, filtering water. Each column is a vertical ecosystem, and the ecosystems are identical from column to column, which is to say they are not ecosystems in the sense of contingent, improvisational communities but rather in the sense of permanent installations, structures that were determined so long ago that variation has been selected out of them. Every column is the same column. Every column is a copy of a template that was perfect the first time.

You realize, gradually, that the columns are not growing from the floor. They are growing from the ceiling. Or rather, they descend from above, from some structure too high to see, and they root in the floor, and the root-network below the floor is the same vascular system that lights the leaves. The whole architecture is a single organism. Not a colony, not a community — a single organism, with one vascular system, one set of genetic instructions expressed across a body so large that "body" is the wrong word. *Architecture* is the wrong word. *Landscape* is the wrong word. There is no word, because words were made by creatures whose bodies end at their skin, and this creature's body does not end. It extends in every direction as far as you can perceive, and it has been doing so for a length of time that makes the idea of "extending" feel like a verb in the wrong tense. It has extended. It is done extending. It is simply *being*, now, and being is a state so complete that it does not require time.

## VIII. The Equilibrium

Nothing changes here. This is not a metaphor. You spend what you estimate to be several hours in this place — your time-sense is unreliable, waterlogged, stripped of its usual referents — and in that time, nothing changes. The light does not shift. The temperature does not fluctuate. The sound does not vary. The drifters drift at the same rate. The shell-bearers click at the same frequency. The vascular fluid moves at the same speed through the same veins. You cannot find a single gradient. No direction is warmer or cooler. No depth is lighter or darker, within the perceivable range. No sound is louder or softer than any other sound of its type.

This is equilibrium, but not the equilibrium of a system in balance — a balance implies forces that could, if perturbed, become unbalanced. This is the equilibrium of a system that has passed through balance and come out the other side, into a state where perturbation is no longer a category. Nothing could perturb this place. Not a current, not a predator, not a geological event, not a star dying. The structure is too vast, too stable, too old. It has outlasted everything that could threaten it, including the concept of threat. It is in its final form. It has been in its final form for longer than the difference between "final form" and "all forms" has been meaningful.

You find this either terrifying or peaceful, and the truth is it is both, and neither. It simply is. The place does not care whether you find it peaceful. It does not care whether you find it terrifying. It does not care at all, not because it is indifferent but because "caring" is a concept that belongs to a different order of being, and this place has passed beyond that order into something that predates and postdates caring equally. It is a thing that exists in a state of total self-sufficiency, and your presence in it is a rounding error, a flicker so brief relative to its timescale that even the word "brief" is too generous.

## IX. The Sound, Again

The thrum is still going. It has not changed. It will not change. You listen to it more carefully now, and you realize you have been hearing it not just with your ears but with your whole body — your skin, your bones, your lungs. The water transmits it with a fidelity that air cannot match. You are inside the sound. You are inside the body that makes the sound. You are inside the vascular system, listening to it pump, and the pumping has no beginning and no end, no systole, no diastole, just a continuous flow at a continuous pressure through continuous channels that have been continuous for so long that "continuous" is not a description but a definition.

The shell-bearers click. The drifters hum at frequencies below the shell-bearers' clicks. The water itself produces a hiss, not from motion but from the dissolved gases and the particulate matter and the faint electrical potential across the surfaces of the leaves, and this hiss is the highest layer, the one you initially mistook for tinnitus, the one that is, in fact, the sound of the world's own chemical activity made audible by the density of the medium.

It is loud. It is always loud. It has always been loud. The creatures that live here were born in this sound and will die in this sound, and their senses have been shaped by it — the shell-bearers' clicks are precisely tuned to cut through the background, which is why you hear them so clearly, and the drifters' low hums resonate with the vascular system's deepest frequencies, which is how they locate each other in the vast dim space. Everything here is acoustic. Everything here is adapted to the sound. The sound is not an environment. It is the environment, as fundamental as the water, as fundamental as the light.

## X. What Remains

You begin to ascend. The water grows no lighter as you rise — the light comes from below, and distance from the source does not diminish it in the way you expect, because the leaf-layers overhead re-distribute what rises from below, bouncing it back, spreading it evenly, so that the entire water column is lit at the same dim level regardless of depth. You ascend for a long time. The columns continue past you, their surfaces unchanged, their inhabitants unchanged, the clicking and the humming and the thrum continuing at their fixed volumes. A drifter passes you, going down, its body tilted at an angle you cannot interpret, its filaments trailing behind it like a train. It does not look at you. Nothing here looks at anything. Vision, where it exists, is a secondary sense, a confirmation of what the sound and the water-chemistry have already communicated.

The leaf-floor above you approaches. You can see its underside — smoother than the top, less textured, the veins more prominent, the light more direct. You pass through another gap, another curling-apart of two leaves, and you are on the upper surface again, and the landscape is the same as it was: columns at their wide intervals, shell-bearers clicking across the floor, drifters in the far distance, the faintest shapes against the faintest light, and the sound, always the sound, and the cool water that you have forgotten you are in, and the dimness that you have stopped trying to see through, and the absolute, settled, completed *thereness* of a place that does not need you, has never needed anything, and will continue exactly as it is long after the water above and the water below and every living thing in between has become a different thing, and then a different thing, and then a different thing, until difference itself is the only constant, and even that will not matter here, because this place has already arrived at the end of all possible differences, and it is resting there, and it will rest there, and the sound of its resting is the loudest thing you have ever heard.
