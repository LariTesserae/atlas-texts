# The Sethan Plateau

## What Remains After Everything Has Finished Happening

The first thing is the flatness. Not the flatness of plains—that is a flatness that happens *to* a place. This is the flatness of a thing that has finished being made. The Sethan Plateau has been worn down so far and so long that there is nothing left to wear. It is not low. It sits at over four thousand meters, a wide high table of pale granite that once, perhaps hundreds of millions of years ago, was a range of mountains so vast they must have made the Himalayas look like vertebrae. Now they are gone. Not gone in the sense of eroded into valleys and ridges—gone in the sense of *resolved*. Every gram of material that could be moved has been moved. Every grain that could fall has fallen. What remains is the surface that was always underneath, revealed the way a floor is revealed when you stop walking on it.

The plateau extends further than you can comfortably hold in your attention. Not featureless—but the features are low. A swell of maybe forty centimeters, running east to west for two kilometers, is the most dramatic change in grade for a day's walk in any direction. The stone is smooth but not polished. It has the texture of something rubbed by time rather than by intention. Running your hand across it feels like touching very old skin. Warm under the palm. The color is hard to name. It shifts between a bone-white and a faint ochre depending on the light, and it is always, always warm.

The warmth is the plateau's defining quality. Not hot. Never hot. But persistently, quietly warm, the way a wall is warm hours after the sun has moved off it. But the sun has not done this. The stone holds its own warmth. Deep radiance from below—magma chambers long cooled to a sluggish paste that still breathes heat upward through kilometers of crust—reaches the surface here and has nowhere else to go. The plateau is a lid. The warmth collects in the top meter of granite and stays. At night, when the thin air at this elevation should carry the day's heat away in minutes, the stone remains warm to the touch. You can lie on it and feel held.

## The Water That Arrives and Does Not Stay

Rain comes, but it comes like an afterthought. A system will move across the plateau every few weeks, or perhaps it is every few months—the rhythm is not regular enough to name a season. Low grey clouds drag themselves across the sky and release a fine, cold rain that is almost mist. It falls for an hour, sometimes two. The stone darkens where it strikes. Pools form in the shallowest depressions—dish-sized hollows that have been ground into the granite over ages, most no deeper than a thumb. These pools are everywhere once you learn to look. Thousands of them. Some no wider than a coin. A handful are large enough to submerge your hand. They hold the rainwater, or they hold the condensation that gathers each morning when the plateau's retained warmth meets the cold air of altitude.

The water does not last. It either evaporates in the midday warmth or it seeps. The granite is not porous in the way sandstone is, but it has been fractured so many times and so deeply that the network of hairline cracks allows slow percolation downward. The water vanishes into the stone over hours. You can watch a pool shrink. It is meditative. The water does not pour or rush. It withdraws. If you put your ear to the stone near a shrinking pool, you can hear, very faintly, a sound like breathing. It is the water moving through cracks that are too small to see. This is one of the only sounds on the plateau.

There are no streams. There are no rivers. There is no runoff. The water that arrives is taken in, or it is lifted. The plateau has no drainage because the plateau has no slopes. It holds and releases, holds and releases, a patient respiratory cycle that has been running for longer than anything alive can remember.

## The Sparse Green That Remains

Plants exist here, but they are so few and so particular that the word *vegetation* feels excessive. What grows is limited to the very narrow seams where fractures in the granite have accumulated the faintest traces of mineral soil—dust that settled into cracks over millennia, broken down by the same chemical patience that flattened the mountains. In these cracks, certain things grow.

Lichens, first. They are the dominant life form of the plateau's surface. Pale grey and pale green crusts that follow the fracture lines like veins on a hand. They grow almost imperceptibly slowly. A patch the size of your fingernail may be a thousand years old. They do not pillage the stone. They do not accelerate its erosion. They simply live on it, their relationship with the granite so settled it is closer to marriage than parasitism. Underfoot, the lichens crunch very faintly when you step on them. They are dry. They are always dry. The rain doesn't seem to register with them. They take their moisture from the air, from the condensation, and they wait.

In the wider cracks—those perhaps two centimeters across—a single species of grass grows. It is related to nothing you would recognize. It stands no higher than an ankle and grows in tussocks of three or four stalks, always the same number, as though the plant has a rule. The stalks are stiff and the color of old hay year-round. They do not flower. They do not seed visibly. They simply persist, and occasionally a new tussock appears in a crack where one was not before. The grass is warm to the touch, though it should not be—it takes the stone's heat and holds it in its fibers as though it, too, is a piece of the plateau that has decided to stand up.

There is one other. In the very deepest fractures, where you cannot see the bottom, a fibrous root system exists—dark, almost black, descending into the stone along the crack walls. No one has seen the plant these roots belong to. The roots are the plant. It lives inside the granite, in the darkness of the fractures, taking what it needs from the moisture and the minerals that percolate down. It is the oldest living thing on the plateau. It may be the oldest living thing anywhere. It has been growing downward, centimeter by century, for longer than the mountains took to erode.

## What Lives Here in Overwhelming Number

This is the plateau's secret, the thing that does not make sense until you have been here for days.

The animals.

There should not be this many. The vegetation is almost nothing. The water is scarce. The elevation is extreme. And yet the plateau is *dense with life*. Not the kind of life you expect—not herds of grazing animals, not flocks of birds. These are small things. Invertebrates. Arthropods. Organisms that have no common names and would require a lineage of taxonomists to classify, because they appear to belong to no known lineage. They are the plateau's true inhabitants.

They live in the cracks. All of them. The fractures in the granite are not empty channels—they are highways, warrens, cities. The density of life in each crack is staggering. You discover this when you kneel beside a wide fissure and look in. The walls of the crack, to a depth of several centimeters, are *moving*. A seething, ordered motion of thousands of organisms going about their lives in the narrow vertical space between stone walls.

They are dark. Most of them are the grey-black of the granite itself, which makes them nearly invisible until they move. They are not insects—they have no wings, no antennae in the familiar arrangement. Their bodies are elongated, with many legs, but the legs are arranged differently than in centipedes or millipedes. Some have legs that extend laterally and grip the stone walls like climbing gear. Others have legs beneath them for walking on horizontal surfaces. The largest are the length of your finger. The smallest are near-microscopic. They exist in a continuous range of sizes, as though the species does not have discrete stages but grows continuously throughout its life, molting whenever the stone around it permits.

Their activity is constant but slow. They do not hurry. They move with the same patience as everything else here. They tend the lichens—you can see them grazing on the crusts, methodically clearing patches, which then regenerate over decades. They tend the grass roots, weaving among the black fibers in the deep cracks. They carry things—small fragments of mineral, bits of organic matter, particles that they seem to be depositing in particular places. They are building. The cracks are not just habitats. They are structures, maintained and modified by these organisms over timescales that make beaver dams look like impulse purchases.

The population density is what staggers. In a single linear meter of crack, there may be ten thousand individuals. The plateau is covered in cracks. There are millions of linear meters. The total number of organisms on the plateau is beyond any easy estimate—not millions, not billions. It is a number that requires scientific notation to express comfortably, and the plateau supports all of them on what appears to be nothing. On condensation. On lichen. On the mineral substrate itself. Some of them, you suspect, are not eating anything that you would call food. They are taking energy from the stone's warmth directly, through some metabolic pathway that has no analogue in the literature, converting thermal gradient into biological work.

They are aware of you. When you place your hand near a crack, the organisms nearest the surface slow down. They do not flee. They slow. They wait. When you withdraw, they resume. They have no predators here. They have no fear. They have only caution, and their caution operates on a timescale that means they will be still for an hour after you have gone, just to be certain.

At night, some of them emerge. The surface of the plateau, under starlight, develops a texture that was not there during the day. The cracks are visibly occupied—dark shapes along the fissure edges, moving with exquisite slowness across the open stone. They are feeding on the condensation. They are tending the lichen surfaces. They are doing what they do. If you lie still on the warm stone and watch, you can see them by the thousands, each one doing its small precise work, and the plateau surface looks like a city seen from very high up, with traffic in every street.

## The Remains of the People Who Came Before

There were people here. Not many, and not for long, but they came. The evidence is scattered and ambiguous.

In a shallow basin roughly three days' walk from the plateau's center, there are stones that do not belong. They are a darker granite, imported from somewhere else—perhaps from the lower elevations, perhaps from farther. They have been shaped. Not carved—shaped. Rounded, smoothed, arranged in a pattern that might have been a circle or might have been a spiral. The arrangement has been disturbed by time and by the organisms, who have undermined several of the stones by working the cracks beneath them. What remains is a suggestion of intention. Someone placed these. Someone chose this pattern.

The stones are warm like the rest of the plateau, but they hold the warmth differently. If you touch one, it seems to resonate very faintly—not vibration, not sound, but a sense of presence in the stone, as though the shaping gave it a different relationship to the heat below. This may be imagination. It is difficult to tell on the plateau, because the place does things to certainty.

There are marks on the plateau surface elsewhere. Grooves cut into the granite, straight lines that run for ten or twenty meters before stopping. They are too regular to be natural. They are not deep—perhaps a centimeter. They have been filled with lichen and are nearly invisible unless the light is at a low angle. Someone marked this place. Cut lines into the oldest, hardest, most finished surface on the continent, as though trying to add something to its perfection, or trying to read something from it. The lines do not form a pattern that anyone has decoded. They may not be a pattern. They may be a record. They may be a protest.

There are no tools. No pottery. No bones. Whatever the people used, they carried it with them or it has been absorbed by the plateau's patient chemistry. Only the stones and the grooves remain. The plateau has been eroding those, too. In another million years, the imported stones will be flat, the grooves will be gone, and the only evidence of human presence will be a slight discoloration in the granite that no one will know how to read.

## The Quiet That Is Not Silence

The plateau is almost silent, and the almost is important.

The dominant sound is wind, but the wind here is strange. Because the plateau is so flat and so high, the wind does not gust or swirl. It moves in long, steady sheets that slide across the stone with a sound like silk on silk. It has no edges. You cannot feel where it begins or ends. It is a continuous presence that varies in intensity over hours, sometimes dropping to nothing, sometimes rising to a soft moan that is felt more than heard. The wind carries nothing—no dust, no moisture, no scent. It is the cleanest wind you will ever encounter, and it makes almost no sound against the stone because the stone offers no resistance. It flows over the flat surface and is gone.

The other sounds are biological. The crack-dwelling organisms make a sound that is below the threshold of conscious hearing for most people—a low, collective rustling, like the turning of pages in a very large book, that seems to come from the ground itself. You feel it in your chest when you lie on the stone. It is the sound of millions of organisms moving slowly in their channels. It does not stop. It is the plateau's heartbeat.

Occasionally, one of the larger organisms will emerge from a crack and cross the open surface at night, and you can hear it if you are very close—a faint scraping of legs on warm granite, barely distinguishable from imagination. The sound lasts a few seconds and then it is gone, and the organism has returned to its channel, and the silence resumes.

Rain makes a sound, when it comes. A soft, universal ticking, like the plateau is being tapped by a thousand small fingers. The rain is so fine that most of it evaporates before reaching the stone, and the sound is more in the air than on the ground. It is a pleasant sound. It does not last.

And there is one more sound. Very deep. Below everything. A sound that you can only hear when you have been on the plateau for several days and your ears have recalibrated to the silence. It is a low, rhythmic pulse that seems to come from beneath the stone. It is not the organisms. It is not the wind. It is not the water in the cracks. It is the plateau itself—the deep granite, the cooling magma, the slow geological breath of a landmass that has been settling into its final form for longer than life has existed. It pulses once every thirty seconds or so. It is felt in the stone. It is the oldest sound in the world.

## How the Plateau Changes

It doesn't, really. Not in any way you can perceive.

The day cycle brings the sun across a sky that is deeper blue than any sky at lower elevation, and the stone warms slightly more than it does at night, and the lichens photosynthesize at their glacial rate, and the organisms in the cracks adjust their activity by a few percent. The night cycle brings the condensation, and the organisms emerge, and the stone cools by a degree or two before its internal warmth reasserts itself. That is the extent of daily change.

Over a year, the rain comes a few times. The pools fill and empty. The grass tussocks may gain a new stalk, or lose one. A lichen patch may expand by the width of a hair. A new crack may open—perhaps one every few centuries, as the thermal cycling stresses the granite in a new location, and the organisms discover it within a day and begin to colonize, their territory expanding by a centimeter.

Over a thousand years, the grooves cut by the people who came before will have lost another fraction of a millimeter of depth. The imported stones will have settled. The root system in the deep cracks will have descended another centimeter. The plateau will be very, very slightly lower—not measurably, not perceptibly, but in principle. The erosion continues. It will always continue. But it has so little left to work with that its progress is nearly philosophical. The plateau is the place where erosion comes to contemplate its own completion.

Nothing dramatic happens here. Nothing has happened here for a very long time. The plateau is the end of a story that took the whole age of the Earth to tell, and it ends not with drama but with flatness, with warmth, with the quiet industry of organisms that have found a way to live in the final chapter and have made it their home. It is not bleak. It is not sad. It is the most settled place you will ever stand. Everything that was going to happen has happened. What remains is the ongoing, the maintained, the slow breath of stone that has nothing left to prove.

You will not want to leave. This is the plateau's only danger. It is so finished, so resolved, so free of event, that it feels like rest. Like the rest after everything. You will lie on the warm stone and feel the organisms moving beneath you and the wind above you and the deep pulse below, and you will think: *I could stay here. I could settle into this the way the plateau settled into itself. I could be part of the final form.*

And you could. People have. The plateau does not refuse. It simply goes on, and you go on with it, and eventually you are part of the flatness, and the organisms find your cracks and move in, and the wind smooths your edges, and the rain fills your hollows and drains away, and you become, over a time that is not long by the plateau's measure, part of the most patient place in the world.
