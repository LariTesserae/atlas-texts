# The Hollow Quarter

It was once a street. That much is obvious — or would be, if anyone remained to read the shape of it. Two long ridges of fitted stone rise on either side of a narrow channel, the channel worn to a smooth shallow trough by ten thousand years of the water that collects here and has nowhere else to go. The ridges are the walls of buildings, or were. Their upper stories fell long ago, and what remains is the ground floor, the foundation, the stubborn bones of a settlement that might have been a town or might have been a single sprawling complex — difficult to say now. The stone is a pale volcanic tuff, porous and light, shot through with veins of something darker that might be mineral staining or might be the fossilized remains of something organic. It weeps. That is the first thing you notice.

Not rain, exactly. Rain comes, but rarely, and when it does it comes as a fine cold drizzle that lasts hours and soaks without ever quite falling — a mist that has made up its mind. More constant is the condensation. Warm air from somewhere — some thermal breath rising through fractures in the deep rock — meets the cold stone surfaces and deposits a sheen of water that never fully evaporates. The walls shine like the inside of a mouth. The channel between them holds a thin skin of water, never more than ankle depth, never dry. It flows, but barely. It seeps. It has been seeping for longer than anything in this place has been alive.

---

The vegetation is the thing that takes you by surprise, because the cold is genuine — a high-altitude cold, thin and dry, the kind that makes your lungs work and your fingers clumsy within minutes. And yet the walls are thick with life. Not the green you would expect. A pale moss, almost white, grows in overlapping plates across the tuff, its edges curled and crystalline with frost on mornings like this one. Between the moss plates, in the gaps where mortar has crumbled and the stone has cracked, a darker growth takes hold — something fungal, something that fruits in tiny shelf-like brackets the color of bruised skin, lavender and grey. Long trailing filaments hang from the upper edges of the ruined walls, pale threads that sway in the air movement and collect the condensation, drip it downward in a constant quiet rain of their own making. Where the filaments are densest, the air beneath them is several degrees warmer. They are breathing, in their way. Holding heat.

The effect is that the street — the channel, the gutter, the hollow between the walls — forms a narrow corridor of living dampness in a landscape that is otherwise austere and ancient. Above the walls, the sky is a flat pale grey, and the wind moves through with a thin keening sound, but down here the air is still and wet and surprisingly temperate, and the sound is different.

---

The sound. That is the second thing — maybe the first, depending on which sense you trust. This place is *loud*. Not with any single source. The wind above funnels between the ridges and produces a sustained note, a low sustained chord that shifts pitch as the air thickens or thins. The water moving through the channel makes a sound like whispering, but rhythmic, like breathing. The fungal brackets creak and click as they expand and contract with temperature changes, tiny percussive sounds that carry in the hard acoustic of the stone corridor. The moss, of all things, makes sound — a faint crackling, like static, where the frost forms and breaks and reforms on its surface, a process that happens continuously because the thermal moisture keeps the temperature hovering right at the freezing point.

Together it is a layered, constant, textured noise. Not cacophony — more like a drone composition, a standing wave of sound that fills the hollow from end to end. You feel it in your chest. The stone vibrates with it, very faintly, and the water in the channel shivers with micro-ripples that have nothing to do with flow.

If you stand still long enough, you start to hear patterns in it. They are probably not real. Probably.

---

The scale is small. The hollow is perhaps three hundred paces long, the walls rise no higher than twice your height, and the channel is narrow enough that you could almost touch both walls with outstretched hands. It is a single feature. A single fragment of something larger that once was and is no longer. Beyond the walls, the terrain opens into a broad, flat expanse — high and cold and eroded nearly to nothing. The landscape here has been ground down by time into its essential geometry. Gentle ridges, shallow depressions, the last remnants of what might have been mountains or mesas, now worn to undulations. The stone everywhere is smooth. Not polished — *finished*. As though the land reached its final form and then stopped, and has been resting in that final form ever since. There is no active erosion. Nothing left to erode. There is no fresh rock, no uplift, no visible process of weathering or deposition. The land is done. It is at rest.

Except for the water. Except for the vegetation. Except for the sound.

The hollow quarter is the only place where something is still happening.

---

There are no animals. You might hear something, once — a scratching or a faint cry carried on the wind from over the ridges — but if you track it, you will find nothing. The ecology here is almost entirely botanical, almost entirely fungal, almost entirely still. Insects exist, but you will see them rarely: a pale winged thing, like a termite but translucent, that emerges from the fungal brackets and drifts on the still air before returning. A tiny black beetle that moves across the water's surface in the channel, pushing a dimple before it. These are the only fauna. They are barely fauna at all. The ecosystem is so reduced, so minimal, that it feels more like a thought experiment than a living place — as though someone asked, *what is the least amount of life needed to keep something going?* and this was the answer.

---

And the strangeness. It is not dramatic. Nothing here will hurt you, or transform you, or show you something beyond comprehension. But there is a wrongness to the hollow that accumulates slowly and does not leave.

The first sign is the sound patterns. They are not real. You know they are not real. But they persist.

The second sign is the warmth. The air in the hollow is warmer than it should be — not by much, perhaps two or three degrees, but enough to notice, enough to sustain the moss and the fungi in a place where the ambient temperature should kill them. The source is not the thermal vents. The thermal vents are real, but the warmth they provide is negligible. The warmth comes from the walls themselves, from the stone, from somewhere inside the fitted blocks of volcanic tuff. The stone is warmer than the air. It has been warmer than the air for a very long time.

The third sign is the shape of the channel. It is not straight. It curves, very gently, along its three hundred paces, and if you map the curve and extend it, it forms a circle. A circle perhaps a kilometer in diameter. The hollow is a quarter of that circle — one arc, one segment. The other three quarters are buried, collapsed, grown over, or simply gone. But the geometry is there, hidden in the land's final form, a pattern too large to see from inside and too subtle to notice from above unless you are looking for it.

Something was built here. Something circular. Something that required warmth and water and stone fitted with precision. And whatever it was, it has been gone for so long that the land itself has forgotten it, has worn it down to a single damp corridor between two low walls, a place where moss grows and fungi click and the wind plays a note that almost sounds like language.

---

You stand in the hollow quarter. The water seeps around your feet, cold but not freezing. The walls breathe their condensation. The sound surrounds you, fills you, settles into your bones. Above, the sky is pale and featureless. Beyond, the land stretches in every direction, flat, ancient, finished, waiting for nothing.

Nothing is waiting for anything here. That is the feeling. That is the strangeness, in the end — not that something uncanny is happening, but that something happened for so long that it wore itself and everything else out, and what remains is not a ruin but a residue. The last dampness of a process that completed long before you arrived. The final shape of a thing that was built and used and abandoned and forgotten and eroded and eroded and eroded until there was nothing left to erode, and then — only then — it became this.

A hollow. A quarter. A sound that is almost a word.

The moss crackles. The water moves. The pale insects drift. The stone is warm.
