# The Bloom Vats

You enter through a brass port that screws shut behind you — no handle on the inside, though it hardly matters. The chamber is not large. Perhaps twenty meters long, eight wide, and the ceiling lost behind a slow drift of condensation that never quite finishes falling. Everything is wet. Not the wet of a surface that has been rained on, but the wet of a place that has never been dry, that was assembled wet, that would not know how to begin being dry.

This is a room inside a machine, or a machine shaped like a room. The distinction collapses here.

## The Water

The water comes from everywhere. It does not pour. It extrudes. Every vertical surface — the riveted iron pillars, the walls of stratified copper sheeting, the bundled clusters of pipe that run along the ceiling like veins in a forearm — weeps continuously. Not dramatically. A sheen. A slow, patient beading that swells until gravity makes a claim on it, and then a single line running down, down, until it reaches a horizontal surface and spreads into a film too thin to see but thick enough to make every footing uncertain. The air itself is saturated. You breathe water that has not yet committed to being water. Your lungs adjust within an hour or they don't. Locals — there are no locals, only occupants — say the chamber teaches you or kills you and is indifferent to which.

The floor is grated. Iron lattice, close-set, and beneath it: more water. A channel, perhaps a meter deep, flowing so slowly that you would only notice it by dropping something buoyant into it and watching it drift left over the course of ten minutes. The channel is not natural. It is milled. The grates are numbered. Every fourth slat is stamped with a code that means something to someone, somewhere, in an office that has never been wet.

Above, the ceiling is a thicket. Pipes of varying caliber — some no wider than a finger, some broad enough to crawl through — intersect and overlap in configurations that suggest not design but accretion. Each generation of engineers added their own circulation. The oldest pipes are ceramic, glazed green, and they sweat the least. The newest are a dull gray polymer, and they sweat the most. The whole ceiling gleams. It looks like the interior of a throat.

## The Growth

And growing from everywhere — from the seams between pipes, from the bolts that hold the grates down, from the junction boxes and the valve housings and the cable runs and the inside of spent lamp housings — is a vegetation so dense and so specific that it could only exist here, in this exact arrangement of moisture and warmth and metal and neglect.

It is not green. That is the first surprise. The dominant color is a deep amber, almost bronze, shading to oxblood where the growth is thickest. The plants — if that is what they are; they have no leaves, no roots in any recognizable sense — are tubular. They emerge from crevices as thin as wire, swell to the width of a thumb, and then branch, and branch again, until a single colonizer has become a bush of interconnected amber tubes, each one turgid with liquid, each one warm to the touch. Not body-warm. Engine-warm. They grow toward the heat sources. They cluster around the transformer boxes, the pump housings, the junction points where electrical current enters the chamber and does its work. They are thermophilic. They are also, as far as anyone has determined, photosynthetic — but the light here is not strong, and what they seem to want from light is not its energy but its *direction*. They grow up. Not toward the sun, which they have never seen, but toward the ceiling lamps, which are the only up they know.

Between the amber tubes, in the gaps too small for them to colonize, grows a second stratum: a flat, overlapping flora like wet shingles, slate-blue, each tile no larger than a thumbnail. They cover the pipe surfaces completely where the tubes have not yet reached. They are damp. They are always damp. If you touch them, they feel like the inside of a cheek. They do not grow on the grates. They cannot abide iron that has been walked on. They are, in their own way, selective.

And beneath both, in the film of water that covers every surface, a third form: a haze of something between algae and rust. It is orange. It is thin. It moves — not with current, but with something that might be intention. Over the course of hours, it migrates from one end of a pipe to the other. It pools at the low points. It avoids the blue shingles. Whether it is plant or mineral or something else is a question that has been asked and not answered.

## The Sound

Almost nothing.

The pumps that must exist — that must drive the water through the channel, that must pressurize the pipes — are somewhere else. Far away. Behind walls, below floors, in chambers that this chamber depends on but never sees. What reaches you is a vibration so low it is more sensation than sound, a thickening of the air at long intervals, like a heartbeat in the floor. The water dripping makes no sound because it falls such short distances. The amber tubes make no sound because they are still. The blue shingles make no sound because they are flat and have no mechanism for sound. The only thing you hear, if you stand still long enough, is the faint tick of metal contracting as the temperature shifts by fractions of a degree over the course of an hour. And your own breathing, which is loud and wrong-sounding here, mammalian and urgent in a place that is neither.

## The Chamber's Life

There are no animals. None. No insects, no fish in the channel, no birds in the ceiling pipes, no vermin in the walls. The growth does not seem to attract consumers. It is not poisonous — it simply exists outside the economy of eating. Nothing here is eaten. Nothing here eats. The chamber is a closed loop of water, heat, light, and growth, and it has been running long enough that the growth has become the architecture.

The amber tubes, where they grow thick enough, bear weight. You can put a hand on them and lean. They do not give. In places, someone — the engineers, the maintainers, whoever came before — has cut them back to clear access to valves, and the cut surfaces have healed into flat amber pads, waxy and slightly tacky, like cut fruit that has been left to dry. The cuts do not regrow. The plant simply routes around them, sending new tubes from the base, and within weeks the valve is buried again.

The blue shingles, if removed, expose a surface of bright copper beneath — copper that has not corroded because the shingles have been protecting it. They are, in their way, a preservative. The chamber is being maintained by the things that are colonizing it. The vegetation is eating the chamber and simultaneously keeping it alive. This is the central paradox, and the chamber does not resolve it. It simply continues.

## The Made

Everything here is built. There is no bedrock beneath the grates, no soil beneath the channel. The floor of the channel is more iron — iron laid over iron laid over something that might be concrete, or might be very old, very compressed, very dead growth. The walls are built. The ceiling is built. The pipes are built. The growth is the only thing that was not placed here by a hand, and even that is uncertain — the amber tubes may have been engineered, seeded into the seams as an experiment in bio-maintenance that outlived its experimenters. Nobody knows. The chamber does not explain itself. It has stamps and codes and the residue of intentionality, but no instructions, no plaques, no manifest. It is a room that does something. What it does is not clear.

The light comes from lamps set into the ceiling at regular intervals — glass housings, most of them clouded with moisture and the beginnings of amber tube colonization. The light they produce is steady, even, the color of weak tea. It does not flicker. It does not change. The lamps are on a cycle that may be twenty-four hours or may be something else entirely; the chamber is windowless, and time here is measured only by the growth rate of the plants, which is so slow that a day and a night look exactly the same.

## The Atmosphere

It is warm. Not hot. The warmth of a greenhouse, of a body, of a room where machinery is running somewhere nearby. The humidity is absolute. Your skin does not dry. Your clothes do not dry. Paper left here would warp and soften within an hour and dissolve within a day. Metal left here would be colonized within a week. The air tastes of iron and something vegetal, something sweet and not quite organic, like the smell of a greenhouse filtered through a machine shop.

You move slowly because the surfaces are wet and the grates are narrow and the growth narrows them further. The chamber forces a particular posture: head slightly down, hands slightly out, feet placed with care. You become aware of your body as a thing that takes up space in a place where space is fully accounted for. Every surface is occupied. Every gap is filled. The amber tubes grow in the spaces between pipes. The blue shingles grow on the surfaces of pipes. The orange haze grows on the water that covers the surfaces. The condensation forms on the ceiling that shelters the surfaces. There is no vacancy. There is no margin. The chamber is a machine that has been running so long that it has become an ecology, and the ecology has no room for anything that is not already here.

You are the only thing in this room that was not part of the plan. You are wet, and warm, and quiet, and very, very temporary.
