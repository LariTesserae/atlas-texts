# The Terrace Quarter

The rain comes at intervals here, as it always has. Not storms, not downpours — just a steady, patient wetness that arrives every few hours, moves through, and leaves the stone damp until the next time. You can almost set your watch by it, except no one here has a watch. The sky is a flat pale grey, and the light is the kind of light that has no source — it just exists, evenly, without shadow or emphasis, as though the world is being illuminated from inside its own walls.

---

The Terrace Quarter is old. Not ancient in the sense of ruins — nothing here is ruined — but old in the sense of a thing that arrived at its final form a long time ago and has simply continued. The buildings are low, two stories at most, built from the same grey stone that makes up the hillside they sit on. You can see where the stone was cut from the terraces themselves — shallow rectangular hollows in the earth behind the highest row of houses, like wounds that healed over clean. The stone is the earth and the earth is the stone, and the distinction between what was built and what was always here has been lost to weather and time.

There are eleven terraces, descending the hill in steps. Each one is perhaps four meters wide and thirty meters long, and on each terrace sit two or three buildings, separated by stretches of bare, packed ground. The buildings share walls in places, but not always — there are gaps, narrow passages where you can stand and look down along the face of the hill and see the edges of the other terraces stepping away below you, each one a clean horizontal line of stone and earth.

The walls are thick. Half a meter, maybe more. They have to be, because the hillside behind them is always pushing, always settling, and the buildings were built to hold their ground. The stone is fitted without mortar — each block shaped to sit against its neighbors with a precision that must have taken enormous patience. After so long, the joints are tight enough that water runs down them in thin lines during the rain but doesn't get through. Moss grows in the seams, a dark green that is almost black when wet, almost grey when dry.

The roofs are flat, made of stone slabs laid across timber beams. The beams are old — not original, but old. You can tell because the ones that have been replaced are lighter in color, a pale wood that hasn't yet darkened to the near-black of the originals. There is always a replacement happening somewhere, but slowly. A beam here, a slab there. The Quarter doesn't decay so much as shed and regrow, one piece at a time, over decades.

---

There are thirty-seven buildings. Not all are lived in. Some are workshops — you can tell by the wide doors and the stone benches built into the exterior walls. Some are storage, their interiors dark and cool and smelling of dry dust. A few are empty, their doors tied shut with rope, their function forgotten or simply no longer needed. No one is in a hurry to fill them. They wait.

The people here — there are perhaps sixty, maybe seventy — work stone and wood and fiber. You can hear the sounds of their work, but only barely. The tap of a chisel carries across the terraces in the damp air but arrives soft, as though the sound itself has been worn down by the journey. Someone is always shaping something — a bowl, a building block, a tool handle. The work is slow because the work has always been slow and because there is no reason for it to be otherwise.

The tools are stone and bone. There is metal here, but not much — a few iron blades, dark with age, used for the finest cutting and kept wrapped in oiled cloth. They are old enough that no one remembers how they were made, and careful enough that no one has broken one. For everything else, the stone serves. Flint for cutting. Basalt for grinding. A grey-green schist that holds an edge and comes from a place two days' walk down the hill, a place the people here know about but rarely visit, because the stone is brought to them by others who come up the hill a few times a year.

---

There is a channel that runs along the back of the highest terrace, cut into the stone, and from it, thin pipes of baked clay feed down to each level. The water comes from a spring above the Quarter — not a large one, but constant. It runs through the channel and down through the pipes and collects in stone basins at the edge of each terrace. The overflow runs down the face of the hill in thin streams that find the seams between the terrace walls and disappear into the earth. The water is cold and tastes of mineral and stone.

The rain supplements this, though it doesn't need to. The rain comes because the rain comes. It fills the basins further. It runs along the channels and the paths and the packed-earth surfaces of the terraces. It beads on the stone walls and the moss and the wood of the doors. Then it stops, and the dampness remains for an hour or two, and then the surfaces are merely dry again, waiting for the next rain, which will come when it comes.

The terraces face west. The view from the edge of the lowest one is of more hills, descending in soft folds toward a valley that is sometimes visible and sometimes lost in haze. The haze is always there, a pale veil that softens the distance. It moves — slowly, without wind — but it never clears entirely. The world beyond the Quarter is known but remote. People go down. People come up. The Quarter continues.

---

There is one tree. It grows on the fifth terrace, in the gap between two buildings, its roots splitting the packed earth and finding their way into the seams of the stone below. It is a juniper, thick-trunked and twisted, its bark grey and fibrous. It is taller than the buildings. Its canopy spreads unevenly, heavier on the side that faces away from the hill, as though it is leaning toward the view. It is old — older than the buildings around it, possibly older than the Quarter itself. Its berries are small and dark blue and fall in autumn to the terrace below, where they are collected or ignored, depending on who is living in the adjacent building that year.

Under the tree, there is a stone bench. Someone carved it from a single block, a long time ago. It is smooth from use and weather. People sit there. Not always, not often, but it is not unusual to see someone sitting and looking out at the haze and the hills. They don't stay long. They sit, they look, they go back to what they were doing.

There are a few other plants — tufts of grass in the seams of the stonework, a patch of moss on the north face of one building, a low, spreading herb with grey leaves that someone grows in a stone trough on the seventh terrace. But the juniper is the only thing that rises above the level of the rooftops. Against the grey sky, its dark mass is the only irregular shape in the Quarter's clean geometry.

---

The animals here are few. There are birds — small, grey-brown, quick — that nest in the juniper and in the gaps under the rooftops. They eat insects and the juniper berries and whatever scraps they find. They are not afraid of people, but they are not tame. They simply exist in the same space, occupying the same territory, ignoring each other with the comfort of long cohabitation.

There are insects — thin, dark things that skim the surface of the water basins, and slow beetles that live in the moss and under the stone slabs of the paths. They are not numerous. The Quarter is too high and too cool for abundance.

Occasionally, something larger comes through. A goat, wandered up from lower down. A fox, passing through at dawn. These are events, noted and then forgotten. They do not stay. The Quarter is not a place that invites staying, except for those who were already here.

---

The sound of the Quarter is the sound of water and work. The channel at the top of the hill, running its thin, constant stream. The rain, when it comes, a soft percussion on stone. The tap of a chisel, distant and rhythmless. The scrape of a broom — someone is always sweeping the packed earth, keeping the paths clear of debris. These sounds do not combine into anything. They exist separately, layered but not coordinated, each one occupying its own space in the quiet.

There is no wind here, or almost none. The hillside is sheltered by the higher ground above, and the buildings block what little might come. The air is still. It is cool — not cold, but the kind of cool that settles into you if you stand too long without moving. It smells of wet stone and moss and the faint, dry dust of the empty buildings.

---

The Quarter does not change. This is not a rule, not a law — it is simply the nature of the place. Buildings are repaired, not rebuilt. People are born, live, and die in the same terraces their parents did. The tools are the same tools, the methods the same methods. The spring runs. The rain comes. The juniper drops its berries. The chisel taps. The broom scrapes.

If you came here, you would not be turned away. You would be given a bench to sit on, water to drink, and you would be left to exist in the space for as long as you wanted. No one would ask you why you came. No one would ask you to leave. The Quarter would continue around you, its rhythms as steady and unhurried as the water in the channel, and you would either settle into those rhythms or you would not, and either way, the Quarter would remain.
