# The Vermillion Levels

## I.

There is a city beneath a perpetual amber dusk, and it is still being born.

You come to it by way of a long descent — not down stairs or slope, but down through a gradual thickening of the air itself. The transition is invisible. One moment you walk through dry scrubland under a wide pale sky; the next, the horizon has crept closer, the light has turned the color of cured resin, and your skin is beading with moisture that seems to condense from nowhere, as though the air itself is sweating.

The city is called nothing. Or rather, it has had seven names across the gradual centuries of its emergence, each one discarded when it no longer fit, and the current name — which translates loosely to *the place that is still happening* — is already falling out of use. The residents don't name it. They point to it. They say *there*. They say *where we are*.

It is built across a series of low, wide terraces — not carved terraces, not engineered ones, but natural formations, shelving rock that steps down in long shallow laps toward a river basin that may or may not contain water at any given time. The terraces are young. Geologically young. The stone is sharp-edged, unrounded by time. You can run your hand along the faces and feel the crisp mineral fracture lines, the clean breaks where the rock split and heaved itself into these stepped platforms perhaps a few thousand years ago — yesterday, in stone terms. There is no moss on the vertical faces. No smoothing. The edges catch light like recently broken glass.

And on these terraces, the city.

## II.

It is not a dense city. That is the first surprise. For all its scale — and it extends for what might be three kilometers along the terrace gradient, building after building stepping down and down toward the intermittent basin — there is enormous space between structures. You can stand at the upper margin and look out across the whole of it and see gaps, courtyards, empty platforms of bare stone between clusters of construction. The buildings do not crowd. They breathe. They are arranged in loose constellations, each cluster separated from the next by stretches of raw terrace stone that have been left untouched, as though the builders wanted to preserve the original ground, the newness of it, the memory of what was here before the city decided to happen.

The buildings themselves are low. Two stories, three at most. They are constructed from a material that is not quite concrete and not quite ceramic — it has the poured quality of the former and the faint translucence of the latter, so that in the dim amber light the walls seem to hold a faint interior glow, as if something behind the surface is gently luminous. The material is a pale ochre, almost the same color as the stone terraces, so that from a distance the city seems to grow out of the rock, to be a secretion of the landscape rather than an imposition upon it.

There are no roads. There are paths — worn tracks in the stone, faintly polished by foot traffic, winding between the clusters. Some of these paths are covered: simple overhead structures, lean-to roofs of the same ochre material, providing shelter without walls. Beneath these covered paths, condensation gathers on the undersides of the roofs and falls in slow, irregular drops — *plip, plip* — landing in shallow worn depressions in the path stone where the water pools briefly before evaporating or running off into thin cracks. These drips are the closest thing to weather in much of the city. The residents walk beneath these covered paths with the practiced ease of people who know which sections drip and which don't, stepping around the wet spots without looking.

## III.

The light is the thing you notice third, after the space and the stone.

It is always approximately this: dim, amber, sourceless. There is no visible sun. The sky — if it is a sky — is a low, even dome of warm color that shifts through a narrow range over the course of a day-cycle. It deepens toward burnt orange during what must be the middle of the cycle, then fades to a pale tallow at the edges, but it never becomes bright. It never becomes dark. The light simply is, at a level somewhere between late afternoon and the last moments before dusk, perpetually.

The effect is that everything in the city is seen in a warm half-light. Shadows are soft and shallow. Colors are muted but warm. The ochre buildings glow faintly. The green of the vegetation — and there is vegetation, surprising, abundant vegetation — reads as deep and saturated against the pale stone, almost luminous in the dimness.

You realize, after a time, that the light is not coming from above. It comes from the air itself. The moisture in the atmosphere — the perpetual damp, the condensation that beads on every surface and gathers in slow drops beneath every overhang — holds the light. Suspends it. The water in the air acts as a diffuse medium, scattering whatever source illuminates this place into a uniform, directionless glow. You cannot find the light by looking up. You can only find it by looking at things — at the way the stone catches it, the way water surfaces hold a faint reflection of it, the way the broad leaves of the terrace gardens seem to collect it in their waxy surfaces and hold it, briefly, like cupped hands.

## IV.

The vegetation is the fourth revelation, and perhaps the strangest.

It is everywhere. Not climbing the buildings — the buildings are too clean-lined, too precisely made for that — but filling every space between them. The terraces, in their natural state, are bare stone. But the residents have cultivated something in the gaps, in the courtyards, in the wide empty stretches between clusters, that is not quite a garden and not quite a farm and not quite a wild ecosystem, but something that holds qualities of all three.

The dominant plant is a broad-leafed, low-growing thing — knee-height at most — with leaves like pale green shields, waxy on top, slightly furred underneath. It grows in dense, interconnected mats across the open stone, sending roots into every crack and crevice of the terrace surface. It is, you realize, breaking the rock. Slowly, patiently, inexorably. The roots find the fracture lines of the young stone and push into them, widen them, fracture the sharp edges into something softer. The plant is old enough here — established enough — that the spaces between buildings are no longer bare stone but a soft, uneven carpet of green over gradually crumbling rock. The sharp geology of the terraces is being slowly ground down by biology.

Among the broad-leafed mats, taller things grow. Stemmed plants, chest-high, with spiral leaves and thick, almost woody stalks. They cluster in the courtyards, and their upper leaves move — slowly, almost imperceptibly, turning to track something. Not the light; the light is directionless. They track moisture. You watch one leaf-panel rotate over the course of several minutes, orienting itself toward a covered path where condensation drips, its surface subtly cupped to catch what falls. The plants drink from the air. They drink from the condensation. They drink from the intermittent rain that comes — you are told — in long, slow, unpredictable cycles, a fine mist that can last for hours and leaves every surface filmed with water.

There are no trees. There is nothing tall enough to be called a tree. The vegetation is low and dense and spreading, a ground-level ecology, and its effect is to make the city feel as though it is sinking slowly into a green sea. The buildings rise from the mats like the last solid things in a gradual inundation. The paths between them are kept clear by constant foot traffic, but the edges creep inward. The residents trim the growth back from their doorways with a casual, habitual motion — you see someone walk out of a building, bend, snap a few stems that have encroached on the threshold, and continue on, without breaking stride. The stems are set aside in small piles to dry. Later, you will learn what they are used for.

## V.

The city has infrastructure. This is not immediately apparent, because the infrastructure is invisible.

There is no exposed wiring. No pipes. No ducts. No grates, no vents, no junction boxes. The buildings appear, at first glance, to be simple stone-and-ceramic structures, elegant in their clean lines but technologically primitive. This impression is wrong.

The technology here operates at scales below and above the visible. The building material itself — the ochre pour-stone, as the residents call it — is a programmable substrate. It is not inert. It is, on a molecular level, a lattice of nanoscale structures that can be configured to perform a range of functions: structural support, thermal regulation, light channeling, moisture management, computation. The walls of the buildings are also their wiring, their plumbing, their insulation, and their computational substrate. When a resident places a hand on a wall and the wall becomes slightly warmer — directing heat away from the interior — this is not a mechanical system responding to a sensor. It is the wall itself, the material itself, adjusting its properties at a molecular level.

This is the technology that lies beyond the modern. Not machinery. Not devices. *Material*. The residents of this city do not build with inert substances and then add technology to them. They build with technology itself, structured as material. The distinction is profound. There is nothing in this city that is merely a wall. Every surface is functional. Every surface is, in a sense, alive — not biologically, but in the way that a system is alive, responsive, adaptive, processing.

The results are subtle in the extreme. A room is warm where you sit and cool two meters away. A wall is opaque until you touch it, and then a section becomes translucent — not a window, but a region of the wall itself reconfiguring to admit light. A doorway knows when you approach it and opens not by sliding or swinging but by *becoming an opening* — the material reconfiguring, the solid wall briefly discontinuous. You walk through. The wall reconstitutes behind you. There is no seam. There is no mechanism. There is only material that is, for a moment, not there.

## VI.

The river.

You walk down through the terraces — it takes twenty minutes, descending the shallow steps, crossing the wide green mats, passing through clusters of ochre buildings — and you reach the lowest terrace, the basin, and you find the river.

Except it is not always a river.

The basin is a wide, flat shelf of stone — the youngest stone of all, the sharpest, the most recently exposed — and across its surface water moves in a branching, anastomosing pattern, splitting and rejoining, spreading into shallow sheets and gathering into narrow channels, never more than ankle-deep, never less than a film. The water comes from somewhere upstream — you can see the pattern converging from the far end of the basin, where a low escarpment feeds a series of seeps and springs — but it does not flow in a single channel. It *distributes*. It finds every crack, every depression, every shallow in the stone, and fills it, and moves on. The result is a wide, glittering, ever-shifting mosaic of water and stone, a delta with no mouth, a river that cannot decide what shape it is.

This is where the condensation comes from. The water in the basin evaporates steadily in the warm air — and it is warm here, warmer than the upper terraces, the heat rising from the stone and the shallow water — and the vapor rises and meets the cooler air above the terraces and condenses on every available surface. The city's damp, the dripping covered paths, the beaded walls, the moisture that the plants drink — it all originates here, in this wide shallow spreading river that never settles, that never stops moving, that feeds the whole system from below.

The river sounds like almost nothing. A thin, shifting whisper. The sound of water moving over flat stone in sheets less than a centimeter deep. It is the quietest river you have ever heard. You have to stand at its edge and hold your breath to hear it at all — a soft, continuous susurration, like fabric moving, like breath through a slightly open mouth.

## VII.

The residents are few.

For a city of this scale — the terraces extending out of sight in both directions, the clusters numbering in the dozens, perhaps the hundreds — the population is remarkably low. You walk for ten minutes between clusters without seeing anyone. The covered paths are empty. The courtyards are still. The buildings stand in their loose constellations with their faint interior glow and their reconfiguring walls, and no one is in them, or no one is visible, which may not be the same thing.

When you do encounter residents, they do not startle you. They appear at a distance — a figure moving between buildings, a shape bending over the green mats, someone standing at the river's edge — and they move with a slowness and deliberation that matches the pace of the place. They are not hurried. The city does not demand hurry. Its systems operate at the speed of stone, of water, of growth. The residents have calibrated themselves to this tempo.

They are — and this is difficult to describe, and you are not certain you are perceiving it correctly — they are not entirely physical. Or rather, they are physical, but their physicality is negotiable in a way that yours is not. You watch a woman step through a wall — not through a doorway, not through a reconfigured opening, but *through* the solid material, her body seeming to lose resolution at the boundary, becoming briefly indistinct, a soft-edged figure passing through a soft-edged surface, and then resolving on the other side with a faint sound like a held breath released. She does not appear to notice this as remarkable. She continues walking.

You ask about this. You are told — in the unhurried, indirect manner of the residents, who speak in long, looping sentences that arrive at their meaning the way the river arrives at the sea — that the bodies they wear are made of the same substrate as the buildings. That they are, in a sense, living in the same way the walls are living: not biologically, but as systems. As material that processes. The residents are not born. They are *poured*. They are given shape and then given agency, and the two — shape and agency — learn to coexist, and the result is a person. When they pass through walls, it is not a trick or a technology. It is two pieces of the same material, briefly recognizing each other, briefly allowing each other's patterns to interpenetrate.

## VIII.

There are animals here. You see almost none of them.

The residents tell you about them in passing, the way they tell you about everything — indirectly, slowly, with more attention to texture than to fact. There are, apparently, small creatures that live in the green mats. Not insects — something else, something soft-bodied and many-legged, that feeds on the broad leaves and in turn feeds the soil. You see evidence of them: tiny perforations in the waxy leaf surfaces, small burrow-openings in the root-compacted soil between stones. But you never see the creatures themselves. They are too small, too quick, too well-hidden in the dense growth.

There is also something in the river. You catch a glimpse of it once — a shadow moving across the shallow water, fast, low, barely disturbing the surface. A flat, wide shape, like a ray, skimming over the stone in the millimeter of water above the riverbed. It is gone before you can focus on it. The residents do not name it. They call it *the thing in the water* and seem to regard it with a kind of affectionate indifference, the way you might regard a sparrow outside your window: acknowledged, appreciated, unexamined.

## IX.

The city is active. Not bustling — never bustling — but *active*, in the way that a slow fire is active, or a tide is active. Processes are underway. You become aware of them gradually.

There is construction. On the upper terraces, where the stone is newest and sharpest, new buildings are being poured. You watch a resident — a tall, thin figure, their ochre body catching the dim light — kneel at the edge of a bare stone platform and place both hands flat on the surface. They hold this position for a long time. You cannot see what is happening, but after perhaps fifteen minutes, a faint discoloration appears in the stone beneath their palms — a pale ochre tint, spreading slowly outward, the bare stone beginning to acquire the color and texture of the building material. They are *seeding* a building. The substrate is being introduced into the raw stone, and it will, over the coming days or weeks, self-assemble into a structure, growing like a crystal, finding its form through a process that is half engineering and half organic growth. The resident will return periodically to guide the process, to shape the emergence, to decide where walls will be and where doorways will form. But the material does most of the work itself. It knows what it is. It needs only to be told where.

There is also deconstruction. Lower down, near the river, an older cluster of buildings is being *unpoured* — the substrate gradually reabsorbing into the stone, the walls losing their faint glow, becoming thinner, more transparent, dissolving. You learn that this is intentional. Buildings here are not permanent. They are poured, used for as long as they are needed, and then returned to the raw material. Nothing in this city is built to last. Or rather, everything is built to last exactly as long as it should, and no longer. The young stone is the lesson: everything here is recent, everything is still forming, everything is still happening. Permanence is not the goal. *Appropriate duration* is the goal.

## X.

You stay for what you think is three days. It may be more. The light does not change enough to measure time by, and your sense of duration begins to blur, to soften, to take on the quality of the place — the unhurried, unmeasured, continuous present of a city that is not going anywhere because it is already where it is becoming.

At night — if it is night; if the slight dimming of the amber light constitutes a night — the city is almost silent. The condensation drips. The river whispers. Somewhere in the green mats, the soft-bodied creatures move, and the leaves of the tracking-plants rotate with a faint, dry sound like paper being turned. The buildings glow. The material breathes.

You stand on an upper terrace and look out across the whole of it — the loose clusters of ochre buildings, the green mats between them, the covered paths with their slow drips, the descending terraces stepping down and down to the wide shallow river with its branching, shifting, never-settling waters — and you understand, finally, what the place is.

It is a garden. The whole city is a garden. Not a garden built by people for people, but a garden built by material for material — stone learning to be structure, water learning to be weather, plants learning to be architecture, people learning to be place. Nothing here is separate from anything else. The buildings are stone. The people are buildings. The plants are breaking the stone that the buildings are made of and the people are made of, and this is not destruction but the next stage of the process, and the river is feeding the plants and the plants are feeding the air and the air is feeding the light and the light is feeding the material and the material is feeding the people who are seeding the buildings which are being dissolved back into stone which is being broken by plants which are being drunk by the river.

It is a closed loop. It is a slow, warm, dim, quiet, endless loop, and it is still happening, and it will keep happening, and no one who lives here has any interest in it ever stopping.

You leave the way you came. The air thins. The light brightens. The horizon retreats. You look back once, from the dry scrubland above, and the city is a faint ochre smear in the amber distance, still becoming, still happening, still there.
