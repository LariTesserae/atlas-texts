# The Exhalation Plateaus

## I.

The first thing you notice is that the land is breathing.

Not metaphorically. The ground beneath your feet rises and falls in slow tidal swells, perhaps six meters of vertical displacement over a cycle of ninety seconds. When the land exhales, warm air moves outward across the surface in a visible shimmer, carrying moisture and the smell of iron and vegetal rot. When it inhales, the surrounding atmosphere presses inward and the dim light bends subtly, as though the world is drawing breath through a mouth you cannot see. You learn quickly to widen your stance during the inhale phase, because the friction coefficient of the surface changes as it shifts. The crust here is not rock in any conventional sense. It is something between slate and bark and chitin — a laminate material that fractures in long curved shards when stressed, revealing underneath not magma or sediment but dense, wet, living tissue the color of a bruise. Plum and yellow. Ochre mottling. You do not touch it for long.

This is the first paradox of the Exhalation Plateaus: a landmass the size of a small continent that is also, in some quantifiable sense, an organism.

## II.

The plateau system sits at an elevation that should be alpine. You can tell by the way the atmosphere thins — not dangerously, but enough that the horizon has a certain cut-glass clarity to it, a sharpness in the distance that doesn't resolve the way it should. The light is permanently crepuscular. Not dark, not twilight, but that quality of illumination you find in the minutes before a storm arrives: amber-gray, diffuse, directionless. There is no visible sun. There is no visible sky, exactly. Above the plateaus, the atmosphere has the appearance of stained glass held up to a window on an overcast day — darkly luminous, faintly textured, as though clouds or something like clouds have congealed into a solid (or at least viscous) ceiling at perhaps three kilometers. Light comes through this ceiling the way light comes through a lampshade. It comes from everywhere and nowhere. Shadows are soft and shallow and do not behave — they lie in the wrong directions, they persist when they should not, they stretch when objects move but contract again slowly, reluctantly, like something alive and irritable.

The warmth comes from below. The ground is warm. Not hot — not volcanic — but body-temperature warm, the way a stone in direct sunlight is warm, except the sun has nothing to do with it. The heat rises in slow plumes, and where it meets the cooler upper atmosphere, moisture condenses. Rain falls here, but it falls in strange, localized patterns. Not from the ceiling-clouds above but from the convection itself — rain that seems to begin ten meters above the surface, materializing out of nothing, falling in dense curtains that last thirty seconds and then vanish. You walk through patches of weather. A column of rain the width of a house. A corridor of dry warmth between two such curtains. The rain is warm. It tastes of salt and chlorophyll.

## III.

The vegetation is what you would call lush if you had to choose a word, though the word is wrong. Lush implies a richness, an Edenic generosity. What grows here is generous, yes, but it is also desperate and competitive and deeply, deeply strange. The dominant flora is a family of plants that seem to have evolved to parasitize the living crust directly. They do not root in soil — there is no soil, not in the conventional sense. Their root systems are translucent, gelatinous tubes that fuse with the exposed tissue of the plateau surface when it fractures, drawing nutrients and moisture directly from the land itself. The largest of these plants are towering fan-shaped structures, perhaps twenty meters tall, with broad, pleated surfaces that catch the condensation drifting down from the ceiling-clouds. They are not green. They are the color of garnets, of oxidized copper, of dried blood reconstituted with water. When the ground exhales, these fans vibrate at a frequency you feel more than hear — a low, persistent hum that might be a kind of feeding.

Between the fans, in dense layered profusion, grows everything else. Thick, mat-like groundcover with the texture of velvet and the color of sulfur. Climbing vines that do not climb but instead build themselves upward like coral, layer by layer, depositing their own structural material in slow accretions that harden into lattice-like columns. Fungal networks — or things that behave like fungi — that web across every surface in patterns that look, depending on how long you stare at them, either completely random or aggressively intentional. The air in the vegetated zones is thick with spores and pollen and tiny drifting organisms you cannot classify. The density is extraordinary. You cannot walk in a straight line for more than ten meters without encountering a wall of growth. Every cubic meter is occupied. The layering goes vertical: groundcover at ankle height, mid-story at waist to shoulder height, the garnet fans above, and above them, in the dim ceiling-lit canopy, a tangle of hanging structures that drip a continuous slow rain of condensate and organic particulate. The air has texture. You feel it on your skin like gossamer.

The plants do not stay still. Not in the sense of growth — growth is slow — but in the sense of movement. The vines shift. The groundcover ripples. When the ground breathes, the vegetation responds. During the exhale, the fans open fully and the vines extend and the whole system seems to lean outward, to stretch. During the inhale, everything contracts, pulls tight, closes. It is the rhythm of a lung. It is the rhythm of a heart. You begin to suspect that the distinction between the land and what grows on it is a fiction you have brought with you and that the place does not recognize.

## IV.

There are animals here. They are abundant in the way that insects in a hive are abundant — not merely present but constitutive, part of the fundamental texture of the place. You cannot look at any surface and not see life on it.

The most common are small, six-limbed creatures with bodies like flat, articulated plates that move by expanding and contracting their segments in a caterpillar-like ripple. They are the color of the crust, which is the color of the bruise-tissue beneath the crust, which is to say they are very hard to see. They graze on the groundcover. They graze on the crust itself, their mouthparts scraping at the laminate surface in a sound like someone running a fingernail along a ruler, which is one of the only sharp sounds in this landscape. They exist in groups of hundreds. When the ground exhales and the vegetation opens, they emerge from wherever they shelter and spread across the surface in dark, rippling carpets. When the ground inhales, they retreat. They are synchronized to the breathing in a way that suggests not learned behavior but something deeper, something chemical, something the ground and everything on it share.

There are larger things. Things that move through the canopy in slow, deliberate arcs, their shapes only partially visible in the dim light. They have long, many-jointed limbs and broad, flat bodies and they seem to navigate by touch, their appendages reading the surfaces of the fans and the lattice-columns the way a blind person reads Braille. They make sounds — not calls, not communication, but a kind of continuous low tonal exhalation, a murmur, a drone. The sound they make is almost exactly the sound the fans make when they vibrate during the exhale. Whether the creatures are mimicking the landscape or the landscape is mimicking the creatures is a question that becomes less answerable the more you observe.

There are things in the air, too, or in the space between the canopy and the ceiling-clouds. They are not birds. They are not insects. They are briefly-glimpsed forms with translucent bodies that catch the dim light and refract it into brief prismatic flashes, there and gone, there and gone. You hear them as a kind of high, thin piping, almost ultrasonic, almost at the edge of your perception. You never see one clearly. You are not certain they are individual organisms. They might be weather. They might be light behaving strangely. They might be the exhalation of the plateau itself, made briefly visible.

And there are things that do not fit any category at all. Deep in the vegetation, in the densest zones where the layering is so thick that you would need a machete to pass through — except there are no machetes here, there is nothing made here — you sometimes see shapes that appear to be assembled from the landscape itself. Vaguely bipedal forms made of vines and groundcover and fungal matter and fragments of the laminate crust, standing motionless in the shadows, composed and then decomposing in slow cycles. They appear, persist for a time — minutes, hours — and then disarticulate, their components settling back into the general mass of growth as though the attempt at form has been abandoned. Some of them appear to have faces. Not functional faces, not faces that see or breathe, but faces in the way that a knot in a piece of wood has a face: the suggestion of features, the echo of pattern. You do not know what these things are. You do not know if they are alive or if they are the landscape trying to remember something. You suspect that these are the same thing.

## V.

There is no construction here. No structures, no artifacts, no evidence that anything has ever been built. And yet.

And yet there are engines.

The sound is the first indication. Not the breathing of the ground, not the hum of the fans, not the scraping of the six-limbed grazers or the piping of the things in the air. Underneath all of this, at the very bottom of the audible register, there is a thrum. It is mechanical. It is rhythmic. It is unmistakably the sound of machinery — not one machine but many, a vast complex of engines running in approximate synchrony, their rhythms interfering and reinforcing in patterns that rise and fall over periods of minutes. You feel it in your sternum. You feel it in the ground, which vibrates in addition to breathing.

The engines are underground. Or rather, they are in the crust, or they are the crust. When you find a place where the laminate has fractured deeply — and such places exist, where the breathing has stressed the surface beyond its tolerance and the sharp, young edges of the crust have peeled back like the skin of an orange — you can see down into the structure of the plateau. It is not geology. It is not stratigraphy. It is engineering. Layers of material — some organic, some metallic, some that are neither and both — arranged in configurations that serve no natural function. Tubes and chambers and galleries and what can only be described as conduits, carrying fluids and gases in directed flows. Gears, or structures that function as gears, their teeth interlocked with bands of tissue that contract and expand in rhythm. Pistons. Heat exchangers. Valves. But all of it is alive, or was alive, or is in the process of becoming alive or ceasing to be alive. The metal is corroded in patterns that look like vasculature. The tissue has grown over and through and into the mechanical components. The whole thing — the whole plateau, the whole system — is a machine that has been colonized by life, or an organism that has developed machinery, or something in between for which there is no word.

This is why the light is dim. Not because of the ceiling-clouds, which are a symptom and not a cause, but because the system is running at low power. The engines are laboring. You can hear it in their rhythm — not the smooth pulse of a well-maintained system but the rough, uneven cadence of something operating past its design parameters, compensating, adjusting, struggling. The breathing of the ground is the mechanical cycle of enormous pumps, their cylinders grown over with tissue that serves as both gasket and parasite. The heat is waste heat. The condensation is the result of thermal cycling. The rain is the system sweating.

Someone, or something, built this. A long time ago. The construction is too old and too deeply integrated with the biology to have been done by anything currently present. The plateau was engineered and then abandoned, and in the absence of its builders, life — whatever life is here, whatever life means here — moved in. Grew in. Became the machine. The distinction between the mechanical and the organic has dissolved entirely over what must have been geological time, and what remains is something that is neither and both: a living engine, a breathing factory, a continent-sized organism that is trying to keep running without anyone to tell it what it was supposed to do.

## VI.

The turbulence is constant. Not violent — not usually — but unceasing. The breathing alone would be enough to make this a place of continuous change, but the breathing is only the baseline. On top of it, everything is in motion.

The fractures in the crust shift and heal. You watch a crack open over the course of a minute, two meters wide and twenty meters long, the bruise-colored tissue beneath glistening with moisture and pulsing in time with the engines. You watch it close again over the next three minutes, the laminate edges growing together, fusing, the surface becoming seamless. A new crack opens elsewhere. The plateau is a single continuous surface that is constantly breaking and mending itself, like skin over a wound that will not close, like ice on a sea that is always moving. The vegetation responds. Plants that had rooted in a fracture lose their footing when it closes and topple, their root-tubes tearing free with wet, fibrous sounds. New plants colonize the next fracture. The cycle is continuous. Nothing here is older than a few weeks, and nothing here is newer than a few seconds, and both of these statements are true.

The ceiling-clouds move. Not with wind — there is no wind in the conventional sense, the air is thick and still and heavy with moisture and spores — but with their own internal logic. Masses of the luminous gray-amber material drift and coalesce and separate, and where they thicken, the light dims further, and where they thin, it brightens to a pale, watery gold that lasts only seconds before the clouds close again. The light has weather. The illumination has fronts and systems and patterns. You can stand in one place and watch the light change like you would watch clouds cross the sun, except there is no sun, only the ceiling and its moods.

The landscape itself rearranges. Not rapidly — nothing here is rapid except the rain and the glimpsed things in the air — but persistently, continuously, in a way that means the map you make in your mind is always wrong by the time you finish making it. Ridges of the laminate crust rise and subside. The vegetation shifts in slow tides, the garnet fans leaning toward fractures that offer better anchorage, the groundcover spreading and retreating in patterns that might be driven by the thermal cycle or might be driven by something you have not yet identified. The forms in the deep vegetation — the assembled bipedal shapes, the things with faces that are not faces — appear and disappear with greater frequency in certain zones, as though the landscape is concentrating its attention, or its memory, or its attempts at something it cannot quite achieve.

And through all of this, near-silence.

Not true silence — the breathing is always there, the hum of the fans, the far-off mechanical thrum of the engines, the scrape of grazers, the piping of the things in the air. But it is a silence in the way that a library is silent: full of quiet sounds that together create an atmosphere of held breath, of attention, of something listening. The loudest sound is the rain when it comes, and even that is soft, warm, intimate. You speak and your voice is absorbed by the vegetation and the moisture and the dense air, swallowed, muffled, as though the landscape does not want sound to travel. As though sound is an interruption.

## VII.

The weirdness is not a layer on top of this place. It is the place. It is what happens when a machine becomes an organism becomes a landscape and nothing has been in charge of any of those transformations. The physics here are not broken — they are stretched, thinned, pushed past a point of coherence. You notice it first in small ways. Objects dropped from shoulder height fall slightly slower than they should, and when they land, they bounce once and then stop, no matter the surface. Light from the ceiling reaches the ground, but the color of things is not stable: the garnet fans are red, and then they are brown, and then they are the deep violet of the bruise-tissue, and then they are red again, and none of these shifts correspond to changes in the light. They correspond to the breathing. Color is a function of the ground-state here, not of illumination.

More unsettling: the place responds to observation. Not to you specifically — you are not sure the place knows you are here — but to the act of looking. When you stare at a patch of groundcover for more than a few seconds, it grows. Slowly, imperceptibly at first, but measurably. The growth is directed toward you. When you look away, it stops. When you look at the forms in the deep vegetation — the bipedal assemblages with their suggested faces — they are more likely to persist, to become more detailed, more coherent, the longer you watch them. Attention is a nutrient here. The landscape is performing for itself, and you are a new audience, and the performance is changing because you are present.

Most unsettling: the boundary between the landscape and your perception of it is not firm. You have been here for — how long? You are not sure. The light does not change in a way that marks time. Your memories of arriving are clear but feel as though they belong to someone else, or to a version of you that was more solid, more singular, more defined. You suspect this is because the place is incorporating you. Not aggressively, not maliciously, but the way a river incorporates a stone: slowly, by erosion, by the persistent gentle pressure of being in contact with something that does not stop moving. You are becoming part of the pattern. Your body heat is contributing to the convection. Your exhalations are contributing to the moisture. Your attention is contributing to the growth. You are, in a very literal sense, being woven into the fabric of the plateau.

You do not think this is dangerous. You do not think it is safe. You think those categories do not apply here, in this place that was built by something that is gone and is now maintained by something that does not know it is maintaining anything. You think the plateau does not want anything. You think it is not alive in the way that you are alive, not conscious, not aware. But it is doing something. It is processing something. The engines are running. The breathing continues. The forms with the faces appear and disappear in the shadows of the garnet fans, and the faces are always slightly different, and sometimes — if you watch long enough, if you hold your attention steady, if you let yourself become part of the pattern — the faces look like yours.

## VIII.

The plateau goes on. That is perhaps the final and most important thing to understand about it. It goes on. The scale is not continental in the sense of a province or a region — it is continental in the sense that the plateau is a world, a self-contained system, a place large enough to have its own weather and its own physics and its own relationship with light and time. You could walk for weeks and not reach an edge. If there are edges, they are not edges in the geographic sense — no cliffs, no shorelines — but transitions, zones where the breathing slows and the engines quiet and the vegetation thins and the crust becomes something else, something you cannot see from here, something that might be the rest of whatever this place was meant to be before whatever happened to it happened.

But here, in the interior, the plateau is what it is. A warm, dim, living machine the size of a continent, breathing and growing and breaking and healing, running its ancient engines through tissue that has grown over them like moss over a statue, populated by creatures that do not know they live inside a mechanism, producing forms that might be art or might be malfunction or might be the closest thing to communication that a place like this is capable of.

The rain falls in its small curtains. The ground breathes. The fans hum. The grazers ripple across the surface in their hundreds. The things in the air pipe and flash and vanish. The deep forms assemble and disassemble and assemble again with faces that are not faces.

The engines labor on. No one is coming to fix them. They do not know this. They would not care. They will breathe until they cannot, and then the breathing will stop, and then the plateau will be something else — something quiet, something still, something that does not produce faces in the shadows — but that time is not now. Now the plateau is alive with the energy of a system in motion, a system that has found a way to continue without understanding, a system that is turbulent and dense and vast and warm and strange and almost, almost, almost silent.

You stand on the breathing ground and you feel it rise beneath you and you let it carry you up, and you let it lower you down, and you begin to learn the rhythm, and you do not leave.
