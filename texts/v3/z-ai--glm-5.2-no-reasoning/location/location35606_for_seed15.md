# The Immersion Chamber

It was built to hold exactly one person, and it has not held anyone for a long time.

---

You enter from above. A hatch in the ceiling — bronze, greened at the hinges — drops you three body-lengths down through warm water into a room that is almost a cube. Four meters by four by four. The fall is slow because the water is thick, slightly denser than the sea, slightly warmer than skin. You descend like a coin sinking through honey. Your feet touch the floor gently. There is no impact.

Everything here is made. The walls are fitted stone — basalt slabs cut square and mortared with a pale compound that has gone the color of old bone. The floor is the same, but grooved in a grid pattern, channels cut half a finger deep, all leading toward a drain at the center that does not drain. The ceiling is a single slab of something darker, smoother — a stone that may not be stone at all. The hatch above is the only opening. It closes behind you. You hear it seal with a sound like a throat swallowing.

Then the sound begins.

---

The room is loud, but the loudness is not aggressive. It is layered. A low vibration comes through the floor — a pulse, regular, every three seconds, felt more than heard, like a distant engine or a slow heartbeat amplified through stone. Over this, water moves. Not the water you are standing in, which is still, but water in channels behind the walls. You can hear it flowing — a rushing, textured sound, changing pitch as the channels branch and narrow. Sometimes a pipe knocks. Sometimes the flow surges and the wall thrums. The acoustics are strange because you are submerged: the sound travels through your chest, your teeth, the fluid in your ears. The room is speaking to you through your skeleton.

And there is a third sound. Higher, thinner. A hissing that comes in cycles — five seconds on, three seconds off — from nozzles set into the upper corners of the walls. They are injecting something. You cannot see what. The water does not change color. But each time the hiss comes, the temperature rises by a degree, and the light brightens.

---

The light. The light is the most surprising thing here.

You expected darkness, underwater, enclosed. Instead, the room is blazing. The four upper corners each hold a cluster of what appear to be thick glass bulbs, hand-blown, imperfect, each one slightly different in shape. They are filled with a pale substance — a paste, perhaps, or a culture of something bioluminescent that was bred for this purpose centuries ago and has not died because the conditions in the bulbs are perfectly maintained. The light they produce is white with a faint blue undertone, strong enough to read by, strong enough to see every detail of every stone, every groove, every hairline crack in the mortar. The water is clear — you can see across the room with perfect clarity. Your own body is lit from all four sides. You cast four shadows on the floor, overlapping into a dark star.

The bulbs are mounted in bronze housings that have been bolted to the stone. The bolts are hand-forged. You can see the hammer marks. Someone made this with tools that were simple, patient, and sufficient.

---

The vegetation is sparse, but it is here.

Along the lower edges of the walls, where the floor meets the stone, there is a growth — not moss, not algae, something in between. It is dark green, almost black, and it clings to the mortar lines like a child holding to the cracks in a door. It does not spread across the stone. It does not float. It grows only in the seams, following the grid, so that when you look down at the floor from above, the growth draws the architecture in dark green lines on gray. It is the only living thing in the room besides you.

It does not move. It does not respond to your touch. It has no smell. But it is growing, slowly, because in one corner the green lines are thicker than in the others, and in another corner they have not yet reached the next seam. It is winning its territory at a rate of perhaps a millimeter per year.

There are no animals. No fish, no insects, no larvae, no worms. The water is sterile enough that nothing larger than the seam-growth can survive in it. You are the largest body this room has held in a very long time, and you can feel the room adjusting — the hissing nozzles cycling faster, the floor-pulse quickening slightly, as if the chamber has noticed you and is responding to your metabolism, your warmth, your exhaled carbon.

---

This is a bathhouse. Or it was. It was built as part of a larger complex — you can sense the weight of architecture above and around you, other chambers, other channels, the whole mountainous bulk of a structure that extends far beyond this one room — but this particular chamber was private. One hatch. One person. The grooves in the floor, you realize, are not drainage channels. They are guides. They tell the bather where to stand, where to sit, where to lie. The room was designed to hold a body in a specific position, submerged in water at a specific temperature, under light of a specific intensity, for a specific duration.

What was the treatment? You do not know. The room has forgotten, or the room never knew — it was only the instrument, not the prescription. The operator who understood it is gone. The culture that built it is gone. The bronze has greened. The mortar has yellowed. The bulbs still glow. The nozzles still hiss. The floor still pulses. The water is still warm. The seam-growth still grows.

Everything works. Nothing here has broken. The chamber has been running, empty and bright and loud with its own mechanical breathing, for longer than anyone has been alive to hear it.

---

You stand in the center, where the grooves converge. The water is at your chest. The light is equal from all sides. The sound is in your bones. The room is exactly the size of your attention. You have the feeling, not unpleasant, of being inside something that was waiting for you — not eagerly, not desperately, but the way an empty chair waits. The chamber was made for a body. You are a body. The match is simple and sufficient.

When you leave — when you climb the bronze ladder set into one wall and push through the hatch — the room will not stop. The light will stay on. The nozzles will hiss. The floor will pulse. The water will hold its warmth. The green will continue its millimeter crawl along the seams.

It will wait for the next one. It has time.
