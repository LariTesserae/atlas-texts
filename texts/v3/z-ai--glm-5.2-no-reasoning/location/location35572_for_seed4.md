# The Gallery Under the Moss

You find it by sound first — not a sound, but the place where sound goes thin. The forest above is all drip and creak, the usual work of water moving through living wood. Then you step through a certain fold in the hillside where the root-curtain hangs dense and your ears pop subtly and the world goes muffled, and you are inside.

---

It was a town once. You can tell by the grid.

The walls are still here — but walls is perhaps too strong. They were walls. They were cut timber and mortared stone and they stood in rows defining streets and doorways and the logic of a settlement meant to hold perhaps three hundred people. That was before. The work is perhaps a generation old, perhaps two — the erosion parameter reads young and it is young, not ancient, not ruined in the romantic way. The walls haven't crumbled so much as they have been *occupied*. Every surface has been claimed. The timber beams wear sleeves of pleurococcus, that powdery green alga that means moisture has been working on the wood for at least several seasons. The mortared joints of the stonework have been forced apart by roots — not the dramatic buttress roots of old forest trees, but the fine, threadlike, relentless roots of ground-cover and understory plants that have found the mortar's chemistry hospitable and pushed into every crack, widening, widening, with a patience that has nothing to do with intention.

The streets are still navigable. That is the strangest thing. The plants have not swallowed the paths — they have *become* the paths. What was once packed earth is now a continuous mat of creeping vegetation, springy underfoot, dark green and wet. You walk on a living carpet through a town that has been digested.

---

The scale is small. This is not a city — it is barely a hamlet, perhaps forty structures, arranged in a rough grid across a low terrace that someone chose because it was flat and because a spring seeped from the hillside above. The spring still seeps. It is the reason everything here is wet and the reason the town was built here and the reason the town was lost. Water moves through this place constantly — not flowing in the dramatic sense, but *infiltrating*. The ground is saturated within a few inches of the surface. You can see this where the path-mat thins and the dark soil shows beneath: it glistens. It is not mud, not quite. It is soil that has reached field capacity and is now in the process of becoming something else.

Everywhere, water expresses itself. The stone walls weep. Not metaphorically — you can stand close to the mortared stonework of what was probably a granary and watch water bead along the joints, gather into trickles, descend. The roots that have forced the mortar apart have created channels. The wall has become an aqueduct. The air itself is wet. You breathe in and your lungs feel the weight of it. In the cold — and it is cold, the kind of cold that isn't sharp enough to bite but steady enough to settle into your bones over an hour — the moisture condenses on every surface. On your skin. On the waxy leaves of the ivy that has claimed the eastern wall of the meeting hall. On the dark fronds of the ferns that grow from the gaps in the collapsed roof-beams of the smithy.

It is dark. This needs to be said plainly. The root-curtain at the entrance is not a door — it is a membrane. Light does not pass through it in any useful quantity. Once you are ten paces inside, you are in a green darkness, a darkness that has texture and depth because the plants give it shape. Your eyes adjust, and you realize that the darkness is not total — it is filtered. There are places where the root-ceiling above thins enough to let a diffuse, greenish-gray light descend, and in those places the vegetation is slightly thicker, slightly more upright, reaching. The rest is shadow. Deep, biological shadow. The kind of darkness that exists inside a living thing.

---

Sound is rare here and when it comes it has weight.

The predominant state is quiet — not silence, because silence implies absence, and there is no absence in this place. There is the constant near-sound of water moving through plant tissue, which is not really a sound but a presence that your ears register as a kind of pressure. There is the settling of the root-ceiling as it grows, microscopic adjustments, the creak of wood fiber under tension. These are not sounds you hear so much as sounds you feel in your jaw.

Occasionally something drips. The drip has a particular quality — it is cold, it is mineral, it falls from a surface that is organic. The *plink* when it lands in a shallow pool has a clarity that seems wrong in the dimness, too precise, like a note struck on an instrument in a room where you expected no music.

And there are the others.

---

The fauna is the thing that keeps you from settling into contemplation.

This is their place. You are in it, but it is theirs, and they make you aware of this not through hostility but through sheer, overwhelming presence.

It begins with the invertebrates. The path-mat you walk on is not still — it shifts in small ways, and when you kneel and look closely you see that it is *moving*. Springtails, hundreds of them, leaping in arcs too small to track. Mites, red and slow, climbing the stems of the creeping plants. Earthworms working at the edge of the saturated soil, their casts visible as small towers of dark excrement. The surfaces of the walls crawl with pseudoscorpions, tiny and intent, moving among the moss colonies with the purposeful gait of something that is hunting.

The spiders have built in every corner. Sheet webs span the gaps where doorframes once held doors. The webs are wet — everything is wet — and the droplets caught in the silk make them visible as geometric patterns of light in the darkness. The spiders themselves hang in the sheets, dark and patient, and they are not the small spiders of a garden. They are the kind that have lived in wet darkness long enough to become substantial. Their bodies are the size of your thumbnail. Their legs are longer than they should be.

But it is the vertebrates that make the place feel crowded.

The amphibians first. The cold and the wet are their conditions, exactly, and they have found this place and filled it. Toads — small, dark, warty, with the gold-mottled skin of species that live in leaf litter — sit in the path-mat at intervals of perhaps two paces, and you must step around them. They do not move. You are close enough to see their throats pulse. There are hundreds. The path-mat seems to have a resident toad at every point where one could comfortably sit, and between the toads, the frogs — smaller, greener, more volatile — occupy the lower surfaces, the wet soil exposed by root-action, the small pools that have formed in depressions in the path. They are silent. That is what strikes you. In a wet place in the cold, you expect frogs to call. These do not. They have learned that sound draws attention, and attention draws the things that eat them, and so the amphibian population of this consumed town exists in a state of collective, watchful silence.

The salamanders are in the walls. You find them when you touch the stonework — your hand goes to a mossy joint and your fingers meet something cold and slick and alive, and you pull back, and a salamander — dark, with a pale ventral stripe, its skin wet and metallic in the faint light — slides deeper into the wall through a gap that the roots have made. You touch another wall. Another salamander. The walls are full of them. They have become, in a sense, the population of the town, occupying the structures that the humans built, living in the spaces the humans left, in densities the humans never achieved.

And above — in the root-ceiling, in the living architecture that has replaced the roofs — there are bats. You hear them before you see them: the high, thin clicking of echolocation, a sound that is almost beyond perception, more a feeling in the teeth than a sound in the ear. They are small. They move through the darkness with a precision that your eyes cannot follow. You see them only as moments, as flickers of shape against the dim green of the root-ceiling, and you understand that the airspace of this place — the volume between the path-mat and the living roof — is their hunting ground, and that the insects the place generates in such abundance are their prey, and that this system, this consumed-and-rebuilt town, is now a food web.

The spiders eat the springtails. The toads eat the spiders. The salamanders eat the mites and the worms. The bats eat the flying insects that hatch from the pools. And something else, something you have not yet seen, eats the bats — you know this because you find a wing, single, detached, on the path-mat, its membrane intact, its bones picked clean. Something with teeth. Something that hunts in the dark.

You become aware, gradually, that you are the largest thing to enter this space in some time, and that this fact has been noted.

---

The technology here is the technology of the town-that-was, and it is primitive, and it is being digested.

You find tools. A hand-axe, its iron head orange with a fine, even rust that means constant moisture, its wooden handle soft and splitting, colonized along its grain by white fungal mycelium. A clay pot, unbroken, sitting in what was probably a kitchen, half-buried in the path-mat, its rim at the level of your ankle. Inside it, water — rainwater that has filtered through the root-ceiling, or condensation that has gathered over months — and in the water, a dead beetle floating, and mosquito larvae turning in slow circles. A grinding stone, its surface furred with moss. A length of rope, hemp, now more fungus than fiber, its form maintained by habit rather than structural integrity. When you touch it, it collapses into wet powder.

These were the tools of people who worked with their hands. The iron is simple iron. The clay is local clay, fired poorly, its color the orange-brown of the soil it came from. The wood is local wood — you can see the grain, and it matches the grain of the beams in the walls, and you understand that these people built from what grew around them, and then what grew around them took it all back.

There is no metal in use. There is no fired clay in use. There are no tools being used. The technology here is the technology of the plants — roots that split stone, hyphae that reduce wood to soil, leaves that capture the faint light and turn it into the energy that drives the whole machine. The human technology has been reduced to substrate. The tools have become habitat. The axe-head is a warm place where a toad sits. The pot is a pool. The rope is soil. The grinding stone is a surface for moss.

This is not decay. This is succession. The town was a clearing. The clearing is closing. The plants are doing what plants do, which is to fill every available space with life, and they are doing it efficiently, and they are doing it now, and the process is active — you can feel it in the dampness, in the growth, in the constant small motions of living things occupying and reoccupying and dividing and spreading.

---

The town had a center. You find it. It is the place where the root-ceiling is highest — perhaps four meters above the path-mat — and where the spring emerges from the hillside. The spring has been shaped. Someone built a stone basin around it, and the basin is still intact, its walls only partially root-broken, its bottom still holding water. The water is clear. You can see the source — a crack in the underlying stone, probably a joint in the bedrock, where groundwater under pressure forces its way up. The flow is modest, perhaps a liter every few seconds, but it is constant, and it is the reason for everything.

The basin overflows into a channel that was once stone-lined and is now plant-lined. The channel winds through the center of the town, along what was probably the main street, and the water in it is barely moving — it is more a seep than a stream, but it moves, and where it moves, the vegetation is different. Denser. Greener. The plants along the channel are not the ground-cover of the paths but something taller, something with stems, something that roots in the saturated soil at the channel's edge and reaches toward the dim light. Small ferns. Horsetails, their jointed stems clicking against each other in the faint air movement. Moss of a different kind — taller, more structured, with spore capsules on thin stalks that sway.

At the basin, the light is strongest — still dim, still green-gray, but enough to see by, and the life here responds. The stone of the basin is covered in a thin biofilm, a slick of algae and bacteria that is the color of jade and is, you realize, the most vivid color in this place. The amphibians are thickest here — the toads sit on the basin's rim in a row, seven of them, evenly spaced, their eyes reflecting the faint light as points of gold. They are watching you. You understand this is not paranoia. They are watching you because you are large and unfamiliar and they have no frame of reference for you, and their stillness is not calm but assessment.

In the water, the salamanders are visible — three of them, clinging to the stone sides of the basin just below the waterline, their bodies undulating gently in the current. Their gills, if they have gills — and some of them do, you can see the external tufts, red and feathery, waving in the flow — are the only bright color underwater. Everything else is dark, is green-dark, is the color of wet stone and living shadow.

---

You try to map the strangeness and find it resists mapping.

The strangeness is not in any single element. A wet, cold, plant-consumed town with abundant amphibians is not strange in its components. The strangeness is in the *integration*. It is in the feeling that this place has reached a kind of agreement. The plants are not attacking the town. The animals are not invading the town. The water is not destroying the town. All of these forces are, instead, *becoming* the town, and the town is becoming them, and the result is something that functions as a single entity in a way that none of its parts do alone.

The root-ceiling, for instance, should not be here. The plants above — the trees on the hillside, the understory shrubs, the ground-cover — should not have organized themselves into a continuous canopy over this space. And yet they have. The roots have grown across the gap, have interlocked, have formed a lattice that is now itself growing, putting out fine root-hairs that descend into the space like filaments, collecting condensation, dripping it back to the path-mat below. The ceiling is not a roof that was built. It is a roof that *grew*. And it has the characteristics of a living membrane — it responds to moisture, thickening when the air is wet (which is always), thinning in the rare places where more light is needed below (which is near the spring, where the plants around the basin need light to photosynthesize). It regulates. It is, in the most literal sense, an organ.

And the fauna has organized itself around this organ. The bats roost in the root-ceiling's densest patches, where the lattice is thick enough to support their weight. The spiders build their webs at the ceiling's edge, where flying insects pass through. The toads sit beneath the thinnest patches, where the light that comes through is enough to warm the stone slightly and to attract the insects the toads eat. The system has spatial logic. The system has structure. The system was not designed but it has *emerged*, and it is emerging still, and you are inside it.

This is what 1.6 on the weirdness scale feels like: not the shock of the impossible, but the slow, uncanny recognition that the ordinary has organized itself into something that has the properties of a single organism, and that you are standing inside its gut.

---

You spend time. The place rewards it.

After an hour, you notice the smells. Before that, the cold and the wet had suppressed your olfactory sense, but now the place opens to you. The base smell is mineral — wet stone, iron-rich water, the ozone-tang of water under pressure emerging from rock. Over that, vegetal — the green, slightly sweet smell of growing tissue, the sharper smell of decomposition where something has died and is being processed, the damp-earth smell of the saturated soil. Under everything, animal — the musk of the toads, the faint ammoniac tang of the bat colony above, the cold reptilian-clean smell of the salamanders. These smells do not compete. They layer. The place smells like all of its components simultaneously, and the combination is its own thing, a smell that belongs to no other place.

After two hours, you notice the pace. This is not a static place. It is moving, but it is moving on multiple timescales that are all present simultaneously. The water moves fast — the spring flows, the condensation drips, the channel seeps. The plants move at medium speed — you can, if you watch a root-filament closely for ten minutes, see it extend, see it grow toward a source of moisture, a movement that is slow but visible, like watching a clock's minute hand. The animals move at their own speeds — the springtails too fast to track, the toads too slow to notice, the bats somewhere in between, their movements too quick for the eye but their patterns too regular to be random.

And the stone moves slowest of all. But it moves. You can see it in the walls — the gaps the roots have made are wider than they would be if the roots were merely occupying existing cracks. The roots are *pushing*. The stone is *giving way*. Over years, over decades, the walls will become soil, and the roots will be the soil's skeleton, and the path-mat will cover everything, and the town will be gone.

But it is not gone yet. It is in the process of being gone. And that process — active, wet, cold, dark, dense with life, quiet, strange — is what you are standing in.

---

You leave the way you came. The root-curtain parts when you push it, and the forest above receives you, and the sound returns — the drip, the creak, the wind, the world — and the cold is different out here, sharper, more honest. You look back through the curtain and see only darkness, and perhaps a single point of gold where a toad's eye catches the light, and then the curtain settles and the place closes behind you.

It does not need you. It was working before you arrived and it will work after you leave. The spring will seep. The roots will grow. The toads will sit. The bats will hunt. The stone will crack. The moss will spread. The town will be consumed. And in its place, something else will persist — not a ruin, not a forest, but the thing that a town becomes when water and cold and plants and animals agree on what to do with it, which is to make it into themselves.

This is the gallery under the moss. It exhibits nothing. It is the exhibit.
