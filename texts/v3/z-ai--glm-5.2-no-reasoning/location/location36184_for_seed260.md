# The Ascendant Veil

## I. First Approach

You hear it long before you see it.

Not sound in the way you understand sound — not pressure waves traveling through a medium and striking membranes. This is closer to vibration felt in the tooth roots, in the marrow-cavities of long bones, in the fluid of the inner ear where the tiny hairs bend and bend and do not recover. A thrum. A chord. A sustained planetary note that has been sounding for what the few remaining oral histories call "three generations of the long-lived trees," which is to say, somewhere between six hundred and nine hundred years, depending on which species you ask.

The Ascendant Veil sits at the roof of a continent that no longer has a name because the people who lived at its base either left or were subsumed. It is a plateau, though that word is insufficient. Plateaus are erosional remnants — things left behind after the softer material washes away. The Veil is the opposite. It is still arriving. Still rising. The crust here has been bulging upward for longer than human memory, driven by forces beneath that no one fully understands, and it continues to rise at a rate that you can almost feel if you lie flat on the ground and press your ear to the stone. One finger-width per year, the surveyors said, before the surveyors stopped coming. The last survey team left a brass marker embedded in the eastern scarp seven decades ago. It is now chest-height on a standing adult. The ground rose to meet it, swallowed the lower third of the marker's shaft, and kept going.

You climb to reach it because there is no other way. The approaches are steep on all sides — not vertical, but close enough that your hands bleed from the sharp new stone and your breath comes in torn rags because the air is thin here and getting thinner. The temperature drops as you ascend. Not dramatically — this is not a place of ice or killing frost — but steadily, so that by the time you reach the upper shelves, you are pulling your woven wraps tighter and your exhaled breath fogs in the perpetual dimness, though it does not dissipate so much as linger, joining the general haze that makes everything at distance uncertain.

The light here is strange. The sun, when it is visible, sits low and bloated on the horizon, as though it too is reluctant to climb the full distance to this altitude. It casts no direct light onto the Veil's surface. Instead, the illumination is ambient, scattered, arriving from no clear direction — a pale grey-gold wash that seems to emanate from the air itself, or from the haze, or from something in the stone. Shadows do not behave correctly. They fall in directions that do not correspond to the apparent light source. They are too sharp in some places and entirely absent in others. A standing person casts two shadows — one where you would expect it, and one at a right angle, fainter, as though cast by a sun that does not exist.

You learn not to look at the shadows too carefully.

## II. The Surface

The top of the Veil is vast. Not infinite — you can see the edges where the plateau breaks and falls away on three sides — but the interior stretches for what would be a half-day's walk in any direction, and the poor light and constant haze conspire to make the far margins seem to recede as you approach them. The ground underfoot is raw. This is the first word that comes to mind: raw. The stone here has not yet decided what it wants to be. It is not granite, not basalt, not sandstone. It is the precursor to all of these — a hot, mineral mash that has cooled just enough to bear weight but still carries the warmth of its own creation. In places, it is soft enough to mark with a boot heel. In others, it has hardened into unexpected geometries — columns, blades, fins — that rise from the surface like the bones of a creature that is still being assembled. Nothing here has been weathered. Nothing here has been rounded by wind or rain. Every edge is the edge it was born with. Every surface still holds the texture of its emergence — the slick, the rough, the bumpy, the fibrous. The ground looks like a wound that has not yet scabbed.

There is no water. This is absolute. No streams, no pools, no seeps, no springs. No rain falls here, and if it did, it would be immediately absorbed by the warm porous stone or evaporated by the heat that still rises from below. The air is not dry in the way desert air is dry — it has a weight to it, a density, a faint taste of mineral salts and sulfur and something else, something almost sweet, like the air inside a greenhouse. But there is no moisture. If you lick your lips, they chap within minutes. If you weep, the tears evaporate so quickly they feel cold on your cheeks for only an instant before they are gone.

And yet.

## III. The Growth

The Veil is covered in life. Not despite the absence of water, but because of it — or rather, because of what replaces it.

The dominant organisms here are not plants, though they look enough like plants that the first visitors called them that. They have stems. They have structures that resemble leaves. They grow upward and outward, seeking the dim light with a patience that borders on the geological. But they do not photosynthesize. They do not drink. They do not draw anything from the ground in the way that roots draw water and dissolved minerals from soil.

They eat the stone.

Or perhaps they eat what is rising through the stone — the warm plumes of vapor and gas that vent from fissures across the entire plateau, carrying with them compounds that exist nowhere else because the conditions that produce them exist nowhere else. These organisms — the Veil's botanists, had there been any, would have called them something like *lithotrophs* or *chemoautotrophs*, but those terms are too small for what they do — have evolved to metabolize the inorganic directly. They take the mineral exhalations of the rising plateau and convert them into tissue. Their stems are translucent, amber-to-violet, filled with a sap that is not sap but something closer to a biological solvent — a fluid that is not water, that has never been water, that performs the functions of water in these organisms' internal chemistry but does not behave like water in any other respect. It is heavier. It is slightly luminous. If you cut a stem — and the stems are soft enough to cut with a sharpened stone — the fluid that leaks out does not fall. It hovers. It drifts. It rises slowly, like a soap bubble, and eventually dissipates into the haze.

The largest of these organisms are the Tower-Growths. They are magnificent and they are terrifying and they are the reason the place is called the Veil.

A Tower-Growth begins as a single translucent stalk, no thicker than a finger, emerging from a crack in the raw stone. It grows upward — fast, by the standards of this place, which means perhaps a hand-span per day — and as it grows, it thickens, and it branches, and it branches again, and again, until what was a single stalk becomes a candelabra, and what was a candelabra becomes a structure the size of a mature oak, and then larger, and then larger still. The oldest Tower-Growths — the ones that the oral histories say were already tall when the first people arrived — rise forty, fifty, sixty feet into the dim air. Their stems, at the base, are as thick as a person's torso. Their canopy spreads wider than it is tall, a fractal architecture of amber and violet tendrils so dense that it creates its own ceiling, its own architecture of light and shadow.

And they sing.

## IV. The Sound

Every Tower-Growth resonates. The warm gases rising through their hollow stems — for the stems are hollow, like bamboo, like reeds, like the pipes of an organ — produce sustained tones as they pass through constrictions and chambers in the plant's internal structure. Each growth produces a different fundamental pitch, determined by the length and diameter of its primary stalk. The branches produce harmonics. The smallest twigs, at the canopy, produce overtones that shimmer at the edge of human hearing, high and thin and constant.

A single Tower-Growth, alone, would be a curiosity. A sound like a glass harmonica, like a bowed saw, like a wet finger on the rim of a crystal goblet. Beautiful. Strange. Tolerable.

There are thousands of them.

The Veil is forested with Tower-Growths, and they are all sounding simultaneously, and the combined acoustic output is — there is no other word — deafening. Not deafening in the sense of a single overwhelming noise, like an explosion or a waterfall. Deafening in the sense of total. Saturating. A wall of sound that has texture and depth, that has been building for centuries as growth after growth reached maturity and added its voice to the chorus. Standing at the center of the Veil, the sound is not something you hear. It is something you are inside of. It fills your chest. It vibrates your sternum. It buzzes in your sinuses and behind your eyes. It drowns your thoughts. You cannot speak loudly enough to be heard by a person standing next to you. You cannot hear your own footsteps. You cannot hear your own heartbeat, though you can feel it, and you notice, after a while, that it has synchronized with the nearest Tower-Growth's fundamental, which is a low B-flat that you feel more than hear.

People do not stay long in the deep interior. The sound is too much. It induces a state that the old visitors called "the Veil-drift" — a trance, a dissolution, a loosening of the boundary between self and sound. After several hours, you begin to lose track of where you end. After a full day, you may forget your name. After two days — no one has stayed two days and returned with a coherent account, though a few have returned with accounts that are incoherent in ways that suggest something happened to them that they do not have the language for.

The sound changes over time, but slowly. As new growths mature and add their voices, the chord shifts. As old growths die and their stalks crack and vent their internal gases in a brief, descending wheeze, voices are subtracted. The overall pattern — the harmonic structure of the Veil's song — has been documented, imperfectly, by the few visitors who thought to bring instruments. It appears to cycle. The chord shifts upward over decades, reaches a peak, shifts downward, reaches a trough, and begins again. The cycle is estimated to last between eighty and one hundred and twenty years. No one has been present for a full cycle. The Veil does not care whether anyone is present.

## V. Between the Towers

The spaces between the Tower-Growths are wide. The plateau does not have the closed, claustrophobic feeling of a forest. It has the feeling of a cathedral, or rather of many cathedrals joined together, their columns spaced generously, their ceilings high and interlocking. The ground between the growths is bare stone, cracked and fissured, venting warm gas from countless small openings. The fissures are the Veil's breath — the plateau exhales continuously, a slow, warm exhalation that carries the smell of hot minerals and, faintly, the sweet chemical signature of the Tower-Growths' internal fluid.

Smaller organisms live in the lee of the towers. Mats of low, spreading growth — not Tower-Growths but something related, something creepier and flatter — cling to the stone in the sheltered areas where the venting is less intense. These mats are dark green, almost black, and they are warm to the touch, metabolizing the stone's surface directly, converting the mineral substrate into more of themselves with glacial patience. They grow outward at a rate of perhaps an inch per year. Some of the mats are enormous — twenty feet across, forty, one that the last surveyor mapped at over a hundred feet in diameter, making it the oldest living thing on the plateau, perhaps a thousand years old, perhaps older, still spreading, still consuming the stone one molecule at a time.

There are no animals. This is absolute, like the absence of water. No insects. No birds. No rodents. No grazing creatures. Nothing moves except the Tower-Growths' slow upward reach, the creeping mats' slow outward spread, the gas rising from the fissures, and the occasional structural failure of a mature growth, which happens perhaps once per month somewhere on the plateau — a crack, a groan that is inaudible beneath the chorus, a slow collapse that takes hours and adds a descending cascade of new frequencies to the sound before the fallen stalk dries, hollows, and is absorbed by the creeping mats over a period of years.

The absence of animals is not merely an ecological fact. It is a rule. The things that live here — the Tower-Growths and the mats and the other, smaller organisms that cling to the fissure edges and metabolize sulfur compounds — do not eat each other. They do not eat anything organic. They do not compete for resources in any way that produces the familiar dynamics of predation, parasitism, herbivory. They have no defenses because they have no predators. They have no predators because there is nothing here that eats living tissue. The ecosystem, such as it is, runs entirely on the plateau's geological exhalations. When a thing dies, it is not consumed. It is slowly dissolved by the creeping mats, which treat dead Tower-Growth tissue the same way they treat stone — as mineral substrate, raw material, something to be metabolized and converted into more mat. The dead become geology. The geology becomes the living. The cycle does not require water and does not require animals and does not require decomposition in any form that would be recognizable to a biologist from a more temperate world.

## VI. The Structures

There are buildings. Not many. Six, perhaps seven, depending on how you count.

They are at the eastern edge of the plateau, where the ground begins to slope toward the scarp and the Tower-Growths thin out, their density decreasing as the venting becomes less intense — fewer fissures, less gas, less food for the lithotrophs. The buildings are small. The largest is perhaps twenty feet long and ten feet wide, built from the same raw stone that constitutes the plateau, fitted together without mortar. The walls are rough. The construction is old — the oral histories suggest that these were built by the first human visitors to the Veil, the people who climbed up and heard the sound and decided to stay, or to try to stay, or to at least leave a mark.

They did not stay long.

The buildings contain a few artifacts. Stone tools — hand axes, scrapers, a few blades that might be knives or might be something else, their purpose unclear. Woven fiber, remarkably well preserved by the dry mineral air, including what appears to be a bag or satchel and several lengths of cordage. A stone cup, stained on the inside with a residue that matches the Tower-Growth fluid — someone collected it, someone tried to drink it, or to use it, or to preserve it. A few marks on the interior walls — not writing, not pictographs, but deliberate scratches, grouped in clusters, suggesting counting or tallying or some system of notation that no one has deciphered because no one has had the opportunity to study it at length because no one can tolerate the sound for more than a few hours at a time.

One of the buildings has a cairn beside it. The cairn is built from plateau stone and stands about waist-high. It is old enough that the creeping mats have begun to colonize its base, and the warm gas from below has begun to push it slightly off-vertical, so that it leans to the northwest, very slowly, imperceptibly unless you return after a decade and notice the change. Beneath the cairn — this is known from the oral histories, which include an account from someone who helped build it — there is a body. A person who climbed to the Veil and could not leave. Not because they were trapped, but because the sound had done something to them. They sat down at the eastern edge, where the chorus is at its quietest, and they stopped speaking, and they stopped eating, and they sat, and they listened, and after some days they died, and the people who had come with them built the cairn because they could not carry the body down and they could not leave it exposed and they could not burn it because there is nothing here that burns.

The cairn leans. The mats climb. Someday, perhaps, the cairn will fall. The body inside will be exposed to the mineral air and will be slowly, over decades, converted to stone. This is not a metaphor. The Veil's processes do not distinguish between organic and inorganic. Everything here becomes stone eventually. The buildings will become stone, which they already are, but they will become stone that is indistinguishable from the plateau's own stone. The cairn will become a bump in the ground. The body will become a fossil, or something like a fossil, a mineral record of a human shape embedded in the rising surface.

## VII. The Unmaking

There is a problem with the Veil that the early visitors did not know about and that the later visitors did not understand and that the current state of things has made impossible to ignore.

The sound is not merely sound.

This is the part that is difficult to explain. The Tower-Growths produce acoustic energy, yes — pressure waves in the atmosphere, measurable with instruments, detectable by ears. But the acoustic energy is not the whole of what they produce. The Veil's chorus also generates something else, something that does not have a name because the people who experienced it did not have the framework to name it and the people who might have had the framework did not stay long enough to study it.

The closest analogy is interference. Not acoustic interference, not the beating patterns that emerge when two tones close in frequency interact. This is something else. The Tower-Growths' combined output creates patterns — not in the air, but in something else, something that the visitors called "the fabric" or "the weave" or, in one account that is either the most or least reliable, "the story that things are made of." These patterns are not stable. They shift as the chord shifts, as new growths add voices and old growths lose theirs, and the shifting produces effects that are not merely psychological.

Things come apart at the Veil's center. Not quickly. Not violently. But measurably, over time, in ways that are difficult to document because the documentation itself is affected. A visitor who spent six hours at the Veil's deep interior reported that the edges of their notebook had begun to blur — not the writing, but the paper itself, the fibers loosening, the structure of the material degrading, as though the bonds that held the cellulose together were being slowly loosened by the sound. Another visitor reported that their woven bag, after a day near the central growths, had developed a texture it had not had before — softer, less coherent, as though the weaving had been partly undone at a level too small to see. A third visitor — the one who stayed longest, who spent a full day in the interior and emerged changed in ways that went beyond the Veil-drift — reported that their fingernails had become slightly translucent and that they could see, faintly, through them, the dim light of the Veil visible through the keratin of their own body.

The Tower-Growths are not doing this intentionally. They are not doing anything intentionally. They are organisms metabolizing minerals and producing sound as a byproduct, the way a fire produces heat. But the sound — or the thing beneath the sound — has an effect on the structure of matter. On the bonds between things. On the coherence of form.

At the Veil's center, where the chorus is densest and the oldest growths stand, the effects are most pronounced. The stone there is softer than it should be. The air is thicker. The Tower-Growths themselves seem to be less than fully solid — their translucent stems, already strange, become almost transparent, and looking through them produces a sense of seeing something that is not merely behind the stem but somehow elsewhere, as though the stem were a window not to a different place but to a different state of being, a place where the rules that govern solidity and separation and identity are more like suggestions than laws.

The few visitors who have approached the deep center and returned describe it in terms that are not consistent with each other but share certain features. They speak of a feeling of permeability. Of the boundary between self and environment becoming unclear. Of the sound entering the body and not stopping at the ears but continuing through the flesh, through the bone, through the organs, through every cell, resonating at frequencies that should not be able to resonate in biological tissue but that do, or that seem to, or that the brain interprets as resonating because the alternative interpretation — that the boundary between the sound and the self has dissolved — is too strange to accept.

One visitor said: "I was a shape the sound was making."

Another said: "I was a sentence in a language made of pitch."

A third said nothing, but was observed by their companions to be weeping, and when the tears fell, they did not fall. They rose. They joined the haze. And the weeping person watched them go with an expression that was described, in the account, as "relief."

## VIII. The Rules

The Veil has rules, though they are not posted and cannot be learned from any text. They are inferred from the accounts of those who visited and the absence of those who did not return.

**First:** the sound is cumulative. The longer you stay, the more it affects you. Brief visits — an hour, two hours — produce the Veil-drift, which is reversible. You leave. You descend. The sound fades. Your thoughts re-cohere. You remember your name. You are yourself. Longer visits produce effects that are slower to reverse. A day's exposure may take a week to fully recede. Some effects — the translucence of the fingernails, the softening of woven fiber — may not reverse at all.

**Second:** the effect is distance-dependent. The eastern edge, where the buildings stand, is the quietest part of the plateau. The Tower-Growths are sparse there, and the chorus is thinned. You can stay at the eastern edge for days without significant effect. The interior is different. The deep center, where the oldest growths stand, is a place that no one has entered and returned from with their coherence intact, and the question of whether anyone has entered and not returned at all is open, because the people who might not have returned would not have left a record and their companions, if they had any, would not have been able to follow them in.

**Third:** the Veil is rising. The plateau is still ascending. The fissures are still venting. New Tower-Growths are still emerging from the stone. The chord is still shifting. The effects are, if anything, becoming more pronounced. The buildings at the eastern edge, which were safe enough for habitation a century ago, are now closer to the threshold than they were. The sound at the eastern edge is louder than it was when the buildings were constructed. The Veil-drift comes faster. The time available for safe exploration is shorter.

**Fourth:** the Veil does not care. This is not indifference. It is not malice. It is the thoroughness of a process that has no room for the categories that human minds use to organize experience. The Veil is a geological event. It is also a biological event. It is also an acoustic event. It is also something else, something that the word "event" does not capture because an event is something that happens and then stops, and the Veil is not stopping. It is still beginning. It has been beginning for a thousand years. It will continue to begin for a thousand more, or ten thousand, or however long the forces beneath the crust continue to push this place upward and the gases continue to vent and the Tower-Growths continue to grow and sing and the sound continues to do what the sound does, which is to loosen the bonds that hold things together, to unmake the made, to return form to formlessness, not as destruction but as a kind of generosity, a giving-back, a return to the potential from which all form emerged.

The Veil is not destroying. It is un-inventing. It is returning things to the moment before they became themselves. And it is patient. And it is thorough. And it has all the time in the world.

## IX. What Remains

If you stand at the eastern edge of the Veil at the hour when the dim light is at its dimmest — and there is no clear day or night here, only a slow waxing and waning of the haze's luminosity, as though some source behind the atmosphere is breathing — you can look west, toward the interior, and you can see the Tower-Growths rising in their thousands, their amber and violet stems catching what little light there is and holding it, and the sound is immense, and the ground is warm beneath your feet, and the air tastes of salt and sulfur and that strange sweetness, and you can feel the plateau rising, slowly, a millimeter at a time, pushing you upward, pushing everything upward, toward something that has no name because it has not yet arrived.

And if you listen — not with your ears, which are overwhelmed, but with the rest of you, with your bones and your blood and the spaces between your cells — you can hear, beneath the chorus, beneath the harmonics, beneath the shimmering overtones, a single note that is not produced by any Tower-Growth, that does not come from the fissures or the gas or the stone. It comes from below. From the deep. From whatever is pushing the Veil upward. It is very low. It is very steady. It has been sounding for longer than the Tower-Growths have been growing. It will continue sounding after they are gone.

It is the sound of something arriving.

You should not listen to it for long.

You should go down.
