# The Veldt of First Surfaces

## I.

It begins as a floor. That is the first wrong thing, and you do not notice it until you have walked for some time, because a floor is so ordinary, so easily mistaken for ground.

But there is no ground here. There was never ground. The floor extends in every direction — a manufactured plain of dark, vitreous substance like obsidian poured in a single continuous sheet across the breadth of a continent. You cannot find its edge. You have tried. It stretches past the horizon in every bearing, and the horizon here is far, because the floor is flat in a way that no natural surface is flat — machined flat, *intentionally* flat, the way a maker would flatten a thing if the maker had never seen a landscape before and had only the idea of *surface* to work with.

The second wrong thing: it is warm. Not warm like sun-warmed stone, which holds heat in its outer skin and releases it grudgingly. This warmth comes from below, from *inside* the floor, as though the material itself is generating heat, or as though something beneath it — but there is no beneath. You have knelt and pressed your ear to the surface and the warmth comes from the material, from its own internals, from whatever process keeps it at a temperature just below discomfort. The floor is alive in the way a furnace is alive. Not biology. Thermodynamics.

And the floor is wet.

## II.

Not covered in water. Not submerged. But wet in the way a breathing mirror is wet — condensation, constant and total, a fine film that forms and reforms on every square meter of the dark surface, never pooling, never drying. The atmosphere above the plain is so saturated with moisture that it deposits water onto anything cooler than itself, and the floor, despite its warmth, is cooler than the air. This is the third wrong thing: the air is hotter than the warm floor. You feel it on your skin — a thick, aqueous heat, not dry, not oppressive in the desert sense, but close and humid, like stepping into a greenhouse that has been sealed for centuries. The moisture enters your lungs and sits there. Your skin does not dry. If you stand still long enough, water beads on your forearms, on your cheekbones, along the edges of your ears.

The condensation is not rain. There is no rain here. No clouds, no sky — not exactly. Above the plain, the atmosphere is a low, dense ceiling of luminous vapor, amber-dark, like the inside of a kiln seen through steam. It produces light, but poorly. The illumination is dim and directionless, arriving from everywhere and nowhere, casting no shadows. You cannot tell the time of day by it. You are not sure there is a time of day. The light does not change. It has not changed since you arrived.

If you press your palm flat to the wet floor and hold it there, you can feel vibration. Not the static hum of a machine, but something arrhythmic and deep, like a heart that does not keep time. The floor is doing something. You do not know what.

## III.

There are no plants. There has never been a plant here. The concept of *plant* — of photosynthesis, of cellulose and chlorophyll and root systems threading through soil — has no purchase in this place. There is no soil for roots. There is no sun for light-harvesting. There is no ecological niche for a sessile autotroph to fill, because there is no ecology.

But there are *forms*.

At intervals across the plain — and the intervals are irregular, and the distances between them vast, hundreds of meters or sometimes kilometers — there are structures rising from the floor. They are not built upon the floor. They grow from it, as though the floor itself bulged and extended and solidified into shapes. Some are low and wide, like tables or altars or the palettes of a printer. Some are tall, columnar, five meters, eight meters, tapering to points or to flat platforms or to shapes that are difficult to name because they are not quite geometric — they are *almost* geometric, almost cubes or pyramids or spheres, but the angles are slightly off, the curves slightly asymmetric, as though the maker understood the *idea* of geometry but had never seen an example.

The material of the structures is the same as the floor: dark, glassy, warm. But where the floor is smooth, the structures are textured. The texture varies. Some are ribbed, like the bark of a tree that has never existed. Some are pitted with small concavities that collect the condensation and hold it in tiny silver beads. Some are covered in fine grooves that spiral from base to tip in patterns that might be writing, might be decoration, might be the natural fracturing of a cooling substance, might be nothing at all.

There is no way to know. There is no one to ask. The structures do not respond to touch, to voice, to force. They simply *are*, the way a shelf is, the way a wall is, the way a curb is. Functional. Patient. Made for purposes that no longer require purpose.

## IV.

The sound — or the near-absence of it.

The plain is quiet in the way that a sealed room is quiet. Not silent — there is always something — but the sounds are small, close, and strangely domestic. The drip of condensation gathering on the underside of a structure and falling two meters to the floor: a sound like a fingertip tapping a table. The low, arrhythmic thrum of the floor's internal vibration, felt more than heard, more felt in the teeth than the ears. Occasionally, a groan — deep, tectonic, as though the floor is flexing over some vast distance, a continental shrug. And something else, harder to identify: a sound like breathing, like the slow expansion and contraction of a lung, but at a scale too large to hear properly. You feel it in the pressure of the air against your eardrums. The atmosphere is moving. Inhaling. Exhaling. The whole volume of air above the plain is breathing.

You have tried to be quiet and listen. The listening tells you nothing except that you are alone with a sound that is not for you.

## V.

The turbulence.

This is the hardest part to describe, because it is not visible, not exactly. The floor does not move. The structures do not move. The light does not change. And yet the place is *unstable*. Not physically — the floor is solid, the structures are solid, your feet find purchase, nothing collapses or shakes. The instability is in something else. In the *quality* of the space.

Here is what happens: you walk. You choose a direction — north, you decide, although there is no north here, no magnetic field, no stars, no compass heading that means anything. You walk north. The floor stretches ahead. You see structures in the distance — low shapes, dark against the dark amber light. You walk toward them. You walk for an hour. You reach them.

But they are not the structures you saw. They are different. Not subtly different — *different*. One that you saw as a column is a low platform. One that you saw as a platform is a structure you cannot name, a shape that seems to change as you approach it, not in a sudden flicker but in a slow, continuous revision, as though the thing is being *edited* in real time, each step you take bringing a new version of it into existence.

You turn around. The structures behind you — the ones you passed an hour ago — are also different. Or they are gone. Or they were never there.

The plain is not rearranging itself. That would imply that it has an arrangement. The plain is *generating* itself, continuously, and the generation is not stable. Each moment produces a slightly different plain, a slightly different set of structures, a slightly different distance between them. You are walking through a landscape that is being made and remade, and the making is not neat, is not careful, is not the work of a builder but the work of a *process* — a process that does not know what it is making and does not care.

You think: this is what a world looks like before it becomes a world. Before the geology settles. Before the forms stabilize. Before the rules decide what they are. This is the scaffolding, the draft, the first surface.

But it has been like this for a long time. The structures are worn by the condensation. The grooves in their surfaces are deepened by centuries of water. The floor is scuffed and smoothed by — by what? Not feet. Not wind. Not water erosion in any way you recognize. Something has been *here*, acting on these surfaces, for a duration that the surfaces themselves record in their textures.

The turbulence is not coming. The turbulence *is*. This is the storm state. Not a storm of wind or rain or lightning. A storm of *becoming*. The plain is not in chaos. It is chaos — chaos that has taken the form of a plain, of a floor, of structures, of atmosphere, and is holding that form, barely, the way a wave holds its shape for one second before breaking.

## VI.

There are tools.

You find them near the structures, sometimes. Not on the structures — on the floor beside them, as though set down by a hand that intended to return. They are simple. A stone, shaped on one edge, flaked to a crude blade. A length of something like glass, thicker than the floor material, with a wrapped grip made of — not leather, not fiber, something that feels like stiffened fog, like condensed vapor pressed into a solid. The wrapping dissolves if you hold it too long, the moisture in your skin reactivating it, and you set it down quickly and watch it reform over the next hour, the vapor re-condensing, the grip rebuilding itself.

There are marks on some of the structures that match the tools. A scraped surface. A groove deepened by repeated scoring. Someone has been here, working on these surfaces. Not building them — *modifying* them. Carving. Scraping. Polishing. The tools are primitive — stone-age, hand-held, manual — and the work is slow, incremental, the work of years or centuries, one groove at a time, one surface at a time.

But you cannot find the workers. There are no workers. There are no bones, no waste, no camps, no paths worn between structures. There are only the tools, set down and left, and the marks they made.

You pick up one of the stone blades. It fits your hand. You press it to the surface of the nearest structure — a low, wide thing, table-height, its top covered in fine spiraling grooves. You scrape. The blade bites into the glassy material with surprising ease, producing a clean, bright line and a small curl of dark shavings. The shavings fall to the wet floor and dissolve.

The groove you made is already closing. Not healing — *reforming*. The surface is re-generating, filling in the mark you made, smoothing over the cut. In ten minutes, the groove is gone. In twenty, there is no trace.

You understand, then, what the work is. Not construction. Not destruction. *Maintenance*. The workers who left these tools were not building the structures. They were not decorating them. They were keeping up with the plain's continuous self-revision — carving the grooves back when the grooves smoothed themselves away, re-scoring the marks that the plain unmade. A Sisyphean craft. A tradition of opposition to the turbulence. Not to stop it — they could not stop it — but to keep pace with it, to mark their presence in the only way that mattered: by making a mark that had to be made again and again and again, the making of it the point, not the mark.

## VII.

The scale of the place presses on you.

You have walked for what feels like a day, and the plain has not changed in its essential character. The floor. The wet. The dim amber light. The structures at their vast, irregular intervals. The air breathing around you. The heat, steady, sourceless, the kind of heat that does not tire you but wears at something else — patience, maybe, or the conviction that you are moving through a real space.

Because the space is real. The floor is solid under your feet. The water on your skin is real water. The tools are real weight in your hand. The structures are real surfaces. And yet the space *behaves* as though it is not real — as though it is a thought being thought, a computation being run, a draft being revised, and you are inside the revision, inside the draft, inside the thought.

You are inside a thing that is being made.

The maker is absent. Perhaps the maker never existed. Perhaps the plain makes itself — a self-generating surface, a floor that produces floors, structures that produce structures, a world that produces world. The turbulence is the making. The quiet is the making. The wet and the warmth and the dim light are all the making. The condensation is not weather. It is *material*. The air is depositing the substance of the plain onto the plain, building it up, layer by molecular layer, and the structures are where the deposit has thickened, where the making has accreted into form, and the forms are unstable because the making does not know what forms to make.

It is trying. You can feel it trying. The floor is trying to be a floor. The structures are trying to be structures. The air is trying to be air. Everything here is trying to be what it is, and the trying is the turbulence, and the turbulence is the closest thing this place has to a law of physics.

## VIII.

You stop walking.

There is no point in continuing. The plain ahead is the plain behind. The structures will be different when you arrive. The marks you make will unmake themselves. The tools will be set down and picked up by hands that do not exist. The water will condense and fall and condense again. The air will breathe.

You sit on the floor. It is warm. It is wet. The condensation forms on your skin and does not dry. The amber light does not change. Somewhere, at a distance you cannot measure, a structure groans into a new shape, or an old shape, or a shape that has not been decided yet. The floor hums beneath you, arrhythmic, intentional, the sound of a surface making itself out of nothing, or out of itself, or out of the idea of surface.

It is not a hostile place. There is nothing here that wishes you harm. The heat is moderate. The wet is gentle. The light is sufficient. The structures are not dangerous. The turbulence does not threaten. This is simply a place that is *in process*, and you are in it, and the process does not know you are here, and does not need to know, and will continue whether you observe it or not.

You sit. The floor makes itself beneath you. The air breathes. The plain goes on.

That is all.
