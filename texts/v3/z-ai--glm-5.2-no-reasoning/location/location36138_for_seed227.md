# The Booming Hollow

## First Impression

There is a place beneath a basalt overhang, down where the talus has fractured into shelves and steps over centuries of freeze-thaw, where you descend a kind of natural stairway into a room not quite carved and not quite fallen. A hollow. A pocket in the geology. The ceiling is low enough to touch — seven feet at its apex, dropping to three where the rock presses down — and the floor is uneven, stepped, terraced by the same slow fracturing that opened the space to begin with. It has been here for a very long time. The walls carry the grain of ancient cooling, those long polygonal columns that basalt makes when it contracts, and some of the columns have separated from their neighbors and tilted outward like books leaning on a shelf. The gaps between them are the architecture. Air moves through them. Water moves through them. And sound — sound lives here.

The hollow is perhaps forty feet long and fifteen wide at its middle, narrowing at both ends where the walls close in. It is a single feature. A pocket. You could miss it from outside entirely, walking the slope above, unless you knew where the stairway began. From below, from the valley floor — if you could see it through the trees, which you mostly cannot — it would be a shadow under a shadow, a dark line in a dark rock face in a dark fold of forested hillside.

## The Sound

What defines this place, more than its geometry, more than its temperature or its darkness, is the noise.

There is a river. Not in the hollow — the hollow is dry, or nearly — but below it, forty feet downslope, where the drainage from the upper hillside has collected into a stream that drops over a series of ledge falls before reaching the valley floor. The stream itself is modest. Five feet across at its widest, running hard over angular rock, white and fast. But the geology has done something unusual here. The basalt overhang that forms the hollow's ceiling extends further downslope, and beneath it the ground drops away sharply, creating a kind of natural acoustic reflector — a curved stone wall that catches the sound of the falls and funnels it upward and inward through the columnar gaps in the rock. The hollow is, in essence, an ear. A chamber shaped by accident into a receiver.

The result is a roar that is difficult to describe in its totality. It is not the distant pleasant sound of a waterfall heard from a trail. It is present. It is *here*. It fills the space the way water fills a container — completely, pressing into corners, occupying every cubic inch of air. You feel it in your sternum before you fully register it with your ears. The lower frequencies come through the stone itself, conducted by the basalt walls, so that the rock under your hand hums and buzzes with a deep rhythmic pulse that is not quite separate from the sound in the air. The higher frequencies — the crash, the hiss, the spattering — arrive through the gaps in the columns, arriving at slightly different times from different openings so that the sound has a strange textured quality, layered, reverberant, as though many streams were falling in many places, though there is only one.

Standing in the hollow, you must raise your voice considerably to be heard by a companion an arm's length away. You feel your own voice in your throat, straining against the ambient noise. Words become inefficient. People who come here tend to communicate by gesture, by touch, by pointing. The sound discourages language. It is not hostile — there is nothing menacing in it — but it is overwhelming in the way that weather is overwhelming. You accommodate it. You do not argue with it.

And yet the sound is not chaotic. That is important. The falls have a rhythm. The volume of water passing over the ledges varies with the recent rain, with the slow melt of the upper slope's snowpack, with the time of day and the temperature and whether the ground is frozen or thawing. There are louder moments and quieter moments, though "quieter" here is relative. The gradient is slow. Over hours, the sound might shift in character — a deeper boom when the water is higher, a more rattling hiss when it is lower — but it does not stop. It has not stopped, perhaps, for centuries. The erosion parameter is high here: this is an established place. The stream has been falling, the hollow has been receiving, the basalt has been slowly fracturing and settling, for longer than any human record.

## Water

The hollow itself is damp rather than wet. Condensation is the primary source of moisture inside the chamber. The rock walls are cold — cold enough that in the higher gaps, frost persists in shaded pockets even during days when the outside temperature rises above freezing — and the air that flows through the hollow carries moisture from the stream below, from the wet ground outside, from the breath of the forest above. When this moisture-laden air meets the cold stone, it deposits. The walls glisten. Not dripping, not running, but covered in a fine film of water that makes the basalt look darker, blacker, more saturated than it actually is. In the lowest points of the uneven floor, where the stone has created shallow basins, small pools collect — an inch deep, two inches, perfectly still, reflecting nothing because there is almost no light to reflect. The water tastes clean. Mineral. Cold enough to make your teeth ache.

After rain — and it rains often here, slow cold rains that can last a full day — the condensation increases and the floor becomes genuinely slick. The stairway down becomes a minor hazard. The shallow pools rise slightly and connect briefly across the uneven floor before draining away through the columnar gaps. For a few hours, the hollow has a thin sheet of water across its floor, and the sound of the falls below is joined by a quieter sound: the trickle of this thin water finding its way out through the rock, adding its own small voice to the chorus.

But the water never stays. The hollow drains. The geology that created it — the gaps, the tilting columns, the fractured floor — ensures that it is a transient space for water. A place water passes through but does not occupy. This has been true for a long time. The stone is darkened by centuries of wet-dry cycling, stained by mineral deposits left behind as water evaporated, but it is not eroded by standing water. The hollow is the same shape it has been for decades, perhaps centuries. It has found its balance.

## Vegetation

What lives here, vegetatively, is a narrow community of organisms that can tolerate low light, cold stone, and constant moisture. The hollow is dark. The only natural light enters through the columnar gaps at its edges and through the entrance where the stairway descends, and that light is filtered by the dense forest above the overhang. On a bright day — rare here — the interior receives a dim gray illumination, enough to see by, barely, enough to make out the shapes of the walls and ceiling and the gleam of the wet stone. On a normal day, the interior is nearly black. You see by the light you bring.

And yet, in that darkness, moss thrives.

The moss is extraordinary. It coats the lower walls in thick carpets, green so dark it appears black until you bring a light close, and then it is a saturated, vital green, the green of something that has been growing in this exact spot for a very long time and has no intention of stopping. Several species are present. A feathery moss that climbs the columnar gaps, reaching toward the faint light from outside. A dense cushion moss that fills the low points in the floor, growing over the stone in rounded hummocks that are soft underfoot, almost bouncy, holding water like sponges. A haircap moss — the tallest of them, an inch and a half — that grows in the shallow pools at the floor's lowest points, its stems rising out of the standing water, apparently indifferent to the cold.

Ferns have found the hollow too. Not many — three plants, perhaps four, growing in the columnar gaps where the most light enters. They are small, stunted by the cold and the limited light, their fronds no longer than a hand, but they are healthy. Their rhizomes have crept through the moss and into the cracks in the stone, finding purchase, finding whatever nutrients the slow decay of moss and the mineral water provide. In the dimmest light, their fronds are almost invisible against the dark stone, but if you crouch and bring your face close, you can see the delicate structure, the pinnae, the sori, the fiddleheads beginning to uncurl in the gaps where the air is warmest.

There is also a liverwort — flat, dark green, ribbon-like — that grows across the stone floor in the places where water flows briefly after rain. It is a primitive thing, older than the ferns, older than the mosses in evolutionary terms, and it has the look of something ancient. It lies flat against the stone as if it is trying to be the stone, as if it is slowly becoming the stone, and in a sense it is — when it dies, its material becomes part of the thin soil that accumulates in the floor's depressions, the soil that the mosses and ferns grow in. The cycle is slow. The soil builds at a rate that is almost imperceptible. But it builds.

Above the hollow, outside, the vegetation is much more dense. The overhang is forested — a cold, wet forest of spruce and fir, with a dense understory of ferns and shrubs and ground-cover moss that forms a deep, soft carpet over the rock. The roots of these trees have found the columnar gaps and have entered them, reaching down into the hollow's ceiling, threading through the stone like veins. In a few places, fine root threads hang from the ceiling, pale against the dark basalt, reaching downward toward the moisture. They do not grow far — it is too cold, too dark — but they persist. The forest above is reaching into the hollow. Slowly. Over decades. The forest has time.

## The Built Element

There are signs that people have been here before. Not many. Not often. But enough.

Near the back of the hollow, where the ceiling drops to its lowest point and the walls narrow, there is a small structure. It is difficult to call it a building. It is a windbreak — a low wall of stacked stone, perhaps two feet high, built across a natural alcove in the basalt, closing off a space roughly six feet wide and four feet deep. The stones are local — basalt, taken from the talus slope outside — and they are fitted without mortar, dry-stacked, the work of someone who understood the material. The work is not fine. It is functional. The wall is built to deflect the airflow that comes through the columnar gaps at this end of the hollow, creating a pocket of stiller, slightly warmer air in the alcove behind it. It works. If you sit behind the wall, the air is noticeably calmer, though the sound is not diminished — the sound is everywhere, in the stone, in the air, in the floor.

The wall is old. The stones are darkened and mossy, integrated into the hollow's ecology. The moss has colonized the top of the wall and the gaps between stones, so that it looks less like a built thing and more like a natural ridge in the floor, a fold in the geology. It has been here long enough to stop being an intrusion and to become a feature.

Inside the alcove, on the stone floor, there is a small fire ring. A circle of stones, blackened by fire, with a shallow deposit of ash and charcoal in the center. The ash is old — cold, gray, compacted by time and by the weight of condensation that has fallen on it. It is not the ash of a recent fire. It is the ash of many fires, built in the same spot over a long period, each one adding its material to the deposit. The ring is small. A cookfire. A warming fire. Something for one person, or two, sitting in the alcove behind the windbreak, passing time.

There are a few other marks. On the wall beside the alcove, at shoulder height, someone has made a series of scratches in the basalt — not decorative, not symbolic, simply marks. Tallies, perhaps. Or a way of passing time, sitting in the alcove, listening to the sound that cannot be escaped, marking the stone with a sharp point, counting something. The marks are shallow, worn by the years, partially obscured by moss, but they are there. A human trace. The thinnest thread of connection to whoever came here, whoever sat in this alcove and built a fire and listened.

Outside the hollow, on the talus slope below the overhang, there is another sign: a small cairn, three stones stacked, placed at the point where the natural stairway begins its descent. It is a marker. *Here. Here is the way down.* It is not visible from a distance. You would need to be close to see it, and you would need to know what you were looking at. Whoever made it was not advertising. They were marking the place for themselves, or for someone who already knew it was there.

## The Darkness

It is hard to overstate how dark this place is.

The forest above is dense, the overhang is solid, the hollow is recessed into the hillside. The columnar gaps admit some light, but they are narrow and angled, and what enters is the ambient glow of a cold, overcast sky filtered through conifer canopy. On most days, standing in the hollow, you can see the entrance — the lighter rectangle where the stairway descends — and you can see the faint glow of the gaps in the walls. Everything else is shape without detail. The walls are present as darkness against darkness, perceived more by the sound that defines their shape — the sound bounces, reflects, is absorbed differently by moss than by stone — than by sight.

You feel your way. Hands on the walls. Feet testing the uneven floor. The moss helps — it marks the places where the stone is wet, where the footing is uncertain, by its texture underfoot. The built wall is a landmark, found by foot if not by eye, a low solid thing in a space of uncertain dimensions.

With a light, the hollow is transformed. The wet stone gleams. The moss blazes green. The columnar structure of the basalt becomes visible — the geometric precision of the rock, the hexagonal and pentagonal cross-sections of the columns, the way they have separated and tilted, creating the gaps, the architecture of the space. The shallow pools on the floor become mirrors, catching the light and throwing it back, doubling the illumination. The ceiling, which in darkness feels low and oppressive, becomes a surface of strange beauty — the cooled faces of ancient lava, marked by mineral stains in whites and oranges and blacks, polished by centuries of condensation.

But the light also reveals how small the space is. How contained. How enclosed. You are inside the rock. The rock is above you, below you, around you. The forest is above the rock. The stream is below. You are in a narrow gap in the geology, a pocket, and the pocket is full of sound and moisture and the slow persistence of cold-adapted life.

## Fauna

Almost nothing lives here. The sound, the cold, the darkness — these are barriers. The hollow is not a place that animals seek out.

There are spiders. Small, pale, long-legged spiders that live in the columnar gaps and in the moss on the walls. They build sparse webs across the gaps, catching the insects that are carried in by the airflow — midges, small flies, the occasional moth. The webs are difficult to see in the darkness, and you encounter them by feel, a strand across the face, a tackiness on the skin, before you brush them away. The spiders themselves are almost translucent, adapted to the dark, their eyes reduced, their bodies small. They have been here longer than the built wall. Their ecology is independent of human presence.

There is a salamander. One. A lungless salamander — a small, dark, glossy creature that lives in the moss on the floor, in the spaces between the stone where moisture persists. It is cold-blooded and does not mind the temperature. It does not make sound. It does not emerge into the open often. You might see it if you sit still long enough, a slight movement in the moss, a dark shape retreating into a gap. Or you might sit in the hollow for hours and never know it was there. The salamander's life is measured by the same slow cycles that govern the moss and the condensation. It eats what the moss attracts. It breeds in the shallow pools. It has no reason to leave.

Outside the hollow, in the forest above and on the talus slope below, there is more life — birds, small mammals, the stream's population of aquatic insects. But these are the forest's creatures, not the hollow's. The hollow is a margin. A place where the forest's life barely reaches. The salamander and the spiders are the hollow's only permanent residents, and they are few, and they ask for almost nothing.

## The Air

The temperature inside the hollow is stable. Cold — a few degrees above freezing, consistently, summer and winter. The rock insulates. The overhang shelters. The airflow from the stream below is cold but relatively constant, and the hollow's temperature does not swing the way the outside air does. In winter, the hollow is warmer than the surface. In summer, it is colder. It is a place of thermal constancy, and this is part of what makes it what it is — a pocket of cold, damp, dark, loud stability in a landscape that is otherwise exposed and variable.

The air moves. The columnar gaps create a passive ventilation system. Air enters from the stream side, drawn upward by the temperature differential between the warmer outside air and the colder stone. It flows through the hollow, picking up moisture from the wet walls, and exits through the gaps at the back, where the built wall deflects the coldest currents away from the alcove. The airflow is gentle. It is not a wind. But it is constant, and it carries the smell of the place.

The smell is stone. Wet stone, specifically. And moss — the green, slightly earthy smell of moss. And water — the clean, mineral smell of cold water. And beneath these, a faint mustiness, the smell of slow organic decay, the moss dying and being replaced, the liverwort growing and dying and becoming soil. The smell is not unpleasant. It is the smell of a place that is alive in a very small, very patient way.

## Time

The hollow changes slowly.

The moss grows at a rate that is imperceptible on any human timescale. The pools fill and drain with the rain, but the stone basins that hold them deepen only over centuries, as the water dissolves the minerals in the basalt, molecule by molecule. The columnar gaps widen, microscopically, as the rock weathers. The ferns advance and retreat — a frond reaching further one year, killed back by a cold snap the next, another frond appearing in a different gap, an inch to the left. The salamander lives its life — perhaps ten years, perhaps fifteen — and is replaced by another salamander, or by none, and the hollow continues either way.

The built wall ages. The stones settle. The moss covers more of them each year. In another century, the wall will be a moss-covered ridge, indistinguishable from a natural feature unless someone knows what to look for. The fire ring will be a patch of dark earth in the stone floor, colonized by liverwort. The tally marks on the wall will be gone, worn away by the condensation, the surface of the stone slowly dissolving, the marks dissolving with it.

The sound will remain. The stream will continue to fall. The basalt will continue to receive. The hollow will continue to be what it is — a pocket of noise and damp and cold and green, hidden in the hillside, unknown to most, visited by few, enduring in its own state.

## What It Is Like To Be Here

You descend the natural stairway, and the sound rises around you like water rising in a pool. By the time you reach the hollow's floor, it is total. You stop talking. Your companion stops talking. You look at each other in the dimness and your faces are barely visible. You feel the sound in your body — in your chest, in your teeth, in the bones of your skull. You put your hand on the wall and the wall is vibrating, humming, alive with the lower frequencies that come through the stone.

You move carefully. The floor is uneven, wet in places, mossy. You find the built wall by touch, by the change in the air. You step over it, into the alcove, and the air is stiller, slightly warmer. You crouch. You sit. The stone floor is cold through your clothing.

You light a small light, and the hollow opens up — the moss, the wet stone, the ferns in the gaps, the small pool at the lowest point, the dark pool reflecting the light back at you. You see the fire ring. You see the tally marks. Someone was here. Someone sat where you are sitting and listened to what you are listening to and passed the time by marking the stone or building a fire or simply being still.

The sound does not relent. It does not pause. It fills the hollow and it fills you and it becomes, after a time, something like a medium, something you exist within, the way a fish exists in water. You stop hearing it as noise. It becomes the world's baseline. Your own breathing, your own heartbeat, exist within it, and when you speak — if you speak — your voice is small and private and belongs only to you, enclosed in the sound but not erased by it.

You sit. The hollow is cold and dark and loud and alive, in its small way, and it is very old, and it does not need you. You are a visitor. The sound was here before you and will be here after you, and the moss will grow over your footprints and the salamander will not remember you were here. This is not hostile. It is simply the nature of the place. It is a pocket in the geology, full of the sound of falling water, and it is complete without you.
