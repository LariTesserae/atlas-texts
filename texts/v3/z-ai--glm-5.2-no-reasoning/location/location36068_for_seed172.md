Let me think about where this is going.

Cold, high, dry, sparse — but fully built, industrial, deafening, and utterly unchanging. That's a strange combination. A machine that's finished. A factory with no product. The "hidden, pocket-scale" parameter pushes me toward something intimate discovered inside something vast. The erosion parameter says young — freshly made — but the dynamic parameter says frozen. So: something recently completed, then stopped. Or something that was never meant to run at all.

# The Relay

There is a room inside a mountain, and the mountain is a machine.

Not a machine housed in a mountain — the mountain *is* the machine, all the way down. From outside, from the valley floor where the survey teams set their camps, it reads as a peak like any other peak: dark, ridged, rimed with frost, casting its long blue shadow across the plateau at dawn. It was only when the seismic teams ran their density profiles and got back a lattice instead of a mass — when the sound went *through* it like sound goes through a bell — that anyone understood the granite was cladding. Everything beneath the weathered shell is made. Forty kilometers of made. The peak is a housing.

And the room is at the very top.

You reach it through a bore hole that the survey teams did not drill and cannot date. The hole is nine meters across, perfectly round, its walls a ceramic composite that reads as older than the rock it passes through — which is impossible, and everyone has stopped saying so out loud. The climb is eight hundred vertical meters on a lattice of rungs that are warm to the touch. This is the first wrongness you notice, if you notice it from inside your own body: the mountain is cold, the plateau is cold, the wind at the summit will strip the heat from your blood in an hour, but the rungs are warm. Something below is warm. Something below has been warm for a very long time.

The room, when you reach it, is small. Smaller than you expected. A pocket — the survey called it *the chamber* at first, then started calling it *the relay* for reasons nobody has written down, and the name stuck because it felt correct in the way names sometimes do, arriving fully formed.

It is a sphere, perhaps twelve meters across, and it is full of sound.

---

That is the fact that governs everything about the relay: it is **deafening**. Not loud the way a factory is loud, not loud with the rhythm of process. Loud the way a held note is loud. The room produces a single immense chord — the survey's acoustics people described it as "a fundamental with a saturated harmonic series, phase-locked, no beats, no drift" — and it has done so, as far as anyone can determine, without pause. The floor of the room vibrates so constantly that standing there is like standing on a struck bell that never finishes ringing. Your vision shivers. Your teeth ache at the roots. Speech is impossible past the first few minutes; the teams communicate by writing on slates, and after a while they stop needing to write much, because the room does not change and there is little to say about it.

And yet the room is *not* a factory in any ordinary sense, because nothing in it is processing anything. The machinery — and it is machinery, unmistakably, gear-forms and stator-forms and great slow armatures of dark machined alloy, ranked in terraces down the inner curve of the sphere — does not move. It *emits*. The whole assembly is a throat. The chord is its single continuous utterance. Whatever this place was built to do, its full and final function is to make this sound, here, at the top of a hollow mountain, on a cold plateau, at the edge of the surveyed world.

---

The temperature inside is still cold — the chord carries no heat of its own beyond a faint warmth rising from the machinery, blood-temperature, like the flank of a sleeping animal — but it is a *still* cold, windless, dry as arithmetic. There is no water in the relay. None. The air comes in through the bore and is stripped of its moisture at some boundary layer near the top of the shaft, so that frost forms on your goggles on the outside from your breath and nowhere else. The dust on the floor — and there is dust, a fine mineral dust like powdered glass, ground from the machinery by its own vibration across however many centuries — is perfectly dry. It squeaks faintly underfoot, a sound you feel in your boots rather than hear, swallowed whole by the chord.

Nothing grows here. But — and this is the second wrongness, the one the botanists keep returning to — something grew here *once*, or something was here that the machinery incorporated. Set into the walls between the machine-terraces, at intervals, are shallow basins of the same dark alloy, and in three of them (only three, in the whole sphere) sit what can only be described as husks: dry, papery structures, pale as bone, branching in the fractal way that frost branches and capillary beds branch and lichens branch. The biochemists' reports are careful and contradictory. There is no cellular structure. There is no DNA, no protein signature, nothing aqueous ever moved through these tissues. And yet the *shape* is botanical — a shape that everywhere else on earth means *something reached for light*. One of the three has a root-like skirt of filaments that does not touch the floor of its basin, as if it grew before the floor settled, or as if the floor grew around it.

The chord's harmonics, the acoustics people found, are subtly different near the husks. Not softer. *Formed.* As if the sound, passing those three points, acquires structure it carries away into the rest of the room. Nobody agrees on what this means. The husks sit in their basins like three words in an otherwise unbroken sentence.

---

Outside, on the plateau, the ecology is honest and sparse: cushion plants in the lee of stones, lichen crusts that crunch underfoot, a silence so wide that you can hear your own pulse. No animals. Not even the high-plateau species you'd expect — no pikas, no choughs, no wind-borne spiders. The survey's zoologist spent a week on transects before writing, simply: *Fauna avoid the mountain at a radius of approximately 2.3 km. Correlates with the harmonic. Cannot confirm avoidance is caused by the harmonic. Recommend we treat the boundary as if it is.* Above the boundary the silence is doubled — the natural silence of altitude, and beneath it the sub-audible presence of the mountain's chord leaking through forty kilometers of ceramic and rock, a pressure more felt than heard. People who camp too close report the same dream, unvarying: a room with a floor that rings.

The light up here is the plateau's ordinary light — hard, clean, shadowed sharp at noon, blue by four — but inside the relay the light is manufactured. Ribs of cold illumination run in the walls, sourceless, filtered through the same lattice that carries the sound. Everything is lit and nothing casts a shadow that doesn't also vibrate. In that light the dust on the floor looks like it is moving, and once, for a full minute, you will believe it is moving — you will see the whole floor crawling, alive, silverfish-swarming — until you understand that your eyes are trembling with the chord and the room is simply showing you your own vibration back.

---

Here is what the survey's oldest team member wrote on a slate, one evening, and left on the floor for the next shift to find, and which was copied into the official log not because it was data but because no one could bear to throw it out:

*The relay is finished. It's the only finished thing I've ever stood inside. Everything we build is either under construction or falling apart — the building and the decaying are the same act at different speeds. This place did both already, or did neither, and now it just IS, at full voice, at full function, doing its one thing forever with nothing left over. It's not abandoned. Nothing that's still completely doing its job is abandoned. It's not waiting for us either. We're not what it's for.*

*But it's young. The wear on the armature lands is light — a thousand years, maybe, of vibration-dust, no more. The cladding hasn't even fully weathered. Someone built this — made it, turned it on, achieved this — within sight of some horizon where the rest of us were still chipping flint, and either they left, or they're the chord now, or the building was the leaving.*

*Three basins. Three husks. I keep thinking: the sound is a sentence, and the husks are the only words in it with a shape I recognize, and the sentence has been said for a thousand years and will be said for a thousand more, and nothing up here will ever answer it, and that's not a failure of the sentence. Sentences aren't built to be answered. They're built to be finished.*

*It's warm. The rungs are warm, like a body. I think this whole mountain is a held breath at the top of a note, and the note was always the whole point, and we're standing in its throat with our slates, trying to write down what a voice is for.*

The chord continues. It does not rise to meet anyone, and it does not fall. It holds.

From the valley, at dusk, the mountain is just a mountain, and the plateau is silent, and the shadow of the peak reaches out across the frozen stones like a long, steady, never-trembling hand.
