# The Chorale Cavity

You find it through a wound.

Not a cave mouth — nothing so dignified. A split in the root-mat of a colossal fallen ironwood, where the wood pulled apart like wet rope as the tree died and folded. The split is barely wide enough for a shoulder. You go through sideways, and then down — not far, only the height of a tall man — into a space that should not be.

It is not a cave. It is a room. Or it was, once, before it became this.

---

The floor is root. Not roots covering a floor — the floor *is* root, a dense interwoven platform of living wood the color of opened pomegranate, damp and slightly yielding underfoot. It gives like cork. Each step produces a sound you do not expect: a low, resonant hum, as if the entire root-plate were a drum membrane and something underneath it were listening. The hum is not a single tone. It layers. It harmonizes with itself. You step again, and the pitch shifts — higher, or lower, depending on where you place your weight. You realize after a time that you are walking across an instrument.

This is the first wrong thing. The second comes when you look up.

The ceiling is also plant. Pale, almost white — a felted mass of mycelium and fine root-hair, matted so densely it holds its own shape, sagging in low swags like a tent of wet linen. It is only three meters above you. The space is intimate. A pocket. A secret kept by the earth and the dead tree together. Condensation collects along the mycelium fibers and falls — not rain, not drip, but a constant fine precipitate, a misting downward, so that the air itself is wet. You taste it. It tastes of tannin and something mineral, something that is not quite iron and not quite blood.

The light should not be here. There is no source. No crack, no opening to the sky, no bioluminescence. And yet the chamber is lit — lit normally, as if by diffuse daylight filtered through a canopy, though you are underground and the canopy is *below* you, far above, dead and rotting. The light has no origin. It is simply present, as if the space remembers being lit and has not yet noticed that it is not. When you move your hand, the light does not follow a source. It does not cast a shadow from a single direction. Your shadow lies in several directions at once, faintly, as if lit by a sun that cannot decide where it is.

This is the third wrong thing. The fourth, fifth, and sixth arrive together, as sound.

---

The noise is immense. Not painful — not at first — but vast in a way that fills the skull. It is layered. The lowest stratum is the hum of the root-floor, your own footfalls feeding into it, but beneath your stillness there is a deeper drone, felt more than heard, a pulse like a heartbeat belonging to no animal. Above that drone, a sustained choir — a chord, held by no voices, produced by the cavity itself, by the geometry of the space, by the pressure of root against mycelium against soil against the dead weight of the tree above. The sound is the sound of a shape resonating. The chamber is singing.

And woven through the song, a static. A hiss. Not white noise but *torn* noise — sound with holes in it, gaps that move, that flicker past the ear like a bird darting through silence. When the gaps pass, you lose fragments of the other sounds. A syllable of the chord drops out. A beat of the drum-floor vanishes. It is as if the silence is alive, and mobile, and hunting for sounds to eat.

You stand still. The chorus adjusts around you. Your presence is a new resonant body — warm, dense, water-filled — and the chamber incorporates you. The pitch drops by a quarter-tone. Something in the mycelium ceiling tightens. A bead of condensation swells at the low point of a sag and falls, and when it strikes the root-floor, the impact produces a clear, singular *note* — perfect, bell-like, a tone that should be impossible from a surface of wet wood.

The note hangs. It does not decay. It joins the chord, sustains, and you realize with a crawling unease that the sounds in this place are *cumulative*. Nothing fades. The chorus is built from every sound ever made in this cavity, layered and sustained and sustained and sustained.

You do not know how long it has been here.

---

The vegetation is the space. There is no substrate that is not plant. The walls — for there are walls, roughly oval, perhaps six meters along the long axis — are a cross-section of the earth itself, held in place by a lattice of roots so fine and so numerous they have become a fabric, a weave, a living textile. Through the gaps in the weave, you see soil. Dark, almost black, shot through with threads of pale mineral that catch the sourceless light and glint. The roots are not still. They move. Slowly, perceptibly — a centimeter over a minute — they extend, retract, thicken, thin, as if the walls are breathing. The chamber is not a fixed volume. It is a slow lung, expanding and contracting on a timescale you can feel in your jaw, in your teeth, a pressure change that is not quite comfortable and not quite painful.

Where the root-walls and the mycelium-ceiling meet, there is a seam — a join so tight it looks welded. But at the seam, small things grow. Mosses, yes, but also structures that are not moss, not exactly. They are flat, pale, translucent, like flakes of shaved soap, and they grow in spirals. The spirals are not decorative. They are functional. You watch one for a time and realize it is rotating — the whole flake, turning on its root-point, very slowly, and as it turns, it draws condensation from the air along its spiral groove, channeling the water inward, toward the seam, where it vanishes into the join between wall and ceiling. The chamber is watering itself. The chamber is a thing with a metabolism.

This is when the weirdness settles in fully — not as shock, but as atmosphere, as the room's own gravity. Because you realize this is not a place that was made and will end. This is a place that is being made, continuously, and will unmake itself, continuously, and neither the making nor the unmaking follows rules you recognize.

---

There are creatures. You see one, and it changes what you thought you were looking at.

It is small — the size of a thumb-joint — and it moves along the root-floor with a motion that is not walking, not crawling, but *revising*. It exists in one spot, and then it exists in another spot, and you did not see the movement between. It is not fast. It is not teleporting. It is simply that the space between the two spots has been removed, briefly, and then put back. The static — the torn-hiss, the holes in the sound — intensifies as it moves. It is eating the distance. It is eating the sound. The gaps in the chorus are this thing's wake.

It is pale. It is soft-bodied. It has no visible features — no eyes, no mouth, no limbs — and yet you have the overwhelming sense that it is a creature, an animal, a being with preferences. It is moving toward the seam where the spiral-flakes gather water. It is thirsty.

It reaches the seam and presses itself flat against the wood, and the wood gives around it — not breaking, not denting, but *accommodating*, as if the root-floor recognizes the creature as part of its own body. A small dimple forms. Condensation pools in the dimple. The creature drinks.

You realize the creature is a part of this place the same way the spirals are a part of this place. Not resident. Not visitor. Organ. The chamber has moving parts, and some of them are animals.

You count them. Three. Four. No — more, but most are so small and so still that you mistook them for blemishes on the wood. They cluster along the seams and the edges and the low points where water collects. They are rare. You could count them on your fingers. They are all that lives here that is not plant.

---

A tool lies on the root-floor. It is the only sign that a human being has ever been here.

It is a digging stick — a length of heartwood from a branch, stripped of bark, sharpened by rubbing against stone, worn smooth at the grip by a hand. It is not old. Weeks, perhaps. It has not yet begun to rot. Someone was here. Someone found this cavity, entered it, brought a tool, and left the tool behind.

Why?

The stick lies across a spot on the root-floor where the wood is different — darker, tighter-grained, with a slight raised pattern like a scar or a knot. The pattern is regular. Too regular. It is not a natural shape. It is a shape made by intention, by repetition, by a hand pressing and scoring and pressing again over days or weeks, training the root to grow in a particular direction. The pattern is a groove. The groove is a channel. The channel carries condensation from one part of the floor to another, pooling it in a small reservoir no larger than a cupped palm.

Someone was farming. Someone found this singing, breathing, impossible pocket beneath a dead tree, and recognized that the roots could be trained, that the water could be directed, that the spirals could be cultivated. Someone brought a digging stick and began to shape the floor the way one might shape clay — not by carving, but by teaching the living wood to grow differently. Primitive. Patient. Pre-metal. A technique older than agriculture, maybe. A technique that consists of touching a surface for a long time until it learns your shape.

They are not here now. The tool is. The groove is. The small reservoir holds a finger's depth of water, clear, brown, warm. The chorus has incorporated the reservoir into its architecture — when a drop falls from the ceiling into the pool, the pool rings with a note that is higher and thinner than the floor's deep hum, a note like a glass harp, and this note, too, does not decay. It joins the chord. The chord is enormous now, in your skull, in your sternum, a sound that is almost a feeling, almost a weather.

---

The air is warm. Not hot — warm the way breath is warm, the way a living body is warm, which makes sense, because you are inside something living. The cavity's temperature is the temperature of a mouth. The dampness is the dampness of a mouth. You are inside a structure that is breathing and sweating and singing, and you are inside it, and it does not mind.

Or it does not know.

Or — and this is the thought that sends you back toward the split, toward the surface, toward the grey sky and the rotting tree — it has always known, and the song is not a byproduct. The song is the purpose. The chamber is an instrument, and the instrument is being played, and the player is the plant itself, and the song it is singing is the song of its own growth, its own structure, its own slow breath, and you are not a visitor. You are a reed. A stop. A new body in the resonant space, and the chamber has tuned itself to you, and your leaving will change the chord, and your returning will change it back, and the groove in the floor, and the reservoir, and the digging stick — these are not artifacts. These are notes. A human being came here and became part of the music, and the music remembers, and the music is loud, and the music is holding.

You leave. The split closes behind you. Not all the way. Not immediately. But you hear it — the fibers of the root-mat catching, tightening, pulling together, the way a wound closes, and the sound that the closing makes is a note, and the note is in a key that you will not be able to stop hearing for some time. A long time. The chord goes with you. You carry it in your jaw, in the bones of your middle ear, in the warm wet space behind your thoughts.

You go back to the surface and the sky is grey and the tree is dead and the world is ordinary and you are standing in a field, and underneath you, far enough down that you can almost pretend it is imagination, the chord is still playing, and it has your note in it, and it will not give it back.
