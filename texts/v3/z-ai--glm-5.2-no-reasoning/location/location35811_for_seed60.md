# The Percolation Gardens

## A Description

You descend through a throat of living fiber.

The passage — if it can be called a passage — is the interior of a root system whose diameter exceeds the height of a standing person. Flattened, shelf-like lateral roots form a spiraling ramp that corkscrews downward through a cross-section of earth and wood and more wood, and the walls weep. Not dramatically. Not a cascade. A persistent, patient secretion of water that beads along every surface, collects into threads no thicker than a finger, and traces downward in paths too numerous to follow. The air is warm and close and tastes of tannin and iron, and the sound — the sound begins before you have fully entered.

It is a sound without source, or with too many sources to isolate. A low, compound groan, like a cathedral organ fed through gravel, underlaid by a rapid, arrhythmic ticking, like dozens of mechanical clocks running at slightly different speeds inside a padded box. The sound is the garden's respiration. You understand this not because anyone tells you but because the walls around you are visibly, slowly, inperceptibly *working* — flexing, swelling, contracting — and the sound rises and falls in time with that labor, and it is loud enough that you feel it in your sternum before you hear it with your ears.

The ramp deposits you in a room.

---

## The Room

The room is roughly cathedral-scale — perhaps forty meters across at its widest, perhaps fifteen meters from the floor of exposed root to the vaulted ceiling of interlocking canopy — but the word *room* implies a space, and there is barely space here. There is barely an absence. The living mass of the garden has occupied nearly every cubic meter, and what remains is not empty so much as *permitted* — channels and alcoves and corridors carved by the garden's own logic, maintained by its own processes, as deliberate in their way as the halls of any built structure.

The floor is root. Not a floor of root — a root, singular, a single lateral span of the garden's primary organism, flattened by its own weight and by the slow pressure of centuries into a surface firm enough to walk on. It is warm to the touch. It gives slightly, like cork. Its bark has been worn smooth by something — water, perhaps, or the passage of maintenance organisms that you will encounter later — and its surface is etched with a pattern of shallow grooves that radiate outward from the room's center like the spokes of a wheel, each groove carrying a thin, continuous film of water toward the perimeter, where it falls away into darkness through gaps in the root structure that are too narrow to accommodate a hand.

Above, the ceiling is canopy. Not the open, sky-broken canopy of a forest but a closed, woven, interlocking mesh of branch and leaf and epiphytic growth that admits light in a patchwork of filtered gold and deep green shadow. The light is normal daylight, or once was — by the time it has passed through the layers above, it has been partially consumed, partially diffused, and what remains is a warm, even, sourceless illumination that casts no hard shadows. You can see, but you cannot tell where the light comes from. It seems to originate from the leaves themselves, from the wet surfaces, from the air.

The density of the space is total. There is no point, standing in this room, at which you can extend your arm and not touch something living. Ferns erupt from every joint and seam in the root structure. Moss — though it is not moss, exactly; it is something with the morphology of moss but the cellular structure of something far older — coats every surface in a thick, waterlogged pelt. Vines and lianas drape from the canopy in curtains so dense they read as walls. Epiphytes cluster in the crotches of branches overhead, their own root systems reaching downward like fingers, tapping the host structure for water and minerals. Fungal networks — pale, bioluminescent in the deeper shadows, though not brightly enough to illuminate — thread through everything, visible as faint, webbing lines beneath the bark surfaces.

You stand in a space that is entirely, completely, wall-to-wall alive, and the only reason you can stand at all is that the garden has, for its own reasons, decided to keep this particular chamber open.

---

## The Water

Water is everywhere, but it is not dramatic. There are no waterfalls, no rivers, no standing pools of significant depth. The water moves by means you have to watch carefully to perceive.

The root floor is, as noted, etched with drainage channels, but above you the canopy performs a similar function on a larger scale. The upper surface of the garden — which you cannot see from here but which you can infer — collects rain, condensation, and fog, and directs it downward through the interior. Every surface in this room is wet, but not uniformly. The water moves. It beads, coalesces, threads downward along vines and roots and fungal filaments, drips from leaf-tips into channels that carry it to the perimeter, and falls away. The garden is a machine for the collection and distribution of water, and it has been running for a very long time.

The air itself is saturated. Not quite raining, not quite dry. A persistent molecular wetness that condenses on every available surface, including exposed skin, so that after a few minutes in this room you are as uniformly damp as everything else. The temperature — warm, not hot, perhaps equivalent to a late summer afternoon in a temperate climate — means this is not uncomfortable. It is simply the baseline condition of the place. You are wet. Everything is wet. The air is wet. The wetness is not weather; it is *state*.

The sound of the water is subsumed into the larger sound of the garden's respiration — the ticking is partly water, partly the mechanical flexing of root structures under hydraulic pressure, partly something else that you cannot identify. The groan is partly the slow deformation of wood under tremendous, distributed load, and partly something vocal, something produced by the garden's deeper processes, a sound that has more in common with the groaning of a large animal's gut than with the creaking of a tree in wind. It is loud enough that conversation would require raised voices. It is constant enough that, after a time, it begins to feel like silence — a silence with texture, a silence that fills the ears so completely that it becomes a kind of quiet.

---

## The Garden's Body

The garden is one organism, or nearly. The root structure that forms the floor is connected to the trunk structures that form the walls is connected to the branch structures that form the ceiling is connected to the leaf canopy above, and all of it — all of it — is a single genetic individual, a single plant that has, over a span of time you cannot easily estimate, grown to fill this space entirely.

But it is not alone. The garden is a host, and the host is occupied.

The moss-like coating on every surface is a distinct organism, or rather a symbiotic complex of several organisms — something between a lichen and a moss, with properties of both and properties of neither. It breaks down the garden's shed material — bark fragments, dead root tissue, leaf litter — and returns the nutrients to the host's root system through the drainage channels. It is the garden's digestive system, living on the garden's skin.

The fungal networks threading through the structure are also symbiotic. They are the garden's nervous system, or its closest analog — a communications network that links the most distant parts of the organism, transmitting chemical signals at speeds far exceeding anything a normal mycelial network achieves. When the garden flexes — when it groans and swells and contracts — the fungal network is what coordinates the motion, directing hydraulic pressure from one zone to another, maintaining the structural integrity of the whole.

The ferns and epiphytes are less tightly integrated. They are opportunists — organisms that have colonized the garden's body, finding niches in its structure, drawing water and nutrients from its surfaces. The garden tolerates them. They may serve a purpose — intercepting light, retaining moisture, providing a buffer against mechanical damage — or they may simply be below the threshold at which the garden's maintenance processes bother to remove them.

And there are the flowers.

---

## The Flowers

In the deepest part of the room, in the alcove where the drainage channels converge and the water falls away into the sub-floor, there is a cluster of flowers unlike anything in the canopy above. They are large — the size of a human head, or larger — and they are not attached to the garden's body in any way you can see. They emerge from the junction of the root floor and the root wall, from a seam where two major structural branches meet, and they emerge in a way that suggests they are produced *by* the garden but are not *of* the garden — a distinction without a clear mechanism, but one that feels correct when you see them.

They are pale. Not white, but the color of something that was once white and has been steeped in tannin water for a long time — a bone-cream, a skin-color, an unsettling organic tint. Their petals are thick, waxy, and beaded with moisture. Their centers are deep, dark wells that you cannot see to the bottom of, and from these wells comes a smell — not floral, not sweet, but mineral and warm and slightly metallic, like the smell of a hot spring or the smell of blood on warm stone.

They are warm. Significantly warmer than the surrounding air. If you bring your hand near — not touching, but near — you can feel the heat radiating from them. They are the warmest surface in the room, warmer than the root floor, warmer than the walls, warmer than the air. They seem to be generating heat independently, metabolizing something, consuming something, and the something is not light, because they are in the deepest shadow in the room.

The flowers pulse. Not visibly — or not quite visibly. There is a change in them, a slow cycle of expansion and contraction that takes perhaps thirty seconds to complete, and it is synchronized — all the flowers in the cluster pulse together, and when you are close enough to feel their heat, you realize that the pulse is also a sound: a low, resonant tone, below the threshold of the garden's groaning, felt more than heard, and it is the deepest layer of the compound sound that fills the room. The flowers are singing. They have been singing the whole time you have been here, and you did not know until you were close enough to feel it in your chest.

What they are singing for — or to — is not clear. But the fungal networks in the walls near the flowers are denser, more complex, more active than anywhere else in the room, and the signals they carry spike in intensity with each pulse of the flowers, as if the flowers are issuing instructions and the network is carrying them to the farthest reaches of the garden's body. The flowers may be the garden's brain. Or they may be the garden's mouth. The distinction may not be meaningful.

---

## The Built Things

There are structures in the garden that are not part of the garden.

They are sparse — you find three in this room, and you suspect there are more in the chambers beyond — and they are old, and they are being slowly consumed.

The first is a pipe. A metal pipe, ceramic-lined, approximately fifteen centimeters in diameter, emerging from the root wall at chest height and extending about a meter into the room before terminating in a corroded fitting that once connected to something else. The root structure has grown around it, has incorporated it into its own architecture, and the pipe is now as much a part of the wall as the wood itself. Water still drips from its broken end — not garden water, but something else, something with a faint chemical smell, a trace of something industrial that has been leaching through the pipe's ceramic lining for a very long time. The pipe is warm. Warmer than the roots around it, not as warm as the flowers. It is delivering something to the garden, or it was, and the garden has grown around it to capture whatever it delivers.

The second is a housing. A metal box, roughly cubic, perhaps half a meter on each side, mounted — or once mounted — on a platform that has since been consumed by root growth. The box is rusted through in several places, and through the holes you can see the remnants of machinery inside: gears, a housing for a motor or actuator, copper wiring turned green with verdigris. It does not function. It has not functioned for a long time. But the garden's root structure has grown *into* it, threading through the corroded gaps, wrapping around the internal components, and the fungal network is particularly dense in and around the box, as if the garden is still trying to read whatever information the machine once processed.

The third is the most intact: a pump station, or the remains of one. A small platform — two meters by three, elevated on metal legs that have been half-swallowed by root growth — supporting a pump mechanism that is still, impossibly, marginally functional. The pump is connected to the pipe system, and it cycles — slowly, irregularly, with a mechanical wheeze that adds one more layer to the room's complex sound — and when it cycles, it moves water from one part of the garden's drainage system to another, rerouting flow in a way that the garden has not bothered to correct. The pump is powered by the garden. Not by electricity — the power source is long dead — but by the garden's own hydraulic pressure, which has been tapped by root structures that have grown into the pump's intake and drive mechanisms. The garden has enslaved the machine, or the machine has become part of the garden, or both.

These built things are not recent. They are not the marks of current habitation. They are artifacts of a previous era — an era in which this place was, perhaps, an industrial facility of some kind, a processing plant, a water management station — and the garden has grown over and through and into them, incorporating them into its own structure, digesting them slowly the way the moss-coating digests its shed bark. The garden does not remove them. It does not reject them. It metabolizes them. Given enough time, they will be entirely consumed, and the only evidence they were ever here will be a slight thickening in the root structure at the points where they once stood.

---

## The Maintenance Organisms

You see them eventually, though they are sparse and they avoid the open parts of the room.

They are not animals. They are not insects. They are plant-matter — small, mobile constructs of leaf and stem and fiber that the garden produces and deploys for specific tasks. Each is about the size of a human hand, and each is composed of a tightly-furled leaf-body, several fibrous leg-structures that operate by hydraulic pressure, and a cluster of root-like tendrils at the anterior end that serve as both sensory organs and tools.

You see one on the ceiling, tending the canopy. It moves slowly along a branch, probing the junctions between branch and leaf with its tendrils, and where it finds damage — a split, a lesion, a point of decay — it excretes a resinous substance from its anterior that seals the wound. It is a maintenance worker, an immune cell, and the garden produces thousands of them, each operating autonomously within its zone of the structure.

You see another on the floor, working the drainage channels. It moves from groove to groove, clearing blockages — fragments of bark, clumps of moss, the occasional dead leaf — and ensuring the water flows freely. It works methodically, slowly, and it does not react to your presence in any way you can detect.

A third you find inside the corroded machinery housing, threading its tendrils through the copper wiring, and you cannot tell what it is doing — repairing the machine, or dismantling it, or simply exploring it with the same blind curiosity that the garden explores everything that enters its domain.

These organisms are rare. In an hour of exploration, you see perhaps six. They are the only moving things in the garden besides you. They are the garden's hands, its eyes, its tools, and they have been doing this work for a very long time.

## The Deeper Rooms

Beyond this room, the garden continues. The drainage channels at the perimeter empty into lower chambers, and the root structure descends through levels — each one slightly smaller, slightly denser, slightly warmer — and the sound changes as you descend. The groaning deepens. The ticking quickens. The flowers' subsonic pulse grows stronger, more present, until it is the dominant sensation, a physical pressure that rises and falls in your chest like a second heartbeat.

The lower chambers are darker. The canopy above is thicker here, and less light penetrates. The fungal networks glow more brightly — a pale, greenish luminescence that picks out the webbing of the walls in ghostly lines — and the flowers are more numerous, larger, hotter. In the lowest chamber you reach, the flowers are the only source of light, and they glow faintly, a dull amber radiance that barely illuminates the space around them, and the heat they generate is enough to raise the air temperature by several degrees.

The built things are denser down here. More pipes, more housings, more corroded machinery, all of it more thoroughly consumed by root growth. The garden has been working on these artifacts longer, here, and some of them are barely recognizable — lumps of metal and ceramic embedded in the root structure, with only a corroded edge or a fragment of wiring exposed to indicate they were ever anything other than part of the plant.

The lowest chamber has no floor, exactly. The root structure here is a mesh — a grating of living wood with gaps wide enough to see through, and below it is darkness and the sound of falling water. The drainage channels from all the chambers above converge here, and the water falls through the grating into a space below that you cannot see. The flowers cluster around the edge of the grating, their roots extending down through the gaps, reaching into the darkness, and the smell — mineral, metallic, warm — is strongest here.

The garden is drinking from something down there. Something deep, something old, something that was here before the garden and that the garden has grown down to reach. The flowers are the interface. The pipes are the remnants of the previous system that reached the same thing. The garden has not built a new system; it has grown into the old one, replacing the metal pipes with living wood, replacing the pumps with hydraulic pressure, replacing the machinery with its own body.

It has been doing this for a very long time, and it is in no hurry, and it is not finished.

---

## Rules of the World

This place operates by a set of rules that are not yours.

**The garden is the world.** There is no outside, or if there is, it is so far above and so thoroughly occluded by the canopy that it does not matter. The garden is the floor and the walls and the ceiling and the air and the water and the light. Everything that happens here happens within the garden and because of the garden.

**The garden is patient.** It does not grow quickly. It does not react quickly. The maintenance organisms move slowly. The flowers pulse slowly. The root structures flex slowly. The garden operates on a timescale that makes human lifespans feel like the flicker of a firefly, and it has been doing what it does for long enough that the distinction between *grown* and *built* has ceased to apply.

**The garden metabolizes everything.** Metal, ceramic, stone, machinery — everything that enters the garden is eventually incorporated. The built structures in the upper chambers are being consumed now, slowly, and in the lower chambers, they are nearly gone. In the deepest level, the garden has reached down into something older than itself — a geological feature, an aquifer, a deposit of something it needs — and it is drinking, and it has been drinking for centuries.

**The garden is not hostile.** It does not attack. It does not defend. It does not react to your presence with any more urgency than it reacts to a fallen leaf. It will grow around you if you stand still long enough. It will incorporate you if you die here. But it will not pursue. It does not care. You are below the threshold of its attention, and this is not a comfort — it is simply a fact.

**The garden is strange.** The flowers are not decorative. They are functional, and their function is not one you fully understand. The subsonic pulse they produce is not a byproduct; it is a signal, and the fungal network carries it to every part of the garden's body, and the garden responds — the flexing, the groaning, the hydraulic shifts, the slow rhythmic respiration of the whole structure. The garden is thinking. Not in words, not in concepts, not in any way you would recognize. But something is being computed in the interaction between the flowers and the fungal network and the root structure and the deep water below, and the result is the garden's continued existence, its continued growth, its continued slow replacement of everything around it with itself.

**The garden is not alone in its strangeness.** The deep water — the thing below, the thing the flowers are drinking from — is not ordinary water. It carries something. A mineral, a chemical, an isotope, something that the garden needs and that the flowers concentrate and the fungal network distributes, and without it, the garden could not maintain its size, its complexity, its near-total occupation of this space. The built structures — the pipes, the pumps, the machinery — were here to reach the same thing, and the garden has replaced them because the garden is better at it than they were.

---

## Atmosphere

Standing in the lowest chamber, at the grating of living wood, above the dark water and the deep sound, you feel the following:

Warmth, from below and from the flowers around you. Wetness, on every surface and in the air. Pressure, from the subsonic pulse, rising and falling in your chest. The smell of minerals and hot stone. The sound of the garden breathing, groaning, ticking, all of it compounded into a single overwhelming noise that you have stopped trying to parse. The faint amber glow of the flowers, illuminating nothing beyond themselves. The knowledge — not fear, not awe, something quieter than either — that you are standing inside a living thing that is larger than you can see and older than you can imagine and that it does not know you are here.

The garden breathes. The flowers pulse. The water falls.

The garden does not wait, because waiting implies an expectation, and the garden does not expect. It simply continues, as it has continued, as it will continue, and you are welcome to stay, and you are welcome to leave, and it will not notice either way, and if you stay too long, the moss will grow over your boots and the root structure will grow around your ankles and the maintenance organisms will eventually, gently, without urgency, begin the process of incorporating you into the body of the garden, and it will not be painful, and it will not be quick, and it will not be personal.

It will be slow. It will be patient. It will be complete.

The water falls. The flowers pulse. The garden breathes.

You leave.
