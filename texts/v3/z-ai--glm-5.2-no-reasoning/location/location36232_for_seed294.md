# The Drowned Cathedral Plain

## First impression

Water presses in from every direction. Not the restless, sun-cut water of a surface ocean — this is older. Heavier. It carries the patience of something that has been falling for longer than falling should take. Visibility extends perhaps thirty meters before the suspended mineral haze closes like a curtain, and beyond that curtain there is only the blue-dark suggestion of more distance, more structure, more of whatever this place used to be.

You are deep. The cold confirms it — not the sharp, theatrical cold of ice, but the steady, total cold of depth, the kind that doesn't bite so much as replace your warmth with its own absence so gradually you might not notice until your fingers stop obeying. It comes through the water and through whatever you're wearing and through your skin and settles into the small joints as if it intends to live there.

And yet — this is not the seafloor. The ground beneath you, when you touch it, is dry to the hand in a way that makes no sense. Smooth. Too smooth. The water passes *over* it but does not seem to *wet* it. This is the first wrong thing. There are others.

## The ground

The floor is paved.

Not paved in the casual sense — not stones fitted together, not brickwork, not the work of hands laying individual pieces. This is *monolithic paving*. Great slabs of a material that is not stone and not metal and not glass but carries properties of all three. Dark, like polished basalt, but with a faint translucence at the edges where the dim light catches — you can see *into* it, just a little, as if the material were a frozen dark liquid rather than a solid. Each slab is perhaps twenty meters across, hexagonal, fitted to its neighbors with seams so fine they're nearly invisible. The surface is textured with shallow grooves in patterns that might be decorative, might be functional, might be both — they look like the routing marks on old circuit boards, if circuit boards were the size of city blocks and made for currents no one has named.

The slabs are ancient. Not cracked — the material resists cracking in a way that suggests it was engineered to resist cracking, that its longevity was a specification rather than a hope. But the surfaces are *worn*. Smoothed in the way river stones are smoothed, though no river ran here. The edges between slabs have been rounded by some force that pressed and pressed and pressed over a span of time the word "ancient" can't quite hold. Whatever made these slabs, it made them to last, and they have lasted, and the lasting itself has become a kind of erosion — not the loss of material but the loss of *intent*. The patterns are still there but softer now, like a word repeated too many times. You can see that they meant something once. You cannot see what.

There is no natural substrate beneath. You know this not because you can dig through the paving — you can't, the material refuses tools, refuses fingernails, refuses the idea of penetration entirely — but because at certain points where the slabs have shifted slightly apart, you can look down into the gap and see: more structure. Layers. Tier beneath tier, receding into a darkness that the faint ambient light cannot reach. This place is not built *on* something. It is built *all the way down*.

## The structures

Above the paving, rising from it without seams — as if extruded upward from the same continuous pour — are columns. Not columns in the classical sense, though the word comes closest. They are vertical members, widely spaced, each perhaps forty meters tall, tapering from a base fifteen meters across to a crown of three or four. They are spaced far apart — thirty, fifty, seventy meters between them in no consistent grid. The spacing feels intentional but the logic of it is lost. Some lean. Not from damage but from the slow geological shrug of the underlying structure settling into a final position over epochs. They lean at angles that should look precarious but don't. They lean as if they were always going to lean, as if the lean was accounted for in the design.

The material is the same as the paving — dark, smooth, faintly translucent. But the columns are hollow. You can tell because at certain heights, fractures — not cracks, but *openings*, precise as doorways, though far too small for any door — reveal interiors. Inside each column, descending in a spiral, are steps. Or something like steps — shallow horizontal ridges, evenly spaced, running in a helix down into the interior. The steps are too small for human feet. They were made for something else, or for nothing at all, or they are not steps but grooves, and the resemblance to architecture is a coincidence of geometry.

Between the columns, stretched taut and intact after an age that should have reduced them to sediment, are membranes. That is the only word that fits. They span column to column like the webbing between fingers, thin sheets of a material that is translucent amber where the faint light hits it and opaque where it doesn't. They are not fabric. They are not glass. They flex — slowly, almost imperceptibly — in the current, bowing inward and outward in cycles that take minutes. Breathing. The membranes are *breathing*. Not metabolically. Mechanically, like bellows. They move water. They are still functioning — whatever they were built to do, they are still doing it, blindly, faithfully, long after whatever built them has stopped caring.

The columns and the membranes and the paving are all one system. You begin to understand this not through analysis but through the slow accumulation of pattern: the grooves in the floor lead to the bases of the columns, the hollows of the columns connect to the membranes through small apertures at their crowns, the membranes regulate flow between the columns, and the whole thing moves water in slow, vast, purposeful circuits. It is a machine the size of a valley. It is a machine the size of a cathedral. It is a machine built by hands or forces that thought at this scale — that found it natural to work in forty-meter verticals and twenty-meter hexagons and spans of time that make "maintenance" an irrelevance.

It has no technology. Not in the sense of devices, of circuits, of parts that can be named and replaced. This is not a pump, a valve, a filter. It is a *geometry* — a shape that does work because of its shape, the way a sail does work because of its curve. There is nothing here to break because there is nothing here that was ever separate from anything else. It was poured as one thing. It functions as one thing. When it finally stops, it will stop as one thing.

## The water

The water is not ocean. It is not fresh. It is not brackish. It is a third thing — a mineral solution so saturated with dissolved compounds that it has a viscosity slightly higher than water should, a density that makes movement through it feel like wading through silk. It carries no sediment. Everything that could dissolve already has. It is the end-state of a solvent's long work — water that has done all the dissolving it can do and now simply *holds* what it has taken.

It is cold. Four degrees, perhaps three. Not freezing — but close enough that the cold is a presence, a medium, something you move through rather than something you merely feel. The cold comes up from the depths between the slabs and down from the distant ceiling — is there a ceiling? — and meets in the middle where you are, and it is not hostile but it is *indifferent*, and the indifference is worse.

The water moves. This is the turbulent core of the place — not violence, not storm, but *restlessness*. Currents cross the plain in complex interference patterns. You can see them by the way the mineral haze shifts — denser streams threading through lighter water, lighter water pushing back, the whole volume of the place in constant slow turmoil. The membranes between the columns redirect these currents, channel them, accelerate some and diffuse others, and the columns themselves seem to affect the flow by their mere presence, the way boulders affect a river. The turbulence is not random. It has a structure, or rather it has *many* structures, overlapping, competing, canceling and reinforcing in cycles that take minutes or hours or days. The dynamic of this place is its defining quality. Nothing is still. Nothing is settled. The structures are fixed but the water is not, and the water is the majority of what exists.

There are surges. Periodically — you feel it before you see it, a pressure change in the water against your chest, in your ears — a current will intensify, rush across the plain with sudden purpose, slam against a column, curl around it, rebound, and set up a new pattern of interference that takes the next half hour to unwind. These surges come from somewhere. The gaps between the floor slabs. The hollow interiors of the columns. Somewhere beneath, where the structure descends through layers it is built all the way down into, there are chambers or spaces or voids that periodically expel water with force. The whole place *breathes* — not the membranes alone but the entire structure, inhaling and exhiling mineral water in a rhythm that might be tidal, might be mechanical, might be the residual motion of a process that was started long ago and has not yet run down.

The sound is mostly the water. A low, pervasive susurrus — not the crash or roar of surface water but the deep, continuous murmur of a medium in motion at scale. It is almost below hearing. You feel it in your sternum more than your ears. The surges add a second note: a deep, resonant hum when the current hits a column at the right angle, a frequency that makes the hollow interior ring faintly, like a bell made of dark glass rung by the ocean. These hums are the loudest sounds the place makes. They are not loud. They are simply *present* — audible over the murmur the way a spoken word is audible over breathing.

## The fauna

They are not fish.

They move like fish — or rather, they move the way fish would move if fish were made of different material and had different intentions. They are elongated, perhaps a meter long, tapering at both ends, and they are *translucent* — not transparent, but veiled, as if their bodies were composed of the same mineral water they swim in, thickened slightly, organized into a shape. You can see the faint internal structure when they pass close: not organs, not a skeleton, but a *density gradient* — denser material at the core, diffusing to near-transparency at the edges. They look like lenses with intentions.

There are many of them. Hundreds visible at any time, more suggested by movement in the haze. They school — loosely, in groups of ten or twenty, breaking apart and reforming as the currents push them. They do not fight the current. They *use* it, orienting their bodies to let the water carry them where it will, adjusting with small movements of their posterior ends — not fins, but *ripples*, traveling waves of contraction along their surfaces, like the peristalsis of a gut turned inside out and given ambitions.

They are not electric. They are not luminous. They have no eyes, no mouths, no features of any kind. They are smooth, continuous, and featureless, and yet they are clearly *alive* — they respond to each other, to the currents, to the structure. When a surge comes, they cluster. When the water is calm, they disperse. When two schools meet, there is a pause — a hesitation in the movement of both groups, a moment of suspended drift — and then they merge or separate with what looks uncomfortably like *decision*.

They are not made of meat. You understand this with a certainty that is not entirely rational. They are made of the place — of the water and the minerals and the slow energy of the structure. They are what happens when a system this large and this old and this *active* runs long enough: the patterns of movement and exchange become stable enough to self-perpetuate, to take on a shape that moves through the medium, to become entities. They are not built. They are not evolved. They are *precipitated* — condensed out of the ongoing process like minerals condense out of a saturated solution. The water holds what it holds, and what it holds, eventually, is this: life of a kind that has nothing to do with sunlight or carbon or the warm surface of a planet. Life that belongs to the deep, to the cold, to the ancient machine that is still running.

They avoid you. Not dramatically — they don't flee, don't startle. They simply adjust their trajectories with enough lead time that the avoidance looks like coincidence. You are an obstacle. You are a current they haven't learned. You are a pattern they don't recognize, and they flow around you the way they flow around the columns, with minor corrections and no concern.

There are other forms. Deeper in the haze, larger shapes move — slower, more deliberate, with a mass that suggests a different scale of organization. You catch glimpses: something flat, undulating, three meters across, settling onto a slab and then lifting away. Something that clings to the columns, clustered near the membranes, too still to be alive and too irregular in distribution to be mineral. The ecology here is not rich — not by surface standards — but it is *consistent*. It has found a way. The energy comes from the structure, from the water, from the thermal gradient between the deep layers and the upper. It is not much energy. It is enough.

## The light

There is no sun. There is no surface. The light comes from the structure itself — or rather, from the water as it moves *through* the structure. Where the current passes through the membranes, where it enters the hollow columns, where it rushes through the narrow gaps between slabs — at these points of constriction and acceleration, the mineral water glows. Faintly. A cold, blue-grey luminescence, like the light of phosphorescence but steadier, less alive. The glow is brightest at the points of highest flow and fades to nothing where the water is still. The plain is lit by its own motion.

This means the light is never constant. The shifting currents produce shifting light — a patch of brightness moves across the floor as a surge passes through a membrane overhead, dims, and is replaced by another from a different direction. The columns catch the light and hold it faintly in their translucent material, becoming dim pillars of blue-grey for minutes after the current that lit them has passed. The effect is underwater aurora — dim, silent, sourceless, a light that comes from everywhere and nowhere and makes the whole place feel like the interior of something alive.

At the edges of visibility, the light fails. The haze closes. Beyond the thirty-meter visibility, there is only the suggestion of more columns, more membranes, more structure — the same place, extending. The scale is open. You cannot see the far edge of it, and walking — if walking were possible — would not bring you to an edge in a day's time. This is a region. A territory. A province of the built deep.

## The atmosphere of time

This is the oldest thing about the place. Not the structure — the structure is merely old. The *pattern* is oldest. The water moving through the machine, the machine moving the water, the water precipitating life, the life cycling through the water — this has been happening for so long that the distinction between the structure and the process has dissolved. The columns are not objects in the water. They are *shapes the water takes* — slow shapes, mineral shapes, shapes that last. The fauna are not creatures in the water. They are *movements the water remembers*. The whole place is one thing doing one thing, and it has been doing it since before the word "since" had meaning, and it will go on doing it until the structure finally wears smooth and flat and the currents find their final path and the light goes out.

It is not there yet. The turbulence — the surges, the shifting currents, the constant reconfiguration of the flow — is the sign that the process is still running, still searching for its equilibrium, still *working*. It has not settled. It is not dead. It is not dying. It is in the middle of something so long that the middle looks like an end from one angle and a beginning from another.

The place does not care about you. This is its final quality, the one that sits under all the others: *indifference*. Not hostility. Not welcome. The cold does not care. The water does not care. The columns do not care. The fauna avoid you not out of fear but out of the same principle that makes water avoid a stone — you are a fact of the geometry, and they route around you. You could die here and the currents would move your body the way they move any other density gradient, and eventually the minerals would take you, and you would become — not part of the machine, because the machine does not have parts — but part of the *process*. Another pattern in the water. Another shape the current moves around.

The place was built. Everything in it was built. But what it was built *by* — and what it was built *for* — has been worn away the way the grooves in the floor have been worn away. The intent is gone. Only the shape remains. Only the shape, and the water, and the light that comes from their meeting, and the cold that comes from their depth, and the slow, constant, exhausting *motion* of a system that will not stop because it was never given a stopping condition, that will run and run and run in its ancient, purposeless, beautiful way until the floor is smooth and the columns are stubs and the water finds its final path and the last glow goes out of the deep.
