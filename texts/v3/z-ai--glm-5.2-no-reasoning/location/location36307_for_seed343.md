# The High Canopy Plateau of Ythel

## I. Approach

The climb takes three days from the lowland forests, and on the second morning the character of the world changes. Below, the trees had been trees — recognizable in shape and intent, their trunks rising from soil, their canopy a ceiling. But as the trail steepens past the cloud line, the distinction between forest and ground begins to fail. Roots surface and resurface, braiding over stone until stone itself becomes the exception, a gray remnant showing through like bone through thin skin. By the third morning there is no trail, because there is no ground. Only plant. Only the living mass of it, spreading upward and outward in every direction, and you walk on its back.

You have arrived at the Ythel Plateau.

---

## II. Terrain and Structure

The plateau sits at roughly three thousand meters, though no one has measured it precisely with instruments, and the people who live here do not think in numbers. It is ancient — geologically ancient, so old that the volcanic cones that birthed it have worn to soft mounds, and the basalt that once capped the summit has cracked and crumbled and been swallowed. What remains is a broad, undulating expanse perhaps forty kilometers across, rimmed on most sides by steep declivities where the plateau's edge has eroded into hanging valleys. The erosion is old. Stable. The cliffs have been cliffs for longer than human memory holds, their faces softened by lichens and hanging mosses into something more like draped fabric than broken rock.

But the rock is largely irrelevant now, because the plant matter has overtaken it.

This is not forest. Forest implies trees rising from earth. Here, the entire substrate is living, or recently dead and being consumed by the living. The surface you walk on is a deep mattress of root fiber, decomposing humus, intertwined stem and rhizome, built up over what must be thousands of years — layer on layer on layer, each generation growing from the corpse of the last. In places this living soil is ten meters deep, in places thirty. Beneath it, somewhere, is stone, but you would have to dig for a day to find it, and no one does.

The plateau is not flat. It rolls. Broad swells and shallow depressions, the kind of terrain you cross without noticing the grade until you look back and realize you have descended into a basin. The edges of the plateau drop away in long, vegetated folds — not cliffs exactly, but steep enough that descending means holding onto roots and sliding on your backside through curtains of moss. Water collects in the basins. The whole place is damp — not wet, not drowned, but consistently moist in the way a wrung cloth is moist. Condensation forms on every surface every morning. By midday it has evaporated into a haze that makes the far edges of the plateau shimmer.

The scale is open. From any elevated point — and there are a few, where the root-mass has heaved upward into low ridges — you can see across kilometers of living surface to the plateau's rim. The sky above is wide and pale, the sun diffused through perpetual light cloud cover. You can see weather coming from a long way off: the dark columns of rain that move across the plateau in the afternoons, visible for an hour before they reach you.

And in every direction, across that whole vast surface, there is almost nothing.

---

## III. The Emptiness

This is the strangest thing about Ythel, and it takes time to feel it properly.

The plateau is overwhelmingly alive. Every surface is covered. Every surface grows, or decomposes, or both. And yet the place feels empty. Not because it lacks mass — it is almost entirely mass — but because that mass is homogeneous. The plant life here has achieved a kind of consensus. A single biome, a single strategy, a single organism nearly, spread across forty kilometers and meters deep. Walking across it is like walking across a vast calm sea. There are no landmarks. No clearings, no paths, no boundaries between one zone and another. The same mosses, the same creeping stems, the same low dense mat of vegetation repeats and repeats and repeats.

The emptiness is spatial. The distance between any two distinct *things* — any two features that break the surface — can be enormous. You can walk for an hour and pass nothing taller than your knee. The horizon is flat and green and unbroken. Occasionally a ridge of exposed root fiber rises a meter or two. Occasionally a basin of standing water opens in the surface, dark and still, reflecting the cloud cover. But these features are rare, and the space between them is vast, and the silence of that space — no, not silence, the plateau is never silent, but the *uniformity* of that space — is what settles into your bones.

You feel exposed out there. Not to predators, though there are predators. Exposed to the sky. Exposed to the surface. Exposed to the sheer biological indifference of a place that has solved every problem and is now simply resting in its solution.

---

## IV. The Plant

I call it plant, but it is many plants, and I call it many plants, but it functions as one. The dominant organism — if that word means anything here — is a creeping rhizomatous thing, something like a ground-cover fern but with woody stems and broad, waxy leaves the color of lichen. It spreads horizontally by runners, vertically by rooting its own fallen stems, and in every other direction by sheer persistence. It has covered the plateau the way moss covers a rock. Nothing else competes, because nothing else can gain purchase. The rhizome mat is too dense, too thick, too complete. Seeds of other species land and germinate and die in a season, smothered before they can reach light.

But within the mat, in the layers of decomposing fiber, other things live. Fungi, mostly — pale threads of mycelium running through the humus like veins, occasionally fruiting into small shelf-like brackets on the surface. Bacterial films. Nematodes. Mites. The ecosystem below the surface is complex, but invisible, and you walk on it without seeing it.

Above the surface, the vegetation rarely exceeds knee height. There are exceptions. Around the basins where water collects, the growth thickens into waist-high mounds of moss and root, and in a few sheltered depressions near the plateau's edge, where the clouds hang low and deposit their moisture directly, there are stands of something approaching actual trees — waist-high, then head-high, their trunks no thicker than an arm, their canopies a tangle of small leaves. These stands are maybe a hundred meters across. There are four of them that I have found. They feel like rooms in the empty corridor of the plateau.

The living surface is warm. Not hot — the temperature is mild, almost body-temperature, so that standing still feels like standing in a bath. This is partly the climate, which is warm and humid and still, and partly the composting mass beneath your feet, which generates its own heat. On a still morning, if you crouch and press your palm to the surface, you can feel it: a gentle warmth rising, as if the ground itself were alive and breathing. Which, of course, it is.

## V. The Sound

The first thing every traveler remarks on is the sound. The plateau is deafening.

Not wind, not thunder, not machinery. Insects.

The insect population of Ythel is beyond counting, beyond comprehension, and they are loud. The dominant sound across the entire plateau, day and night, is a sustained, uniform, roaring drone produced by the collective stridulation of what must be billions of individual insects. It is not a chorus — a chorus implies parts, harmonies, variation. This is a wall. A single continuous frequency, or so close to a single frequency that the ear cannot separate its components. It vibrates in the chest. It fills the skull. After an hour it ceases to be heard and becomes instead a condition of being, like air pressure, like gravity. You stop noticing it the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat.

At dawn and dusk the pitch shifts — rises, falls, rises again — as different species take over. There are moments of transition, brief, when two layers overlap and the sound takes on a beating quality, a pulse, and you realize that the uniform drone is actually two or three drones nearly identical in pitch, interfering with each other like waves. Then the transition passes and the sound is flat again, a single oceanic roar, and you forget it.

The insects themselves are mostly invisible. Small. Dark. Swarming in the low vegetation, rising in clouds when disturbed, settling again. They do not bite. They are not interested in you. They are interested in the plant, in the fungi, in each other, in the endless cycles of consumption and decomposition that drive the plateau's biology. You walk through them and they part around you like a current and close behind you and the surface is unchanged.

When rain comes, the insect sound drops — not to silence, but to a lower register, a softer hum, as if the billions of voices had muffled themselves against the wet. The rain itself is loud, drumming on the waxy leaves, pattering on the standing water, hissing on the moss. And beneath both sounds — the muffled insects and the rain — there is a third: a deep, organic creaking from the root mat itself, expanding as it absorbs water, fibers shifting against each other, the whole vast surface of the plateau adjusting its shape by millimeters. The ground moves under your feet when it rains. Not dangerously. Just perceptibly. The plateau is breathing.

## VI. The Fauna

The insects are the sound, but they are not the only life here. The plateau supports an extraordinary density of animal life — extraordinary because the vegetation seems so uniform, so lacking in the structural diversity that usually supports diverse fauna. But the animals here do not need diversity of habitat. They need quantity of food. And the plateau provides quantity beyond measure.

The most visible are the beetles. Huge, slow, palm-sized things with armored shells the color of the moss they live on. They move across the surface in loose processions, dozens of them, grazing on the vegetation, cutting small arcs of leaf and carrying them in their mandibles to concealed entrances in the root mat — tunnels leading down to fungal gardens where they cultivate the decomposing plant matter. Their tunnels riddle the living substrate, and if you walk carefully, you can feel the ground give slightly where a tunnel runs just beneath the surface. These beetles are the primary engineers of the plateau's interior, the ones who pull the surface material down into the depths where it can be decomposed and incorporated.

Above the beetles, the lizards. Small, quick, warm-colored, they dart across the surface hunting the smaller insects. They are everywhere — dozens per square meter in some areas, running between the stems, pausing on exposed roots to do push-ups and display their throats. They are the visible motion of the plateau, the thing that makes it feel alive when the plant surface itself seems static. And above the lizards, the birds.

The birds are the reason the sound is bearable. They call — sharp, bright, whistling calls that pierce the insect drone and give the ear something to hold onto. They are small, finch-like, flocking in the hundreds, landing on the surface and probing the vegetation for insects, rising in clouds when disturbed. Near the standing water, taller birds — heron-like, stilt-legged — stand motionless for long minutes before striking into the shallow basins for amphibians. Their calls are harsh, croaking, a different texture entirely from the finches, and at dusk the two sounds — the finch-whistle and the heron-croak and the insect-drone beneath them — layer into something almost orchestral.

There are mammals, but you do not see them often. Something small and rust-colored moves through the vegetation at the edges of the tree-stands, leaving trails of parted stems. Something larger — a heavy bodied, low-slung creature with dark fur and a long snout — roots through the surface at night, leaving cratered patches where it has torn the root mat open. You find these patches in the morning, fresh, the exposed humus steaming faintly in the cool air. The animal itself is gone. It has a territory, perhaps, that covers the whole plateau, and you are simply inside it.

The predators are here too. A low, sinuous, weasel-like thing hunts the lizards, moving through the vegetation like water through grass. I have seen one, once, crossing an open patch of root — it paused, looked at me with an expression of absolute indifference, and vanished. And there are raptors — broad-winged, dark, riding the thermals at the plateau's edge where the warm air rises from the surface. They hunt the finches and the small mammals, stooping from above with a speed that seems impossible against the plateau's overwhelming stillness.

The fauna is overwhelming not in its size but in its totality. Every layer of the ecosystem is saturated. The plant provides. The insects consume. The lizards consume the insects. The birds consume both. The mammals consume the plant and the insects. The predators consume everything smaller than themselves. And all of it — all of it — dies and falls into the root mat and is decomposed and incorporated and rises again as plant. The plateau is a single vast body, eating itself, and the animals are its blood.

## VII. Water

Water on the plateau is a presence but not a dominant one. There are no rivers. No streams. No lakes of any size. What there is, is accumulation.

The basins — the shallow depressions in the plateau's rolling surface — collect water from rain and condensation. Some of these basins hold water year-round: dark, still pools, maybe ten meters across, maybe waist-deep at their centers, their surfaces covered in a film of duckweed so thick it looks like solid ground until you step on it and sink to your knee. The water is warm and smells of tannin and decomposition, sweet and dark, like tea brewed too long. Small organisms live in it — larvae, worms, a species of blind amphibian that I have never seen but whose calls I hear at night, a low rhythmic thumping from the direction of every pool, as if someone were drumming on the water's surface with their palm.

Other basins are seasonal, filling with rain and drying to damp patches within days. The vegetation around these seasonal pools is different — denser, greener, more lush — as if the plants here have learned to take advantage of the brief moisture and are poised, always, to grow. When rain falls, they grow visibly. You can almost watch them extend.

The water comes from above, mostly. The plateau sits in a band of frequent cloud cover, and the clouds deposit moisture directly on the vegetation — not as rain, but as condensation, a constant wetting of every surface, every leaf, every stem. In the early morning, if you stand still, you can hear it: a soft, continuous dripping as the condensation gathers and runs off the waxy leaves, down the stems, into the root mat. The plateau waters itself, in a sense. The moisture lands on the plants, runs down to the surface, is absorbed into the living substrate, and is held there.

At the edges of the plateau, where the ground drops away, this moisture emerges. Springs and seeps along the rim, the water running down the steep slopes in thin films, feeding the lowland forests below. The plateau is a water-tower, in its way. A vast sponge sitting at altitude, catching moisture from the clouds and releasing it slowly downward. The civilizations below — such as they are — depend on this, though they may not know it.

## VIII. Built Environment

There are people here. Not many. A scattering.

The structures are built from the only material available: the living substrate itself. In a few places, near the plateau's edge where the root mat is thickest and the material has some structural integrity, people have cut blocks of compressed root-fiber and stacked them into low walls. The walls are not tall — chest-height at most — and they enclose small spaces, maybe three meters by four, roofed with woven mats of dried stem and leaf. These are shelters. They are not houses. They are places to sleep, to store tools, to retreat from the rain.

The construction is primitive, but not without skill. The blocks are cut with stone tools — the plateau has no metal, and the people here have not developed metallurgy, though they know of it from below — and they are cut with an understanding of the material's grain, its moisture, its tendency to expand and contract. A wall built well will stand for years. A wall built poorly will warp and collapse in a season as the living root-fiber inside the blocks continues to grow, pushing the structure apart from within.

The people here have solved this problem in an ingenious way. They do not kill the root-fiber when they cut it. They prune it. The blocks are still alive, still growing, and the builders shape their walls to accommodate and direct that growth, training the roots to bind the blocks together, the new growth filling the gaps, the wall becoming more solid over time rather than less. The result is something between construction and topiary — a built structure that is also a living plant, a wall that is also a hedge, a shelter that grows.

There are perhaps thirty of these structures across the plateau, clustered in small groups of three or four near the tree-stands and the permanent water. The population is small — I would guess fewer than two hundred people on the entire plateau. They live spread out, each family group in its own cluster of structures, separated by kilometers of empty surface. They see each other rarely. They do not seem to mind this.

The structures are the only visible sign of human presence. There are no paths — the vegetation is too low to need them, and the surface is uniform enough that you simply walk in the direction you want to go. There are no monuments, no markers, no fences, no boundaries. The plateau does not lend itself to ownership. It is too vast, too uniform, too empty. You take what you need from where you are and move on.

## IX. Tools and Craft

The people of Ythel have stone tools. Specifically, they have a type of chipped basalt — brought up from the lowlands, since the plateau's own stone is buried too deep to access — that they work into small hand-axes, scrapers, and cutting tools. The tools are simple but well-made, the edges sharp, the ergonomics of the handles considered. They use them for cutting vegetation, preparing food, and working the root-fiber into building blocks.

They also have tools of plant material. The woody stems of the rhizome can be split and shaped into stakes, pegs, and small implements. A strip of the outer bark, dried, makes a serviceable cord. The broad leaves can be woven into mats, baskets, and rain-cloaks. There is a kind of woven basket-thing, large and deep, that they use for collecting insects — they sweep it through the low vegetation like a net, gathering the swarming creatures, which they then roast on flat stones over small fires.

Fire. They have fire, but it is a precarious thing here. The vegetation is too damp to burn well, and the root mat, being alive, resists fire stubbornly. They build their fires on flat stones, kept in shallow pits lined with more stone, and they feed them with dried material brought from the tree-stands or dried in the sun. The fires are small. They are for cooking and for light, not for warmth — the plateau is warm enough that warmth is not needed. At night, the little glowing pits dot the plateau's surface, visible from a long way across the flat expanse, lonely stars brought to earth.

They have no pottery. No metal. No woven cloth except what they make from plant fiber, which is rough and not very durable. They have no glass, no leather, no paper. What they have is the plant, and the stone, and the animals, and the water, and each other, and the enormous empty living surface of the plateau, and the sound, the sound, the sound.

## X. Atmosphere

The light on the plateau is strange only in its consistency. The cloud cover — light, high, perpetual — diffuses the sun into an even, shadowless illumination. There are no sharp shadows. No contrast between light and dark. Everything is lit equally, from all directions, and the result is a flattening of depth and texture that makes distance hard to judge. The far edge of the plateau, visible from any slight elevation, seems closer than it is. The surface, stretching away, seems to curve upward at its edges, an optical illusion produced by the even light and the low horizon.

At dawn and dusk, the light shifts. The clouds take color — soft pink, soft orange — and the entire plateau is briefly, gently tinted, as if someone had washed it in diluted watercolor. These moments are the most beautiful I have seen on Ythel. The green surface, the dark water of the basins, the distant tree-stands, all of them softened and colored, and the insect-drone rising and falling in its daily transition, and the air still and warm and damp on your skin.

Night is dark but not black. The cloud cover reflects what little light there is — firelight, starlight filtered through clouds — and the surface is dimly visible, a gray-green darkness, the vegetation a soft texture underfoot. The insects are quieter at night but not silent. The amphibians in the pools begin their thumping. Occasionally a bird calls — a single sharp note, repeated, as if lost. The plateau feels larger at night. The emptiness expands. You lie in your shelter and the walls are alive around you, growing, and the surface outside is alive, and the sound is alive, and you are a small, warm, temporary thing in the middle of it, and the plateau does not care, and the plateau does not notice, and the plateau goes on.

## XI. Rules of the World

There are rules here, though no one has written them down.

The first rule is that the surface holds. The root mat is dense and stable, and despite being alive and growing, it supports the weight of a walking human without giving way. There are soft spots — near the basins, where the saturation is highest, and in the seasonal depressions after rain — but these are manageable. You learn to feel the surface through your feet, the way you learn to feel ice. The surface speaks. It tells you where to step.

The second rule is that nothing here is permanent except the plateau itself. The structures grow and decay. The tools break. The water pools fill and dry. The insects die in their billions and are replaced by their billions. The people live and die and their shelters, untended, are swallowed by the surface within a decade. The plateau abides. Everything on it is temporary.

The third rule is that the plateau provides. There is food here — insects, lizards, the occasional bird, the edible inner stems of certain plants — and there is water and there is material for shelter and there is warmth. But the provision is not generous. It is not a feast. It is a subsistence, and it requires knowledge to access. You must know which insects are edible and which make you sick. You must know which pools are safe to drink from. You must know how to read the weather, how to find your way across the featureless surface, how to live in a place that does not care whether you live.

The fourth rule is that the sound never stops. You do not get used to it. You integrate it. It becomes part of your body, part of your thinking, and when you leave the plateau — if you leave — its absence is deafening. You will miss it. You will lie in silence in the lowlands and feel the hole where the sound was and you will want to go back.

---

## XII. The Feeling of the Place

Ythel is a place at the end of its story. Not dying — it is deeply, overwhelmingly alive — but finished. The geological processes that shaped it have run their course. The biological processes that covered it have reached equilibrium. The human processes that touch it are small, light, transient. Nothing here is striving. Nothing here is becoming. The plateau has become. It is what it is, and it has been what it is for a very long time, and it will continue to be what it is for a very long time to come.

This is the feeling of ancient places. Not mystery, not grandeur, not awe — those are feelings of encountering something beyond your understanding. This is something else. This is the feeling of encountering something that has finished understanding itself. The plateau knows what it is. It does not need you. It does not need anything.

And yet it is not hostile. It is not cold. It is warm — literally warm, beneath your feet. It is damp, softly, gently damp. It is alive in every fiber, every cell, every surface. It hums with life, roars with life, drowns in its own life. It simply does not need *your* life.

You walk across it, and the surface holds, and the insects drone, and the lizards dart, and the birds whistle, and the clouds drift, and the water pools, and the root mat grows, and the plateau goes on, and on, and on, and you are here, and then you are not, and the plateau does not notice, and the sound never stops.
