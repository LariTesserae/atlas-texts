# The Springs

The road comes in from the north and goes nowhere after that. It was laid down for reasons no one presently living in the settlement can name — some calculation about freight, about access, about the logic of building where heat and water both rise to the surface. The asphalt has crumbled into a pale gravel track by the time it reaches the first structures, and beyond the structures it dissolves entirely into a mineral crust that looks like road but isn't. Someone has left marker posts, metal stakes driven into the crust at intervals, their orange paint peeling. The posts suggest the road might have continued once. Or was meant to.

This is a settlement of maybe three hundred, though no one is sure because the counting changes with the season and with the movement of the water. It is called the Springs, or simply the Springs, the definite article dropped long ago the way place-names erode through daily use. The town sits in a shallow basin, a wide heat-dimple in the earth, ringed by low ridges of fractured basalt that break the horizon into a ragged, uneven line. The basin floor is flat or nearly so — a plain of hardpan and mineral deposits that extends perhaps two kilometers in every direction before the ridges rise. From any elevation within the settlement you can see the entire town, and you can see what surrounds it, and the sense is that the town is small here, not because it was poorly conceived but because it was never meant to be large.

The heat determines everything. The air is hot in the way that is not aggressive but inescapable — not the dry punishing heat of a desert at noon but something deeper, a humid warmth that sits on the skin and stays. The temperature hovers around thirty-seven degrees most of the year and creeps toward forty in the long afternoons. The heat does not come from the sun, though the sun is present and pale behind a permanent high haze that scatters light into a flat, directionless gray. The heat comes from below. The ground itself is warm. You feel it through the soles of your boots if you stand still too long, a low steady warmth that rises through the hardpan and through the mineral crust and through the cracked concrete pads the buildings sit on. At night the ground stays warm and the air cools by a few degrees and the difference produces fog — not dramatic fog, not a rolling bank, but a low persistent mist that forms in the hours between midnight and dawn and lies across the basin like something that has settled there.

The water is the reason for the town and the cause of its character. There are springs. A line of them, following a geological fault that cuts roughly east-west across the basin floor, surfacing as pools and seeps and slow upward flows that have been building their mineral terraces for longer than anyone has been here to watch. The water is hot — fifty, sixty degrees at the source — and it cools as it spreads across the mineral crust, forming shallow channels that cross the basin in branching patterns, always shifting, always finding new paths. The water carries minerals. Calcium carbonate, silica, sulfur compounds. It deposits these as it cools and evaporates, building terraces and rims and pale platforms that look almost constructed. The ground in places is a layered thing: original basalt, then mineral deposits meters thick, then the hardpan crust on top. The crust is white or pale gray or faintly yellow, and it is rough and textured, etched by the flow of water and by the wind, and in places it is fragile — you learn quickly where you can walk and where you cannot, where the crust will hold and where it will break and your foot will go through into warm water and soft mineral mud below.

There are no plants. This is the first thing a visitor notices, or rather it is the thing that takes longest to notice because its absence is so complete there is nothing to catch the eye. No grass, no shrubs, no trees, no moss, no lichen. The ground is mineral. The air is mineral. The surfaces are mineral. The only colors are whites and grays and pale yellows and the orange-brown of iron deposits and the faint greenish tint that sulfur gives to some of the pools. Against this palette the settlement's structures stand out with the starkness of things that do not belong — though they have been here long enough, some of them, to have acquired their own mineral coating, a fine pale crust on walls and roofs that makes the older buildings look almost geological, as though they are a variety of formation the earth produces here.

The buildings are concrete and metal and glass, or they were. The concrete has held. The metal has rusted where the water touches it and held where it does not. The glass has cracked in some places and in other places has developed a milky opacity from the mineral deposits that form on its surface over years. The structures are low — one or two stories, nothing taller — and they are arranged in a rough grid that follows the old road alignment, though the grid has been adapted to avoid the springs and the active water channels. Some buildings are empty. Not abandoned in the dramatic sense — no broken doors, no looting — but simply vacated, their interiors slowly accumulating mineral dust and the faint sulfur smell that pervades everything. These empty buildings have their own quality of silence, which is different from the silence of the occupied buildings, and a person who has lived here long enough can tell which is which from the outside, by the quality of the light in the windows and the particular shade of crust on the walls.

The industry of the Springs is extraction. Not mining — nothing is dug from the ground. The extraction is of what the water brings. The springs carry dissolved minerals up from deep sources, and the settlement exists to capture these. The water cools, the minerals precipitate, and what remains is a crystalline residue that is scraped up and loaded into containers and shipped out on the one road. The work is simple and slow and requires patience more than strength. It requires attention to the water — its temperature, its flow rate, its mineral content, which changes seasonally and with the patterns of deeper geological processes no one fully understands. There is a foreman, or there was, and there is a small office near the main spring where records are kept in ledgers that have been maintained for decades, the handwriting changing every few years as one record-keeper replaces another.

The machinery is old but functional. Pumps, where the water needs to be moved. Valves, where the flow needs to be controlled. Pipes — metal, some of them, and some newer, a heavy plastic that resists the mineral buildup better. The pumps run on electricity from a generator that sits in a low concrete building at the edge of town, burning fuel brought in on the road. The generator is loud, or it was, but it has been running so long that its sound has become part of the silence here — not absent but underneath, a low vibration more felt than heard, and when it occasionally stops for maintenance the silence that follows is not more silent but different, emptier, as though a layer has been removed and what is left is the raw acoustic texture of the place.

Because the place is quiet. This is the second thing a visitor notices, after the absence of plants. The sounds that exist are sparse and each one is distinct. The water, where it flows in the open channels, makes a sound — not a rushing but a continuous soft sliding, the sound of something moving slowly over a smooth surface. Where the water surfaces at the springs there is sometimes a gentle bubbling, a glub-glub that is almost comic in its regularity, like a pot on a stove. The wind is present but light, moving across the basin without obstruction, carrying nothing — no sand, no dust, no pollen — and it makes sound only when it passes through the metal structures and the gaps between buildings, a low moaning that is not eerie but simply the sound of air in a pipe. The generator, underneath everything. Footsteps on the mineral crust, which has a specific crunch, a dry sound like walking on packed snow. And voices, when people speak, which carry clearly in the clean air and are absorbed by nothing — no vegetation to muffle, no soft surfaces, just hard mineral ground and hard concrete walls and the open sky.

The animal life is the surprise.

There are no large animals here. No mammals, no birds, nothing with a spine. The heat and the minerals and the absence of plant life would seem to exclude everything, and yet the Springs is full of life of a particular kind. The springs themselves — the warm pools and seeps and slow channels — are thick with organisms. Thermophilic bacteria, archaean colonies, microbial mats that form layered structures in the water and along its edges. These are not visible as individuals but as masses: colors in the water, textures on the submerged surfaces. The hottest pools, near the springs' sources, are clear or faintly blue, too hot for most life. But as the water cools, moving outward through the channels, the colors appear. A pale orange band where the temperature drops below seventy degrees. A yellow zone, slightly farther out. A range of greens — some vivid, some muted — where different communities of organisms establish themselves at different temperatures. At the coolest margins, where the water is merely warm, the mats are thick and complex, layered communities of organisms that have been growing and competing and building for longer than the settlement has existed.

The microbial mats are alive in a way that is visible and continuous. They move, slowly, as the water shifts its channels and the temperatures change. They die back in one area and surge in another. They build small structures of their own — tiny terraces and rims of biological origin, distinct from but continuous with the mineral terraces, the living and the mineral formations merging at their edges so that in some places you cannot tell where the rock ends and the biology begins. The mats also produce gas — small bubbles that rise from their surfaces and break quietly, releasing a faint sulfur smell that mixes with the mineral scent of the water. At certain times of day, in certain light, the surfaces of the cooler pools shimmer with these rising bubbles, and the shimmer is alive in a way that the mineral pools are not.

But the microbial mats are not what makes the animal life overwhelming. That distinction belongs to the flies.

There are flies. There are more flies than anything else. The springs and their mineral margins support a population of organisms that can only be called staggering in its density. Ephydrid flies — brine flies, thermal flies, flies that have adapted to conditions where no other insect survives — breed in the warm mineral water and along its edges. They are small, dark, dense. They coat the surfaces near the water. They coat the ground in patches where the mineral crust is damp. They rise in clouds when disturbed, and settle again. The sound of them is a low hum, a vibration that is just at the threshold of hearing, and it is always present during the warmer hours, and at its peak it fills the air the way the heat fills the air — not from a direction but from everywhere.

The flies do not bite. They are not interested in people or in blood. They are interested in the mineral deposits, the microbial mats, the microenvironments along the water's edge where they lay their eggs and where their larvae develop in water that would kill any other insect. They are a nuisance and a constant and they have been here longer than the settlement and they will be here after it is gone. The people of the Springs have made their peace with them. Screens on the windows. Patterns of movement that avoid the densest concentrations. An acceptance that the flies are part of the air here the way the heat and the sulfur smell are part of the air. Visitors never accept this. Visitors wave their arms and gag and leave quickly. The people who stay have stopped noticing.

There are other organisms, less visible. Nematodes in the microbial mats, feeding on the bacteria. A species of mite that lives on the fly larvae. A kind of worm, pale and thin, that drifts in the cooler pools and is eaten by nothing and seems to serve no purpose. These are the herbivores and the carnivores of the Springs — a food web with no plants, built entirely on the chemical energy the springs bring up from below. It is a complete ecosystem, tiny and strange and functional, and it has been running for thousands of years, entirely independent of the sun.

The dynamic of the place is the dynamic of water and mineral and heat. The springs shift. Not often, not dramatically, but over the years the channels change, new seeps open, old ones close. A pool that was clear becomes cloudy. A terrace that was growing stops. A new outlet appears ten meters from the old one and the water divides its flow and the mineral deposits begin building in a new place. The people of the Springs monitor this, not scientifically but practically. The evaporation basins need to be fed. The channels need to be maintained. When a spring shifts, the channels are re-dug, the basins re-routed. There is a quality of patience in this work that matches the landscape — an acceptance of slow change, of processes that operate on geological time but must be responded to on human time.

The town is not dying. The town is not thriving. The town is continuing. The extraction work provides enough to justify the road and the fuel shipments and the generator and the presence of people. The buildings that are occupied are maintained in a practical way — repairs when something breaks, replacement when something cannot be repaired, no improvements, no expansions. The buildings that are empty are left empty. There is no plan to fill them and no plan to demolish them. They are simply passing through a phase, and the phase may last decades, and the mineral crust is slowly building on their walls, and at some point they will be more geological than architectural, and at some later point they will be indistinguishable from the terrain, and this is not a tragedy but a process, and the people who live here understand this because they live inside the same process.

The light is the thing that stays with you. The permanent high haze — not clouds, not fog, just a diffuse moisture in the upper atmosphere that catches and scatters the light — produces a flat illumination that has no source and no shadow. Everything is lit equally and nothing is lit well. The mineral crust, which is white and reflective, adds to this, bouncing the light upward so that the undersides of things are lit too, and the overall effect is of being inside a luminous sphere, softly bright, featureless. At dawn and dusk the haze takes on color — first a dull gold, then a rose, then a pale violet — and these colors are reflected in the pools and on the wet surfaces and for a short time the Springs becomes beautiful in a way that has nothing to do with what is built or what is alive here but is only about the light and the water and the particular geometry of this shallow basin under this particular sky.

At night the haze becomes a different thing. It holds the darkness near the ground, and the stars, if there are stars above it, are not visible. The darkness is not absolute — the mineral surfaces are faintly luminescent in some way that has to do with the bacterial activity on their surfaces, or with the mineral content itself, or with something else — and on the darkest nights the ground glows very faintly, a pale cold light that is barely enough to walk by but that gives the basin a quality of being lit from within, as though the earth here is generating its own small light, private and steady and not meant for human eyes.

The Springs is not a place that invites. It is a place that is. It does not need to be seen or known or described. It runs its processes. Its water flows and deposits and shifts. Its organisms live and die in their narrow zones. Its people work and sleep and eat and maintain their structures against the slow mineralization that touches everything. The road goes out the way it came in, and the settlement continues, and the springs continue, and this is not a story but a condition, and the condition is stable, and the condition is warm.
