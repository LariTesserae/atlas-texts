# The Ascendant Mill Town of Verkhol-Above

## Where the World Hasn't Finished Deciding What It Is

The first thing you notice is the sound — not loud in the way of collision or explosion, but loud the way a held breath is loud when the whole room is listening. A low, continuous mechanical drone rises from somewhere beneath the town, vibrating up through boot soles and into the marrow. It is the sound of engines turning over slowly, perpetually, the sound of a heart that never quite settles into rhythm. Underneath that: the drip and trickle of water finding its way through new stone. And underneath *that*, something harder to name — a tonal hum that seems to come from no single direction, as though the air itself is resonating at a frequency just below the threshold of language.

Verkhol-Above sits at an elevation that should not support a town of this size. The air is thin enough that visitors feel it in their fingertips, a faint tingling numbness that never quite graduates to real danger but never quite fades. The peaks surrounding the settlement are not finished. That is the only word for them. They are *finished* in the sense that stone exists, that mass is present, that angles have been struck, but they feel provisional — like a sculptor's rough block before the chisel work begins. The rock here is a pale, fibrous granite veined with threads of something darker, almost metallic, that catches what little light reaches this altitude and gives it back in dull, oil-slick iridescence. The formations are sharp, incomplete. Cliffs rise and then stop at odd heights, as though truncated mid-thought. Arches begin and don't end in a pillar. Stone bends in directions that stone, by the inheritance of its own physics, should not bend. There is a rawness to the geology — everything still becoming, still deciding. You get the sense that if you left for a year and returned, the mountains might have shifted. Not collapsed. *Shifted*. Reconsidered themselves.

## The Architecture of Unfinished Industry

The town clings to the eastern face of the largest formation, a sprawling complex of terraced platforms, suspended walkways, and squat industrial buildings connected by a lattice of pipes and cable conduits that sag between structures like the webbing of some enormous, methodical spider. The construction is organized — deliberate — but follows a logic that prioritizes function over any concession to symmetry or aesthetic. Buildings are stacked where the rock allowed stacking. Bridges span where gaps required spanning. The whole settlement has the feel of something assembled in response to constant, quiet emergency.

The structures themselves are built from the same pale granite of the mountain, cut and fitted without mortar, held together by iron clamps and the simple stubbornness of mass. Many of the buildings share walls with the raw rock face itself, their back walls simply the mountain, untrimmed, still bearing the rough tool marks of whatever cut the rest away. Windows are narrow, vertical slits — not defensive, just practical. Glass is a rarity here. Most openings are covered with oiled canvas or, in the more established structures, panes of a cloudy, locally-made fused mineral that transmits light as a diffuse grey glow, never revealing what lies outside with any clarity.

Smokestacks rise from the lower district in a row of dark, slightly crooked fingers. They release not smoke but steam — thick, pale, slow-moving clouds that drift laterally across the town rather than rising, defeated by the altitude and the strange, heavy air. The steam carries a smell: wet iron, mineral water, something vegetal and sweet underneath, like crushed ferns. The factories it issues from are pellet mills, ore refineries, and — most importantly — the Great Pumps.

## The Pumps and the Water

Water is the reason Verkhol-Above exists, and water is its central problem.

The settlement occupies a narrow altitudinal band where moisture from the lower valleys condenses against the rising stone. Clouds snag here. Fog forms in the late afternoon and persists through the night, thick enough to taste — a metallic, cold wetness that beads on every surface. By morning, every railing, every rooftop, every exposed pipe is running with condensation. The town collects it. Gutters and collection channels line every walkway, feeding into cisterns that sit beneath the lower terraces like buried organs. This is the primary water supply: not rain, not rivers, but the slow, patient accumulation of fog.

But the Great Pumps are what make the town possible at *this* scale. Three massive steam-driven engines sit at the base of the settlement, each one built into a shaft that descends — nobody is entirely sure how far. The shafts were already there when the first settlers arrived. Bore-holes, perfectly circular, lined with the same dark, metallic mineral that veins the mountain's granite. The Pumps draw water up from somewhere far below — an aquifer, a subterranean river, something else — and the water they produce is wrong.

Not wrong in a way that is immediately apparent. It is clear, cold, potable. It does not make anyone ill. But it is heavier than it should be. You feel it in the mouth, a density that lingers. It pours strangely — slow, almost gelatinous, as though it has to be convinced to leave its container. And it does not freeze, even at temperatures where it should. The cisterns of pump-water sit at a steady, liquid 4 degrees regardless of the ambient cold. People have learned not to ask questions about it. They drink it. They run it through the boilers. They use it for the steam that powers the engines that drive the pumps that bring up more of it. The cycle is slow, mechanical, patient. It has been running for decades. Nobody has ever been to the bottom of the shafts.

The sound of the Pumps is the sound of the town. That low, turning drone. That held breath.

## The Vegetation

Where the condensation collects, life follows. The mountain around Verkhol is, by the standards of this altitude, conspicuously green. Not the green of lowland forests — nothing so confident. This is a creeping, clinging, opportunistic green. Mosses that coat the north-facing stone in thick, wet carpets of grey-green and deep emerald. Lichens that spread across the rooftops in slow, encroaching maps of orange and pale yellow. Ferns — small, hardy, preternaturally lush — that grow in the sheltered crevices where the rock has cracked and the condensation pools. They are not sparse. Where the moisture is, they *thrive*. In the narrow gutters, in the shadowed spaces between buildings, along the collection channels where overflow runs constant, the ferns grow in dense, fractal profusion, their fronds unfurling in slow spirals that seem to take pleasure in the act.

There are no trees. The altitude is too extreme, the soil too thin, the rock too unfinished. But in place of trees, there are the Columns — towering formations of what appears to be calcified vegetable matter, rising from the lower slopes in irregular clusters. They are the color of old bone, tapered, hollow, and they hum. Not audibly, not quite. But if you stand close to one, you feel it in your teeth, a vibration that syncs, briefly, with your own pulse before drifting out of phase. The Columns are alive, or were, or are in the process of becoming. Botanists who have studied them cannot agree. They grow at a rate of perhaps a centimeter per decade. They are not petrified wood. They are not mineral deposits. They are something that has no analogue in any taxonomy that was designed to describe a world that behaves itself.

The vegetation does not enter the town. It respects some invisible boundary that the residents do not acknowledge but the plants seem to. Moss grows on the outer walls. Ferns cluster at the edges of walkways. But within the town proper — within the grid of buildings and platforms and pipes — the stone is bare. This is not due to any maintenance effort. The residents do not clear the growth. It simply does not come.

## The Fauna

What lives here, lives in abundance.

The first creatures any visitor encounters are the hopper-rats — small, six-legged rodents the color of wet charcoal, with flat, wide feet and eyes that reflect light in a shade of green that is not found anywhere else in the local palette. They are everywhere. Underfoot. In the walls. On the pipes, where they run in single-file processions that stretch the length of the settlement, following the warmth of the conduits. They are not pests. They are, as much as anything here, infrastructure. The residents leave scraps for them in designated areas, and in return, the hopper-rats keep the smaller insects in check and — though no one will say this directly — seem to *know* things. They stop moving when the Pumps change rhythm. They mass at the edges of town before a fog rolls in. They avoid certain corridors, certain buildings, and the residents have learned to avoid them too.

Above, in the permanent overcast, fly the kettle-birds. They are not birds. They have the silhouette of birds — wings, body, a head that darts and tilts — but they are too large, too slow, and their wings do not flap. They ride the air currents above the town in wide, patient spirals, turning in the dim light like the hands of separate clocks, and they make no sound. Not silent — *soundless*. When a kettle-bird passes directly overhead, the ambient noise of the town drops, as though the bird has eaten it. People who have stood beneath one describe a sudden, absolute stillness that lasts only as long as the bird is above them, and then the drone of the Pumps returns as if it never left. The birds are not common, but they are not rare. You see them every day. You try not to stand beneath them.

In the rock itself, in the cracks and the unfinished crevices where the stone is still becoming, live the borers — creatures that may be insects, may be crustaceans, may be something that predates both categories. They are the color of the granite they inhabit, and they are almost impossible to see until they move. They are the size of a human hand, flat, articulated, and they grind slowly through the soft veins of metallic mineral, consuming it, processing it, and excreting a fine, dark powder that the residents collect and sell to the refineries as a crude ore. The borers are the basis of the local economy, and they are treated with a respect that borders on reverence. There are more of them than anything else. The mountain, if you listen closely enough, is *full* of them — a constant, faint, grinding murmur beneath the Pumps, beneath the wind, beneath everything.

## The Dimness

Light here is a negotiation, not a given.

The altitude places Verkhol-Above within a permanent cloud layer. The sky, when it can be seen at all, is a uniform grey-white that offers no indication of sun position or time of day. Most of the time, the sky cannot be seen. Fog sits on the town like a lid. The light that reaches the settlement is diffuse, directionless, a flat, even illumination that casts no shadows and gives every surface the same muted, pearlescent quality. It is not dark. It is *dim* — the word is precise. There is enough light to see. There is never enough light to see *well*. Edges blur. Distances become uncertain. The far end of a walkway might be ten meters away or thirty; the fog and the flat light conspire to make the distinction meaningless.

At night — and night here is determined by clock, not by sky, since the light does not change — the town illuminates itself with electric lamps wired along the major walkways and at the entrances to the industrial buildings. The light is a cold, blue-white that makes the granite look like wet chalk and makes the steam from the stacks glow faintly, spectrally, as it drifts. The lamps flicker. Not unpredictably — there is a pattern to it, a slow, rhythmic pulse that some residents claim syncs with the Pumps and others claim syncs with the Columns and others claim is just the generators being old. The flicker is not dramatic. It is just enough to make you notice that the light is not constant, that even here, in this place of machines and stone, nothing is steady.

## The Weird

There is a building in the middle district that the residents call the Fold House. It was built forty years ago as a boiler room. It is still a boiler room. The boilers work. Steam rises from the pipes. Workers enter and leave on shifts. But the interior of the Fold House does not obey the geometry of its exterior. The building, from outside, is a cube of perhaps eight meters per side. Inside, the far wall is further than eight meters. Not by much. Not so much that you would notice on a casual visit. But if you pace it — and some visitors do, unable to shake the sense that something is *off* — the interior is longer than the exterior allows. The discrepancy is consistent. It has been measured. It does not increase. It simply *is*. The residents do not discuss it. The workers who staff the Fold House have developed a habit of not looking at the far wall directly, of keeping their attention on their immediate tasks, of treating the room as though it is exactly the size it should be. This appears to work.

There are other such places. A stairway in the upper district that descends to a landing, turns, and arrives at a floor it should not reach. A stretch of pipe that, when followed, passes through a wall and emerges from a different wall on a different terrace, with no visible connection between them. A corridor in the refinery building that echoes wrong — shout into it, and the echo returns a half-second too late, in a tone slightly lower than the original sound, as though the corridor is repeating your voice from somewhere deeper than its own length.

None of these are dramatic. None of them are dangerous, as far as anyone knows. They are simply *incorrect* — small, persistent errors in the world's own accounting, as though the unfinished nature of the mountain has begun to infect the structures built upon it. The residents live with them the way they live with the fog and the dim light and the heavy water. They are features of the landscape. They are *here*.

## The Pace of Things

Nothing in Verkhol-Above happens quickly.

The Pumps turn at a rate that seems glacial — each cycle of the piston taking perhaps thirty seconds, the steam building and releasing in a long, slow exhalation that shakes the pipes but does not rattle them. The fog rolls in over the course of hours and sits for the rest of the day. The ferns unfurl over weeks. The hopper-rats move in quick darts, but even their quickness feels contained by the larger slowness — like fish moving inside a body of water that itself barely moves. The borers grind through stone at a rate that would make watching them an exercise in patience even by geological standards. The town's economy turns on shipments that arrive every two months, carried up the switchback trail by mule teams that themselves move with the unhurried, inevitable determination of processes that do not recognize the concept of haste.

Even the weirdness is slow. The Fold House has not grown. The wrong-echo corridor has not changed. The geometry errors are static, as though whatever caused them happened once, briefly, and then the world caught itself and held still. If reality is breaking down here, it is breaking down at a rate that makes the word "breakdown" seem too urgent. It is more like *settling* — the way a foundation settles, the way stone under pressure deforms over centuries. The world here is settling into a shape that is not quite the one it was intended to have, and it is doing so with the same patience as everything else.

## What It Is Like to Be There

You stand on the main walkway of the middle district. The fog is in. It sits against your skin, cold and damp, not quite wet. The granite beneath your feet is slick with condensation. To your left, the town descends in terraces, pipes and platforms and narrow buildings layered down the mountain face, steam rising from the lower stacks in slow, horizontal drifts. To your right, it rises, the buildings growing sparser, the walkways narrower, until the town gives way to raw, unfinished stone that climbs into the fog and disappears.

The Pumps turn beneath you. You feel it more than hear it — a low, slow vibration, a mechanical heartbeat. Somewhere nearby, a hopper-rat pauses on a pipe, its green eyes catching the flicker of a blue-white lamp, and it watches you, and you have the uncanny, calm certainty that it is *assessing* you, and that its assessment is neither hostile nor friendly but simply *thorough*.

The air smells of wet iron and ferns. The light is flat and directionless and gives no shadows. Somewhere below, in the shafts, the heavy water rises. Somewhere in the rock, the borers grind. Somewhere in the middle district, the Fold House holds its impossible, quiet, extra space.

The town is here. It is working. It is not finished. It may never be finished. It is a place where the world is still being decided, and the people who live in it have decided to live in it anyway, which is, when you think about it, perhaps the most extraordinary thing about any of this.

You pull your coat tighter. The fog does not lift. You learn to see in the dimness, or you leave. Most people learn to see.
