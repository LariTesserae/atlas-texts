# The Seepage Works

It is not a large place. You could walk its known extent in twenty minutes if the ground permitted straight lines, which it does not.

## What the ground is

The ground is a graduating series of decisions made by water over a very long time. Not dramatic water — not river-carved or gorge-cut — but the patient, bureaucratic kind: infiltration, percolation, the slow negotiation between liquid and stone that produces, eventually, a landscape nobody designed but everybody can read.

The bedrock here is a soft, lime-heavy shale laid down in shallow seas and pressed into flat, gray-beige sheets that split along their bedding planes with a sound like tearing stiff cloth. Above this sits a layer of dense clay, blue-gray when wet, pale as ash when dry — though it is never fully dry. Above the clay, a skin of dark organic muck: a few inches to a foot deep, granular, waterlogged, smelling of iron and tannin. You sink between two and four centimeters with each step. The compression is audible. A quiet, intimate sound, like pressing a wet sponge.

The shale is close to the surface. In places it breaks through the clay entirely, forming flat ledges that gleam after rain as though someone has oiled them. These ledges — anything from a dining-table's footprint to a small parking lot's — are the closest thing the landscape has to solid ground. They are slightly tilted, all of them, dipping toward the southeast at angles between three and seven degrees. This is not accident. The entire area is the ragged edge of a broad, shallow syncline that has been settling since before anything alive watched it settle.

Water emerges from the shale's bedding planes in persistent, narrow seeps. Not springs. Seeps. You do not see water flowing from these openings. You see dark stains spreading downward from horizontal cracks in the rock face, and at the bottom of each stain, a film of water that collects, thickens, and falls — drop by drop, three to five seconds between drops — into shallow basins the water itself has carved into the clay over centuries. Each basin overflows along a path of least resistance, and these paths converge into others, and the convergences converge, and the result is a network of shallow, braided rills — each no wider than a hand, none deeper than a finger — that thread the entire area in patterns that shift seasonally but never abandon the same general courses. The rills are the drainage. The drainage is the place.

It has been like this for a long time. The erosion is not rapid. It is so established that it has become a kind of architecture.

## What is built there

Three structures survive, though "survive" suggests they are losing, which is imprecise. They are not losing. They are being slowly and deliberately transformed, and the distinction matters.

**The Pump House** is the most intact. It sits on the widest shale ledge — roughly twelve by six meters — where the seepage is heaviest. Brick, laid in English bond, with mortar that has gone soft and pinkish and supports colonies of a dark, gelatinous algae that nobody has named because nobody has come to name it. The Pump House is a single room, three meters at the ridge, with a gabled roof of corrugated iron sheet that has rusted to a deep, warm brown except along the channels where condensation runs, which remain bright and wet. The door is missing. The windows are missing. The floor is shale, unfinished, and in its center a square shaft descends — perhaps four meters, perhaps more; the water at its bottom is dark and still, and you cannot see its floor — into the rock itself.

A pump mechanism stands over the shaft, bolted to a cast-iron base plate that is itself bolted to the shale with iron bolts that have swelled with rust so completely that they have become permanent, chemically fused to the stone. The pump is a vertical-lift design: a cylinder, a piston, a connecting rod that once linked to a crankshaft driven by a belt from an external engine. The cylinder is brass, green-patinated but dimensionally sound. The piston is iron, seized. The connecting rod is iron, seized. The crankshaft is iron, seized. The belt is gone. The external engine — a small, single-cylinder gas engine, weatherproofed with a tin housing that has half-collapsed — sits on a concrete pad two meters from the Pump House's east wall. Its housing collects rainwater and breeds mosquitoes in warm months. A single, small pipe, perhaps four centimeters in diameter, exits the Pump House's south wall at floor level and runs along the ground for about fifteen meters before terminating at a rusted flange, open to the air. Water no longer flows through it. But water has found other paths through the pump's iron joints and out through cracks in the brick, so that the Pump House weeps from a dozen points on its south and east faces, and the ground below is particularly soft, and a particularly dense mat of the gelatinous algae grows there, dark and slick and quietly respiring.

What was pumped, and to where, and for what purpose, is not obvious. The shaft descends into the shale. The water in the shaft is not the same water as the seepage — or rather, it may be the same water but it has traveled farther and longer and has picked up a mineral load that gives it a faint, cloudy, pale-green color and a faint, dry taste of chalk. It was perhaps a water supply for something that is no longer here.

**The second structure** has no name because its function is unclear. It may have been a small foundry, a workshop, a processing shed of some kind. It is a concrete slab — four by five meters — with the lower courses of four walls remaining, no higher than knee-level. The walls are concrete block, and the blocks are spalling: their faces flaking off in flat shards as moisture enters, freezes, expands, and departs, leaving each block a little smaller, a little more rounded, a little more like a river-stone than a manufactured object. On the slab, within the walls, sit three objects: a steel drum, open-topped, half full of rainwater and dead leaves; a wooden workbench, its legs splayed with rot, its top bowed and split and colonized along its grain by a white filamentous fungus that appears to be digesting the wood with great patience and no apparent haste; and a low, iron box, the size of a suitcase, with a hinged lid, closed, its contents unknown. The box has been closed for long enough that its lid has rusted shut along the seam. Whatever is inside is dry, because the box sits on two bricks and has not been submerged, and the lid's seal, though rusted, appears intact. The box may contain tools. It may contain nothing. It may contain something that was put there for a reason that made sense at the time and has not made sense since.

A narrow-gauge track runs from this structure's slab to the edge of the shale ledge and then terminates. The track is iron rail on wooden sleepers. The sleepers are rotten. The rails are rusted but dimensionally intact, and the gauge — sixty centimeters, approximately — suggests that hand-pushed ore carts or small wagons once moved along this track, carrying something from somewhere to somewhere. The track's other end is underwater. It descends the ledge's edge and disappears into a shallow, permanent pool that has formed where seepage, rill-convergence, and the clay basin's lowest point have agreed on a meeting place. The pool is perhaps three meters across and twenty centimeters deep. The rail tops are visible just beneath the surface, dark and smooth and coated with a fine, brown biofilm. Small, dark shapes move over the rail tops. They may be insect larvae. They may be something else. They move in ways that suggest purpose, but the purpose is not legible.

**The third structure** is a chimney.

It stands eight meters tall, perhaps nine. Brick again, but a different brick than the Pump House — harder, redder, fired from a different clay. It is square in section, sixty centimeters on each side, and it tapers slightly as it rises. It is in good condition. It has been in good condition for decades. The brick is hard enough and the mortar joints thin enough and the exposure mild enough that the weather has barely marked it. It stands in the middle of the softest, wettest ground — not on a ledge, not on a foundation, but on the clay and muck itself — and it does not lean. It does not settle. It is perfectly plumb. This is the most unusual thing about the landscape. In a place where the ground is saturated and soft and where everything else that was built has been slowly and steadily undermined, the chimney stands as though the ground beneath it is bedrock. It is not bedrock. It is the deepest, wettest part of the mire. And yet the chimney is plumb.

This is the structure that people who know about this place — and very few people know about this place — will mention if you ask them. Not the Pump House, not the workshop, not the seeps or the rills or the shale ledges. The chimney. Because the chimney should not be standing, and it is standing, and no one can explain why without using words like "anomaly" and "unusual foundation conditions" and "further investigation warranted," and these are not explanations. They are postponements of explanation.

The chimney's flue is open at the top. Rain enters it. Birds do not, because birds do not come here, or almost never. A pair of swallows was seen once, in May, circling the chimney's cap three times and then departing. They did not nest. The flue descends into the ground. It does not connect to any visible structure. It is a chimney with no firebox, no hearth, no building. It is just a chimney, and it goes down.

If you stand near the chimney — within a meter or two — and you are quiet, which is easy because the whole landscape is quiet, you can hear, very faintly, a sound that might be dripping water echoing in a cavity below, or might be something else. The sound is rhythmic but not regular. It is not a drip's metronomic fall. It has pauses of varying length. It has what might be called phrasing. If you listen long enough — five minutes, ten — you may begin to feel that the sound is responsive, that the pauses correlate with your own breathing, but this may be pattern-matching, the ear's tendency to find structure in randomness, and you cannot confirm it because you cannot stop breathing to test the hypothesis.

The chimney is the landscape's still center. Everything else moves. The chimney does not move, and the thing beneath it, if there is a thing beneath it, does not move, or moves so slowly that movement is the wrong word.

## What lives there

Very little, and what lives there lives at the margins.

The gelatinous algae on the Pump House walls is the most visible organism. It is not truly gelatinous — it is a biofilm, a consortium of bacterial species held in a matrix of extracellular polysaccharides, and it is dark, nearly black, and glossy, and it moves — not quickly, not visibly — but it has covered the entire south face of the Pump House in the time since the structure was last maintained, and it is progressing around the east corner. It is doing something to the mortar. It may be dissolving it. It may be consolidating it. It is difficult to tell whether the mortar is softer or harder where the biofilm has been, and the question may not have a single answer.

In the rills, small invertebrates live. They are not remarkable. Flatworms, small enough to be nearly invisible, glide along the rill bottoms. Ostracods — seed shrimp, bivalved crustaceans a millimeter long — pulse through the shallow water in short, jerky trajectories. Nematodes, too small to see, exist in the muck in numbers that would be surprising if you could count them. These are the landscape's primary inhabitants. They do not know they live in a landscape. They live in a medium.

The white fungus on the workbench is the other prominent organism. It is a basidiomycete, probably, though it has not produced fruiting bodies and so cannot be identified by anyone who is not a mycologist with a microscope and a willingness to culture it. It is digesting the wood. It is also, possibly, digesting the iron bolts that hold the workbench together, because certain fungi can do that, and the bolts are loose, and the fungus's filaments extend along the bolt shafts into the wood and perhaps into the metal itself. The workbench will eventually cease to exist as a workbench and become a feature of the ground, and the fungus will have accomplished this, and then the fungus will eat itself or be eaten by bacteria, and the cycle will have closed, and no one will have been watching.

Fauna above the level of invertebrates is almost entirely absent. No mammals. No reptiles or amphibians, despite the water — which is strange, because amphibians should be here, and their absence is the kind of silence that becomes louder the more you attend to it. Insects are present but not abundant. Mosquitoes breed in the engine's housing. Midges cluster over the still pools on still evenings. A few beetles — ground beetles, dark and fast — hunt along the rill margins, and their tracks appear in the muck as delicate, punctuated lines. A single species of spider builds small, horizontal webs between the rills' edges, anchoring them to bits of stick or to the upright stems of dead rushes. The webs are often destroyed by rain and rebuilt. The spiders are patient. They do not catch much.

Birds are the great absence. The landscape is open enough, wet enough, full enough of insects that birds should be here. They are not. Something has discouraged them. It may be the lack of trees or substantial shrubs — there is no cover, no place to perch and survey — and it may be something else. The swallows that came and circled and left are the exception, and their departure was decisive, and it was not the departure of birds who found nothing. It was the departure of birds who found something and did not want it.

## What is growing

The vegetation is sparse in a way that feels intentional, though it is not.

Dead rushes grow in stands throughout the lower ground. They are a species of *Juncus* — hard-stemmed, sharp-tipped, waist-high, and entirely dead. Their color is a pale, bleached tan. Their stems are hollow and dry and they rattle against each other in wind, which is one of the only sounds the landscape produces that is not water. They do not appear to be regrowing. They are the remains of a living community that existed when the water was perhaps a centimeter or two higher, and they have been standing dead for years. New rushes do not appear. The conditions that suited them have changed, and the change was small — a slight drop in water table, a slight increase in acidity from the brick and mortar, a slight shift in something else — and it was enough.

In the wettest areas, where the rills converge and the water stands, a mat-forming moss grows. It is bright green, vivid against the gray and tan and dark. It is the only vivid color in the landscape. It grows in patches that follow the water's distribution exactly: where the ground is saturated, the moss is; where the ground is merely wet, the moss is absent and the muck is bare. The moss's edge is sharp, as though cut. It does not colonize gradually. It occupies ground or it does not, and the boundary between occupation and emptiness is a line.

On the shale ledges, where the surface is too hard and too slick for anything to root, there is nothing. Lichen does not grow here. This is unusual — shale ledges in similar climates usually support at least a crust of lichen. Here the rock is bare. It may be too slick. It may be too chemically active — the seepage water, with its mineral load, may discourage colonization. Or it may be that the rock is too new, in the biological sense: too recently exposed, too recently scoured by water that wants it clean. The water is still cleaning it. The water has been cleaning it for centuries.

A single stunted shrub grows near the workshop's slab. It is a willow, probably *Salix cinerea*, and it is less than a meter tall, and it has been less than a meter tall for a long time. It is not dead, but it is not growing. It is in a state of arrest: leafing out in spring, holding its leaves through summer, dropping them in autumn, producing no new wood, no new height, no new spread. It is a willow that has decided to remain exactly what it is. Its roots extend into the muck and perhaps into the clay, and they have found what they need to survive and not what they need to thrive, and the difference between those two quantities is the difference between a shrub and a landscape.

## What it is like to be there

It is quiet. The quiet is not silence. It is the sum of very small sounds: water dropping into basins at intervals that feel regular and are not; water moving in rills at a volume below hearing, felt as vibration in the soles of your feet if you stand still; the moss breathing; the rushes rattling; the Pump House's iron roof ticking as its metal expands and contracts with the movement of clouds across the sun. Wind is intermittent. When it comes, it carries the smell of wet stone, iron, and a faintly vegetal sweetness that might be the moss or might be the muck or might be the fungus on the workbench doing its work. When the wind stops, the air is still and cool — perhaps twelve degrees, perhaps less — and damp in a way that condenses on skin and on surfaces and on the inside of clothes.

The light is ordinary. The sky is overcast more often than not, and the overcast is low and flat and gray and casts a diffuse, shadowless light that makes everything look like a photograph of itself. When the sun breaks through, the light becomes directional and warm and the shale ledges flash wet and the moss blazes green and the chimney's brick glows a deep, saturated red, and for a moment the landscape is beautiful in a way that is almost aggressive, and then the sun is gone and the landscape returns to its resting state of gray and dark gray and pale green and rust.

But nothing in the landscape is actually at rest.

The water is always moving. The rills shift their courses — not quickly, not visibly, but over weeks and months the braided patterns reorganize, and a path that was dry becomes wet, and a path that was wet becomes dry, and the moss follows the water, and the bare muck follows the moss's departure, and the shale is exposed in new places and buried in others. The seeps change their output rates. The Pump House's weep-points migrate as mortar joints open and close with thermal cycling. The biofilm advances and, occasionally, retreats. The workbench's fungus extends its filaments into new wood, retreats from wood it has fully digested, and the workbench's shape changes — not weekly, not monthly, but over the span of a year you would see it: a corner that was solid is now soft; a leg that was vertical is now tilted; the top's warp has deepened.

This is what "turbulent" means here. Not storm. Not chaos. But a constant, multi-directional reorganization of small things, each acting on its own timescale and its own logic, the sum of which produces a landscape that is always different from what it was and always recognizably the same. The shale does not move. The chimney does not move. The clay moves very slowly. The water moves constantly. The organisms move at their own rates. The artifacts move as they are consumed and transformed. Everything is in flux, but the flux is the system, and the system is stable, and the stability is not stasis but a dynamic equilibrium maintained by the fact that nothing here has enough energy to overwhelm anything else.

Except the chimney. The chimney does not participate in the equilibrium. The chimney simply stands. And this is the landscape's strangeness: not that it is strange, but that it is almost entirely understandable — water, rock, clay, organisms, rust, time — except for one thing, the one thing in the center, the one thing that is plumb and dry and silent and goes down, and that one thing is a question that the landscape does not answer and does not seem interested in answering, and the not-answering is itself a kind of answer, and the answer is: this place was here before you and will be here after you, and the chimney will be here, and the water will be here, and the water will be doing what the water does, and the chimney will be doing whatever it is that the chimney does, and you are not required for any of this, and you are welcome to stay, but you should understand that staying here is like staying in a room where someone has been working for a very long time and has not finished and will not finish and does not require your help.

Stay as long as you like. Watch the water. Listen to the chimney. Do not open the iron box.

You probably cannot open the iron box.
