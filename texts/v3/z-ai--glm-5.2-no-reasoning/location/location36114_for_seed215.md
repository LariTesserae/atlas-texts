# The Drumplain

The first thing you notice is that the ground is breathing.

Not metaphorically. The basalt slab beneath your feet rises and falls in a slow tidal rhythm — four minutes up, four minutes down, a vertical displacement of perhaps nine centimeters at the crest. You can feel it through boot soles. You can see it in the way the standing water shivers each time the substrate falls, sending concentric ripple-rings outward across the flooded gutters of the causeway. The whole plain does this. All nine hundred visible kilometers of it, heaving like the chest of something vast and asleep.

You are standing on a causeway that does not pretend to be a road. It is a spine. A single ribbon of engineered material — dark, carbon-laminated, warm to the touch — running straight as a held breath across a landscape that has no other straight lines. It was laid down eleven years ago by machines that no longer exist, for reasons that were never published, and it is the only artifact of intent in a place otherwise still becoming.

---

## The Ground Itself

This is new land. Not newly discovered — newly *made*. The Drumplain is a tectonic eminence that began pushing above the mist line only decades ago, a broad shallow dome of basalt and gabbro rising out of what was, before living memory, a deep cold sea. The uplift continues. You can measure it: survey markers along the causeway's northern edge record the altitude, and every year the numbers increase by roughly the height of your knee. The rock is still arriving.

Because it is so new, nothing here is finished. The basalt has not yet learned to be crags. It comes in slabs — flat, wide, surprisingly thin — layered like the pages of a book no one has written yet, separated by films of moisture that weep continually from the stone's interior. The water does not fall from the sky, though it rains also. It emerges from the rock itself, forced upward by pressure from below, finding the hairline gaps between strata and spreading across the upper surfaces in sheets so thin they double as mirrors. Every horizontal surface on the Drumplain is wet. Every vertical surface weeps.

The stone is dark. Not black — closer to the blue-black of deep water, or the inner bark of certain trees. Where water crosses it, the color deepens to something almost purple, and in the low light of afternoon, the plain can look like a frozen sea viewed from a great height, dark and gleaming and only intermittently solid.

There are no mountains. The Drumplain is a plateau, and its edges are the most dramatic thing about it — sudden escarpments where the basalt layers have sheared away, dropping a thousand meters into misted lowlands that you can smell but not see. The cold air pools on top of the dome like a lens, five to seven degrees colder than the air that tumbles off the edges, and this inversion layer is permanent. It is not fog. It is the atmosphere itself, compressed and chilled by altitude, sitting on the rock like a hand on a drum.

The causeway was built because the ground is not yet trustworthy. Off the ribbon of carbon-laminate, the basalt can crack without warning — a slab detaching, tilting, dropping thirty centimeters in a single report that echoes for kilometers. The water that sits between layers acts as a lubricant, and the ongoing uplift means stresses are constantly accumulating and releasing. Walking on the bare plain is not death, but it is inconvenience and occasional injury. The causeway floats. Its foundations are tuned to the breathing frequency, and it rises and falls with the ground without cracking, a thread of stability across a surface that has not yet decided what it wants to be.

## Water

Water is everywhere but does not flow. It *seeps*. It *condenses*. It stands.

The atmosphere at this elevation is at the dew point almost constantly. Moisture collects on every surface — on stone, on the causeway, on your sleeves, on the few plants that have found root-holds, on the carapaces of the animals that graze here. The air tastes of wet mineral, of iron and calcium, of the dissolved inside of a new world. When the ground exhales — that four-minute rhythm — it pushes warm vapor upward through the fissures, and this vapor immediately condenses in the cold surface air, producing a thin spectral fog that rises knee-high and dissipates within seconds. The plain does this four times an hour, day and night. It looks like the land is smoking. It looks like the land is meditating.

Between the slabs, in the wider fissures, water collects into long linear pools — rarely wider than a hand's span, but stretching for dozens of meters, following the grain of the basalt. These interstitial waters are clear, cold, and chemically unusual. They carry dissolved compounds from the deep rock — silicates, transition metals, sulfides — and they precipitate a thin iridescent film on their surface that breaks into rainbow fragments when disturbed. The film reforms within minutes. It is not oil. It is a mineral skin, the water attempting to seal itself against the cold air, producing a boundary layer that is neither liquid nor solid.

Larger pools form in the shallow depressions where subsurface slabs have settled unevenly. These can be several meters across and are the closest thing the Drumplain has to lakes. They are never more than a few centimeters deep. They freeze at night — a thin glassy pane — and thaw by midmorning, and the freeze-thaw cycle is slowly, slowly working the stone, widening cracks, rounding edges, beginning the long process of turning raw geology into something a landscape could be made of.

But that process is in its infancy. The erosion parameter is almost zero. This is a place where construction outpaces destruction, where the ground is still being added to faster than weather can subtract from it. The pools are not carved basins. They are incidental — puddles in the wrinkles of a surface that is still being born.

## Vegetation

There are plants here, but they are sparse, and they are strange only in the way that pioneers are always strange — not in their forms, which are recognizable, but in their *choice* to be here at all.

The first colonist is a moss. Not a named species — or if it was named, the name was assigned by a survey team that has since been reassigned, and the designation sits in a file somewhere unreferenced. It is a dark olive-green moss, almost black when wet, that grows in the narrowest fissures where moisture is constant and where the mineral film provides a chemical substrate it can metabolize. It does not spread across the open slabs. It stays in the cracks, following the water, and from the causeway you can see it as a faint webbing of dark thread connecting the geometric panels of basalt — a slowly growing network, reticulate, patient. In some places it has been growing for twenty years and covers a square meter. In most places it covers a few square centimeters. It is not in a hurry.

The second colonist is a grass — or a grass-analogue, a plant that follows the architecture of grass without being a grass in any taxonomic sense you could verify without a microscope. It grows in clumps at the edges of the larger pools, where the water-saturated film of mineral precipitate has accumulated enough to form a pseudo-soil: not earth, not humus, but a chemically complex paste that can hold roots. The grass is pale, almost white, and each blade is thick and transluscent, carrying water visibly inside it — you can see the fluid move when the wind pushes the blades. It grows to perhaps fifteen centimeters and then stops, as if it has reached a decision about what is possible here. It does not flower. It reproduces by runners, extending itself outward in radial lines from its point of origin, and each clump is therefore a single individual, cloned in a circle, slowly enlarging its territory.

The third and rarest is a lichen that grows on the causeway itself. This should not be possible — the carbon-laminate is engineered to resist biological adhesion — but the lichen has evolved or been engineered to match the laminate's surface chemistry, and it holds on. It is a luminous pale blue-grey, and in the early morning, when condensation covers everything, it brightens to almost white. The maintenance machines that once traveled the causeway to remove such colonists have not come in years. The lichen is winning.

There are no trees. There are no shrubs. The horizon is stone and water and air, and the plants are a rumor at the joints.

## The City

The causeway leads to it, and the city is the reason the causeway exists.

It is not a city in the way that word normally functions. It is a settlement, certainly — organized, constructed, infrastructure-bearing, populated. But its population is not human, or not primarily human, and its construction follows a logic that is legible only in retrospect.

The city sits at the approximate center of the Drumplain, where the dome is highest and the basalt is most stable. It covers roughly forty square kilometers, which sounds large until you stand at its edge and realize that forty square kilometers in this landscape is a small thing — a mark, a notation, barely a smudge against the nine hundred kilometers of open plain that surround it.

The structures are low. Nothing exceeds four meters. They are built from the basalt itself, cut and re-laid in interlocking patterns that use the stone's own layering as structural logic — each building is essentially a reassembled section of the plain, the slabs separated and restacked with precision, creating walls and shelters that look, from a distance, like the ground has simply chosen to stand up in certain places. The architecture is not beautiful in a designed sense. It is *inevitable* — it looks like the buildings were always going to be there, waiting in the stone, needing only to be rearranged.

Between the buildings, the original surface of the plain is preserved — the wet basalt, the weeping cracks, the thin standing water. The city does not drain. It does not pave. It builds around the water and lets it pass through, and the interstitial pools continue to form between structures, and the moss grows in the cracks between walls, and the city is less a separation from the landscape than a denser version of it.

There are passages. Covered walkways, roofed with carbon-laminate like the causeway, connecting the buildings in a network that keeps the inhabitants dry — or drier, since the condensation means nothing here is ever truly dry. The walkways are narrow, designed for bodies that move differently than human bodies, and the doorways are shaped for forms that are not yours.

The city was built by and for the animals.

## Fauna

They are called — by the surveyors, by the maintenance logs, by the file that nobody references — *gravi*.

The gravi are large. Not large in the sense of elephants or whales — large in the sense of *substantial*, *dense*, *present*. An adult stands roughly a meter and a half at the shoulder, and its body is a low, wide, six-limbed frame, like a cross between a bison and a centipede, if that cross were designed by someone who valued stability above all else. The legs are short, thick, splayed wide, ending in broad pads that distribute weight across the thin basalt slabs without cracking them. The body is covered in a dense matte pelage the color of the stone — blue-black, darker when wet — and condensation beads on it constantly, giving each individual a faint glitter in the low light.

Their heads are wide and flat, close to the ground, and their mouths are broad and forward-facing, designed for cropping the pale grass at the pool edges and for scraping the mineral film from the interstitial waters. They eat the grass. They eat the moss. They eat the lichen, when they can reach it. They also eat the mineral precipitate itself — the iridescent surface film — which provides compounds their metabolism requires and which no plant here synthesizes. They are, in a sense, eating the landscape into existence — their waste is chemically altered, deposited across the basalt as a thin organic layer that is slowly, slowly becoming something like soil.

There are thousands of them. Tens of thousands. The Drumplain supports a population that, from the causeway, you can see spread across the stone in every direction — dark shapes grazing, moving, resting, their bodies half-hidden in the knee-high condensation fog that pulses with the ground's breathing. They are the dominant lifeform here. They are the reason the city exists.

The city is theirs. They built it.

Not with hands — gravi do not have hands. With behavior. The city is a *behavioral structure* — the same instinct that drives a beaver to build a dam or a termite to build a mound, but operating at a scale and complexity that neither beavers nor termites approach. The gravi rearrange the basalt slabs by pushing them — slowly, collectively, dozens of individuals aligning themselves against a slab and walking together — and the interlocking patterns are not designed but *emergent*, the product of rules encoded in gravi neurology that no one has fully mapped. The city grows as the population grows. New structures appear at the edges, pushed into place by gravi who have never seen a blueprint and would not understand one if they did.

They do not live in the buildings. They live *among* them. The structures provide windbreaks, thermal mass, and shelter for the young. The covered walkways are their migration paths — routes between grazing grounds, protected from the worst of the condensation. The city is infrastructure for a herd, built by the herd, and it functions as a single organism might function: waste recycled, young sheltered, grazing rotated, the population self-regulating through mechanisms that look like culture from a distance and like chemistry up close.

They are quiet.

This is the thing that strikes you most. Tens of thousands of large animals, and the plain is almost silent. The gravi vocalize below the range of human hearing — deep subsonic rumbles that travel through the basalt itself, felt in the feet and the chest rather than heard in the ears. They communicate through the ground. When the plain breathes — that four-minute oscillation — the gravi's own signals ride the same frequency, and the whole population is synchronized to the geological rhythm, their movements, their grazing patterns, their reproductive cycles all tuned to the breathing of the land beneath them.

Standing on the causeway, you feel this more than you hear it. A low vibration in your sternum. A sense of *attendance* — of being in a place where something enormous is happening just below the threshold of perception.

## Technology

The causeway is the highest expression of technology on the Drumplain, and it was not built by the gravi.

The carbon-laminate is a material that does not appear in nature and cannot be produced by any known manufacturing process currently operating on this continent. Its surface structure is nanoscale — a fractal lattice of carbon allotropes arranged in patterns that simultaneously repel water, resist biological adhesion (the lichen notwithstanding), and dynamically flex with the ground's breathing frequency. It was not *constructed*. It was *grown* — nucleated from a seed pattern and propagated outward along the causeway's path, the material assembling itself from atmospheric carbon and trace minerals pulled from the rain.

The machines that planted the seed patterns are gone. Not destroyed — *completed*. They were self-limiting assemblies, designed to deploy the causeway's foundation and then disassemble themselves into raw components that the gravi could metabolize. The survey team found the causeway already laid, already functional, with no evidence of builders except the causeway itself. The file notes this. The file does not explain it.

The causeway is the only piece of post-modern technology visible on the Drumplain, and it is also the only piece of technology that seems to *belong* here. The gravi do not use it — their wide pad-feet are adapted to the bare basalt, and the carbon-laminate is too smooth for their gait. But the causeway runs directly to the city's center, and its width is precisely calibrated for something — a vehicle, a process, a presence — that has not yet arrived. Or has arrived and departed. Or arrives periodically, at intervals the survey logs do not specify.

The maintenance machines that once cleaned the lichen from the causeway were smaller, simpler — conventional robotics, solar-powered, operating on a schedule that has lapsed. They sit in a shelter at the city's edge, inert, their power cells depleted, their surfaces growing the same pale blue-grey lichen they were designed to remove. They look like artifacts. They look like the stone has begun to digest them.

The technology of the Drumplain is therefore a ruin, but a ruin of something so advanced that its ruined state is indistinguishable from its functioning state. The causeway works. It does not need maintenance. It was engineered to outlast its own context, and it is doing so.

## Atmosphere and Light

The light here is the light of high places — thin, clear, blue-white — but filtered through the permanent condensation layer, it softens. Shadows are not sharp. Edges are not emphatic. The plain is lit like a watercolor, and the wet basalt surfaces double the available light by reflecting it upward, so that the undersides of things are illuminated as well as the tops. The gravi are lit from below as well as above, and their dark bodies carry a faint halo where the reflected light catches the condensation on their pelage.

There is no night in the conventional sense. The Drumplain is far enough north that, depending on the season, the sun either circles the horizon without setting or dips briefly below it for a few hours, and during those hours the condensation layer catches the last light and holds it — a diffuse amber glow, sourceless, that fades so slowly you cannot identify the moment of darkness. The plain is never fully dark. It is never fully bright. It exists in a perpetual twilight that shifts between silver and copper and back to silver over the course of a day that feels longer than twenty-four hours.

The horizon is visible in every direction — a clean line where the dark basalt meets the pale sky — and at the edges of the dome, where the escarpment drops away, the horizon is *double*: the near line of the plateau's edge, and the far line of the lowlands beyond, separated by a band of mist that is sometimes white and sometimes the exact color of the stone, so that the plain appears to float.

## Sound

The Drumplain is quiet, but it is not silent.

What you hear: the ground. The four-minute breathing produces a low sound — not a groan, not a creak, closer to the sound a large hull makes in a calm sea, a deep flexural note that you feel in your teeth before you hear it with your ears. It repeats. It is the only constant.

What you hear: water. The weeping from the cracks is barely audible — a faint tick-tick-tick where droplets form, swell, detach, and run. Near the larger pools, there is a soft continuous whisper where the ground-breath pushes vapor upward through the water's surface, agitating it gently, producing a sound like distant applause from a very small audience.

What you hear: the gravi. Rarely. Occasionally a herd will move across the basalt near the causeway, and their pad-feet produce a collective rhythmic percussion — soft, muffled, like someone drumming their fingers on a table covered in cloth. When they vocalize, it is below your hearing range, but you feel it — a pressure in your chest, a tightening, as if the air has thickened for a moment.

What you hear: wind. Not often. The cold air pooled on the dome is stable, but occasionally a pressure differential sends a breeze across the plain, and it moves through the city's covered walkways and produces a low organ-note that sustains for several seconds. The gravi alter the walkways — adding or removing covers, widening or narrowing passages — and the notes change. Whether this is intentional, whether the gravi are *composing*, no one has determined.

What you do not hear: machines. Birds. Insects. Traffic. Voices. The Drumplain is a place where sound has been subtracted to its minimum — the geological, the hydrological, the biological — and the subtraction is itself a presence. The silence between the ground's breaths is not empty. It is full of the plain's attention to itself, the new land listening to its own becoming.

## Being Here

You stand on the causeway. The carbon-laminate is warm beneath your feet — twelve degrees, maybe fourteen, while the air is two or three. The condensation has soaked your sleeves. Your breath makes clouds that join the general mist and vanish.

The ground rises. The ground falls. The vapor pushes up around your ankles and disperses. A thousand meters to the east, a line of gravi moves across the bare stone, their dark bodies barely distinguishable from the basalt except when they pass a pool and the reflected light catches them from below, and for a moment they are outlined in silver, and you see how many there are — a column of them, fifty or sixty wide, stretching back further than you can resolve, walking in a direction that the ground's breathing seems to have chosen for them.

The city is behind you. Its low basalt structures look like the plain has folded itself into rooms, and the covered walkways connect those rooms in a pattern that, from above, might be a circuit, might be a spiral, might be a word in a language that is being written for the first time in stone and behavior and condensed water.

The file is in your pack. You have read it. It does not explain the causeway. It does not explain the gravi's architecture. It does not explain why the ground breathes, or who grew the carbon-laminate, or what happens when the plateau rises another hundred meters and the air above it becomes too cold and too thin for anything to live.

The file is in your pack, and the ground breathes, and the gravi graze, and the water weeps from stone that has never been dry, and you are here, and the plain is still becoming, and it does not know what it will be when it finishes, and neither do you.

The causeway leads forward. The causeway leads back. The plain continues in all directions, vast and new and patient with itself, and the only sound is the breathing and the water and the soft percussion of pad-feet on stone, and the light is silver, and the stone is dark, and the city is growing, and the ground is rising, and everything here is just beginning.
