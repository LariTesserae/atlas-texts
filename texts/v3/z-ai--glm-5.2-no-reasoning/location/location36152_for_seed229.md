# The Chime Fields of Vashti Plateau

## First Approach

You come up through a crack in the world.

The passage — if it can be called that — is a fissure in old compressite, a cream-and-rust stone that rings dull when you knock it. The climb takes hours. The stone is warm under the hands, not hot, but warm the way a sleeping animal is warm: from within. By the time you emerge through the narrow opening onto the plateau, your breathing has adapted to the thinner air, and your eyes have given up on the darkness below.

Because above is not dark. Not exactly.

## The Light

There is no sun here. The sky — if it is a sky — is a low ceiling of dense, faintly luminous cloud cover, perhaps three hundred meters up, perhaps less. It is the color of weak tea held up to firelight. Amber-brown-pale. It does not move. It produces light without producing direction; shadows do not fall so much as faint, barely legible, agreeing to exist. You can see, but everything looks like a photograph taken through gauze. Colors are present but uncommitted to. The green of the growth here — and there is growth, so much growth — is a green that has given up on brilliance and settled into something murkier, more knowing. Olive. Moss-loden. A yellow-green that in certain angles becomes grey.

If you look long enough at the cloud-ceiling, you can see slow shapes in it. Not creatures. Not weather. More like the cloud itself is a membrane and something vast is pressing against it from the other side, leaving impressions that travel and fade. You learn not to look long enough.

## The Plateau

You are standing on a highland — the summit of a formation that rises from lower terrain you cannot clearly see from here. The plateau is perhaps six hundred meters across, roughly oval, bounded on most edges by sheer drops into fog or by walls of the same compressite stone, worn smooth in places, jagged in others. The stone is everywhere underfoot, but it is rarely *bare* — there are only a few places where you can see it clearly, and those are the paths, the gaps, the breathing room.

The rest is covered.

## What Grows Here

There is no water. None. Not a stream, not a pool, not a drop of dew. This is not a world where water is the medium of life. You discover this quickly because you are thirsty and nothing here offers you relief. The air is warm and dry without being arid — it has the character of a sealed room, a terrarium, a place where the atmosphere is recycled rather than refreshed. There is a faint taste of mineral salts and something else, something that sits on the back of the tongue like ozone's quieter cousin. Not quite metallic. Not quite chemical. A flavor that suggests reactions are happening, have been happening, will continue.

What grows here does not drink.

The dominant growth — the thing that covers the plateau like a forest seen from above, if a forest were only knee-high — is a plant analog that has no name in any language you know. It grows in stiff, branching stalks, each as thick as a finger, segmented like bamboo but covered in a bark that is pale and papery and peels in strips. These stalks rise from a communal mat of root-analogue tissue that spreads across the stone like a taut skin, clinging through some chemical adhesion rather than through any conventional root system. They do not seek soil. There is no soil. They have made their own substrate from the stone itself — or rather, from what they have done to the stone.

Because the compressite is not inert here. The root-mat secretes something — an enzyme, an acid, something biochemical — that breaks down the stone's mineral structure and frees compounds the plants use as raw material. Silicates. Iron oxides. Calcium salts. The plateau's bedrock is, in a slow and patient sense, being eaten. The root-mat is the stomach. The stalks are what the body does with what it digests.

The stalks grow densely — three or four hundred per square meter in the thickest clusters — but the clusters themselves are scattered. Between them, bare stone. Between them, silence — except this place is never silent. More on that shortly.

Among the stalks, other things grow. A moss-like organism that is not moss, which forms dark velvety patches on the stalks' lower segments and seems to feed directly on the bark. A lichen-analogue that crusts the bare stone between clusters, pale yellow-white, fractal-branching, which you initially mistake for mineral deposits until you see it slowly extending itself, one fractal iteration at a time, over the course of an hour. And hanging from the upper segments of the tallest stalks — those that reach waist-height — there are pendulous, translucent globes the size of a child's fist, filled with a gas that is faintly lighter than air. They pull the stalks into gentle arcs. They glow. Very faintly, very softly, a yellow-green bioluminescence that seems to pulse at the lower end of the visual spectrum, almost infrasonic, more felt than seen.

In the dim light, from a distance, the clusters of stalks look like crowds. You keep thinking someone is standing there.

## The Sound

This is the part that changes everything.

The plateau is *loud*. Not the loudness of a machine or a storm — the loudness of an orchestra of living things all performing simultaneously and continuously, with a zeal that suggests sound is not a byproduct here but a function. Perhaps *the* function.

The stalks are hollow. The wind — and there is always wind here, a warm, restless, turbulent wind that shifts direction every few minutes in sudden, unpredictable gusts — moves across the open mouths of the broken stalks and produces tones. Each stalk is a flute. The diameter of the stalk, the length, the size and placement of the crack or break that serves as its mouthpiece, the thickness of the wall — all of these determine pitch. With hundreds of stalks per cluster and dozens of clusters across the plateau, you are standing inside an instrument the size of a cathedral.

The tones range across what seems like the full spectrum of audible frequency and beyond. Deep bass notes that you feel in your sternum before you hear them, produced by the oldest, thickest stalks that have split along their length. High, thin, piercing whistles from the newest growth, still green and narrow. Mid-range tones, breathy and modal, that carry the closest thing to melody. The wind shifts, and the entire harmonic field reorganizes — a chord collapses, another rises, a single stalk that was silent begins to sing, another goes quiet as its crack is sealed by the motion of its neighbors.

But it is not only the wind. The gas-globes, when they detach from their stalks — and they do, constantly, pulled free by gusts or by their own slowly increasing buoyancy — they drift upward toward the cloud-ceiling, and as they rise, the atmospheric pressure changes, and they expand, and at a certain altitude they rupture with a sound like a wet handclap, a sharp *pop* that echoes across the plateau and sends a shudder through the nearest stalk-cluster, producing cascades of secondary tones, harmonics within harmonics, the whole field shivering and reconfiguring.

And there are the animals.

## What Lives Here

The fauna of the plateau is abundant, visible, and strange.

The most common are the ones you see first, because they move. The stalk-fields are full of creatures you will eventually call the Walkers — though that is not quite right, because they have no legs. They are low, flat bodies the size and shape of a spread hand, a dark chitinous shell — or something like chitin, something like keratin, something that is neither — segmented into five lobes that flex independently. They move by extending a lobe, anchoring it with fine hooked setae, pulling the body forward, extending the next lobe. The motion is slow, deliberate, and eerily silent against the background of the stalk-song. They are dark. They are many. You count forty in a square meter of stalk-cluster and stop counting.

They eat the bark. You see them doing it — a slow, mechanical rasping, their underside pressed to the stalk, their mouthparts — if that is what those are — working in a rhythm that has no relation to the sounds around them. The bark comes away in strips. The stalk, stripped, changes pitch. The Walkers are, in their quiet way, musicians. They alter the instrument by grazing it. The field's song is never the same because the field is being continuously modified by the things that live on it.

There are other animals. Above the stalks, riding the turbulent air, are the Gliders — flat, kite-like organisms that are difficult to see because they are nearly transparent, their bodies a single sheet of some flexible, tensile membrane stretched between a frame of rigid veins. They are not large — the largest has a wingspan of perhaps forty centimeters — but they are fast, and they move in sharp, angular trajectories that suggest they are not gliding passively but actively navigating, reading the wind, choosing their paths. They seem to be hunting. You see one fold its membrane, dive into a cluster of stalks, and emerge with a Walker clamped in its underside, the captured animal still and compliant, possibly paralyzed. The Glider rises on a thermal — and there are thermals here, columns of warm air rising from the stone — and carries its prey above the stalk-tops and out of sight.

In the gas-globes, there are passengers. You discover this when one of the globes drifts low enough to inspect. Inside the translucent skin, suspended in the gas, are tiny dark motes — organisms the size of mites, perhaps smaller, visible only as a kind of particulate darkness inside the globe. They do not appear to be producing the gas or maintaining the globe. They are simply there, riding, the way a flea rides a dog. When the globe ruptures, the motes scatter into the air, and you lose them against the complexity of everything else.

And then there is the thing at the center.

## The Center

The plateau has a depression at its heart — not deep, perhaps two meters below the general level, perhaps ten meters across. The compressite is exposed here, bare, pale, smoothed by something that was not wind. The stalks grow up to its edge and no further. The lichen does not enter it. The Walkers do not cross into it. Even the Gliders seem to avoid the airspace above it.

In the center of the depression, there is a structure.

It is small — no larger than a person crouching. It is made of the same compressite as the plateau, but it has been shaped. Not carved precisely, but shaped — stacked, perhaps, or assembled from pieces that broke from somewhere else. It is a cairn, or a shrine, or a marker. It has the deliberate quality of a built thing. Someone placed these stones.

On the stones, there are marks.

The marks are shallow grooves, made with a hard point — harder than compressite, which is not soft. The grooves form patterns that are not decorative, or not only decorative. They are linear, sequential, repetitive. You have the strong impression, looking at them, that they are instructions. Or a record. Or a name.

The structure is warm. Warmer than the surrounding stone. The heat comes from below, from deep in the plateau, and the structure seems to sit above a conduit for that warmth. This is, you realize, probably why everything grows here — the warmth from below, the mineral substrate, the sealed atmosphere. This is a hothouse, and the structure at its center is its furnace, or its anchor, or both.

## The Turbulence

Nothing here is still for long.

The wind reorganizes every few minutes. The stalk-song shifts. A cluster of gas-globes detaches simultaneously — you never know why — and rises, and they pop one by one over the course of a half-hour, each pop sending a shudder through the field, each shudder altering the harmonic landscape. The Walkers move and graze and change the pitch of their stalks. The Gliders dive and climb and navigate the thermals. The lichen extends its fractal reach by one iteration. The cloud-ceiling presses down, releases, presses again, and the light brightens and dims in slow tides that have no rhythm you can predict.

The built structure at the center sits through all of this — patient, warm, marked, holding its meaning whatever that meaning is, while around it the field sings and feeds and breeds and breaks and rebuilds itself continuously, a system in permanent creative destruction, an ecology that is not in equilibrium but in *performance*.

You realize, standing at the edge of the depression, listening to the field roar and shimmer and reorganize around you, that this place is not a ruin. It is not a remnant. The structure at the center is old — the grooves in it are smooth with time, the stones settled into their arrangement long ago — but it was not left behind by something that ended. It was placed here by something that understood what the plateau would do, what it would become, what it would sing. It is not an artifact in a dead landscape.

It is a podium in an orchestra.

## What You Do Not Know

You do not know who built it. You do not know what the marks mean. You do not know whether the heat from below is geological or artificial or something else. You do not know what presses against the cloud-ceiling. You do not know whether the Walkers are aware that their grazing changes the music. You do not know whether the Gliders navigate by sound or by something else. You do not know what the gas-globe passengers are, or whether they are the true inhabitants and everything else is infrastructure. You do not know if you are welcome here.

The field sings. The structure hums with warmth. The Walkers rasp. The globes drift and pop. The wind shifts, and the wind shifts, and the wind shifts, and the whole plateau becomes a different song.

You came up through a crack in the world. You can go back down.

But you are still standing here.
