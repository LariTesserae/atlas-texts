# The Girneworks

## I. Approach

You come up through cloud. Not the dramatic kind that breaks open to reveal vistas — the dull, persistent kind that sits at a particular altitude and refuses to leave. Around 1,400 meters, give or take. The air has that high-up thinness but not the dryness you'd expect. It's wet in the way a wool coat is wet when it's been hanging in an unheated room for a week. Not soaked. Not streaming. Just *damp through*, in a way that gets to your skin faster than you think it should.

The path is old. Stone flags, most of them still set, the kind of work that takes generations of incremental attention — not engineered, just *persisted*. Moss fills the joints like mortar. The stones are slick but not dangerously so. You learn to walk differently. Slower. Listening with your feet.

## II. What the Girneworks Are

They were never built as what they've become. That much is clear.

The original structures — and you can tell they were plural, separate, built at different times by people who may or may not have known one another — were shelters. Small ones. Built from the stone that was already here, which is a dense blue-gray slate that cleaves in clean planes and a paler, chalkier stone that erodes faster and gives the older walls their irregular, almost organic profiles. The shelters had low walls, no roofs now but probably thatch once. Post-holes in the floor stones suggest timber framing. The timber is long gone.

There were maybe eight or ten of these shelters originally. Room-sized. A person could stand in one but not comfortably stretch their arms. They cluster on a shelf of land — a bench, geologically — where the mountain's slope eases for perhaps thirty meters before dropping again. A pause in the terrain. The kind of place you'd stop if you were traveling and the cloud came in.

What happened, and nobody here remembers when, is that the girnew moved in.

## III. The Girnew

"Girnew" is a word that probably meant something more specific once. Here, it refers to what happens when a particular kind of moss meets a particular kind of moisture meets a particular kind of neglect.

The moss is *Sphagnum* something — but not quite. It's a variant that grows far faster than it should, that builds structure as well as covers it. Given stone and damp and time, it constructs. Not by planning. By *growing according to pattern*, and the pattern happens to be architectural.

Where the shelter walls still stood, the girnew grew over them and *thickened* them. Where the walls had fallen, it grew in their absence and *rebuilt* them. Where there had been gaps between shelters, it bridged them, creating enclosed corridors that follow no logic but the moss's own tropisms — toward moisture, toward still air, toward the particular mineral composition of the slate versus the chalk.

What exists now is a settlement. A town, almost. Tiny, but organized in the way that organic organization organizes — not designed but *emergent*, the way a termite mound is emergent. The girnew has created walls, corridors, partial roofs, chambers of varying size. Some are no bigger than a closet. The largest might fit fifteen people standing close. The total footprint is perhaps forty meters by thirty, bounded by the bench's own edges — where the slope steepens, the girnew doesn't grow. It knows where the ground stops being reliable.

The walls are living. That is the thing that takes the longest to accept. They are green, damp, soft to the touch. When you press your hand against one, it gives slightly, like pressing against a firm cushion. If you hold your hand there, you can feel the moisture moving — a slow, capillary pull, water traveling through the moss from somewhere lower. The walls breathe. Not metaphorically — they expand and contract by millimeters with changes in humidity, which is to say they breathe constantly, because the humidity here never stops changing.

## IV. What Lives Here

This is where it becomes difficult.

The girnew did not create empty architecture. In building its corridors and chambers, it built habitat, and habitat invites habitation, and the habitation here has reached a density that can only be described as overwhelming.

There are insects. You hear them before you see them — a low, pervasive hum that sits just at the edge of audibility, like a vibration more than a sound. The corridors are full of them. Small gnats, something like midges, that move in slow clouds through the damper air. They don't bite. They seem to feed on the girnew itself, or on what lives on the girnew. The walls shimmer when you look closely, because the surface is moving — springtails, mites, things without common names that exist in the inch between moss and air.

There are spiders. Many. They build in the corners where corridors meet, and their webs catch the condensation that drips from the ceiling, so that every junction is hung with tiny screens of water. The webs are not geometric — they're chaotic, dense, architectural in their own way. Some span the full width of a corridor at head height, and you learn to duck, or to hold a hand ahead of you. The spiders are small and brown and numerous and pay you no attention.

There are things in the walls. Centipedes — long, dark, moving with that particular liquid speed that makes your body react before your mind does. They live in the deeper moss, in the structural layers where the girnew is densest and oldest. You see them when a section of wall has been damaged or disturbed. You see a lot of them.

And there are the husk-things.

This is where the strangeness crests into something harder to describe.

The husk-things are not insects. They might have been insects once — in the sense that the girnew moss might have been ordinary *Sphagnum* once. Whatever they are now, they are the size of a human hand, pale, and nearly motionless. They cling to the walls in the deeper chambers, the ones where the corridors twist and the light from outside can't reach. They have a body shape that suggests something arthropod — segments, limbs folded close — but the proportions are wrong. Too many segments. The limbs are too long and articulated in places that don't correspond to any insect you know. They are the same color as the pale chalk-stone, which is to say almost white, and when you look at them directly, they look like shells. Empty. Discarded.

When you look at them from the corner of your eye, they move.

Not fast. Not far. A slow adjustment, a re-positioning of a limb. The kind of movement that could be imagined, that could be a trick of dim light and tired perception, except that it happens every time, and consistently, and in the same direction — always tightening closer to the wall, always pressing themselves flatter, always *concealing*. You cannot catch them moving. But you know they do.

Nobody here calls them anything, because nobody here talks much.

## V. The People

There are seven of them. Maybe nine. It's hard to count because they don't gather all at once and the chambers are small and the corridors are confusing and you can pass through the entire girnew works three times and still not be sure if the person you see in the eastern chamber is the same person you saw in the western one or someone different.

They are human. Short, dark-haired, dressed in layers of undyed fabric — rough-spun, thick, the kind of cloth that holds up in damp. They wear wraps on their feet rather than shoes, which makes sense because the floors are wet and textured and shoes would slip. They have tools. Stone and bone, mostly. A few simple metal pieces — pins, a small blade, things that could have been traded for rather than made. They are not primitive in the sense of unknowing. They are primitive in the sense of *having what they need and nothing more*.

They live in the girnew works. They occupy specific chambers the way someone might occupy a room — one person has a space near the eastern entrance where they sleep on a bed of dried moss that they replace regularly. Another has claimed a small chamber deeper in, where they keep things — stones arranged by size, strips of dried material, tools hung on small pegs driven into the girnew walls. The walls hold pegs well. They grow around them.

They don't seem to have a shared governance. They don't seem to need one. There is enough space — the girnew works are sparse in their habitation, much more corridor and chamber than person. You could walk for five minutes without seeing anyone, and the place is only forty meters across. There is emptiness between presences. The moss fills the space that people don't.

What they do is maintain. They clear the corridors when the girnew overgrows them — cutting it back with stone tools, which works about as well as you'd expect, which is to say slowly and with effort. They redirect water. They move the husk-things.

That last part. They move the husk-things.

You learn this by watching the woman who tends the deep chambers — the oldest, you think, though age is hard to read here. She goes through the inner corridors once a day, and where a husk-thing has appeared in a corridor or a chamber that people use, she picks it up with her bare hands and places it on the wall in a deeper chamber, pressing it gently against the moss until it adheres. She does this without fear. Without ceremony. The way you'd move a stone from a path. The husk-things do not resist. They curl slightly when touched, then flatten again when placed.

You try to ask her about them. She looks at you in a way that suggests the question doesn't parse. Not that she's refusing to answer. That the distinction you're drawing — between the husk-things and the walls, between the living and the structural, between what belongs and what doesn't — is not a distinction she recognizes.

## VI. The Light

There is no light source inside the girnew works.

This is not quite true. There is light, but it is borrowed and weak. The corridors are not roofed in the conventional sense — the girnew grows overhead in dense, interlocking mats that admit light the way a forest canopy does: filtered, scattered, green-tinted. On a bright day, the corridors are lit like the inside of a thick hedge. On a dim day — and most days are dim here, at this altitude, in this cloud — the corridors are almost dark. You can see shapes. You can navigate. But you cannot see color, and you cannot see clearly past about five meters.

The chambers are slightly brighter than the corridors because they're wider, and the girnew canopy overhead is thinner or absent. The deep chambers — the ones where the husk-things cluster — are genuinely dark. You feel your way along the walls. The walls are warm, which is strange, because the air is cool, because the altitude is high, because everything about this place should be cold. But the girnew generates heat. Not much. A degree, maybe two, above the ambient air. The walls are alive and alive things are warm and the warmth is barely perceptible except in the deep chambers where you press your hand against the moss because you can't see, and the moss presses back, slightly, and it is warm, like touching skin.

## VII. The Sound

The girnew works are quiet in the way that places with a lot of soft surfaces are quiet. The moss absorbs. Stone would echo. The girnew swallows. Your footsteps are soft sounds that travel no distance. Your voice, if you speak, falls dead two meters from your mouth. The hum of the insects is the closest thing to ambient noise, and even that is felt more than heard.

But there is another sound. A low one. Deeper than the insects, below the threshold of real hearing, more like a vibration that you notice in your sternum when you stand still in the deeper chambers. It might be the water moving through the walls. It might be the girnew growing. It might be something else. It is continuous and it is everywhere and it is the sound of the place being alive in a way that does not require your attention or your comprehension.

The people who live here make almost no sound. You hear them before you see them sometimes — a footfall, the soft scrape of a tool on moss — but these are small sounds, local sounds. They don't carry. The girnew works don't echo. They absorb. Everyone exists in a small bubble of sound that extends only as far as their own body's noise. Beyond that, silence.

## VIII. The Water

It comes from the cloud. The cloud sits on this bench of land like a blanket that never lifts, and the cloud has water in it, and the water condenses on every surface — on the moss, on the stone, on the fabric of your clothing, on your skin. It doesn't rain. Not here. But every surface is wet, constantly, in the way that surfaces inside a cloud are wet. The moisture is ambient. It is the medium.

The girnew channels it. The moss's structure is such that water condenses on the surface and then moves inward, through the layers, toward whatever internal architecture the moss uses to transport fluid. Some of it emerges lower, where the walls meet the ground, as slow seeps that collect in shallow channels worn into the stone floor. The channels were not cut. They were eroded by the water itself, over years, and they form a network that the original builders may or may not have intended. The current inhabitants clean these channels regularly, using small tools, keeping the water moving. Where it pools, the insects breed more densely. Where it flows, the walls stay healthier.

There is one place, near the center of the works, where the water emerges from the wall in a steady trickle, not more than a liter per minute, and collects in a shallow stone basin that someone carved or found or improved. This is the water source. The only one. It is never dry. The cloud never lifts. The water never stops.

## IX. The Surroundings

The girnew works exist in a clearing. Not a natural one — the moss has simply not grown beyond a certain perimeter, and outside that perimeter, the vegetation is different. Not less lush. Differently lush.

The bench of land, outside the girnew's boundary, is covered in a dense, low forest. Not trees, exactly — they're something between a tree and a shrub, multiple stems from a single base, reaching four or five meters, with small, dark, leathery leaves that drip constantly. The ground between them is covered in a different moss, shorter, denser, less structural. Ferns grow in the gaps. The overall effect is of a compacted, saturated ecosystem — everything growing close to everything else, everything wet, everything green in a way that feels almost aggressive, as if the vegetation is pressing in on the clearing where the girnew works sit.

The fauna here is dense too. You hear it more than you see it — things moving in the undergrowth, things calling to one another in low, short sounds that don't carry far. The birds here are small and dark and move fast through the branches. The things on the ground are harder to identify. Something rustles. Something else responds. The ecosystem has a pace to it that feels accelerated — not frantic, but *busy*, the way tropical ecosystems are busy, everything eating something else, everything in motion. This is strange at this altitude and this temperature. The cool should slow it down. It doesn't. Something about the constant moisture, the constant growth, has created a biological engine that runs faster than its climate would predict.

The girnew works are an island of architecture in this sea of growth. The boundary is clear. You step out of a corridor and into the clearing and the air changes — colder, because the wind can reach you here, and the wind is constant at this altitude. The cloud is thinner at the edges of the clearing. You can see the sky, or what passes for sky — a uniform gray, bright enough to suggest the sun is somewhere above it, but not bright enough to cast shadows.

## X. What You Notice Last

You notice it last because it is the thing that doesn't announce itself.

The girnew is growing. Not just maintaining — *growing*. New corridors are forming at the edges, where the moss meets the clearing. The walls there are lower, thinner, more recent. You can see where the architecture will be in ten years, in fifty, in a hundred — the patterns starting to repeat, the chambers forming in the same irregular shapes, the corridors finding the same lines. The girnew is building. Slowly, according to its own logic, following a blueprint that no one drew.

The people who live here are not building with it. They're not building against it. They're living in the space between its intentions, maintaining what it makes, clearing what it overmakes, moving through it with the particular comfort of people who have understood that they are not the architects of where they live. The architecture is alive and it has its own plans and the plans are not for humans, but humans can live in them, if they're quiet, and if they're careful, and if they don't ask the walls too many questions.

The husk-things are part of it. You understand this finally, on the last day, watching the woman press one gently against a wall in a deep chamber. The husk-thing flattens, adheres, and slowly — too slowly to see, but you see it anyway — the moss grows over it. Incorporates it. The wall thickens by the width of a human hand.

The husk-things are not insects. They are not animals. They are the girnew's way of building. The way a coral reef builds from the accumulated bodies of coral polyps, the girnew builds from — something. Something that looks like a discarded shell, something that moves when you're not looking, something that the moss grows and the walls absorb and the people carry to the places where the walls need to be thicker.

You don't know what the husk-things are. The people who live here don't seem to know either, or don't seem to think the question matters. The walls are warm. The water runs. The cloud doesn't lift. The girnew builds, and the people live inside what it builds, and the husk-things are carried to where they're needed, and the insects hum at the edge of hearing, and everything is damp, and everything is alive, and everything is quiet, and the whole place breathes — slowly, deeply, like a sleeping thing that is also a house that is also a town that is also a forest that is also a reef that is also a thing with no name in any language but the one the moss speaks in its slow, green, architectural tongue.

You leave through the eastern corridor. The cloud closes behind you. You will not be sure, later, that it happened. You will be sure of the warmth of the walls. That, you will not be able to set aside.
