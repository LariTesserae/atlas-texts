# The Seep

It begins as a sound you almost cannot hear — not a sound, really, but the *idea* of one. The low murmur of water moving through something it should not be able to move through. Stone, maybe. Or the spaces between stone. Or the spaces between the *idea* of stone.

You are below. This is the first thing to understand. Not below sea level, though that is also true — the whole basin sits in a gentle depression thirty meters below the mean tide line of the inland sea that used to be here, that perhaps is still here in some sense the water remembers even if the geography has forgotten. Below in a deeper way. The sky above the basin is not the sky above the land surrounding it. The stars, when they are visible — and they are almost always visible, because the darkness here is absolute, because no light escapes the structures to dim them — those stars do not match the constellations you knew before you descended the long, eroded ramp of conglomerate that brought you here. They are *similar.* But shifted. As if the sky had been copied by someone with a good memory but not a perfect one, and the minor errors accumulated.

---

## The Ground

The terrain is young. Geologically young — fresh, unfinished, still deciding what it wants to be. The bedrock is a pale, chalky limestone that fractures along clean planes, leaving surfaces with the sharp, unworn geometry of things only recently exposed. In places, the stone has the character of old paper: brittle, slightly fibrous, willing to take a mark. You can scratch it with a thumbnail. In other places — and this is where the first wrongness enters — the stone is wet.

Not damp. Not moist. Wet. As if the rock itself has been soaked through, as if it is not stone at all but a dense, mineral sponge holding more water than its own volume should allow. You press a palm to the surface of an outcrop and your hand comes away glistening. The water is warm. Warmer than the air, which sits at a humid, close warmth somewhere in the range of a late summer evening in a subtropical climate — perhaps thirty degrees, perhaps a little more. The warmth has weight. It does not move on. It accumulates in the basin like something poured.

The water seeps from everywhere. It emerges from hairline fractures in the limestone in threads so fine they are more felt than seen — a slow, patient weeping that leaves mineral trails down the faces of outcrops, pale ridges of calcium carbonate that build on themselves by fractions of a millimeter per year. The stone weeps. The ground weeps. In the low places where the depressions gather, shallow pools form — never deeper than an ankle, never wider than a few meters — and these pools do not reflect the sky correctly. They reflect a sky that is slightly *ahead* of the one above. Stars appear in the water before they appear overhead. You can track the delay — it is roughly eleven minutes, though the mechanism is not one anyone has been able to explain.

The ground between the outcrops is a grayish, clay-rich sediment, soft underfoot, pocked with the tracks of things that move through here in the dark. The sediment holds water close to the surface — you sink a centimeter with each step, and the compression releases a faint, warm dampness that smells of iron and chalk and something else. Something vegetal, though there is almost no vegetation here.

## What Grows

There is almost no vegetation. This is true. But *almost* is doing a great deal of work in that sentence.

What grows here grows in the fractures. In the seams where the limestone has split open and the water emerges, there are organisms that have chosen a third path — not plant, not fungus, not mineral, but something that partakes of all three and commits to none. They form as mats, at first: thin, dark greenish-black films that spread across the wet stone like a stain. They have no roots. They adhere directly to the calcium surface and appear to feed on the stone itself, etching shallow channels that become, over time, the pathways through which more water seeps. Whether the fractures create the organisms or the organisms create the fractures is a question that has been asked. No answer has been agreed upon.

In the larger fractures — the ones wide enough to accept a hand — these mats thicken into structures. Frond-like. Shelf-like. They are warm to the touch, warmer than the surrounding stone, and they pulse. Not with the rhythm of a heartbeat, which is mechanical, which is a pump, but with something more like breathing. A slow, irregular expansion and contraction on a cycle of perhaps two breaths per minute. If you hold your hand against one of the larger fronds long enough, your own breathing will begin to sync with it. This takes roughly four minutes. It is not recommended that you do this.

There are no trees. No grasses. No flowering plants. The only thing that could be called a plant in the conventional sense grows in the scattered pools — a kind of dark, filamentous algae that forms loose, drifting clouds beneath the water's surface, turning the pools opaque, a black so total it seems to have depth beyond the depth of the pool itself. Looking into one of these pools is like looking into a hole. People have measured. The pools are ankle-deep. The darkness in them reads as fathoms.

## The Structures

There are buildings here. This is perhaps too strong a word. There are *interventions* — placed at intervals across the basin in a pattern that might be deliberate, might be circumstantial, might be the residue of a logic that no longer has an author.

They are industrial in character. Low, blocky, made of a concrete that has the same pale, chalky character as the limestone — possibly because it was made from the limestone, mixed with the water that seeps here, cured in the dark and the warmth. Each structure is roughly the size of a small warehouse: ten meters by twenty, three meters high, with flat roofs and walls unbroken except for a single doorway on each face. The doorways have no doors. They are open rectangles of a darkness that does not match the darkness outside — a deeper dark, an *interior* dark, the kind that suggests enclosure, that suggests a ceiling above and walls around and a floor below, all of which are there but cannot be perceived.

Inside each structure, the same configuration: a machine. Or a system. Or — this is the word that the few people who have studied these things have settled on, with reservations — an *engine.* Not an engine in the sense of a device that converts energy into motion. An engine in the older sense: a device, a contrivance, something contrived. Each one is different, but they share characteristics. They are made of metal — a dark, ferrous metal that sweats in the humidity, that is warm to the touch in the same way the stone and the frond-organisms are warm, as if everything in the basin shares a common low heat. They have pipes, or things that function as pipes: hollow cylinders that connect chambers within the engine to the outside, that run through the walls of the structure and terminate in the stone, in the fractures, in the pools. Water moves through these pipes. Not water that has been pumped — there are no pumps, no power sources — but water that *moves,* slowly, in the manner of water that has been given a direction and is following it.

The engines hum. This is the quiet, persistent sound of the basin: a low, composite hum that emerges from the structures at a frequency just at the threshold of perception. You feel it in your sternum before you hear it. When you hear it, it is not a single tone but a chord — several frequencies layered on each other, the way a pipe organ holds multiple notes. The chord changes. Slowly. Over the course of hours, one frequency will fade and another will enter, and the quality of the hum shifts from something warm and almost pleasant to something that produces, in the listener, a faint, sourceless anxiety. Then it shifts back. It follows no pattern that has been determined.

There are seven structures visible in the basin. The basin extends beyond what can be seen — the darkness is total except for starlight and the faint, bioluminescent glow of the organisms in the fractures, which gives everything the character of a landscape seen by the light of a dying match — and it is known from the sound that there are more. The hum comes from at least eleven directions. There are at least eleven engines. There may be more.

The structures are not old. They are not new. They have the character of things that were placed here at a time that is difficult to specify — not last year, not a thousand years ago, but at some moment that sits outside the usual categories of geological and human time. The concrete is unweathered. The metal is unrusted despite the moisture. But the ground around each structure has subsided unevenly, as if the structures are heavier than they appear, as if they are pressing down into the stone with a weight that is not physical.

## What Lives Here

The fauna is abundant. This is the most immediately unsettling thing about the basin, more unsettling than the wrong stars or the deep pools or the engines that hum in the dark: the place is *full* of animals.

They are not animals that should be here. The basin is warm, wet, low, and dark — a set of conditions that might support a specific ecology, a cave ecology, a marsh ecology — but what lives here does not belong to any single ecology. The basin has assembled its population from sources that do not cohere.

There are amphibians. Large salamander-like creatures, thirty centimeters long, dark and slick, that move through the shallow pools and across the wet stone with a fluid, boneless locomotion that suggests they are more comfortable here than any salamander should be. They gather at the edges of the pools in clusters of a dozen or more, pressed against each other, barely moving. Their skin, in the faint glow of the organisms, has a translucent quality — you can see the dark trails of their veins, the slow pulse of something that is not quite blood.

There are insects. Not flying insects — the air is too wet, too heavy — but walking insects, crawling insects, things that move along the fractures and feed on the organisms. They are beetles, or near-beetles, with shells that are black and smooth and reflect no light. They are numerous. In the fractures where the organisms grow thickest, the beetles cover the stone in a continuous, shifting layer, and the sound of them — the small, dry clicking of shell against shell, of limb against stone — is one of the only sounds the basin produces that is not the hum of the engines or the murmur of water. They are not disturbed by the presence of larger creatures. You can walk through a column of them and they will flow around your feet like a liquid, re-forming on the other side.

There are larger things. In the distance — the basin is open, the scale is regional, you can see for what might be a kilometer in the starlight and the glow — there are shapes that move with the slow, deliberate gait of large mammals. They are visible as silhouettes against the faint luminescence of the stone. They do not appear to have a fixed form. This is difficult to describe accurately: they are not shapeshifting, not in the sense of changing from one shape to another. Rather, their edges are *indeterminate* — as if the animal is there, is solid, is present, but the boundary between the animal and the air around it is not a clean line but a region of probability. You cannot tell, looking at them, exactly where the animal ends. They move slowly. They do not appear to eat. They do not appear to be going anywhere. They are here in the same way the stone is here.

There are birds. This should not be possible — there is nothing for birds to eat in the conventional sense, no seeds, no fruits, no insects in the canopy of a forest that does not exist — but they are here. They are large. Flightless. They stand in the shallow water between the structures, their long legs half-submerged, their bodies hunched in a posture that suggests waiting. They do not call. They do not move. In the dark, they are visible only as shapes, darker than the darkness around them, and you know they are birds only by the silhouette — the long neck, the heavy beak, the way they hold their heads angled slightly upward, as if listening to something above.

You hear them breathe. That is the other sound of the basin: the slow, measured respiration of these birds, which is deeper than a bird's breathing should be, which has a duration — five seconds in, seven seconds out — that you can feel in your chest if you stand near one. Which you should not do. They are not aggressive. They simply *notice.* When you stand near one, its head turns — slowly, with a mechanical precision that is not animal — and it looks at you, and you understand that you have been seen by something that does not see in the way that seeing usually works.

## How Time Passes

Nothing here is fast. This is one of the basin's defining qualities, and it is the one that visitors find most difficult: the slowness is not patience, not stillness, but a genuine *dilution* of time. Things happen at the speed they happen, and that speed is not the speed of the world above.

The water seeps at a rate that is imperceptible unless you watch a single fracture for several minutes. The organisms pulse on their two-breaths-per-minute cycle. The engines shift their chord over hours. The large shapes in the distance move at a pace that might be a kilometer per day, or per week. The birds breathe five seconds in, seven seconds out, and they do not do anything else.

If you sit still in the basin — on one of the outcrops, away from the water, away from the structures, away from the birds — you will begin to slow. Your heartbeat will drop. Your breathing will lengthen. Your thoughts will spread out, becoming less dense, taking longer to form and longer to complete. This is not a danger, exactly. It is a property of the place. The basin does not *do* anything to you. It is simply a medium in which things move more slowly, the way water is a medium in which sound moves faster than it does in air but light moves slower.

People who have stayed in the basin for more than a few hours report that the darkness becomes textured. Not visually — there is nothing to see that was not there before — but *spatially.* The darkness has a shape. It has proximity. It sits near you, and then it sits closer, and then it is not sitting near you but *around* you, and you understand that the darkness in the basin is not the absence of light but a substance in its own right, with properties, with behaviors, with what might be called preferences.

It does not hurt. Nothing here hurts. This is part of the problem. The basin is not hostile. It is not dangerous in the way that a cliff is dangerous or a fire is dangerous. It is a place where the rules are different — where stone holds water like flesh, where organisms breathe for you if you let them, where engines run without fuel on processes that no one has identified, where the animals are abundant and calm and wrong, where the sky is a copy of a sky, where the darkness is a material, where time slows and does not recover the hours it has taken.

You can leave. The ramp of conglomerate that brought you here is still there. You can walk back up it, and the stars will re-arrange themselves, and the air will thin and cool, and the world will resume at the speed at which it usually runs.

But you will remember the breathing. The breathing of the birds. The breathing of the organisms. The slow, composite hum of the engines that are not engines, doing work that is not work, in a place that is not a place but is, nevertheless and undeniably, *here.* Below. Waiting with the patience of stone and water and the eleven-minute delay between what is and what will be, which is visible, if you look down, in the pools that do not reflect what is above them but what is coming.

The water knows. The water has always known. The water is what is here, beneath the stone, beneath the structures, beneath the darkness — and it is moving, and it is slow, and it is not going anywhere, and it is not stopping.
