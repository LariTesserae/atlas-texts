# The Cirque of Weeping Walls

It begins as sound before it begins as place.

You hear it from the approach — a sustained roarity that resolves, as you climb the last switchback of broken shale, into its components: falling water, wind against vertical rock, the grackle-chat of birds echoing off stone in ways that multiply them into thousands. The noise is enormous for such a small place. It fills a bowl in the high mountains like liquid filling a cup.

---

The cirque is perhaps two hundred meters across. Maybe less. A glacial hollow scooped from the mountainside tens of thousands of years ago and left, since then, to settle into itself. The walls rise on three sides — not the highest peaks of the range, but high enough that the sky is a shape defined by stone. Grey rock, darkened with moisture, streaked in long vertical lines where water has run and kept running for so long that the stone itself has been shaped into shallow channels. Weeping lines. The walls are not dramatic in the way of younger mountains — no jagged teeth, no fresh fracture. They've been rounded at their edges, softened into forms that feel almost intentional. Curves where you'd expect angles. The rock has had time to decide what shape it wants to be.

The fourth side opens downhill, and from that edge you can see the valley below — a long green drop into forested distance. But inside the cirque, you see only the walls, the floor, the sky. It is a room. A cathedral with no door.

---

Water is everywhere here, though no river flows through.

The walls weep. This is not metaphor. Moisture condenses on the stone surfaces constantly — the cirque's shape traps humid air rising from below, and the cold rock pulls water from it the way a cold glass pulls condensation on a summer day. But this is not summer. The air is cool, maybe eight degrees, and damp in a way that gets into your clothes. The condensation forms into droplets on the walls, joins into rivulets, runs down the streaked channels, and emerges at the base of the walls as seeps and springs that cross the floor of the cirque in braided, shallow threads. No single channel is deep. The water spreads, finds new paths, retreats, finds them again. The floor is a slow-moving mosaic of water and stone and low-growing plants.

It rained yesterday. You can tell because the upper walls are darker, and the seeps at the base are fuller, more assertive. By tomorrow they'll shrink back. But they'll never stop. The walls have been weeping since before anyone came to see them.

The rock underfoot is wet but not slippery — it's a coarse schist, rough-grained, with enough texture that bare feet would grip it. The water running over it is clear and cold and tastes, if you drink it, like minerals and nothing else. Like the mountain digesting itself at a speed too slow to watch.

---

The vegetation is concentrated at the base of the walls and across the floor, and it is remarkably lush for this altitude. Ferns — small ones, knee-high, with fronds darker green than seems possible at this height — grow in dense clusters where the seeps emerge. Their fronds are wet, always wet, and they catch the light in a way that makes them look almost black from one angle and electric green from another. Moss covers the lower portions of the walls in patches, thick as carpet, in greens and browns and one surprising patch of deep orange near the eastern seep. Sedges grow in the shallower water, their stems moving with the current in unison, like hair being combed. There are flowers — small ones, pale violet, growing from crevices in the rock where soil has accumulated in cracks over centuries. They are not dramatic flowers. They don't need to be. They are the only color in a grey-green-white world, and they are enough.

There are no trees. The altitude is too high, the wind too persistent, the soil too thin. But the absence of trees makes the space feel larger. The walls rise unbroken. The sky is visible entire.

---

The sound is what stays with you.

The walls create a natural amphitheater, and everything that happens inside it is amplified. The falling water — not a waterfall, just the accumulated weeping of stone — creates a constant background hiss and rush that rises and falls with the wind. When the wind shifts and funnels into the cirque from the open side, the sound changes: the water noise sharpens, the hiss becomes more distinct, and you can hear individual droplets striking stone. When the wind drops, the sound smooths into a low continuous roar, like distant traffic, like the sea heard from far away.

Over this background, the birds. They are the loudest thing here. The cirque is full of them — not nesting in the walls, but using the space constantly, flying through, landing on ledges, calling to each other in voices that the stone throws back and forth. The echo is not a single repetition but a cascading one — a call becomes two calls, becomes four, becomes a chattering reverberation that fills the bowl and makes three birds sound like thirty. They are mostly small — finches, pipits, a species of thrush with a dark belly and a bright eye that perches on the ferns and bends them down with its weight. They are feeding. The wet walls and the moss and the seeps support an insect population — midges, small flies, craneflies with long fragile legs — and the birds are here because the insects are here, and the insects are here because the water is here, and the water is here because the walls trap the air and the air gives up its moisture to the cold stone.

It is a complete ecosystem in miniature. A food web contained in a stone bowl two hundred meters across. The pace of it is visible — birds landing and taking off, insects swarming in the stiller air near the seeps, the ferns shivering when a bird lands on them, the water adjusting its paths around roots and stones. Nothing is still. Everything is happening at a speed you can watch.

---

There are signs of people, but only signs.

At the base of the eastern wall, where a natural overhang creates a shallow shelter — maybe three meters deep, four meters wide — there is evidence of use. A fire ring, old, the stones blackened and moss-grown. Not recent. The ash beneath is deep, which suggests repeated use over a long period — seasonal, perhaps, by shepherds or travelers using the cirque as a waypoint on a route that no longer exists or is no longer used. A few stones have been moved to create a rough windbreak at the mouth of the overhang, stacked dry, no mortar. They've been there long enough to weather to match the surrounding rock. You'd almost miss them.

Against the western wall, a single wooden post stands about a meter tall. It's old — weathered grey, the wood split along the grain, the top softened by rain into a fibrous brush. It might have been a marker. It might have been part of a fence or a structure that no longer exists. It is the only vertical thing in the cirque besides the walls themselves, and it has a strange prominence because of that. A stick in a stone room. It says: someone was here, and they wanted something to be noticed.

There are no other structures. No paths, though the floor is easy enough to walk. The scattered signs of human presence are so faint that they feel geological — as much a part of the place's history as the glacial carving that made it. People came, did small things, left. The mountain continued.

---

There is one thing that is unusual.

In the center of the cirque floor, where the braided water channels converge before spreading again toward the open edge, there is a pool. It's small — maybe two meters across, shallow, clear over a bed of dark stone. The water in it is still, or nearly still, while water moves through it continuously. It should be flowing. It should be turbulent, given the volume of water moving through the seeps. But the surface stays flat, reflecting the walls above with mirror clarity, disturbed only by the finest ripple when the wind crosses it.

Looking into the pool is like looking into a second cirque, inverted. The walls reflect downward. The ferns reflect downward. The sky reflects downward, so the pool becomes a window into an upside-down world that is identical to this one but deeper, darker, with the walls extending into infinite distance below the surface. It's a trick of still water and dark stone, nothing more. But standing at the edge of it, with the sound of weeping walls and echoing birds filling the stone bowl, it is hard not to feel that the pool is the point. That everything else — the walls, the water, the life — is arranged around this one quiet surface that shows you the world turned inside out.

The birds avoid it. They use the seeps, the moss, the ferns, the ledges. They fly over the pool without stopping. It might be that there's nothing for them there — no insects, no perch. Or it might be something else. They know what they know.

---

The cirque changes by the hour. The morning light comes over the eastern wall first, warming the ferns, waking the insects, bringing the birds in waves of activity that fill the space with sound. By midday, the sun is high enough to light the floor directly, and the pool becomes a bright circle of reflected sky. In the afternoon, the western wall catches the light and glows warm while the eastern wall falls into shadow. The seeps on the eastern side slow as the temperature drops. The seeps on the western side increase. The birds shift with the light, moving from one side to the other in a slow rotation that is itself a kind of clock.

By evening, the sound changes. The insect activity drops. The birds go quiet, one by one, their calls echoing longer in the stiller air. The water continues. The walls continue weeping. The pool continues reflecting the darkening sky. The noise of the place never drops below a certain level — the water won't allow it — but it shifts from a busy noise to a lonely one. The sound of a place that is doing its work with no one watching.

It will rain tomorrow, or it won't. The walls will weep either way. The pool will show the upside-down world. The birds will come back in the morning. The wooden post will continue its slow transformation back into the mountain. The violet flowers will open, or they won't, and the ferns will be wet regardless.

The cirque is not waiting for anything. It is not going anywhere. It is two hundred meters of weather and stone and water and life, doing what it does, at the top of a mountain, in a country that may or may not know it's there.
