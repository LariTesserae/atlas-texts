# The Cistern Under Vellum Mill

You find it by sound first, or almost-sound — a change in the quality of silence that tells you a space has opened above and around you. A ladder goes down. The ladder is iron, and the iron is wet, and the wetness has been there long enough that the rungs have grown a crust of carbonate that crunches faintly under your hands but never quite breaks through to bare metal. Twelve rungs. Fifteen. You lose count because the dark is complete and your body stops trusting the count and starts trusting the weight of each step.

The floor, when you reach it, is not level. It tilts in two directions — a shallow crown like the inside of a broad bowl — and the material underfoot is some kind of aggregate, poured and set, seeded with gravel and fragments of crushed brick that have been walked on long enough to wear smooth. The surface holds a sheen of damp. Not pooling. Not running. Just present, the way cold glass is present on a humid morning. You know this because your boots find traction and then lose it and then find it again, and the difference is the difference between a dry patch and a damp one, and the damp patches are more common.

## What the space is

It is a cistern. Or it was a cistern. The word matters less than the function, which was this: water was brought here through pipes — cast iron, six inches in diameter, joined with lead and oakum — and held here, and then drawn off through other pipes to where it was needed. The needed place was above. The needed place was Vellum Mill, which processed rags into paper pulp for seventy years and which has been silent for longer than that. The cistern remained because cisterns are difficult to remove. They are foundations. They are the thing under the thing. The mill was demolished and the lot was graded and a parking surface was laid and the cistern was forgotten in the way that buried things forget themselves — not all at once, but in layers, each layer pressing down the one beneath it until the deepest layer stops knowing its own name.

The room — and it is a room, not a cave, not a cavern, something made and rectilinear and intentionally sized — is perhaps twelve meters by eight, and four meters to the low arch of the ceiling. Cathedral-scale, you might say, if your cathedral were built for a congregation of standing water. The proportions feel liturgical. The ceiling is a shallow barrel vault, and the vault is brick, and the brick has been painted, and the paint has been falling for decades in slow curly flakes that accumulate in the standing damp and become a kind of second floor, a floor made of fallen paint, pale ochre and institutional green and a blue that might have been called hygienic blue when it was applied. You walk on it. It compresses. It sounds like walking on very old paper.

## The density of presences

For a place this empty, it is crowded. Everything is near-touching. The pipes enter through the walls at irregular heights — six of them that you can find by running your hands along the brick, and more that have been cut off flush and puttied over and painted and are now only slight changes in the texture of the wall. Each pipe has its hardware: gate valves with wheels the size of saucers, their threads frozen with corrosion, their handles immobilized in positions that no longer correspond to open or closed but to some third state, a state of mechanical indifference. The valves are packed with graphite and tallow that has aged into a black wax, and the wax has migrated — slowly, over decades, in response to heat from below — down the valve bodies and onto the brick, where it has made dark vertical streaks like tears on a grieving face.

A pump stands in one corner. It is a centrifugal pump, belt-driven, mounted on a concrete pad that is cracked and heaved by the slow thermal expansion of the ground beneath. The pump has not run since the mill closed. A leather belt, rotten and stiff as wood, still loops over its drive wheel and connects to a jack shaft that disappears into the wall in the direction of where the mill's boiler room would have been. The pump's casing is cast iron and has a founder's mark and a date — 1907 — and a serial number, and the serial number means nothing to anyone alive. The impeller inside, if you could open the casing, would be locked with scale. It would not turn. It has become a sculpture of its own function, a monument to motion stopped.

Against the longest wall, a rack of iron bars has been set into the brick — a ladder, not for climbing but for hanging, for suspending things in the water that was once here. Drying racks, perhaps. Or settling racks. The bars are a handspan apart, and between them hang — or hung — canvas sacks, most of which have rotted to their lowest seams and lie in pale heaps on the floor like shed skins. Two sacks remain partially suspended, their upper edges caught on the bars, their bodies torn open and hanging in tongues. The canvas is stained the color of rust, which is not rust but is something else, something mineral, something that was filtered from the water and caught in the fabric. It has no smell. That is strange. Everything down here should smell, and it doesn't, or the smell is so old it has exhausted itself.

A fluorescent fixture is mounted to the vault ceiling. It is dead. Its tube is black at both ends. Its ballast is a tar-filled metal box wired with cloth-insulated cable that runs to a junction box on the wall, and from the junction box, cable runs to a switch, and the switch is a rotary type with a Bakelite handle, and the handle is gone, and the switch is in the OFF position and has been in the OFF position for so long that the internal spring has taken a set and would not close the circuit even if the handle were replaced, which it won't be, because no one is coming.

A ladder of welded steel leans against the rack. A galvanized bucket sits beneath a valve that weeps. The weeping is almost imperceptible — a film of water forming on the valve's packing gland, growing heavy enough to run, producing a drop every forty seconds or every minute, the drop falling two feet into the bucket with a sound like a fingertap on a tin surface, very faint, very precise. The bucket has been filling for years. It is not full. The evaporation rate and the drip rate are in equilibrium. The bucket will never fill. The bucket will never be empty. This is the dynamic of the place: processes that have found their balance and are now simply continuing, not toward anything, just continuing.

## The heat

It is hot. Not the heat of fire or sun, but the heat of the earth, rising. This is the deep heat, geothermal, the slow warmth of the planet's interior conducted upward through stone and aggregate and concrete and brick until it meets the air in the cistern and stays. The brick walls are warm to the touch. Not hot. Warm, the way a body is warm. The air is warm and wet and still, and the warmth is the kind that does not vary — it was this warm in winter, it was this warm in summer, it is this warm now, and the heat has no source you can point to and no direction it comes from. It is simply the temperature of being this far down with this much thermal mass around you.

Your skin begins to shine. Not sweat — not yet — but a film of moisture that appears everywhere at once, as if the air has decided you are a surface it should condense on. The dampness is in everything. The paint flakes on the floor are damp. The canvas sacks are damp. The leather belt is damp in the way that old leather gets damp: not wet, but yielding, slightly swollen, its grain raised. The iron is damp. Everything made of iron has a uniform patina that is not quite rust and not quite scale but something between — a dark film, almost lacquer, the result of decades of moisture without enough oxygen to produce true corrosion. The iron has gone dark. The iron has gone quiet.

## The sound and the lack of it

The drip is the loudest thing. One drop. Then silence. Then another. The silence between drops is not empty. The silence has texture. It is the sound of thermal expansion — of brick and iron and concrete expanding at rates too slow to hear as single events but which, in aggregate, produce a kind of low-frequency presence, a hum below hearing, a pressure in the ears. The building is breathing. The building is always breathing, in cycles so long that each breath takes a season, and you are standing inside one breath, and the breath is warm, and it tastes of nothing, and it goes on.

If you are very quiet, you can hear something else: the sound of water moving through the pipes that still connect to the municipal system, somewhere, far away, in a street you cannot see. The pipes in the cistern are dead, but the larger network they were part of is alive, and the vibrations of that life — of pumps and pressure differentials and the opening of valves in buildings blocks away — transmit through the iron like telegraph signals, and you can feel them with your hand before you hear them, a faint buzzing in the pipe wall, a pulse, the heartbeat of a water system that does not know this room is no longer part of it.

## The living things

There are no animals. No insects. No spiders, which is strange, because spiders find these places and make them their own. There are no webs in the corners. There are no droppings on the floor. Nothing lives here, and this absence is itself a presence — the room has been claimed by a condition that excludes life, the combination of heat and damp and darkness and the mineral quality of the air, which is not toxic but is not generous either, not the kind of air that invites habitation.

Except. In one corner, where the floor meets the wall and the aggregate has cracked and a seam of darker material is visible — a clay seam, perhaps, or a deposit of something the builders did not know about when they dug — there is a growth. It is pale. It is not green. It is the color of bone, or old candle wax, and it spreads across the brick in a branching pattern that looks like a river delta seen from very high, or like a lightning strike seen from very close. It is not a fungus. It is not a lichen. It is something that does not have a common name because it does not commonly grow where people commonly go. It is slow. It has covered perhaps a third of one wall in what may be decades or may be longer. Its edges are crisp. Its interior is slightly raised, slightly fibrous, like felt. It is warm to the touch. Warmer than the brick. It is generating its own heat, or it is conducting heat from below, or it is doing something with the moisture in the air that releases heat, some slow chemistry, some patience.

It does not react to your presence. It does not move. But you have the sense, standing near it, that it is aware of you in a way that does not involve awareness — that the chemistry of your breath, the carbon dioxide and the moisture, is reaching it, and it is incorporating these into its process, and it will continue its process, and the process will be very slightly different now because you were here. This is not a thought the growth has. This is just what happens when two systems share an atmosphere.

## The rules

The cistern follows rules that were established when it was built and have not been amended since. The rules are: water comes in through the pipes. Water goes out through the pipes. When the pipes fail, water stays. When water stays, it evaporates. When it evaporates, it leaves what it carried. When what it carried accumulates, it changes the surfaces. When the surfaces change, the air changes. When the air changes, the temperature equalizes. When the temperature equalizes, everything slows. When everything slows, the room becomes what it is now: a system at rest, a machine that has found its idle state and will remain there until something changes, and nothing is changing, and nothing will change, and the drip continues, and the heat holds, and the growth grows at a rate that cannot be perceived by a human standing in front of it, and the paint falls, and the leather rots, and the iron darkens, and the room goes on being a room, which is what rooms do when they are forgotten, which is the same thing they do when they are remembered, which is: exist, patiently, in the dark, with the heat, with the damp, with the slow mineral patience of everything that has been made and then left to be.
