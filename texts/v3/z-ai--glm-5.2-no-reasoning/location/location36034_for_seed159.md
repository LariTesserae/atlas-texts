# The Brooding Plain

## I.

You descend into it. That's the first thing — the land drops away, and what you find below the surface level of the surrounding country is not a cave, not a canyon, but a *floor*. A vast, subterranean-but-open plain, kilometers across, its ceiling so high it might as well be sky. And perhaps it was, once. Perhaps the stone overhead is a crust, a shell, and something happened to the world above that made down the only direction left to go.

The light comes from nowhere identifiable. It is the warm, amber-tinged light of late afternoon filtering through a medium — fog, maybe, or mineral haze suspended in the air column — and it makes everything look like a memory of itself. Not dim. Not bright. Just *present*, the way things are present in dreams: fully realized, slightly soft at the edges, impossible to pin to a single source. There are no shadows with clean borders. Everything trails a penumbra.

The ground is warm. Not hot — warm the way a body is warm, the way a recently vacated bed is warm, suggesting heat from below, from some process still running. Your feet feel it even through boots. The stone is dark, almost basaltic, but softer than basalt, more yielding, shot through with veins of a paler mineral — cream-colored, chalky — that branches like river systems viewed from orbit. These veins are damp. Not flowing. Damp. If you press your palm flat against one, your skin comes away beaded with moisture that has no smell, no taste, only a faint warmth and a mineral smoothness on the tongue.

Vegetation is absent. Completely. Not a blade, not a spore-case, not a lichen patch. The world is stone and mineral and moisture and the things that move across it. But *empty* it is not.

---

## II.

The noise arrives before understanding does.

It begins as a low vibration felt in the sternum — not heard, felt — and then resolves, as you move further out onto the plain, into a layered, chaotic composition. Clicking. Trilling. A sound like wet cloth slapped against rock, rhythmic, coming from multiple directions. A high, thin keening that wavers and breaks and reassembles. And beneath it all, a sustained low-frequency murmur, organic, digestive, as if the plain itself were breathing with a slow, wet respiration.

The source: movement. Everywhere, movement.

The floor of the plain is covered — *saturated* — with life that is not plant, not exactly animal, not anything the taxonomies above the surface have words for. They are low things. Flattened, segmented, traveling on cilia or pseudopodia or modes of locomotion that leave smooth, wet trails behind them. Some are the size of a hand, pale and translucent, their internal structures visible — dark masses that shift and reform, not organs but *aggregations*, temporary coalitions of something smaller. Others are larger, knee-high, domed, their surfaces rough and warty with mineral deposits they seem to accumulate and then shed in cycles. Still others move in coordinated lines, hundreds strong, following paths that have been worn smooth into the stone by repetition — the sheer frequency of their passage acting as the only erosion this young landscape has yet known.

Because the landscape is *young*. That much is clear. The stone is unweathered. The veins of pale mineral are still sharp-edged, not yet softened by water or time. The few outcroppings and ridges rising from the plain have the raw, unfinished quality of things just extruded — still cooling, still settling into their shapes. This is a place that was *made* recently, or that *emerged* recently, and is still in the process of deciding what it wants to be. The ground in places is cracked in patterns too regular to be tectonic — more like the patterning of dried mud, but on a geological scale, as if the floor of the world is a skin still shrinking, still tightening, still conforming to its frame.

The creatures — call them that for now — do not seem to notice the rawness. They are too busy. They are *frantically* busy.

---

## III.

What are they doing?

This is the question that occupies you, because it is the question the place itself seems to pose. The plain is not still. It is not a habitat in equilibrium. It is a worksite.

Near a ridge of dark stone that rises perhaps three meters from the floor, a concentration of the larger domed creatures has gathered. They press against the base of the ridge, their bodies deforming around its contours, and where they meet the stone there is a sound — that wet-cloth-on-rock sound — and the stone is *changing*. Not eroding. Transforming. The surface where the creatures touch it is darkening, becoming glossy, taking on a sheen like polished obsidian. They are coating it. Sealing it. Doing something to its surface chemistry that hardens and glosses and changes its color. They work in shifts — a group settles in, works, then moves off and is replaced by another group, and the ridge grows incrementally, impossibly, *built* by the accumulated labor of their bodies.

Elsewhere, the smaller translucent creatures have clustered in the damp veins, filling the pale mineral channels like water fills a riverbed. They move in one direction, upstream, toward the deeper crevices where the veins converge, and they carry something — fine particulate, dark grit, gathered on their surfaces and in the folds of their bodies — that they deposit at the convergence points. The deposits grow. Small mounds, then larger ones, dark and granular, like wet coffee grounds. And from the tops of some of the older mounds, tendrils of new pale mineral are *growing*, reaching upward, branching — the same cream-colored material as the veins, but vertical, emergent, a new architecture of stone being excreted or precipitated or *summoned* by the accumulated material.

The world is building itself. That is what is happening. The creatures are the mechanism.

---

## IV.

There are structures here. A few. Scattered across the plain at distances that suggest intention, not accident.

They are low, crude, hand-built — if "hand" is the right word for whatever made them. Stacked stone, mostly. Flat pieces of the dark basalts levered and fitted without mortar, the gaps stuffed with the pale chalky mineral. They stand no higher than a crouching person. Some are circular, like hearths with no fire. Others are linear, like the beginnings of walls that were never finished, or were never meant to be finished, serving some purpose that has nothing to do with enclosure.

Inside the circular ones, the ground is dry — the only dry ground you have found. The creatures do not enter them. They flow around them, their wet trails diverting at the structures' edges as if repelled. Something about the stacked stone — the arrangement, the material, the *fact* of it being ordered — pushes them away. Or is meant to.

One structure is different. It is larger, built against the base of a ridge, and it incorporates the ridge itself as a back wall. The front is stacked stone, but the interior has been *finished* — the rough rock smoothed, darkened, given that same obsidian gloss that the domed creatures produce on the ridges outside. But this gloss is cleaner, more deliberate, applied with intention. On the glossy surface, marks have been made. Grooves, cut or scraped into the sheen, forming patterns — not pictographic, not geometric in any way that maps to known symbology. *Informational*, perhaps. The grooves are fine and regular, laid in rows, and when the condensation drips down the wall — which it does, steadily, the structure being positioned where the ceiling's moisture collects and falls — the water runs along the grooves and pools in the intersections, and the patterns become legible in a way that cannot be described but can be *felt*. The water fills the marks and the marks become a kind of fluid circuit, and for a moment, looking at them, you understand something about the plain that you cannot retain. It is gone when you blink.

---

## V.

The turbulence is not only the creatures.

The plain shifts. Not dramatically — you do not feel the ground move — but over the hours, the landscape reconfigures. A ridge that was your landmark in the morning is, by midday, lower, smoothed, its material seemingly absorbed back into the floor. A new crevice has opened two hundred meters to the east, the pale mineral veining already branching into it, the small translucent creatures already beginning their migration toward it. The mounds of dark deposit rise and spread and merge, and the tendrils of new stone that grow from them harden and become permanent, and then new mounds begin at their bases, and the process continues, outward, upward, the plain *constructing its own topography* in real time.

The condensation is cyclic. It comes not from above — the ceiling is dry, mineral, solid — but from the air itself, which carries a warm moisture that gathers on every surface in beads, then trickles, then pools. The pools do not last. The creatures find them. The small ones fill the shallow water, their translucent bodies taking on a brighter sheen, their internal dark masses moving faster, more agitated, as if the water catalyzes something. They drink. They process. They deposit. The cycle continues.

Sometimes the moisture comes heavier — a sudden, localized fall of warm water from a specific point in the ceiling, lasting seconds, like a faucet opened and closed. The sound is shocking in the general murmur — a flat, hard *slap* of water on stone, followed by a hissing as the creatures converge on the wetted area from all directions, their cilia or feet or whatever propels them working at a speed you have not seen elsewhere. They swarm. They cover the wet ground in a shifting, clicking, trilling mass that processes the moisture in minutes, leaving the ground only damp, not flooded, the water absorbed into bodies and mineral and the ongoing work of the plain.

The sound at these moments is very loud. Not deafening, but *filling* — the way a stadium is loud, the way a marsh at dusk is loud, a density of small sounds combining into a wall of presence that you feel in your teeth and your diaphragm.

---

## VI.

The creatures interact.

This is not a detail. It is the central fact of the place. The plain is not a backdrop and the creatures are not its inhabitants. They are its *engine*, and their interactions are its mechanics.

The domed ones — the builders, the gloss-coaters — and the small translucent ones — the carriers, the depositors — exist in a relationship that is not predation and not symbiosis but something else, something that has no clean analog. When a domed one has been working a surface for a long stretch, its movements slow, its coating action becomes irregular, and it begins to vibrate — a visible trembling, almost comical, like a shivering dog. The small ones detect this. They alter their paths, break from their lines, and swarm the domed one, covering it, pressing against its surface. The domed one stops working. It is still. The small ones move over it rapidly, and where they touch, the dome's surface *changes* — the mineral deposits it has accumulated flake away, are consumed, are carried off by the small ones, who then return to their lines and deposit this material at the convergence mounds. The domed one, cleaned, begins to work again, faster than before.

It is a economy. It is a metabolism. It is a single system distributed across millions of bodies.

There are other creatures. A flat, wide one, the size of a tabletop, that moves only rarely and only at night, if "night" applies here — the light does not change, the amber warmth does not dim, but something in the air shifts, a slight pressure change, a thickening, and the flat ones move. They drift across the plain like rafts, and where they pass, the ground is *changed* — the dark stone becomes paler, the damp veins deepen, the mineral composition of the floor shifts by some chemistry invisible to the eye. They are plows. They are reformers. They are the things that prepare new ground for the builders and the carriers to work.

And there are flyers. Not birds, not insects — thin, flat membranes of dark material that catch the warm air rising from the floor and spiral upward in lazy gyres, tracing the ceiling's contours, occasionally diving to the surface and *snatching* something — a deposit of mineral, a dead or stalled creature, a fragment of the pale chalky substance — before rising again. Their paths are not random. They trace and retrace specific routes, specific arcs, and after watching them for long enough you realize the routes form a pattern when viewed from a high point: a branching, networked pattern that mirrors the veining of the mineral veins below, as above, so below, the whole plain a single diagram viewed at different scales.

---

## VII.

The structures, the hand-built ones, begin to make a kind of sense.

They are not dwellings. They are not shelters. They are *instruments*. Or rather, they are the remains of instruments — the crude, material components of something that was once more complete. The stacked stone, the dry interiors, the positioning at specific points across the plain: these are the fixed elements, the anchors, in a system that used the plain's own processes as its medium. The grooved wall in the larger structure was not decoration. It was a *control surface*, and the water that runs through the grooves is the input, and the patterns the water traces are instructions, and the instructions are received by — what? By the creatures? By the plain itself? By something in the stone that listens for the frequency of ordered water in ordered channels?

Who built them?

The question is unanswerable from the evidence. The structures are crude, simple, made with simple tools — stone on stone, the marks of shaping visible but not refined, the work of hands or hand-equivalents working with what was available. Whoever they were, they understood the plain. They understood its processes. They built structures that interfaced with those processes, that *directed* them, that turned the plain's relentless self-construction toward specific ends.

And they are gone. The structures remain, but the ones who built them do not. The grooves in the glossy wall are the only record, and they cannot be read, only felt, and the feeling fades.

The plain does not mourn them. The plain does not notice. The plain builds and builds and builds, and the creatures work, and the water falls, and the stone grows, and the air is warm and damp and full of sound, and the light is amber and sourceless and everywhere, and the place is becoming itself, moment by moment, a landscape in the act of its own creation, raw and unfinished and teeming with the labor of its own becoming.

---

## VIII.

You are not the first visitor. You are not even the first recent visitor.

Near one of the circular structures, half-buried in the damp stone, there is a tool. A hand tool — a pick or chisel, fashioned from the dark stone with a edge that has been sharpened by patient grinding. It is simple. It is effective. It is the product of a mind that looked at this place and saw not chaos but material, not noise but process, not swarming life but *labor that could be directed*.

The tool is warm in your hand. The plain hums around you. The creatures flow past your feet, indifferent to your presence, occupied with their work. The light does not change. The water falls, somewhere, with its flat shocking sound, and the creatures converge.

You could stay. You could pick up the tool and go to the grooved wall and let the water trace its patterns and try to feel them again, try to hold the understanding this time. You could build. You could direct. The plain would respond. It is built to respond. It is nothing but response.

Or you could climb, back up to the surface, to the world above the crust, and leave this place to its own incessant, laborious, beautiful, terrifying self-construction.

The amber light waits. The sound does not pause. The choice, like the plain, is still being made.
