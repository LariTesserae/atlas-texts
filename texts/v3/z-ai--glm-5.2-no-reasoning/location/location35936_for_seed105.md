# The Drowned Mesas

The first thing is the sound. Before understanding — before the eyes adjust and the cold begins to register in the bones — there is the sound. A low, polyphonic drone that rises from everywhere at once, reverberating through the water column like a choir sustaining a single chord that never resolves. It is the sound of the place itself breathing.

---

The water is black, but not empty-black. It carries a faint luminescence — not from animals, for there are none, but from the vegetation itself, a cold bioluminescence that the flora has evolved over countless submerged generations. The light is blue-green, weak, insufficient for human eyes to find color, just enough to define shapes as darker or lighter masses against the surrounding dark. It pulses. Slowly. On a cycle that might be minutes long, might be longer — hard to measure without a clock, and clocks have never worked well here.

The water moves constantly. Not currents exactly, or not only currents. There are upwellings from below, warm fingers threading upward through the cold — relative warmth, four or five degrees above the surrounding near-freezing body — and these upwellings carry dissolved minerals that the plant life has learned to expect. The water is not ocean. There is no salt. It is fresh, mineral-heavy, and very old. It has been here a long time. The flood, if it was a flood, is so far in the past that the concept of "before the flood" has no living witness, not even in the fossil layer. This is simply how the world is.

---

The terrain — if you can call it that, underwater — is a series of high plateaus, the tops of what were once mesas, rising to within twenty or thirty meters of the surface. The surface itself is a mystery; no one who has descended this deep has seen it, and the flora has adapted to the eternal dark so completely that the idea of a sun seems like a story from another universe. The mesas are flat-topped, steep-sided, made of a pale sandstone that has been weathering into the water for so long that the mineral content of the lake is measurably different from what it would be if the stone were inert. The stone is not inert. It is dissolving, slowly, on a timescale that makes the word "erosion" feel too urgent. Settling, perhaps. The mesas are settling into themselves.

Between the plateaus, the bottom drops away. The channels are deep — hundreds of meters deep, possibly more. No one has touched the true bottom. The walls of these channels are where the vegetation is thickest, clinging to the vertical stone in layers that have been building for so long that the older growth has become a kind of secondary substrate, a living scaffolding upon which newer growth attaches. The result is that the channel walls are not stone but biomass — a composite of rock and root and stem and the accumulated corpses of generations, all of it cold, all of it faintly glowing, all of it humming.

---

The vegetation is the dominant life form. There is nothing else.

This needs emphasis, because it takes time to fully understand: there is nothing else. No fish. No invertebrates. No microbes, or at least none that function as fauna in any recognizable way. The ecosystem is a closed loop of plant-mineral-water. The flora photosynthesizes — or rather, chemosynthesizes, since there is no light worth speaking of — drawing energy from the mineral upwellings and the slow chemical gradients between stone and water. It grows. It dies. It decomposes where it falls, dissolving back into mineral-rich water that feeds the next generation. The cycle is complete without any animal presence. It has been complete for longer than the concept of "animal" has existed here.

The plants are strange. Not in the way that deep-sea creatures are strange — no translucent bodies, no oversized eyes, no bioluminescent lures. That kind of strangeness is an animal's response to darkness. This is a plant's response, and it is different.

The dominant form is something that might be called a frond-tree: a tall, slender trunk, flexible, swaying constantly in the shifting water, with broad flat appendages that spread horizontally from the upper portions. These appendages are the photosynthetic — chemosynthetic — surfaces, and they are oriented to catch the mineral upwellings, spreading like dark parasols in the weak current. They glow at their edges. The glow is not for anything. It is not to attract, not to communicate, not to warn. It is a byproduct of the chemosynthetic process, as natural and purposeless as the heat of a compost pile. But it is beautiful. The edges of the fronds trace pale blue-green outlines against the black, and when the current shifts and the fronds overlap, the patterns shift and recombine, making it seem as though the water is full of slow-moving geometric diagrams.

Beneath the frond-trees, carpeting the flat tops of the plateaus, is a low dense growth — a mat of interlocking threads, fibrous, with a texture like wet felt. This mat is the primary structure of the ecosystem. It holds the sediment in place, it filters the water, it provides the substrate upon which the frond-tree seeds attach and germinate. It is always growing, always dying, always being replaced. When you kneel on the plateau top — if you had knees, if you were here, if you could — you would be kneeling on something alive and something dead and something being born, all at once, all in the same handful of material.

There are other forms. A vine-like organism that drapes from the channel-wall biomass, hanging in the dark water like curtains, swaying. A sphere-form that rolls slowly across the plateau edges, dislodged by current, collecting mineral deposits on its surface like a snowball accumulating layers. A crust-form that grows on the pale stone of the mesa walls, darker where the mineral upwellings touch it, lighter where the water is more pure.

---

The sound — the drone — comes from the vegetation.

It is not a sound made deliberately. The frond-trees, swaying in the current, rub against each other. Their trunks are fibrous and slightly rough, and the friction of one trunk against another produces a low-frequency vibration that travels efficiently through water. With millions of frond-trees across the plateau tops and channel walls, all swaying, all rubbing, the vibrations combine and recombine, producing the sustained chord that fills the water. It is never the same chord twice. The current shifts, the trees sway at different frequencies, the harmonics change. But it is always there. It is the loudest thing. It is the only thing.

The volume is considerable. The water carries sound well, and the drone is intense enough to feel in the chest, in the teeth, in the bones of the skull. It is not painful. It is pervasive. After enough time, you stop hearing it and start being it — your body vibrates at the same frequency as the water, and you become part of the instrument.

When the upwellings surge — and they surge on a cycle that seems connected to something deeper, something tectonic or thermodynamic — the current increases, the frond-trees sway more violently, the friction increases, and the drone swells. These are the loudest moments. The water itself seems to shudder. The glow of the frond-edges brightens, as though the increased metabolic activity triggered by the mineral surge produces more light as a byproduct. The whole region, for the duration of the surge — minutes, sometimes longer — becomes brighter and louder and more alive, a periodic crescendo that is the closest thing this place has to weather.

---

The built structures are few, and they are old.

On the highest plateau — the one that comes closest to the unseen surface — there are a handful of stone structures. They are small. Shelters, perhaps. Or markers. Or something else entirely, something whose purpose cannot be inferred from form alone. They are built from the same pale sandstone as the mesas, fitted together without mortar, the joints tight enough to have survived the long submersion. The walls are low, chest-height or less, and the roofs, if there were roofs, are gone. The vegetation has colonized them. The fibrous mat grows over the walls like moss over a gravestone. Frond-trees have rooted in the corners, their trunks emerging through what may once have been doorways.

There are tools. Or objects that might be tools. Stone implements, shaped with deliberate effort, lying where they were set down — on a shelf inside one structure, on the flat ground beside another. They are simple: a grinding stone, a cutting edge, a shaped weight that might have been used for tying something down. The technology is stone-age. Not in the pejorative sense — in the literal sense. Whoever made these worked with stone and fiber and perhaps wood, and they worked well, with evident skill, but with limited materials.

Whoever they were, they are gone. There is no record of them beyond the structures and the tools. No bones. No preserved remains. The water and the vegetation have consumed everything organic. What remains is the stone, and even the stone is being slowly colonized by the crust-form and the fibrous mat. In another few thousand years, the structures will be indistinguishable from the surrounding terrain. The mesas will have swallowed them.

---

The cold is a constant. The water is between one and three degrees above freezing, depending on the proximity to an upwelling. It is not the dramatic, painful cold of arctic water. It is the steady, patient cold of deep time. It does not shock. It seeps. It finds the warmth in anything and takes it, slowly, without urgency. The vegetation does not mind. The vegetation has no blood to cool, no metabolism to regulate. It is adapted to the cold, optimized for it. Its chemosynthetic processes are tuned to the near-freezing temperatures and the specific mineral content of the upwellings. Warmth would disrupt it. Warmth would kill it.

This is a cold place. A dark place. A place where the dominant senses would be touch and sound, where sight is almost useless, where smell is the smell of mineral water and nothing else. It is not hostile, exactly. There is nothing here that wants to harm. But there is nothing here that wants to help, either. The place is indifferent. It is a system, a process, a loop, and it has been running for so long that it has achieved a kind of equilibrium that feels, from the outside, like peace.

---

The scale is regional. The plateau system extends past the limit of perception in every horizontal direction. The channels between the mesas are wide enough that the far walls are lost in the dark. The upwellings rise from depths that cannot be measured from here. The surface — if there is a surface, if the water ends at some point above — is a theoretical concept, not an experienced one. This is a world of middle depths: too deep to see the sky, too shallow to be the abyss. A region. A province. A place with its own character, its own weather-patterns, its own slow rhythms.

And it is sparse. For all the vegetation, for all the frond-trees and their glowing edges, the dominant experience of the place is emptiness. The plateau tops are wide, and the frond-trees are spaced apart — far enough that you can walk between them without touching their trunks, if you could walk, if you were here. The channel walls are thick with biomass, but the channels themselves are open water, vast volumes of blackness with nothing in them. The structures are isolated, separated by long stretches of bare stone. There is space here. There is room. The place is not crowded. It is a landscape of distances, of gaps, of the silence between the drone's sustained notes — and even the drone, for all its volume, is a single sound, not a chorus. It is one thing, repeated, sustained, varied only by the shifting of the current.

---

The strangeness of the place is not dramatic. There are no impossible geometries, no reality-warping effects, no creatures that defy description. The strangeness is structural, existential. It is a world without animals. A world where the plants are the ecosystem, the entire ecosystem, and they have been the entire ecosystem for so long that the concept of "animal" is not missing but unnecessary. It is a world where the loudest sound is the sound of plants rubbing against each other. Where the only light is the light of metabolic byproduct. Where the only architecture is the slow architecture of stone dissolving and biomass accumulating and water circulating and time passing.

It is strange because it is complete. Most ecosystems are chains — this eats that, which eats the other, which depends on the first. This place is a loop, a single loop, and it closes on itself. Stone to water to plant to stone. There is no link missing because there are no links. There is only the loop.

And the loop hums. It hums loudly, in the dark, in the cold, and it has been humming for a very long time.
