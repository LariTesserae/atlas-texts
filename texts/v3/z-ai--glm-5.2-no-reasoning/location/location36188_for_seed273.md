# The Terraces of Warm Grain

## What it is

You are standing on the outside of a wall. The wall is the world.

Everything here is built. Not built *on* — built *as*. There is no ground beneath the terraces because the terraces are the ground. They are the ground the way a staircase is a floor: entirely, functionally, but with the honest admission that someone made each rise and each tread on purpose. The surface under your feet is a composite of crushed slag and fired clay, warm to the touch, the color of old bread. It has been walked on for so long that the center of every path has worn smooth and faintly concave, a shallow gutter that guides rainwater from one level to the next.

The terraces descend in great stacked shelves — hundreds of them, maybe more — down the flanks of something that is either a very tall artificial mountain or a very ordinary mountain that someone wrapped entirely in construction. From where you stand, midway up, you can see the shelves stepping downward until the haze of distance and the perpetual faint steam of the lower levels swallow them. Above, they continue upward, narrowing, the walls between them growing taller, the paths narrower, until the whole structure disappears into a band of light-cloud that isn't quite weather and isn't quite atmosphere.

You cannot find the edge of it. Walking in any direction along any terrace eventually brings you to a junction — a ramp, a stair, a covered passage — that connects to the next level. The place is regional in extent. It is not a building. It is a *region* that happens to be a building.

## How it is built

The construction is old enough to have stopped looking like construction. The retaining walls between terraces are made of brick, but the brick has weathered into a spectrum of ochre, rust, pale cream, and muted black depending on what was in the clay when it was fired and what has leached into it since. Mortar has crumbled in places and been replaced — not always with more mortar. Some gaps are packed with river mud. Some are stuffed with plant matter that has composted into a dark humus and now supports its own tiny ecosystems. Some have been left open and have become homes for the birds.

There is metal here too. Iron railings along the upper terraces, green-black with patina, their finials shaped like seeds or drops. Down lower, the railings give way to wooden fences, and the wood is so old it has gone silver. Nails have rusted into black threads that stain the grain in streaks.

The bones of the structure are industrial. You can see it in the regularity of the spacing between load-bearing pillars, in the way drainage channels are engineered into every surface, in the iron pipes that run along the undersides of overhangs and carry water from the condensation traps above. Someone designed this. Someone calculated load tolerances and flow rates and the optimal angle for a ramp. That someone lived a very long time ago.

The technology is industrial in the patient way — not the frenetic way. There are no electronics. There are engines, but they are large, slow machines that live in the lower terraces and do work that is too heavy for human hands: pumping water, crushing grain, pressing oil from seeds. They run on heat — concentrated sunlight and the combustion of dried plant material — and they throb rather than whine. You feel them through the soles of your feet before you hear them. They are maintained because they must be, and their operators treat them the way farmers treat draft animals: with respect, with routine, and without sentimentality.

Electricity exists, but barely. A few of the upper terraces have wires strung between poles, feeding a handful of bare bulbs that hang outside what appear to be workshops. The light they give is thin and yellow and only matters at night. During the day, the sun does the work.

## The air and light

It is warm. Not hot — warm. The kind of warmth that sits on your skin like a cloth. The sun is bright here, and because every surface is a pale earthen color, the light bounces. It reflects off the walls, off the ground, off the underside of overhangs. The effect is that there is no sharp shadow anywhere. Light comes from above and from the sides and from below, diffused through terracotta and steam until the whole environment glows like the inside of a kiln.

The brightness is softened by water. Not rain exactly — not usually — but a persistent dampness that rises from the lower levels where the engines pump water through the condensation channels. The water flows through open troughs cut into the terrace floors, and where the sun hits it, it evaporates. The vapor rises through the levels, and as it coels against the higher, shadier terraces, it condenses on surfaces. Every wall on the middle and upper levels is faintly wet to the touch. Metal railings are beaded. Brick faces are darkened. The leaves of plants drip with it.

When rain does come, it comes in brief, heavy bursts that last ten or fifteen minutes and then vanish. The terraces are designed for this. Every surface slopes slightly toward a drainage channel. The water is collected, directed, and sent down to the engines. Nothing is wasted.

The air smells of warm clay, wet iron, green sap, and the particular sweet-sour smell of grain being processed somewhere below.

## The vegetation

This is a built place, but it is *full*. Every horizontal surface is claimed. Every wall has something growing on it or against it. Every crack is a garden.

The dominant plants are not trees but something between a shrub and a vine — woody-stemmed, broad-leaved, and aggressive. They drape over railing edges, climb walls, and colonize the gutters. Their leaves are dark green, thick, and waxy, and they catch the condensation in ways that make them shine in the light. They flower in a pale, unremarkable way, producing small white clusters that smell faintly of honey and decay.

Between the shrubs and along the path edges, there are cultivated plants: grains, mostly. A dense, tall grass with heavy seed-heads that have been bred for size. It grows in the shallow soil that has accumulated on the wider terraces — soil that was built over centuries from composted plant matter, human waste, and the dust that blows in from somewhere outside. The grain is the reason for much of this. The terraces grow it. The engines process it. The people eat it.

There are also fruit-bearing things — not quite trees, not quite bushes, trained along the walls on trellises of wire and stick. Their fruit is small and bright, somewhere between a plum and a fig, and it grows in clusters that the birds get to first more often than not.

And there are the mosses and the small things. In the permanent damp of the condensation zone — the mid-to-upper terraces where the vapor settles — every surface that isn't actively walked on grows a fine, dense carpet of moss. It is green, gold, and occasionally rust-red. It softens the edges of everything. The brick walls look like they are upholstered. The drainage channels are lined with it. It absorbs the damp and slowly releases it through the day.

Nothing about this vegetation is wild. It is all, in some sense, planted or at least permitted. But it has been growing here for so long, and has adapted so thoroughly to the specific conditions of each terrace, that the line between cultivation and colonization has dissolved. The plants are not guests. They are residents.

## The animals

You hear the birds before you see them. There are many — dozens of species at least — and they are loud. The acoustics of the terraces amplify them: sound bounces between the walls and the overhangs, and the result is a layered, reverberant callsong that is the dominant sound of the place. The birds nest in the walls, in the gaps where mortar has fallen, in the thickets of the woody shrubs, and under the eaves of the workshops. They are small and fast and colored in the palette of the terraces — rust, cream, grey, and a startling blue that seems to come from nowhere and belongs to a species that lives only on the highest terraces where the condensation is heaviest.

There are other animals. Lizards, thin and quick, that bask on the sun-warmed bricks and dart into the moss when disturbed. Insects everywhere — the air is thick with small flying things that feed on the moisture and the nectar and each other. Bees work the flowers. Something that is not quite a spider but does the same work builds webs in the corners of the drainage channels.

In the lower levels, where the engines are, there are larger animals. Something like a goat, adapted to the stairs, sure-footed and calm, used for milk and occasionally for meat. They are kept in small herds on the wider terraces and their bells are part of the sound — a low, irregular clanking that you stop hearing after a while but that is always there.

There are cats. They are not pets. They are part of the system. They manage the rodents that would otherwise eat the stored grain. They move along the walls at dusk with an absolute confidence that suggests they own the place and merely tolerate the rest.

## The sound

It is loud, but it is a layered loud. Nothing here is silent, but nothing is screaming.

The base layer is the engines. From below, a low, rhythmic thudding — not fast, not urgent. Once per second, perhaps. It comes up through the brick and into your feet. It is the heartbeat of the place, and after an hour you stop hearing it consciously, but you feel it if you stop.

Above that: water. The drainage channels produce a constant, varying trickle. On the wider terraces, where the water flows freely, it is a murmur. Where it drops from one level to the next through a pipe or a gap, it is a small, bright splash. The condensation drips from leaves and railings and eaves — not in rhythm, but in a scattered, irregular patter that is somehow more present than the channels.

Above that: birds. Always birds. They call from every direction, and the walls make their calls overlap and multiply. Occasionally a flock will flush from a thicket — a sudden explosive sound of wings — and then settle back to calling.

And the wind. At this elevation, there is always wind, but the terraces break it. It does not blow through; it eddies. It pushes around corners, rattles loose shutters, hums through the iron pipes, and dies. Then comes again from another direction.

And the bells of the goats, and the creak of the workshops, and the faint, distant clatter of someone on a higher terrace hammering something that needs fixing.

You cannot find a quiet spot. Even the interior spaces — the covered passages, the storage rooms cut into the retaining walls — have the background thud of the engines and the dripping of condensation. Silence is not a thing that exists here.

## The people

There are people. You see them at a distance, mostly. A figure on a higher terrace, moving slowly along a path, checking something. Two people on the level below, working at a grain-drying rack — spreading the seed-heads, turning them. A child's voice from somewhere, shouting something that echoes and is swallowed.

They are not many, but they are present. They wear clothes the color of the terraces — undyed or plant-dyed in ochre and rust and green. They move at the pace of people who know their environment and are not afraid of it and are not in a hurry. They have been here for generations. Their ancestors built the engines. Their ancestors' ancestors built the terraces, or maybe just *maintained* them — the origin is unclear, and they don't seem to care.

They live in the structures that are built into the retaining walls — shallow rooms, cool and dark, with doors of wood and iron. Some have windows. Most don't. They cook on the terraces, on small clay stoves, and the smell of the cooking mixes with the smell of the grain and the damp clay and the green.

They are not poor. They are not rich. They are *situated*. They are where they are supposed to be, doing what they are supposed to be doing, and the place does what it is supposed to do: it holds them, it feeds them, it keeps them warm and damp and surrounded by life.

## The rules

This is a place that runs on patience.

Nothing here is fast. The engines thud once a second and have thudded once a second for longer than anyone can remember. The grain grows on a cycle set by the condensation and the sun, and it grows at the speed it grows. The moss advances by millimeters. The walls wear by fractions of millimeters per year. The drainage channels are re-cut when they need to be re-cut, and the person who re-cuts them uses the same techniques and the same tools as the person who cut them a hundred years ago.

Change happens, but it happens slowly. A terrace wall develops a crack. It is patched with mud and left. The patch holds for five years, then ten, then it fails and is patched again. A shrub dies on a railing and is replaced by a younger shrub of the same species. An engine's piston wears out and is recast in the foundry on the lowest terrace and reinstalled and the engine resumes its thudding.

The system is not fragile because it has no expectations of perfection. Things break. Things are fixed. The fixing is part of the system. The breaking is part of the system. The slowness is the system.

There is no wild here, but there is no sterility either. Everything is built, and everything built is being slowly reclaimed by the life that has been invited in — the moss, the birds, the lizards, the cats. The reclamation is not a failure. It is the plan. The built environment here was never meant to exclude the non-human. It was meant to provide a structure *for* it. The terraces are a frame. The life fills the frame.

The one rule that everything follows: water moves down. The condensation rises from the engines, settles on the upper terraces, drips to the channels, flows to the lower terraces, and is pumped back up by the engines. The cycle is the architecture. The cycle is the weather. The cycle is the world.

## What it feels like to be there

Warm. Bright. Damp. Surrounded.

The light comes from everywhere and nowhere. The sound is constant and varied. The air is wet enough that your skin is always faintly sticky and dry enough that you are never cold. You are always on a path, always on a surface that someone made, and the surface is always slightly worn, slightly imperfect, slightly alive with moss and insects and the patient residue of a million footfalls.

You are always between levels. You are never at the top — the top is lost in cloud and condensation. You are never at the bottom — the bottom is lost in steam and engine-heat and the distance of the descent. You are always in the middle, where the terraces are widest, where the moss is thickest, where the grain grows, where the people live, where the birds are loudest.

You are inside a machine that has become a landscape. Or a landscape that has become a machine. The distinction does not hold here. It hasn't held for a very long time.

The place is old. Not ancient — old. It has been here long enough to have settled into itself, to have found its own equilibrium, to have stopped being a project and started being a *place*. It will be here long after the people who currently live in it are gone, and the moss will grow over their doors, and the birds will nest in their rooms, and the engines will thud until the pistons finally seize, and then the water will stop rising, and the condensation will stop falling, and the moss will dry, and something else will begin.

But not yet. Not now. Now the engines thud. The water flows. The grain ripens. The birds call. The sun bounces off every surface and the air is warm and wet and the terraces hold it all, hold everything, in their patient, earthen, endless arms.
