# The Conclave of Ten Thousand Mouths

## The First Impression

You arrive through a door that was not built for you. It is three meters tall, in a frame that has no frame — just a vertical interruption in the air that decided, at some point in its existence, to be passable. You don't remember walking through it. You remember deciding to, and then being here.

Here is a chamber. No — that word implies walls, and the walls are a courtesy this place does not extend. There are *boundaries*. Something keeps the atmosphere in, keeps you from falling in whatever direction would constitute down, but it is not architecture. It is more like the absence of permission to leave. A subtle insistence. The air tastes of ozone and something sweeter — crushed stems, maybe, the green-smell of living things pressed open.

And the sound hits you.

It is not noise. That is the wrong word, because noise implies chaos without grammar. This is deafening, yes, physically overwhelming — your eardrums flex involuntarily, the tiny muscles in your middle ear contracting in a futile attempt to protect the cochlea — but it is *structured*. Layered. A thousand voices singing in a language you almost recognize, each one at a different pitch, a different tempo, none of them in sync, and yet the aggregate sound has a texture like weather. It moves. It has fronts. There are moments when the wall of sound condenses into something almost rhythmic, a pulse you feel in your sternum before you hear it, and then it dissolves again into the roar.

You learn quickly: it never stops. It never quietens. The quietest moment here is still louder than a train passing through a tunnel.

## The Terrain

The ground is the first thing you understand, because you have no choice. You are standing on it.

It is not stone. Not soil. Not metal. It is a material that gives very slightly under weight — a deep, slow resilience, like standing on something alive that has chosen not to mind. The surface is warm to the touch despite the coolness of the air, and it is the color of old amber, of tea held up to light. Veins run through it — not mineral veins, not geological deposits, but something more circulatory. They pulse faintly, and the pulse is in time with one of the rhythms in the sound, the slowest one, the one you feel rather than hear.

The terrain is not flat. It folds and rises in organic undulations — ridges like the spine of something vast that fell asleep here, shallow depressions that collect fine particulate matter the way a pond collects leaves. The high points are maybe two meters above the low points. It is a modest landscape, contained, but it is a landscape nonetheless, with its own miniature watersheds — except there is no water.

Nothing here is wet.

The air carries a dry humidity, if that contradiction can stand — the sensation of moisture without moisture itself, the way a desert can feel damp just before a rain that never comes. There is something in the air, a particulate, a suspension, something fine enough to breathe and dense enough to taste, but it is not water. It carries no hydrogen. It carries no oxygen in the way you expect. Your lungs process it anyway. The door that brought you here apparently included some negotiation with your biology that you were not consulted on.

The chamber — let's keep using that word, provisionally — extends perhaps eighty meters in the longest direction, forty in the shortest. Not vast. Not a cavern or a canyon. More the size of a small auditorium or a very generous church nave. But the emptiness makes it feel larger. The volume above you is immense — at least thirty meters to the ceiling, and the ceiling is not a ceiling but a slow curvature, like the inside of a skull, like the interior of a dome that someone forgot to build. The amber material continues upward, growing more translucent as it rises, until at the apex it is nearly transparent, and through it you can see something that is not sky.

Light enters through this upper membrane. It is filtered, attenuated, shifted slightly toward the blue-green, but it is recognizably *light* — directional, warm enough to cast shadows, steady. It is not daylight. It has no source you can identify, no sun-equivalent, no luminescent panels. The light simply exists in the upper third of the space and falls downward, and the lower two-thirds receive it and distribute it according to its own geometries. There are no dark corners. There are no sharp shadows. Everything is lit the way a forest floor is lit — dappled, indirect, alive.

## The Vegetation

This is what the light falls on.

The growth is extraordinary. It is the dominant feature of the space, the thing your eyes keep returning to, and it is deeply, unsettlingly alive. Not a single species — not a monoculture. Hundreds of forms, maybe thousands, competing and cooperating in a tangle that has had long enough to reach something like equilibrium.

But nothing here is green.

The photosynthetic surfaces — if that is even the right word, if the chemistry here follows anything like the chemistry you know — are shades of deep indigo, bruised violet, copper, and a pale gold that glows faintly in the filtered light. The largest structures are columnar, rising from the undulating floor in clusters of three or seven or thirteen, tapering as they rise, branching once or twice before terminating in structures that look more like ears than leaves. Wide, thin, slightly concave, covered in a fine velvet down. They are the source of one layer of the sound. Each of these ear-leaves is vibrating at a frequency determined by its size, its tautness, its position in the column. They are listening and singing simultaneously.

Between the columns, filling the gaps, growing over and around and through each other, are smaller forms: tendrils, fans, nets, spirals of fibrous material that climb the columns like vines but are not vines, because vines remember the sun and these things orient toward the pulsing veins in the floor. They tap into the amber material. They feed on whatever moves through it. Some of them are translucent, and you can see the particulate matter from the air being drawn into them, filtered, processed, excreted in new forms.

There are structures like fruiting bodies — clusters of small, dense orbs on stalks, the color of rust, warm to the touch. They do not fall. They do not rot. They simply grow until they reach a certain size and then they begin to emit their own light, a dim amber that matches the floor, and they resonate. Each one adds its own voice to the sound. The chamber is, among other things, an instrument played by its own vegetation.

The smallest growth is a moss-like film that covers the low points of the terrain, the depressions, the shaded areas beneath the columns. It is not a moss. It moves. Slowly, visibly, the way a slime mold moves, extending tendrils across the surface, exploring, retreating, extending again. It avoids the light. It follows the warmth. And when two colonies of it meet, they merge and separate and merge again in patterns that, if you watched long enough, you would start to suspect contain information.

Nothing here has roots. Nothing here has leaves in the way you know leaves. Everything is something like a mouth and something like an ear and something like a lung, and the distinction between flora and fauna is a distinction this place has not made.

---

## The Fauna

Because the things that move are not separate from the things that grow.

The air is full of them. Small, fast, dense with purpose. They are not birds, not insects, not anything you have a category for. They are the size of your thumb, the size of your fist, the size of a child's kite, and they fill the upper volume of the chamber in their thousands, in their tens of thousands, moving in patterns that look chaotic from below but that resolve, when you watch a single individual, into clear trajectories with clear aims.

Some of them feed on the particulate in the air — they are filter-feeders, flying through the suspension with mouths held open, their bodies translucent enough that you can see the accumulated matter inside them, glowing faintly as it processes. Some of them land on the columns and press their bodies against the velvet ear-leaves and stay there for minutes at a time, vibrating, and the ear-leaves change their song in response. Some of them descend to the floor and interact with the moss-film — touching it, being touched by it, sometimes being consumed by it, sometimes consuming parts of it in turn.

They do not seem to notice you. Or rather, they notice you the way a river notices a stone — they flow around you, adjust their trajectories, and continue. You are an obstacle, not an object of interest. This is somehow more disturbing than hostility would be.

There are larger things. Not many. Three, four at most, and they keep to the periphery, to the places where the columns are densest and the light is most diffuse. They are the size of large dogs, low-slung, moving on too many legs with a fluidity that suggests they are not walking but flowing across the terrain the way the moss-film flows. Their surfaces are the same indigo-violet as the vegetation, and when they pass beneath a column, the column's song changes key. Not dramatically. A quarter tone, maybe less. But you hear it, and you hear it again each time one of them moves, and you begin to understand that the sound of this place is not a static drone but a conversation — constant, shifting, the vegetation and the fauna and the floor and the air all in continuous dialogue, all of them speaking at once, all of them listening at once, and the totality of it is the roar that fills your skull and vibrates your teeth.

One of the large ones passes within five meters of you. You see its surface up close. It is covered in the same fine velvet down as the ear-leaves, and the velvet is moving — each fiber independent, each one a sensory surface, each one tasting the air, the sound, the vibration. It has no eyes. It has no mouth that you can see. It has several openings along its sides that pulse in time with the floor-veins, and from these openings it emits its own contribution to the sound: a low, subsonic throb that you feel in your gut before you hear it, a note so deep it seems to come from below the floor.

It does not look at you. It looks at everything.

## The Built

There are structures here that were not grown.

They are easy to miss. They are the same amber as the floor, and they are small, and they are old. But they are angular, and nothing else here is angular.

The first one you notice is a column about half a meter tall, square in cross-section, sitting in a depression filled with the moss-film. The moss grows around it but not on it. The column is perfectly smooth, perfectly regular, and at its top there is a small facet — a flat surface, angled upward — that is doing something to the light. The light that hits it does not reflect. It continues *into* the column and then bends, traveling down the structure's length and entering the floor, where it joins the pulse in the veins. It is a collector. A harvester. It has been here long enough that the terrain has flowed around it like water around a stone, but it was placed here by something with hands or with intent or with both.

There are others. You count eleven in the visible space. Some are taller. Some are horizontal, half-buried in the undulating floor, their surfaces still smooth, still regular, still functioning. One is a ring — a perfect circle of the amber material, two meters in diameter, lying flat on a ridge, and inside the ring the air is different. The sound is different. Muffled, attenuated, as if the ring defines a zone of partial silence, a place where the roar is reduced to a manageable thunder. You step inside it without thinking, and the relief is physical — your shoulders drop, your jaw unclenches, your breathing slows. The sound is still enormous. But inside the ring, it is merely enormous rather than annihilating.

You realize, with a slow dawning, that whoever built these structures built them to *tune* this place. Not to quiet it. To shape it. The square columns harvest light and feed it to the floor. The ring modulates sound. There are others whose function you cannot guess — a spiral of amber material hanging from the upper curvature by a thread of the same substance, rotating slowly, trailing a faint distortion in the air behind it. A set of parallel grooves cut into a ridge, precisely spaced, that produce a humming tone when the wind moves across them — except there is no wind, and yet they hum anyway, fed by something you cannot detect.

The technology is not advanced in the way you understand advancement. It is not electronic, not mechanical, not digital. It is, for lack of a better word, *ecological* — the built structures are part of the ecosystem, functioning the way organs function in a body, integral, not separate. They were made by something that understood this place as a living system and chose to work within it rather than against it. Or they were made *by* the living system, the way a body grows its own bones. You cannot tell which. Maybe there is no difference.

## The Turbulence

Everything here is changing.

Not slowly. Not in the geological, glacial timescale that the weathered terrain suggests. Fast. Constantly. The chamber — the grove, the cathedral, the organism, whatever it is — is in a state of continuous reconstruction.

You watch a column fall. It has been leaning, you realize, for some time — the moss-film has thickened at its base, the floor-veins have shifted their course, and the ear-leaves have been singing a different note for the last ten minutes, a note you now recognize as stress. The column comes down with a sound that is swallowed by the general roar — a soft, fibrous collapse, the structure folding rather than breaking, settling into the depression where the moss waits to consume it. Within thirty seconds, the column is no longer a column. It is a ridge. It is terrain. The moss-film is already growing over it, and from the point where it fell, three new stalks are emerging — thin, eager, rising at visible speed, two centimeters, three, five, already sprouting their first proto-ear-leaves.

In the upper volume, the flying things have changed their pattern. What was chaos is now something with a center — they are swirling, spiraling, forming a vortex that tightens and loosens and tightens again, and the sound they make collectively shifts as the vortex tightens, rising in pitch and intensity until it is a scream, a shriek, a note so high it leaves the range of hearing and becomes a pressure behind your eyes, and then it breaks — the vortex shatters, the flyers scatter, and for one brief second the chamber is almost silent, the components of the sound recalibrating, finding their new positions, and in that second you hear something else.

A voice. A single voice, deep in the mix, saying something that is not a word but is not not a word. A syllable. A phoneme. Something that means — or that meant, once, to someone, to something — a specific thing.

Then the roar resumes, and the new configuration is different from the old, and the column that fell is being replaced, and the moss-film has expanded its territory by three meters, and one of the square collectors has shifted position slightly, pushed by the movement of the terrain, and the light-pattern it feeds into the floor has changed, and the floor-veins are rerouting, and the vegetation is adjusting, and the sound is adjusting, and the fauna are adjusting, and everything is adjusting to everything else in real time.

This is not stability. This is not chaos. This is a system so complex and so densely interconnected that stability and chaos are the same thing to it — a system in constant equilibrium because it is in constant motion. It is an anthill. It is a bazaar. It is a weather system. It is a mind thinking a thought that it will never finish thinking, a sentence that loops back on itself and finds new grammars each time it passes the same point.

## The Atmosphere

The temperature is cool — not cold, not uncomfortable, but cool in the way a high-altitude meadow is cool. The light gives warmth but the air takes it back. You can see your breath, or whatever the equivalent of your breath is in this atmosphere — a faint shimmer of particulate matter condensing and dissipating. The coolness is a relief against the relentless sound. It keeps you alert. It keeps the vegetation from becoming too dense, the growth from becoming too aggressive. It is a governing parameter, a thermostat, and you suspect it is not accidental.

The elevation is real. Not metaphorical. You can feel it in the thinness of the atmosphere, in the way your body works slightly harder than it should to process the air. You are high up — on a plateau, on a shelf, on the upper surface of something that extends downward for a long, long way. The chamber is near the top of something vast. The ceiling membrane is thin, and beyond it there is something else, something that is not sky but is open and empty and enormous, and the light comes from that direction, filtered through the membrane, and the altitude is part of why — part of how this place exists at all. It needed height. It needed thinness. It needed to be near the boundary between inside and outside.

## What You Don't Know

You don't know where you are.

You don't know if this is a building, a body, a machine, a garden, an experiment, a communication, a prison, a performance, a prayer.

You don't know if the thing that built the square collectors is still here, or left, or became something else, or is the floor itself, or is you.

You don't know if the sound is language. You don't know if the voice you heard in the gap was a word or a coincidence or a message or a memory. You don't know if the moss-film is growing toward you because it is curious or because it is hungry or because that is simply what it does and you happen to be in the direction it is going.

You don't know if the turbulence is a storm or a function — if the system is breaking down or performing its primary activity. You don't know if the column that fell was a failure or a stage in a cycle. You don't know if the flyers are inhabitants or components or waste products or offspring.

You don't know how long you have been here. The light doesn't change. There is no day, no night, no cycle to anchor your sense of time. You have a feeling — not a memory, not a thought, but a physical sensation in the tissue behind your sternum — that you have been here longer than you think. That the door you came through adjusted something in you, is still adjusting something in you, is in the process of making you into something that can survive this place.

You don't know if that is a comfort.

---

## What You Do Know

You know it is loud.

You know it is alive.

You know it does not need you, but it has not asked you to leave.

You know the ring of silence is the closest thing here to hospitality, and you stay inside it, and the sound is merely deafening rather than destroying, and the moss-film is growing toward the edge of the ring, and the flyers are scattering and reforming, and the columns are falling and rising, and the floor is pulsing, and the light is falling, and the built structures are tuning, and the whole chamber is singing, and you are inside the song, and the song does not end.
