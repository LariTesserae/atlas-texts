# The Underbloom

## I. The First Descent

You go down a throat.

There is no entrance in the conventional sense — no door, no rupture in a surface. The ground above is a crust of desiccated root-material, cracked and papery, and somewhere in it a seam gives way and you drop through into a space that should not, by any reasonable geometry, exist. The fall is short. Four meters, maybe five. You land on something that yields but does not break — a mat of living fiber, warm, slightly damp with something that is not water. Not sap, either. Closer to lymph.

The air hits you first. Not hot — *warm*, the way a greenhouse is warm, the way a body is warm, held at a temperature that suggests proximity to something metabolic. But it moves. It churns. The atmosphere down here does not sit still; it circulates in vast slow convection rolls driven by heat rising from below, and when a current catches you it feels like being breathed on by something with a very large, very patient lung. The air tastes of cellulose breakdown, of sugar in intermediate decay, of something fermented and mineral-sharp. There is a bitterness beneath it, alkaline, like crushed bone dust dissolved in vinegar.

Then the sound.

## II. The Sound

It is difficult to overstate. The Underbloom is *loud* in the way that a waterfall inside a cathedral is loud — not a single noise but a total saturation of acoustic space, every frequency occupied, every surface contributing. The primary note is a low, almost subsonic thrum that you feel in your sternum before you hear it, a pulse that comes from the root-mass itself, from the slow contraction and relaxation of millions of vascular bundles pumping fluid through the substrate. It is a heartbeat. Not metaphorically. The ground has a heartbeat, and it is at roughly forty beats per minute, and it shakes pollen loose from every surface.

Layered over this: creaking. The living architecture is always moving, always shifting, and the woody elements groan against each other with sounds like a ship at sea, like a barn in a windstorm, like teeth grinding in sleep. Vines as thick as your torso twist in slow contractions and their bark cracks and peels with reports like distant musket fire.

And over *that*: the fauna. The insects, the arthropods, the things that are not quite either. A wall of chirring, clicking, stridulating, rasping, drumming. So many individual voices that they merge into a texture — white noise, almost, but organic, irregular, with rhythms that surface and submerge. Periodically something larger moves through the canopy overhead and there is a hush — three seconds, four — and then the cacophony resumes, rearranged, different voices dominant now, as if the passage of a predator has re-sorted the hierarchy of noise.

You cannot hear yourself think. You can hear yourself *breathe*, barely, if you concentrate. Conversation is impossible at more than arm's length. You learn quickly to communicate by grip, by gesture, by the direction of your eyes.

## III. The Space

It is vast in the way caves are vast — not open, not airy, but *hollow*, a space defined by what surrounds it rather than what it contains. The ceiling is forty to sixty meters above, a tangled lattice of root and aerial stem and parasitic growth that filters the light into a greenish-yellow ambience, diffuse, sourceless. There is no sun here. The light comes from the plants themselves — bioluminescence in waves, patches of living tissue that glow soft blue-white or amber or, rarely, a deep arterial red. These patches pulse, sometimes in rhythm with the heartbeat of the ground, sometimes on their own schedules. The effect is of being inside a chamber lit by slow, scattered lightning — everything in green-gold shadow, then a wash of blue, then shadow again, then amber. Colors shift. Distances lie.

The floor is not flat. It undulates in great swells of root-mass and compressed organic matter, rises into ridges, drops into troughs where the substrate is thinner and you can feel — if you stop and press your palm to the surface — the hard mineral geology beneath, still forming, still raw. This is a young landscape. The stone under everything is soft, sedimentary, barely consolidated. You can crumble it with your fingers. It has not yet decided what it wants to be.

Between the floor and ceiling, the space is *full*. Not open air but a three-dimensional thicket of living material: stems, vines, fungal columns, curtains of hanging moss-analogue, shelves of bracket growth, masses of something that looks like coral but is vegetable, spiraling filaments that catch the light and glitter with trapped spores. The density is near-total. You move through the Underbloom the way you move through a crowded market — by finding the channels, the gaps, the places where the growth has parted or been parted. Everything touches everything else. Everything is in contact. The plants here do not grow in isolation; they grow as a continuous medium, a tissue, and you are inside that tissue like a bacterium inside a body.

## IV. The Plants

The substrate itself is plant. This cannot be overstated. There is no soil — there is compost, living and dead, layer upon layer of growth and collapse and regrowth, compacted into something you can walk on. There is no stone surface exposed except in the deep troughs and the raw places where the geology is still emerging. Everything else is root, stem, leaf, fruit, flower, rot, and re-growth, compressed into a medium that supports more growth. The Underbloom is a plant that grows on itself.

The dominant form — if "dominant" means anything in a system this dense and interdependent — is a kind of climbing/vining structure with woody stems the color of old leather, ranging from finger-thick to body-thick, that spirals around every available support and links the floor to the ceiling in a web of living cable. These vines are not passive; they move. Slowly, over hours, in responses too gradual to see directly but visible in the changed configuration of a space you passed through an hour ago. A gap closes. A new gap opens. The architecture rearranges itself. You learn to not mark your path, because the path will not exist tomorrow.

There are structures that function as trees here — vertical columns of compressed root-mass and woody tissue, three to five meters across, rising from floor to ceiling. But they are not single organisms. They are colonies, or perhaps better, *consortia* — dozens of plant species growing in and through and on each other, so tightly integrated that separating them would kill the whole. Each column is a vertical ecosystem: bracket fungi (or fungal-analogues) jutting from the lower trunk, epiphytic growths clustered at mid-height, flowering structures near the top where the light is strongest, and at the base, a skirt of low spreading foliage that hides the entrances to hollows within.

Those hollows matter.

## V. The Fauna

The sound, most of it, is them.

They are everywhere. On every surface, in every gap, moving through the air in densities that feel like weather. The Underbloom's animal life exists at a scale and intensity that redefines the relationship between organism and environment — the fauna here are not *in* the space, they *are* the space, as much as the plants are.

Start small. The air itself is thick with arthropods: things like midges, like gnats, like thrips, but larger, and with body plans that don't quite parse — too many segments, or wings that are also legs, or mouths that are also reproductive organs. They swarm in columns that drift like smoke, millions strong, and if you walk through a swarm — you will, there is no avoiding it — they coat your skin, your hair, the inside of your mouth if you open it. They do not bite. They are not interested in you. They are cycling through the air in some pattern that has to do with the light-pulses, or the heartbeat, or chemical gradients you cannot sense. You are an obstacle. They flow around you and continue.

On the surfaces: beetles. Or beetle-analogues. Armored, clicking, the size of your thumb or your fist or — in the deep places — your head. They move with a sense of purpose, carrying fragments of plant matter, carrying eggs, carrying each other. Some have established relationships with specific plants and you see them only on specific stems, specific flowers. Others are generalists, everywhere, cycling through the substrate like earthworms, breaking down dead matter, enriching the living medium. The sound of their movement is a constant rasping whisper, like sand poured on paper, and it underlies everything.

In the vines: things that move like caterpillars but are not. Things that hang like fruit but are not. Things that pulse with the light and seem to be photosynthesizing through their skins — or perhaps housing something that photosynthesizes within their skins, symbiotic algae or something like it. The vines are highways, and the traffic is constant and multidirectional, and there are rules to it that the fauna understand and you do not.

And there are larger things. You hear them before you see them — or you hear them and do not see them, which is more common. Heavy movement through the upper canopy. Crashing, settling, a vibration in the vines that propagates like a wave. Something up there is the size of a large dog, or a person, or larger. You catch fragments: a limb, dark, segmented, gripping a vine; a body, low-slung, moving with a deliberate patience that suggests it is not hurrying because nothing here threatens it. You do not see a face. You see, once, an eye — large, dark, wet, set in a surface that is not chitin and not skin but something between — and it sees you, and it does not react, and it moves on.

The fauna are not aggressive. They are not afraid. They are occupied. The Underbloom is their world and they are in the middle of living in it, and you are a feature of it they have learned to ignore, the way a city ignores a pigeon. The overwhelming impression is of being inside a single organism's interior — not a landscape of individual animals but a *tissue* of animals, each one a cell, the whole thing pulsing and shifting and making noise and *being alive* at a level of intensity that is, frankly, oppressive.

## VI. The Built

There are structures.

Not many. Scattered through the Underbloom at intervals that suggest intention but not civilization — four, five, maybe more, across the area you can explore in a day's movement — there are shelters. Huts. They are built into the hollows at the base of the column-consortia, using the existing architecture as walls and floor, extending it with cut and shaped vine-material, woven, lashed with strips of inner bark. The work is simple but not crude. Whoever built these understood the material — they know which stems flex and which hold, which will resprout if cut and which will die, which will harden into something like wood and which will stay green and living in the structure, contributing to its integrity, growing into it.

The tools found inside are few. Stone flakes, sharp, used for cutting. A kind of mallet made from a seed-pod filled with dense fiber. Cordage. Nothing metal. Nothing fired. The level of technology is consistent with a people who have everything they need in the plant-material around them and have not needed — or not yet wanted — to look elsewhere.

But the shelters are empty. Not abandoned — not decayed enough for that, not overgrown enough. The vines have only just begun to reclaim them. Someone was here recently. Days, maybe. A week. The hollows smell of occupation — of body heat, of food preparation, of the specific smell of a living space that has been vacated but not emptied. There are signs: a sleeping platform of woven fiber, still holding the impression of a body. A collection of seed-pods, split open, their contents scraped out. Marks on a wall — not writing, not decoration, but *marks*, deliberate, made with charcoal or pigment, recording something. A tally. A map. A warning. You cannot tell.

## VII. The Dynamics

Nothing here is still.

The Underbloom is not a stable ecosystem — it is a *process*, a continuous event, a system in the act of becoming. The raw geology beneath is still forming, still differentiating, still deciding whether it is sedimentary or metamorphic, whether it will fracture or fold. The plant substrate above is growing faster than it is dying, which means the floor is rising, millimeter by millimeter, as new layers are laid down. The ceiling is rising with it — the aerial growth pushing upward, filling the space from above, and the space between floor and ceiling is maintained not by stasis but by a dynamic equilibrium, a balance between growth upward and growth downward that keeps the cavity open but barely.

The light-pulses are not regular. They cycle, but the cycles drift — faster, slower, clustered, sparse — and when they cluster, the growth responds. You see it happen: a wash of blue bioluminescence sweeps through a section of the canopy and the vines in that section *move*, visibly, a slow reaching, a tightening, a reorientation. The plants are tracking the light. They are following it. And the light is following — what? The metabolism of the substrate itself. Chemical gradients. Heat plumes. The light is a symptom of something happening below the floor, in the deep tissue, and the plants at the surface are responding to it in real time.

And the fauna respond to the plants. When the vines move, the insects move. When the insects move, the larger things move. A pulse of light becomes a pulse of growth becomes a pulse of animal movement becomes a pulse of sound, and the whole system oscillates, and you are inside the oscillation, and there is no outside to it.

Periodically — you cannot predict when — a section of the canopy *opens*. The vines part, the hanging growths retract, and a shaft of stronger light penetrates from above, from the surface, from whatever world exists outside the Underbloom. In that shaft, everything intensifies. The insects swarm into it. The growth orients toward it. Spores drift in it, golden, in quantities that look like snow. The sound reaches a crescendo. And then, after minutes or hours, the gap closes, and the light fades, and the system settles into a new configuration, changed, rearranged, a different set of voices dominant, a different arrangement of architecture.

This is not a storm. This is not an anomaly. This is *how it works*. The Underbloom is a bazaar, an anthill, a Solaris ocean — a system that creates and destroys and recreates itself continuously, and the landscape you see now will not exist tomorrow. Not in detail. The columns will be in different places. The hollows will have shifted. The shelters — will they still be there? Will the people who built them return to find them changed, grown-over, absorbed?

## VIII. The Feeling

The Underbloom is not hostile. This is important to understand. Nothing here wants to harm you. Nothing here is aware of you in a way that includes intention. The plants are growing. The animals are living. The system is processing. You happen to be inside it, and you are small, and you are soft, and you are not part of any of the cycles, and the system does not care.

But it is *much*. It is too much. The sound never stops. The light never steadies. The ground moves under your feet. The air moves around your body. Things land on your skin and crawl and you cannot prevent it and you cannot stop feeling it. The warmth is the warmth of a body, and you are inside the body, and the body does not know you are here, and if it shifted in its sleep it would close around you and you would become substrate. You would become part of the medium. You would be grown into and through and over, and in a week there would be nothing left, and the Underbloom would not notice the difference.

This is the feeling: of being inside something alive that is too large to perceive you. Of being in a space that is not a space but an *interior*. Of being surrounded by process, by becoming, by the raw machinery of life operating at a scale and intensity that does not accommodate human presence — not because it excludes it, but because it does not register it.

The people who built the shelters here — they know this. They have learned to exist in the interstices, in the temporary gaps, in the moments between rearrangements. They move when the system moves. They build where the system permits. They read the pulses and the sounds and the shifting of the vines, and they know when to stay and when to go, and they have been doing this long enough that it is not survival but *habitation*. They live here. The Underbloom does not know that, either.

You stand in the green-gold light, in the sound, in the warmth, on the living floor, and you feel the heartbeat through your feet, and something lands on your neck and crawls, and a vine above you shifts by a centimeter, and a shaft of blue light pulses through the canopy and the insects rise in a column like smoke, and you understand that you are not in a place. You are inside a process. And the process is not finished. And it may never be finished. And that is what it is.
