# The Chamber of the Singing Moss

## I.

There is a place beneath the basalt. You would have to know where the rock splits — not a cave entrance, not a fissure, but a seam, a place where the world's stitching shows. You crawl through it on your stomach with the stone three inches above your spine, and the stone is warm, and you are descending, and you do not see anything for the first forty seconds of the descent, and then you do.

You see green.

It is not the green of forests. It is the green of something that has never seen the sun and has decided, with the full and serene confidence of an organism that has been alive for longer than your particular species has been walking upright, that it does not need to.

The chamber opens below you like the inside of a bell. Like the inside of a throat. The ceiling is perhaps fifteen feet at its apex — a modest, intimate space, no larger than a rural chapel, though the proportions are organic, rounded, as though the air itself were shaped by pressure rather than carved by tool. You drop the last few feet from the crawl-seam and your feet meet a surface that gives slightly, that is soft and fibrous, and this is your introduction to the moss. You will spend a great deal of time thinking about the moss.

But first: the sound.

## II.

The chamber sings.

There is no other verb that fits. It is not a hum. It is not a drone. It is a sound that has components — layered, harmonic, shifting in the way that a chord changes when you move your fingers on a stringed instrument by a single fret. The fundamental tone is low enough that you feel it in the cartilage of your sternum before you hear it in your ears. Above that, there are overtones, and above those, further overtones, a stack of frequencies that is not random but is not entirely regular either. It sounds, at moments, like a glass being rubbed at its rim. At other moments, it sounds like the resonance inside a seashell — that oceanic, compressed roar — except the ocean has been replaced by something more precise.

The volume is considerable. Not painful, not deafening, but insistent. The sound fills the chamber the way water fills a bowl: completely, touching every surface. You cannot escape it by moving to any part of the room. You can only adjust to it, and you do, and after approximately two minutes, it stops seeming like sound and starts seeming like weather, like a condition of being here. Your own breathing seems to happen inside it, muffled, localized.

Where it comes from is the moss. Or more precisely: the moss is the medium. The actual source is a system of small mineral formations — you would not call them stalactites; they are too thin, too regular — that project from the ceiling and from two of the walls. They are perhaps the diameter of a pencil and three to eight inches long, tapered, pale, the color of old bone. When the chamber's air moves — and it does move, slowly, continuously, in a convection pattern driven by the warmth of the rock — these mineral projections vibrate. The moss grows over them and between them in a dense fibrous web, and the web transfers and amplifies the vibration. The sound is the sound of a living instrument being played by geothermal heat.

It never changes. The temperature of the rock does not change because the heat comes from depth, from the same source that warmed the stone when it was first extruded as lava and which has not varied in human timescales. The air currents do not change because the chamber has a single opening and a single thermal driver. The moss does not grow or retreat because it has reached the precise extent that the chamber's mineral content and humidity can sustain. The mineral projections do not accumulate or erode because the chemistry that forms them and the chemistry that dissolves them are in equilibrium. The sound is fixed. It has been this exact sound, these exact frequencies, for a length of time that you cannot estimate and should not try to.

## III.

The moss itself.

It is the dominant life form of the chamber and the reason the place has the quality of a garden rather than a geological feature. The moss covers nearly every surface — the floor, the walls up to about seven feet, the ceiling in patches where the mineral projections do not grow. It is thick. In most places, two to four inches thick, and in some hollows, deeper — a carpet, a mattress, a yielding surface that compresses underfoot and slowly rebounds. Its color is not one green but a spectrum: a deep, almost black-green in the recesses, a vivid yellow-green where it thins over the mineral formations, and in patches where the bioluminescence (there is bioluminescence, yes, I will get to it) is strongest, a green so bright it seems to generate its own shadows.

It is not actually one species. This becomes apparent if you kneel — and you should kneel, because the detail is at ground level — and look closely. There are at least four distinct growth forms interlocking: a coarse, strap-like form that builds the structural layer; a fine, feathery form that covers the structural layer like frost; a gelatinous form that fills the gaps and seems to hold moisture (though where the moisture comes from is a question, because there is no water here, there is no water anywhere here, and yet the moss is wet to the touch, cool and damp on your fingers); and a fourth form that does not grow over surfaces but extends outward into the air in thin filaments, like the threads of a spider's web, catching the convection currents and swaying.

This fourth form is the one that glows.

## IV.

The light is dim. It is green. It comes from the filaments, which produce a steady, low-level luminescence — not bright enough to read by, but bright enough to see by, once your eyes have spent ten or fifteen minutes surrendering their expectation of brighter light. In that time, the chamber reveals itself in stages.

First: the floor, the moss, the immediate vicinity. Green on green, texture on texture. Your hands, your knees.

Second: the walls, the mineral formations, the places where the rock shows through the moss in pale stripes and patches. The chamber is roughly oval, perhaps twenty feet on its long axis and fourteen on the short. The ceiling is domed, irregular, with the mineral projections hanging in clusters, each cluster vibrating at a slightly different frequency, and you realize now that the sound is not one note but a chord made of many notes, each produced by a specific projection of a specific length and thickness, and the chord is always the same because the projections are always the same and the air is always the same and the moss is always the same.

Third: the far wall, and what is built into it.

## V.

There are structures here. Not many. Not large. But unmistakably made by hands.

Against the far wall, three low platforms have been constructed from the same pale mineral material as the ceiling projections — not cut from them, but gathered, perhaps, from where they have fallen, though nothing here suggests that they fall. The platforms are knee-high, smooth on top, and show tool marks: rough grooves consistent with stone-on-stone grinding. They are old. The moss has grown over their bases and partway up their sides, but the tops are kept clear, and the reason they are kept clear is that they are in use.

On the leftmost platform, there are small objects: a cluster of the mineral projections, each snapped to a specific length and laid parallel. A shallow bowl ground from a darker stone. A bundle of the feathery moss, dried, tied with a filament of the glowing variety. Someone has been here, has arranged these things with intention, and the arrangement is recent — within days, perhaps, if the concept of "days" applies to a place where the light never changes.

On the center platform, there is a larger object: a sheet of what appears to be compressed and dried structural moss, flat, about the size of a opened hand, with marks scratched into its surface. The marks are regular. They might be a record. They might be decoration. They might be instructions. There is no way to know without a context that is not available to you.

The rightmost platform is empty except for a thin layer of the gelatinous moss, which has been deliberately spread across its surface. The platform has been prepared for something. Or it is always prepared, and the preparation itself is the function.

## VI.

The fauna.

There are animals here, and there are many of them, and they are strange.

The most visible are the crawlers — and this is not their name, because they have no name, because no one has named them, but this is what they are. They are small, perhaps the length of your thumb, segmented, pale, semi-translucent, and they move across the moss in continuous slow streams, thousands of them, following the structural growth forms like roads. Their purpose appears to be feeding: they graze the feathery moss layer, consuming the outermost growth, and the moss, far from being harmed by this, appears to require it. The grazed areas grow back denser, brighter. The crawlers are the gardeners.

They do not make a sound of their own, but their movement across the moss produces a soft, dry whisper, almost subliminal, a texture beneath the chord of the mineral projections. You feel it more than you hear it. It is the sound of the chamber's ecology doing its work.

Above the crawlers, in the filaments of the glowing moss, there are other creatures. These are smaller still — barely visible, appearing as a faint disturbance in the light, a flicker, a blink. They live in the filaments, moving from one to another, and their motion causes the filaments to sway, which causes the light to shift, which means that the chamber's illumination is not actually static but moves in slow, subtle patterns, patterns driven by the activity of creatures too small to see. The light breathes. It does not breathe in a rhythm — it shifts in complex, non-repeating patterns, the way sunlight shifts through leaves in wind, except there is no wind and no leaves and no sun. There are only the filaments and the creatures and the slow convection of warm air.

In the darker recesses of the chamber, where the moss thins and the bare rock shows in folds and hollows, there are larger inhabitants. You see one. It is pressed flat against a wall in a depression, and you would not have noticed it except that it blinks — two dark points that appear and disappear, and you realize it is watching you. It is perhaps the size of your palm, flat, oval, the color of the stone it rests on. It has legs — you count six, thin, splayed — and what appears to be a mouth on its underside. It does not move. It does not make a sound. It watches you with a patience that is not intelligence and is not stupidity but is something else, something that is simply the result of being an animal that has lived in one room for its entire life and has never had a reason to hurry.

There are more of these. You see them as your eyes continue to adjust: three in the upper wall, two near the ceiling seam, a cluster of four in a corner hollow. They are everywhere, and they are still, and they are watching, and they are not threatening. They are present. They are part of the chamber's equilibrium, another component of the system, and the system has no components that are not necessary.

## VII.

The warmth.

The rock is warm. Not hot — not dangerously warm, not uncomfortably warm — but noticeably, continuously warm, the way a stone in the sun is warm, except this stone has never been in the sun. The heat comes from below, from the deep geology, from the same source that created the basalt and that sustains the convection that drives the mineral projections' vibration that makes the sound that fills the chamber. Everything connects to everything else.

The air is warm and slightly humid — not wet, not damp in the way of a cave with dripping water, but carrying a faint moisture that you can feel on your lips and that is enough, apparently, to sustain the moss. The moss does not need much. The gelatinous species captures and holds what little moisture the air provides, and the structural species is adapted to an environment where the primary water source is the metabolic byproduct of the other organisms — the crawlers, the filaments-dwellers, the flat watchers on the walls. The chamber is a closed system. It recycles. Nothing enters. Nothing leaves. Except you, just now, on your stomach, through the seam.

## VIII.

The rules of the world here, as far as they can be known:

The chamber does not change. It has reached a state that might be called maturity — every species at its maximum sustainable population, every mineral formation at its equilibrium between growth and dissolution, every air current at its stable velocity, every tone at its fixed frequency. The sound has been the same sound for centuries, perhaps millennia. The moss has been the same thickness for the same period. The crawlers have been following the same paths in the same numbers. The flat watchers have been watching from the same positions. The mineral projections have been vibrating at the same frequencies. This is not death. This is not stasis in the sense of something stopped. It is equilibrium in the sense of something so perfectly balanced that motion and stillness have become indistinguishable.

The structures on the platforms suggest that humans — or something with hands and intentions and the ability to grind stone against stone — have been coming here for a long time. The moss has grown over the base of the platforms but not the tops, which suggests maintenance, which suggests repeated visits, which suggests a tradition. Someone has been visiting this chamber, has been arranging objects on these platforms, has been scratching marks on sheets of dried moss, has been doing this for long enough that the moss has adapted to their presence and grown patterns that accommodate it.

The chamber does not care about any of this. The chamber is not aware. The chamber is a system, and the system includes the moss and the minerals and the crawlers and the watchers and the air and the heat and the sound, and it also includes whoever comes here and whatever they do, because they are part of the system too, because everything that happens in the chamber is part of the chamber. The visitors are as much a component as the convection. The objects on the platforms are as much a feature as the mineral projections. The system does not distinguish between the biological and the deliberate, because the system does not distinguish at all.

## IX.

If you stay long enough — and I do not recommend staying long, not because it is dangerous but because it is absorbing, because it is the kind of place that takes the urgency out of you, that makes the concept of leaving seem like a unnecessary complication — you will notice the following:

The sound, which at first seemed uniform, is not uniform. It is the same sound, always, but your perception of it shifts. Your ears adjust. You begin to hear the internal structure of the chord — the way certain frequencies reinforce each other, the way the overtones create phantom tones that are not produced by any projection but emerge from the interaction of the ones that are. You begin to hear notes that are not there. Your brain, given a constant input, begins to generate its own variations. The sound does not change, but your experience of it does, and this is the only dynamism the chamber permits.

The light does the same thing. The filaments glow steadily, but the creatures moving among them create patterns that your visual system tries to resolve into meaning. You see shapes that are not shapes. You see movement that is implied rather than actual. The green light, reflected off the pale mineral formations and the pale stone walls, creates a monochrome world in which depth and texture become ambiguous. You cannot tell how far away the ceiling is. You cannot tell whether the far wall is close or distant. The chamber is not large, but in this light, with these sounds, with this warmth, it is not small either. It is a space that has opted out of the usual agreement about dimensions.

## X.

You came in through a seam in the basalt. You will leave through the same seam. The chamber will not remember you. The moss will fill in the impression of your knees where you knelt. The crawlers will consume the cells of moss you disturbed and convert them back to the web. The flat watchers will not change position. The sound will not change. The chord will not modulate. The light will not brighten or dim.

But the objects on the center platform — the marks on the dried moss sheet — if you look at them again before you leave, you might notice that they are not a language and not a record. They are a shape. A single shape, repeated, varied, the way a fingerprint is varied. The shape is the chamber. The shape is the sound made visible. Someone, at some point, lying on this moss in this green light in this constant warmth with this sound filling their chest, saw what the sound looked like, and scratched it into a sheet of dried moss, and left it on a stone platform, and left.

The chamber does not require this. The chamber does not benefit from this. The chamber simply continues, as it has continued, as it will continue, singing the same chord in the same green dark, until the deep heat changes or the rock above shifts or the seam closes and the air stills and the projections stop their vibration and the moss, with no moisture and no sound, finally, after its long and contented life, lets go.

That is not soon.
