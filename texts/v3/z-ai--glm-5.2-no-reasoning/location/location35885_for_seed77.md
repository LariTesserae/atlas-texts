# The Forming Floor

The first thing you notice is that the ground is not ground.

You are walking on something that was poured — or perhaps extruded — and has not yet decided what it will be. The surface is dark, the grey-brown of raw fired clay before glaze, and it stretches in every direction farther than the eye can settle. It is warm underfoot. Not the warmth of sun-heated stone, but the lower warmth of something that is still, in some chemical sense, *setting*. You can feel it through the soles of your boots: a faint, deep pulse of heat rising from beneath, as though the floor of this world is a kiln cooling from a temperature it never quite reached.

There is no soil. There is no bedrock. There is no sand or sediment or decomposed granite. There is only this material — this made substance — and it continues in every direction with a monotony that becomes, after some hours of walking, its own kind of terrifyng. You cannot find where it was poured because it was poured everywhere. You cannot find its seams because it has none. The closest analogue is the surface of a poured concrete slab the size of a small sea, except that concrete is a simile here and the substance underfoot is nothing so familiar. It is darker than concrete. It is smoother. It has a faintly greasy texture where the heat has brought something to the surface, and in the low places where moisture has collected, it takes on a dark sheen like the skin of an apple.

---

The land is flat. Not approximately flat, not flat the way a salt flat is flat — with its micro-relief of crystalline crusts and polygonal cracking — but *engineered* flat, a planar surface that seems to have been struck level by some instrument of impossible size. The horizon is a perfect line at the limit of vision in every direction. There are no hills. There are no ridges. There are no mountains rising in the distance to give you a landmark, a bearing, a sense that the world has edges. The sky comes down to the ground along a clean ruled edge, and the ground meets it without negotiation.

This flatness is so absolute that it creates its own form of vertigo. You walk for an hour and the view does not change. You walk for three hours and the view does not change. You turn around and look behind you and there is nothing to distinguish the direction you came from — no footprints, because the material seems to fill behind you, settling slowly like wet plaster; no mark of passage, no broken fragments or disturbed surface. The ground records nothing. You begin to suspect that you are not moving at all, that walking is a performance you are putting on for an audience that isn't there, and the only proof that you are in fact progressing is the slow ache building in your calves and the sweat running down the channels of your spine.

---

The heat is oppressive. Not the dry heat of a desert, which has a cleanliness to it, a purity — and not the humid heat of a jungle, which wraps around you like a wet towel. This heat is *radiant*. It comes from below. The air is hot because the ground is hot, and the ground is hot because something underneath it — some process, some reaction, some slowly cooling pour — is still working itself out. The temperature of the air two meters up is perhaps forty-two degrees Celsius. The temperature at the surface is higher. You can feel it when you stop walking and stand still: the heat soaks upward through the soles of your boots and into the bones of your feet, and after a while you begin to step from foot to foot the way you would on a hot sidewalk in summer, except there is no cooler patch of grass to step onto. There is only the same surface, the same heat, in every direction.

The sky above is pale. Not blue, not white — a faintly luminous grey, as though the atmosphere is a dome of frosted glass diffusing a light source from the other side. The light is even. There are no shadows because there is no local light source sharp enough to cast them. You look down at your feet and there is a faint darkening where your body interrupts the downward component of that ambient glow, but it is a soft shadow, a shadow without edge, more a suggestion than a fact. The light does not change. It does not warm toward evening. It does not brighten toward midday. It is the same when you stop to rest as it was when you started walking, and it will be the same when you stop walking for good, because there is no diurnal cycle here. The light is a condition of the place, not a passage of time.

---

There is water here, but it is not anywhere you can reach easily.

The surface is not perfectly flat after all — or rather, it was made flat, but it has since developed the faintest of relief, and in that relief the water gathers. Shallow depressions, some no wider than your handspan, some the size of a small room, collect a film of moisture that seems to appear from nowhere. Condensation, you think at first — the warm surface meeting cooler air, dew forming on a scale too subtle to see. But when you kneel beside one of these puddles — they are barely puddles, more dark patches on the surface where the material's texture has changed — you see that the water is not resting on top. It is *seeping through*. The material is not fully cured. It is still porous. Moisture from somewhere below is working its way upward through the microscopic interstices of the setting matrix, reaching the surface, and evaporating in the heat.

The water is warm. Not hot, not steaming, but noticeably warmer than ambient air. When you touch it — and you touch it, because you are thirsty and have been thirsty for hours — it has a faint mineral taste, like water that has passed through limestone, except the minerals are unfamiliar. Something faintly metallic. Something faintly sweet. You drink a small amount and wait. Nothing happens. You drink more. The heat is relentless and the water is right there, pooling in the shallow depressions, and after a while you drink more freely. It does not quench your thirst the way clean water does. It addresses the deficit in your body without satisfying the *want* of it, as though your mouth and throat cannot quite register what you have given them.

The moisture comes and goes. You walk for an hour over dry surface — bone dry, if bone were the word, the material pale and dusty where the heat has drawn everything out of it — and then you enter a patch where the depressions are closer together, every few meters, and the surface between them has a darkened quality, a dampness that is not quite wet, and the air above these patches is fractionally cooler, fractionally heavier, and you realize you are walking across the surface expression of some deeper hydrology. There is water beneath this floor. A layer of it, perhaps a shallow sea of it, sitting between the underside of the pour and whatever lies below that. The water rises through capillary action, reaches the surface, and evaporates. It is a slow, continual exhalation. The whole vast flat surface of this world is breathing moisture.

---

There are no animals. None. Not the conspicuous absence of a place where animals have been driven out — no tracks, no nests, no bones — but the deeper absence of a place where animals have never been. This environment has no ecology in the biological sense. There are no producers, no consumers, no decomposers. There are no insects landing on the wet patches. There are no birds crossing the pale sky. There is no sound.

The silence is not the held breath of a forest, where the absence of sound is a warning. It is not the hollow silence of a dead city, where the absence of sound is a memory. It is the silence of a place where sound has never had a source. Your footsteps make a noise — a dull, flat tapping, absorbed almost as soon as produced, the material underfoot eating the vibration — and that noise is the only sound the world has ever contained. You hear your own breathing and it sounds enormous. You hear your own heartbeat, faintly, and it sounds like a drum struck in a cathedral. The silence rushes back in behind every sound you make, filling the space, erasing you.

---

The vegetation, such as it is, is not vegetation in any way you would recognize from a field guide.

In the wetter patches — the areas where the surface is darkened and the moisture comes through most persistently — something grows. It is not green. It is not any color associated with photosynthesis. It is a dark reddish-brown, the color of old rust, and it grows in a thin mat across the surface, spreading in irregular concentric patterns from what you assume is a point of origin. When you kneel and look closely, the mat resolves into a structure of fine filaments, each one thinner than a human hair, branching and rebranching in a fractal pattern that covers the ground like a sparse velvet. It is not a plant. It has no leaves, no stems, no roots. It seems to be growing directly out of the material of the surface itself, fed by the moisture seeping upward and the heat radiating from below.

You touch it. It is warm. Warmer than the surface around it. There is a faintly oily texture to it, and when you press your fingertip into the mat, it compresses and then slowly rebounds, filling back in, the filaments reweaving themselves over the course of a few seconds. It is alive. It must be alive — it grows, it responds, it has a structure that implies metabolism — but it is alive in a way that is connected to nothing you know. It is not a plant. It is not a fungus. It is not a lichen or a moss or a slime mold. It is something else. Something that found a niche in this manufactured landscape and is slowly, patiently, filling it.

There are larger growths in a few places. Where the moisture is most persistent — where the depressions are deepest and the water pools rather than merely filming the surface — the reddish-brown mat has thickened and risen into low mounds, some as high as your knee. These mounds have a different texture: denser, more structured, with a faint ribbing visible in their surface, as though the filaments have organized themselves into a more rigid architecture. You press your hand against one and it is firm, almost woody, giving only slightly under pressure. It is warm to the touch, warmer than the air, and you realize with a start that these growths are generating heat. They are not merely absorbing the heat of the surface beneath them — they are producing their own. There is a metabolism here, and it is exothermic.

The mounds are sparse. You might walk for an hour and see none, and then pass through a region where a dozen have formed over an area the size of a football field, each one separated from the others by fifty or a hundred meters of bare dark surface. They are distributed with no pattern you can discern — not evenly spaced, not clustered, not following any gradient of moisture or temperature that you can measure with your senses. They are scattered the way stars are scattered: present, persistent, and indifferent to the distances between them.

---

The world is active, but its activity is slow and largely invisible. You stand still and watch the surface for a long time — ten minutes, twenty — and you begin to notice the changes. A dry patch darkens. A wet patch lightens. The filaments of the reddish growth extend by a distance you cannot measure but can infer, because the edge of the mat has moved, imperceptibly, during the time you have been watching. The heat rises. The moisture rises. The material underfoot continues its slow process of setting, of becoming whatever it will become when it is finished, and it is not finished yet. This is a world under construction. Not in the sense that someone is building it — you have seen no workers, no machinery, no evidence of intent — but in the sense that it is *becoming*. The pour has been made. The surface has been struck level. The moisture has been introduced. And now the physics and chemistry of the thing are working themselves out, over timescales that dwarf your presence, and you are walking across the middle of a process that started before you arrived and will continue after you leave.

The material is eroding, but only just beginning to. In a few places — very few, widely separated — the surface has cracked. The cracks are thin, barely wide enough to admit the edge of a knife, and they run for short distances before sealing themselves again, the material flowing together like a viscous liquid. But for a moment, in those cracks, you can see down. You can see that the material has depth. You can see that below the surface, it is a different color — darker, almost black, and faintly luminous, as though there is a light source at the bottom of the pour that the setting material has not yet fully occluded. You cannot see the bottom. The cracks seal before you can gauge the depth. But you have the impression — and it is only an impression, a feeling that lodges in the gut rather than the mind — that the material is very deep. That this surface is the skin of something vast. That the world underfoot is not a floor but a *roof*, and what it roofs is below you, and it is warm, and it is still moving.

---

You walk on. The heat is the same. The light is the same. The surface stretches in every direction without landmark or feature. The reddish growth comes and goes in patches. The moisture gathers and evaporates. The cracks open and close. The silence absorbs your footsteps and gives nothing back.

You have been walking for hours, or perhaps days — there is no way to measure the passage of time in a place where the light does not change and the heat does not vary and the surface does not record your passage. You are not tired, exactly, but you are attenuated. Stretched thin. The sameness of the place has worn away the edges of your attention, and you find yourself walking in a state that is not quite thought and not quite sleep but something between — a rhythmic, automatic forward motion that your body is performing while your mind drifts somewhere above and behind, unmoored.

And then, in the distance — far, far ahead, at the limit of vision where the pale sky meets the dark surface along that perfect horizon line — you see something. A slight irregularity. A place where the surface is not flat. A shape, low and wide and dark against the dark, that breaks the ruled line of the horizon for just a moment before the heat shimmer swallows it.

You walk toward it. You do not know if it is near or far. There is nothing to provide scale. You walk, and the shape does not grow closer, and you walk, and the shape does not grow closer, and you walk — and then, eventually, slowly, it begins to grow. It is larger than the mounds. It is larger than anything you have seen since you arrived here. And as it resolves in your vision, you begin to make out its features: it is a structure. It is *built*. Not grown, not formed — built. Walls, low and massive, of the same dark material as the surface, rising to a height of perhaps three meters. A roof, flat and featureless, spreading over what must be an interior space. No door that you can see. No window. No opening of any kind. Just a solid, windowless, doorless structure sitting on the surface of the forming floor like a knot in a piece of wood.

It is made of the same material as everything else. But it was made *deliberately*. Someone — or something — shaped this. Chose this form. Built these walls and laid this roof. And the implications of that settle over you with a weight that has nothing to do with the heat: you are not alone in this place. Or you were not, once. And the question that you cannot answer, that you will carry with you as you walk toward that dark shape in the distance, is whether the builder is still here — beneath the surface, below the roof, inside the material, *part of the material* — still setting, still curing, still becoming whatever it will become when the pour is finally done.
