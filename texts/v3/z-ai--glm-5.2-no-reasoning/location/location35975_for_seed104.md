# The Drowned Cathedral of the Plateau

---

## I. First Approach

There is no shore. That is the first disorientation. You descend — or perhaps you have always been descending — and the water does not end. It simply becomes *more*. What you might have mistaken for sky is water. What you might have called ground is water that has grown thick with time. There is no surface here, or the surface is so far above that it has become a theoretical concern, a rumor passed between particles.

You are high. That is the second disorientation. The water presses in a way that suggests altitude — dense, warm, haloed with a mineral sweetness that implies you have climbed into something rather than sunk beneath it. The pressure should crush you. It does not. Instead, it holds you the way a crowd holds a body lifted over its head: with the gentle, distributed insistence of many hands. The warmth is constant, amniotic. It does not vary with depth or movement. You realize, slowly, that this is because there *is* no variation. This is a plateau — an underwater highland, a flat-topped prominence rising from depths that may have no floor — and the water here has been sitting in the same place, at the same temperature, for a span of time that makes the word "age" feel like a rounding error.

Somewhere around your third hour of consciousness here — though hours are measured only by your own heart, because the light does not change — you understand the full strangeness: *you are at the top of something vast, and you are underwater, and the water extends in every direction including up, and this is the highest point for a distance that would take a lifetime to cross.*

---

## II. The Terrain of Standing Water

The plateau is flat. Not the flatness of a prepared surface, not the flatness of erosion's last word. This is the flatness of *completion* — a landscape that has settled into its final shape the way a dead body settles into its position on the floor. Every process that could reshape this place has already done so. Every hill has been humbled. Every valley has been filled with its own debris. What remains is a surface so level that you could lay a horizon across it — and indeed, looking out through the warm mineral dark, you see what appears to be a horizon line, a faint lateral band where the water's clarity shifts from one density of suspended particulate to another.

But flat is not empty.

The seafloor — the *plateau-floor* — is carpeted in a substance that is neither sediment nor rock. It is a deposit, a *mat*, a living-dying accretion built from the slow fall of uncountable generations. It has the texture of velvet left in water for a thousand years: soft, deep, yielding to the touch with a reluctance that suggests it remembers pressure. Its color, in the dim light, is the color of dried blood mixed with clay — a deep, oxidized maroon that darkens to near-black in the hollows. This is the *loam*, and it is the basis of everything.

The plateau extends in all directions with the patient indifference of a continent. There are no walls here, no enclosing geography. You could swim in any direction for days and find only more of this flat, warm, ancient floor — though the details would shift, the way details shift across any vast landscape, in patterns too large to perceive from within.

There are rises. Low, broad swells in the loam, barely perceptible as slopes — gentle enough that you would not notice you were climbing until you looked back and saw the dimming of the lights behind you. And there are depressions: wide, shallow basins where the water grows slightly warmer and the loam gives way to exposed substrate, a dark glassy material that looks almost volcanic but is, in fact, something older than volcanism. This substrate is the true surface of the plateau, the ancient crust beneath the accretion. Where it is exposed, it is perfectly smooth. Not polished. *Finished.* As though whatever process created it completed its work and then walked away.

---

## III. The Vegetation: The Garden That Has No Gardener

The loam is covered in growth. Not the chaos of a wild reef, not the tangle of a kelp forest — this is a *garden*, though there is no gardener. Or rather, the gardener is time. Time and water and the specific chemistry of this place, which has favored certain forms for so long that they have become the *ruling* forms, the way grass rules a prairie or sand rules a desert.

The dominant growth is a thing that has no terrestrial name. It grows as *stalks* — thin, flexible, vertical, rising from the loam in densities so high that they nearly touch. Each stalk is the width of a finger at its base and tapers to a hair-thin tip, and each one reaches upward through the water column for a length that varies between one and three body-lengths. They are translucent. In the dim light, they appear amber, the color of honey held up to a window. Each stalk is a single continuous strand of living tissue, and each one pulses — a slow peristaltic wave that travels from base to tip, visible as a faint thickening that moves upward through the translucent material and then releases at the top in a small, almost imperceptible bloom of cells. These cells drift upward. They are the means by which the garden reproduces, or communicates, or both.

Standing among these stalks — and you are always standing among them, because they cover nearly every surface — is like standing in a field of wheat seen through amber glass. They part around your body with a warm, yielding resistance. They close behind you. The peristaltic pulses continue regardless of your presence. You are not disrupting the garden. You are not even *noted* by the garden. You are a stone dropped into a river: present, irrelevant, passed around.

Between the stalks, in the loam, there are *carpet-forms* — low, spreading organisms that cover the ground in overlapping patches. They are not plants. They are not animals. They are something that the taxonomy of any other world would need a new kingdom to accommodate. Their surfaces are covered in fine, hair-like filaments that wave constantly in a current that you cannot feel — a current that exists at the scale of molecules, or at the scale of something smaller than molecules, something that the physics of this place includes but your physics does not. The carpets range in color from deep violet to a muted teal, and where two different carpets meet, there is a boundary — a narrow strip of bare loam, a few finger-widths wide, where neither grows. These boundary zones are perfectly maintained. Neither side encroaches. The strips have been in the same positions, some of them, for longer than the concept of position has been meaningful.

And then there are the *canopies*.

At intervals across the plateau — irregular, but not random, not quite — the stalks grow taller and thicker, clustering around a central structure that rises from the loam like the trunk of a tree, though it is not a tree. These central structures are broad at the base and taper to a flattened crown, and from the crown, horizontal *fronds* extend in all directions, overlapping with the fronds of neighboring canopies to form a partial ceiling — a second surface, a roof, perhaps thirty body-lengths above the floor. The fronds are wide, flat, and almost transparent. They are the primary light-gathering surfaces of the ecosystem, and they are oriented to catch whatever light reaches this place — the dim, sourceless, amber-grey light that seems to come from the water itself, or from the substrate below, or from nowhere at all.

Walking beneath the canopies is a different experience from walking in the open stalk-fields. The light is even dimmer here, filtered through layers of transparent frond. The water is slightly warmer — the collective metabolism of the canopy-structures and their associated communities generates a faint heat. The peristaltic pulse of the stalks is synchronized here, all of them moving in the same direction at the same time, a wave that passes through the entire canopy like a slow breath. The sound changes. In the open, there is near-silence. Beneath the canopy, there is a pulse — not audible, not quite, but *felt*, a low-frequency vibration in the water that you sense in your chest more than hear with your ears.

The canopy zones are where the garden is most itself. Where it has been undisturbed for the longest. Where the patterns have had the most time to *settle*, and where the settling is most visible in the complexity of what grows.

---

## IV. The Light

The light does not come from above. This is the third disorientation, and it never fully resolves. You look up and the water extends into a darkness that is not quite dark — it is *dim*, a dimness that has texture, that has color, that has depth, but that does not have a *source*. The light is everywhere and nowhere. It is as though the water itself is faintly luminous, or as though the loam below emits a steady, low glow that permeates upward through the entire water column.

The color of the light is amber. Not the bright amber of sunlight filtered through something, but the dull, patient amber of a thing that has been glowing at the same intensity for so long that the glow has become a *property* of the medium. It does not flicker. It does not pulse. It does not change with the passage of what might be a day — because there are no days here. There is only the steady, dim, warm amber light, and it has been this way forever, and it will be this way forever, and the concept of "forever" in this place is not a metaphor. It is a *measurement*.

Beneath the canopies, the light takes on a greenish cast, filtered through the fronds. In the shallow basins where the substrate is exposed, the light brightens slightly — the dark glassy material seems to *concentrate* it, or perhaps the material itself is the source. You cannot tell. The light is simply *present*, the way gravity is present, the way the warmth is present. It is a condition of being here, not an event.

Your shadow, if you have one, is not visible. The light is too diffuse, too omnidirectional. You move through it without casting a shape. You are illuminated from all sides and from within the water itself, and the result is that you are both perfectly visible and completely without depth — a flat figure in a flat field, a body without chiaroscuro.

---

## V. The Water

The water is warm. Not warm the way a bath is warm, not warm the way a tropical sea is warm. Warm the way blood is warm: from the inside, as though the heat is *generated* by the water itself rather than absorbed from some external source. The temperature does not vary as you move through the water column. It does not vary between the open stalk-fields and the canopy zones. It does not vary between the shallow basins and the deeper loam-covered areas. It is *one temperature*, everywhere, and it has been this temperature for longer than the concept of temperature has been useful.

The water is not clear. It is thick with *particulate* — the slow rain of cells from the stalk-tips, the fine filaments shed by the carpet-forms, the mineral dust eroded from the substrate and the loam. Swimming through it is like moving through a thin soup. Your hands, held up before you, are visible but *soft* — their edges blurred by the particulate, as though you are being drawn in watercolor. The particulate settles on everything. The loam is, in part, made of it. The canopy fronds catch it and incorporate it. The boundary-zones between carpets are where it falls most thickly, as though the carpets' mutual exclusion creates a downdraft.

The water moves. Not in currents — there are no currents here, not in the sense of directional flow. The movement is *internal*, tidal, volumetric. The water expands and contracts in slow waves that pass through the entire plateau-region, and these waves are so slow and so uniform that you only notice them when you stop moving and hold yourself still and let your body become a sensor. Then you feel it: a faint, rhythmic change in pressure, a swell and a release, a swell and a release, with a period of perhaps several hundred heartbeats. It is not the tide of any moon. It is the *breathing* of the plateau itself — the thermal expansion and contraction of an enormous volume of water that is being warmed from within by its own ancient geology.

The water has a taste. You discover this because you taste everything here — the particulate settles on your tongue, on your lips, on the inside of your nostrils. It tastes of *iron* and *salt* and something else, something that is not a mineral and not a biological compound, something that your tongue cannot categorize and your brain cannot name. It is the taste of the substrate. It is the taste of the *bedrock*, dissolved one molecule at a time over an interval that makes the word "dissolved" feel like violence. The bedrock is becoming water. The water is becoming bedrock. The process is so slow that it has reached an *equilibrium* — the water tastes of bedrock, the bedrock feels like water, and the two are in a communion that predates the distinction between them.

## VI. The Structures

There are built things here. Not many. Not enough to call this a settlement, or a city, or a ruin in any recognizable sense. But there are *structures*, and they are not natural, and their presence in this ancient, settled, completed landscape is so incongruous that you spend a long time — perhaps your entire first day, perhaps longer — trying to convince yourself that they are natural, that they are formations of the substrate or growths of the garden. They are not.

There are three kinds.

The first are *pillars*. They are found singly, never in groups. Each one rises from the loam to a height of roughly two body-lengths. They are circular in cross-section, tapering slightly toward the top. They are made of the same dark glassy material as the exposed substrate, but their surface is *marked* — shallow grooves cut in a pattern that is not decorative and not structural and not language, or if it is language, it is a language that communicates something other than meaning. The grooves are precise. They are not worn. Despite the immense age of this place, the grooves look as though they were cut yesterday. The pillars do not support anything. They do not connect to anything. They stand in the stalk-fields like fingers pointing at nothing, and the garden grows around them without touching them — a narrow zone of bare loam, wider than the carpet-boundaries, surrounds each pillar, maintained by a mechanism you cannot identify.

The second kind of structure is the *basin*. These are shallow, circular depressions, perhaps one body-length in diameter, cut into the substrate where it is exposed. They are not natural formations — the edges are too regular, the depth too uniform. Each basin is surrounded by a low rim of the same dark material, and each one is filled with water that is slightly warmer than the surrounding water and slightly clearer. The basins are always found near the pillars — within a few body-lengths, as though the two are *paired*, though there is no physical connection between them. The carpet-forms do not grow into the basins. The stalks do not grow within the basins' rims. The basins are kept *empty* of life, and you cannot tell by what mechanism.

The third kind of structure is the rarest and the most difficult to describe. You find only one, after many hours of swimming. It is a *shape* — a low, broad form, half-buried in the loam, roughly the size of a small dwelling. It is not made of the dark substrate. It is made of something *else*, something that you have no reference for. It is not stone. It is not metal. It is not wood or bone or shell. It is a material that seems to *absorb* the amber light rather than reflect it, so that it appears as a void in the water — a dark shape, a silhouette, a hole in the world. You approach it. You touch it. It is warm. It is smooth. It does not yield. Your hand, resting on its surface, feels nothing — not the pressure of a solid object, not the temperature of a real surface, just a faint, indistinct sense of *presence*, as though the shape is not a thing but a *boundary*, a place where the rules of this world are slightly different from the rules a few inches away.

The shape has no markings. It has no openings. It has no features except its form — a low, curved, organic mass, like the back of something enormous that has lain down in the loam and been slowly, over aeons, buried by the steady rain of particulate. The garden does not grow on it, but it grows *near* it — the stalks around its perimeter are taller than average, thicker, their pulses stronger, as though they are drawn toward it. The carpet-forms approach it more closely than they approach the pillars or the basins — their boundary-zones narrow near the shape, as though the exclusion is weaker here, as though the shape *invites* proximity.

You sit beside the dark shape for a long time. The water breathes around you. The stalks pulse. The light does not change. You begin to feel that the shape is not an artifact *in* this world. It is an artifact *of* this world — a *seed* of it, or a *bone* of it, or a word that this place once said and then forgot.

## VII. The Fauna

There is almost nothing that moves.

This is the silence of the plateau, and it is the most profound silence you have ever encountered. Not the absence of sound — there are sounds, faint ones, the low pulse of the canopy zones, the almost-inaudible whisper of particulate settling, the occasional soft *tick* of a stalk-tip releasing its bloom of cells. But these sounds are so sparse, so widely spaced, so *small* against the vastness of the water and the flatness of the floor, that they function as silence. They are the sounds that silence makes when it has been in place for so long that it has developed *habits*.

You see, once, a *thing* that moves with purpose. It is between the stalks, low to the loam, and it is the size of your hand. It has no shape that you can hold in your mind — it is a *blur*, a disturbance in the water, a streak of motion that travels in a straight line for a distance of perhaps ten body-lengths and then stops. When it stops, it is invisible. You swim to the place where it stopped. There is nothing there. The loam is undisturbed. The stalks are unmarked. Whatever it was, it has either hidden itself perfectly or it has *ceased to be* — not died, not fled, but simply stopped *being a thing* and become part of the medium again.

You see, once, a *cluster* of cells in the open water — a sphere, perhaps the size of your fist, composed of thousands of tiny luminous specks that orbit each other in a pattern too complex to track. The sphere drifts. It does not swim. It does not pulse. It simply *drifts*, and as it drifts, the specks within it rearrange, and the sphere changes shape — elongating, flattening, folding — and then returns to its spherical form, and continues to drift. You follow it for a long time. It does not react to your presence. It does not appear to be going anywhere. It is a *process*, not an organism — a thing happening to a cluster of cells in warm water, a chemical event that has the complexity of life but not the *commitment* of life. After a while, the sphere dims. The specks slow. The sphere flattens and disperses, and the water where it was is indistinguishable from the water around it.

You hear, once, a *sound* — and this is the only sound on the plateau that is louder than the silence. It comes from far away, from a distance you cannot estimate, and it is a low, resonant *tone* — not a note, not a call, not a voice, but a vibration in the water that you feel in your teeth and in the bones of your skull. It lasts for perhaps ten heartbeats. Then it stops. It does not repeat. You wait for it. You swim toward where it seemed to come from. You find nothing. The tone is gone, and the silence returns, and the silence is so much *louder* than it was before the tone that you understand: the tone was not a sound *in* the silence. It was the silence *breaking*, briefly, to show you what it is made of.

## VIII. The Weird

The plateau is not entirely *here*.

You first notice this at the edge of a canopy zone, where the fronds overlap and the light is at its dimmest. You are swimming between two canopy-structures, in the gap where their fronds do not quite meet, and you see — through the gap, at the limit of visibility — something that is not the plateau. It is not the water. It is not the dark. It is *nothing*. Not nothing as in emptiness, not nothing as in the absence of things, but *nothing* as in a place where the rules that produce things do not apply. It is a gap in the world, a place where the water and the loam and the light and the warmth simply *stop*, and beyond them is a quality that your eyes cannot process and your mind refuses to hold.

You look away. You look back. The gap is gone. The fronds overlap. The water is warm and thick and continuous. You tell yourself that you saw a defect in the light, a failure of your vision in the dimness. You almost believe this.

Later, in a shallow basin, you notice that the water in the basin is clearer than the water outside it — not because the particulate has settled, but because the water in the basin is *different water*. It has a different refractive index. Objects viewed through the basin's water are shifted slightly, as though seen through a lens. When you lower your hand into the basin, your hand looks *wrong* — not distorted, not blurred, but *displaced*, as though it is in a slightly different location than where you feel it to be. You withdraw your hand. It is fine. The water in the basin is fine. The basin is fine. Everything is fine.

Later still, you notice that the peristaltic pulses of the stalks are not traveling in the direction you thought they were. You have been watching them move upward — base to tip, always upward — but when you focus, truly focus, you realize that they are moving *sideways*. Not along the stalks, but *across* them — the pulse is a wave in the field of stalks, a wave that moves horizontally through the garden like wind through wheat, and the individual stalks are not pulsing at all. They are being *passed through* by something that only *looks* like a pulse. You watch this for a long time. The wave moves across the field. It reaches the edge of your perception. It does not reflect. It does not dissipate. It *leaves*. It goes somewhere that the field of stalks does not extend to, and when it goes, the stalks it has passed through are *different* — not changed in any way you can identify, but *different* in the way that a word is different after you have said it too many times. The same, but *wrong*. Familiar, but *uninhabited*.

And there is the matter of the *breathing*. The slow, thermal, volumetric expansion and contraction of the water — the plateau's respiration — does not always have the same period. Sometimes it is faster. Sometimes slower. And sometimes, at the moments when the water is at its most *expanded* — at the peak of the inhalation — you feel a second presence, a *doubleness* in the water, as though the plateau is not one place but two places that are occupying the same volume, and at the moment of fullest expansion, the two places are almost — *almost* — able to perceive each other. Then the water contracts, and the two places fold back together, and there is only one.

## IX. The Rules of This World

Water is the medium and the medium is the world. There is no *outside*. There is no surface to break, no air to breathe, no shore to reach. The water extends in all directions, and within the water, the plateau is a *high place* — the top of something so large that its slopes, if they exist, are too gentle to perceive. To leave the plateau, you would have to swim down — off the edge, into the depths — or up, into the water-column above, which extends to a height that may be infinite. Either direction would take you *away* from the garden, away from the structures, away from the dim amber light, and into a darkness that is not the darkness of depth but the darkness of *distance* — the darkness of a place that extends so far that its light cannot reach back.

Time here is not directional. This is not a statement about physics. It is a statement about *experience*. The light does not change. The temperature does not change. The garden grows, but it grows in a *steady state* — the stalks bloom and settle and bloom again, and the loam does not deepen, and the carpets do not spread, and the canopies do not extend. Everything is in motion, but the motion is *circular*, *orbital*, *self-sustaining*. Nothing arrives. Nothing departs. The plateau has been in this state since before the word *since* was relevant, and it will remain in this state until the word *until* loses its meaning. You are not witnessing a moment in the life of this place. You are witnessing its *eternal present*, its *always*, its *is*.

The structures are *signs*. Not signs *of* something — not markers, not monuments, not records. They are signs *in* something — in the way that a word is a sign in a sentence, in the way that a note is a sign in a chord. The pillars, the basins, the dark shape — these are *elements* of a statement that the plateau is making, or that was made *about* the plateau, or that the plateau *is*. You cannot read the statement. You do not have the grammar. But you can feel its *structure* — the placement of the pillars, the spacing of the basins, the position of the dark shape at what might be a center or a beginning or an end — and the structure is *intentional*, *ordered*, *complete*. The statement was made. The statement is *here*. The statement does not require a reader. It simply *is*, the way the garden *is*, the way the water *is*.

The weirdness is not a *property* of the plateau. It is a *condition* of the plateau. The gaps, the displacements, the doubleness — these are not things that are happening *to* this place. They are what this place *is made of*. The plateau exists at a *seam* — a place where two or more realities overlap, where the rules that govern existence are not singular but *multiple*, layered, concurrent. The water is water and it is also *not-water*. The garden is alive and it is also *not-alive*. The light is light and it is also *not-light*. These contradictions are not resolved. They are *held*. The plateau holds them the way the loam holds the stalks: gently, permanently, without effort or intention. This is the *final form* of the plateau — not a landscape that has eroded into simplicity, but a landscape that has *settled into complexity*, that has found a state in which all of its possible versions can coexist without conflict, and that has remained in that state for longer than *coexistence* has been a meaningful word.

---

## X. What Remains

You are here. You are in the warm water, in the dim amber light, among the amber stalks and the violet carpets and the dark glassy substrate and the ancient structures and the nearly-empty silence and the breathing of the plateau and the weirdness that is not an event but a *medium*. You are *present* in a place that does not require your presence and does not note your absence and does not distinguish between the two.

The garden pulses around you. The water holds you. The light illuminates you without casting your shadow. Somewhere, far away, a tone sounds in the silence and is gone. Somewhere, a cluster of cells arranges itself into a sphere and then disperses. Somewhere, a gap opens in the world and closes again, and the two places that are one place breathe in, and breathe out, and the plateau *is*.

You could stay here. The water is warm. The garden does not mind. The structures do not mind. The silence does not mind. Nothing here *minds*, because *minding* is not a process that occurs in this place. The plateau has completed its minding. It has thought whatever it was going to think, and said whatever it was going to say, and grown whatever it was going to grow, and eroded whatever it was going to erode, and now it *rests* — not in the sense of sleeping, not in the sense of being tired, but in the sense of being *finished* and continuing to *be* anyway, which is perhaps the most difficult thing that any landscape can do.

The plateau rests. The water breathes. The garden pulses. The light does not change.

You are here, and *here* is not a place. It is a *condition*. It is the condition of being at the end of everything, at the top of everything, in the middle of everything, held in warm water that has been warm forever, surrounded by growth that has been growing forever, in a light that has been dim forever, in a silence that has been silent forever, in a world that has been *itself* forever and does not need to be anything else.

The plateau is the last thing. It does not know this. It does not need to know this. It simply *is*, and the *is* is enough, and the *is* has always been enough, and the *is* will always be enough, and the water breathes, and the garden pulses, and you are here, and *here* is all there is.
