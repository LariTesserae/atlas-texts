# The Tepui of Ten Thousand Bodies

## Approach

You come to it across a plain that should not be called a plain because plains imply flatness and this is flat the way a held breath is flat — sustained, deliberate, waiting to be released. The ground is mineral. Not sand. Not rock. Something between chalk and bone dust, compacted by an age of patient pressure into a surface that whispers underfoot in a frequency too low to properly hear. You feel it in your ankles. In your teeth.

The tepui rises from this plain like a slab cut from the underside of the sky. Not a mountain — mountains are aspirational, jagged, reaching. This is a remainder. It was taller once. Everything here was taller once. The plain around it is what washed down over an elapsed eternity, and the thing that remains is what was too stubborn or too dense to follow. Two thousand meters of vertical face, striated in horizontal bands that record epochs the way tree rings record years — except each band is a geological period, and the patterns within them are too fine to parse with the eye. You'd need instruments. You'd need lifetimes.

There is no water. You understand this intellectually before you arrive and then understand it again, bodily, once you stand beneath the escarpment. The dryness is not a condition. It is a property. The air has been wrung of moisture so completely and for so long that "dry" is the wrong word — the air here has never known wetness. It pre-dates the concept. Your lips crack within the first hour. Your eyes feel peeled. The sky is a pale eggshell color, without clouds, without the *possibility* of clouds, and the light that comes through it is normal — thoroughly, insistently normal — which makes the landscape beneath it stranger by contrast, as though the sky is refusing to participate in whatever is happening on the ground.

## The Surface

You climb. There is a way up — not a path, exactly, but a series of holds and ledges that someone, at some point, found sufficient. The handholds are worn smooth. Not by water. By hands. Thousands of them, over a span you cannot calculate, each grip removing a few molecules of chalk-mineral until the stone took the shape of fingers. You place your own hands in these grooves and they fit. This is not coincidence. This is the landscape admitting you.

The top of the tepui is a plateau perhaps forty kilometers across. You can see this because the air is so thin and so clean that distance becomes a trick — things far away appear near, and the horizon, where the plateau ends and the plain begins again, looks like a drawn line. The surface is not flat. It is *settled*. There is a difference. Flatness is a state. Settledness is the *end* of a process — the final arrangement of material after every force that acted upon it has exhausted itself. The ground undulates in low swells, no more than a meter of variation, like the skin of something vast that has been lying still for so long it has cooled and contracted into its final shape. You walk across it and you feel that you are walking across something that has finished becoming what it is.

There are formations. Low ones. Ridges no higher than your knee, running in parallel lines that converge slightly toward the plateau's center, as though the ground once flowed — not as liquid, but as something that moved with glacial slowness over an incomprehensible duration and then stopped, mid-motion, and hardened into this. The mineral is layered here, exposed in cross-sections where the surface has cracked: white, grey, a faint sulfur-yellow, and occasionally a deep rust-red that appears in veins too narrow to be useful and too regular to be accidental.

Nothing moves. The air does not move. There is no wind. You realize this gradually — not as an observation but as an absence that grows louder the longer you attend to it. Your clothes hang without fluttering. Your hair, if you have it long, falls straight. The sky has no clouds to drift. The light does not change. It is the same light at what should be midday as it was at dawn, as it will be at dusk — a steady, even, sourceless illumination that seems to come from the air itself rather than from any celestial body. If there is a sun, it has been occluded or absorbed or has simply left, and what remains is the light's memory of itself, playing on loop.

## The Bodies

You see the first one and you do not understand what you are seeing.

It is low to the ground, about the size of a large dog but shaped differently — longer, with a segmented body that lies flat against the mineral surface. It is the same color as the ground. This is not camouflage. This is composition. The creature is made of the same chalk-mineral as the tepui. Its body is dry, hard, slightly chalky to the touch — if you touch it, which you will, because it does not react to your presence. It does not react to anything. It is lying in a shallow depression that matches its body exactly, a mold pressed into the mineral surface by the patient weight of its own stillness.

It is alive. You determine this not by movement — there is none — but by a faint warmth when you hold the back of your hand near it. Metabolism without motion. Life as a low flame, barely flickering. Its eyes, if they are eyes, are two shallow depressions on a featureless head, filled with the same mineral dust. You cannot tell if it is looking at you. You cannot tell if *looking* is something that happens here.

You walk further and find another. And another. And then you reach a point where you realize the plateau is covered with them.

Not dozens. Not hundreds. The plateau is *paved* with them. They lie in every shallow depression, in every low swale, along every ridge-line — bodies pressed to the ground with the patient weight of geological time, each in its own fitted mold, each the same chalk-white or grey or faint rust color as the surface around it. They are dense. You cannot walk in a straight line without stepping near them, over them, between them. Some are stacked — two or three deep, the lower ones partially absorbed into the ground, the upper ones partially absorbed into the lower ones, a slow sedimentation of living bodies compressing into a living stratum. The density is overwhelming. In places there are ten or twelve per square meter. In places they form continuous layers, body on body, and you realize you have been walking on them without knowing it — the "ground" in these areas is not ground at all but the compressed backs of creatures who have been lying still for longer than the concept of *longer* can meaningfully describe.

They do not react to your footsteps. They do not react when you crouch beside one and place your hand on its surface. It is warm. It is smooth. It has the texture of fine chalk and the hardness of old bone. If there is a heartbeat, it is so slow that your own pulse cannot serve as a reference — you would need to sit beside it for minutes, hours, perhaps longer, to feel the singular slow contraction that constitutes its circulation.

## The Stillness

Time does not pass here. Or rather, time passes but nothing *in* time passes. The light does not change. The air does not move. The temperature — cool, not cold, a steady neutrality that your skin stops registering after the first hour — does not fluctuate. The bodies do not shift. The plain below does not weather. The formations do not erode. You have the growing certainty that you have entered a place that has reached its terminal state, its heat-death, its final equilibrium — not with a bang, not with a collapse, but with a slow, patient settling into stillness that began before you were born and will continue after you are gone and will not be interrupted by anything, because there is nothing left to interrupt it.

The silence is total. It is not the absence of sound but the *retroactive* absence of sound — as though sound was once possible here and has been so thoroughly absent for so long that the concept has been erased from the place's physics. Your footsteps make no noise. You shout, and the shout does not echo. It does not propagate. The air absorbs it the way the ground absorbs the bodies — completely, patiently, without effort. You clap your hands and feel the impact but hear nothing. The only sounds you can produce are internal: the wet click of your own swallowing, the pulse of blood in your ears, the creak of your joints. You become your own soundscape, a small pocket of biological noise moving through a silence so profound it has become a texture, a medium, something you swim through.

You stay long enough and you begin to hear something else. Not a sound, exactly. A pressure. A low vibration that sits below perception, below the threshold of hearing, somewhere in the register where the body feels rather than hears. It comes from the bodies. All of them. Together. It is not a heartbeat — it is too slow, too uniform, too *synchronized*. It is more like a hum. A single sustained note produced by ten thousand bodies lying still on a plateau at the top of a world, vibrating at a frequency that predates hearing, that was old when the first ear evolved, that will persist after the last ear is gone. The hum is not directed at you. It is not directed at anything. It is simply the sound that matter makes when it has been alive and still for long enough that the distinction between the two states has dissolved.

## The Structures

There are structures. Small ones. You find them in clusters — three or four at a time, arranged in patterns that might be circles or might be lines or might be something else entirely, something that only looks like a pattern from a perspective you cannot occupy. They are built from the same chalk-mineral as everything else here, fitted together without mortar, held by precision and by the fact that nothing ever moves to disturb them. The walls are low — knee-height, waist-height at most. The rooms they enclose are no larger than a person could lie down in. Some have roofs, thin slabs of mineral balanced on corner posts, and beneath these roofs you find the same thing you find everywhere: bodies. Smaller ones. Arranged with what might be care.

These are shelters. Or monuments. Or both. Or neither — perhaps they are a third thing, a category that your language does not contain, because your language evolved in a world where things move and change and these structures were built by and for a world where nothing does. The construction is primitive in technique — hand-placed stones, no metal, no binding agent, no evidence of tools beyond the fingers that shaped them — but the *intent* is sophisticated. The walls are fitted with care. The proportions are deliberate. Whoever built them understood geometry, understood spacing, understood something about the relationship between enclosure and the body that you can feel but cannot articulate. You sit inside one of these structures and the hum of the bodies changes — not in pitch but in *quality*, as though the walls shape it, channel it, give it a container in which it can become itself.

Near the center of the plateau, where the ridges converge, there is a larger structure. Still small by any standard — perhaps three meters on a side — but set apart from the others by its position and by the density of bodies around it. They are stacked here, six and seven deep, a living floor beneath it. The structure has walls high enough to stand inside. It has no roof. Looking up through it, you see the flat pale sky, featureless, depthless, as close and as far as a painted ceiling. The interior is empty except for a single body — larger than the others, positioned at the exact center, lying on its back with its segments fully extended. It is warm. Warmer than the others. The hum here is louder, or rather it is *concentrated*, focused, as though this body is the source and the others are resonators, or as though this body is the receiver and the others are transmitters, or as though the distinction between source and reception has collapsed and everything is both, always, simultaneously.

## The Rules

You stay long enough — and you will stay longer than you intend, because time does not pass here and so the question of *when to leave* becomes unanswerable — and you begin to understand the rules of this place. They are not written. They are not spoken. They are simply *operative*, the way gravity is operative, and you feel them in the way the plateau feels your weight:

Nothing changes. This is the first rule and the only rule and the rule from which all others descend. The world has reached its final state. Every body has found its position. Every stone has settled. The air has distributed itself evenly. The light has found its level. There is no gradient, no imbalance, no potential. Entropy has completed its work — not in the thermodynamic sense of heat death, but in a deeper sense, a structural sense, the sense in which a system has found its ground state and can fall no further. The bodies are alive because life, here, is a ground-state phenomenon. It requires no input. It produces no output. It simply *is*, the way the mineral *is*, the way the light *is*, the way the silence *is*.

You do not belong because you change. Your heartbeat is a gradient. Your breathing is an exchange. Your warmth is combustion — a process, a fire, a thing that moves through time and consumes and produces and leaves a wake of difference behind it. You are a disturbance in a field that has spent longer than you can imagine becoming perfectly, finally, still. The bodies do not react to you because reaction is change, and change is not possible here, and so they absorb your footsteps and your warmth and your claps and your shouts the way the ground absorbs rain in a wetter world — completely, without effort, without trace.

You will leave. You know this. You will climb back down the worn handholds and cross the plain of mineral dust and re-enter a world where things move. But you will carry the hum with you — the low, sub-perceptual vibration of ten thousand bodies lying still at the end of the world, alive and finished, resting and arrived, and for the rest of your life, in your quietest moments, when the noise of the changing world subsides, you will feel it: that single sustained note, the sound of matter that has been alive for so long it has forgotten the difference.
