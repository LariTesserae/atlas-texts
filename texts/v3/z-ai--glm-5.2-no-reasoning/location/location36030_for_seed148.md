# The Floor of the Underspring

## What you first understand

You go down a short way — not far, nothing dramatic — through a frame that might once have been a doorway or might be the gap between two roots of something so large its canopy exists only as idea. The air changes. Not cold exactly. The temperature sits in that range where you feel it in your teeth first, then the forearms, then you stop noticing because something else demands attention. Cool. Cool the way cellars are cool. Cool the way the air above standing water is cool, even when the water is barely water anymore.

The light is almost not light. It is the memory of light, or light that has been filtered through so many layers of living matter that it arrives stripped of certainty. Green-black. Sometimes blue-black. It shifts, but never toward bright. Your eyes adjust, and adjusting, they learn that the darkness is not empty — it is full of things that do not require light to be known.

The space is small. Cathedral-small, which is a paradox, but it's right: you are inside something that feels vaulted, that feels like it has a ceiling and walls and a floor and all the architectural commitments of a room, but the scale is intimate. You could probably touch both walls if you stood in the center and stretched, though you would not want to touch both walls. Not because of danger. Because of what it would mean to be that connected to everything at once.

And it is empty. This is the strangest part. The space is full — full of material, full of life, full of process — and yet the density of *presence* is almost zero. Things are happening at distances from each other that feel deliberate. Each organism, each mechanism, each wet patch of growth occupies its volume and does not spill into the next. The air between them is its own substance.

## What the place is made of

The walls are plant. Not *plants* — not discrete botanical individuals growing against a surface. The walls are plant the way a cliff is stone. The substrate, the structure, the surface, the interior — all of it is living or recently-living vegetable matter, compressed and intergrown and layered into something that holds its shape through sheer biological stubbornness. You press a hand to it (you will; everyone does) and it gives slightly, and the giving is not softness but resilience, the way a living thing yields before it resists.

Fungal hyphae run through everything like rebar through concrete. Not the showy fruiting bodies — those exist, they're here, they push out from the walls in shelf-like progressions and small clustered knobs — but the invisible (or nearly invisible) threads that constitute the actual organism. They bind the plant matter. They are why it holds. Remove them and the whole structure would settle into a heap of damp fiber in minutes.

But there is also metal here. Pipes, or things that were pipes. They emerge from the plant-mass at intervals, gray and cool and sweating with condensation, and they vanishing back into it. Some of them still carry something — you can hear it, when you press your ear close, a faint rhythmic pulse, liquid moving through a tube under mild pressure. The sound is almost below hearing. You feel it in your jaw before you hear it.

This is the built layer. The human layer. Or the layer that was human once, that was organized and intentional, that someone designed and constructed with infrastructure and purpose. It is not gone. It has been *incorporated.* The pipes are still pipes. The junctions still connect. But they are now also plant, the way a sunken ship becomes reef — still ship, still steel, but also and equally habitat, substrate, food, anchor.

There was a settlement here. You can see it in the regularities: the spacing of the pipes is deliberate, architectural. The way the floor (also plant, also rooted through and knitted) drops in a gentle step suggests a designed change in grade. A channel along one wall — rounded now, soft-edged, lined with moss so thick it looks velvety — was a drain or a conduit. Someone built a place here and the place was not abandoned so much as it was *absorbed,* the way a body absorbs a splinter. The splinter remains. The body grows around it.

## The water

Moisture is everywhere but water — collected, pooled, standing water — is rare. The air carries it. You feel it on your skin constantly, a fine dampness that is not quite mist and not quite condensation but something between, something that exists because the temperature of the air and the temperature of the surfaces are in a particular relationship that produces this ambient wetness without ever producing rain.

Except sometimes it does rain. Inside.

You will be standing in the near-dark and suddenly a single drop will fall from somewhere above — you can't see the ceiling, it's lost in the darkness above, but it exists, it is there, it is plant and pipe and darkness — and the drop will hit a surface and you will hear it. One drop. The sound of one drop of water striking a leaf, or a pipe, or a shallow film of water on the floor, in an enclosed space, in the dark. It is one of the loudest things you will ever hear.

Then another. Then nothing for a long time. Then three in quick succession, each landing in a different place, each producing a slightly different note because they strike different materials. Then nothing.

This is the hydrology of the place. Condensation collects on surfaces above — on the undersides of root-masses, on the cool metal of pipes, on the waxy surfaces of leaves turned face-down — and when enough of it gathers, it falls. The water exists in a permanent state of almost. Almost pooled. Almost flowing. Almost a stream. It is riverine in the way that a person pacing in a room is peripatetic: the motion is constant but the destination is absent.

One exception. Along the channel that was once a drain, there is a film of water. Not a flow — a film. It moves, but so slowly that you would have to watch a particular point for minutes to see progression. It is wetness that has organized itself just barely enough to have a direction. The moss along its edges drinks from it. The moss is always drinking.

## What lives here

The fauna is the surprise. The abundance of animal life in a space this small and this dark is the fact that reorganizes everything you thought you understood about the place.

They are not large animals. None of them. The largest might be the length of your hand — and you are not sure about that, because you have not seen it clearly, only felt it move past your ankle in the darkness with a deliberate, unhurried motion, the motion of something that knows it belongs here and is not concerned about you.

What you can see, once your eyes fully adjust, are the insects. But calling them insects flattens what they are.

First: the caterpillars. They are everywhere. Green-white, soft, thick as fingers, moving along the walls in processions of a dozen or more, following silk trails laid by caterpillars before them. They eat the plant matter — the softer layers, the younger growth, the things that are still becoming wall. They are the erosion of this place, the thing that breaks down the living surface and returns it to a state where it can be re-built. They are patient. They are methodical. You watch one work its way along a pipe for several minutes, and its motion is so slow it seems mechanical, a conveyor's pace, and it leaves behind a track of exposed fiber that immediately begins to weep moisture.

There are moths. Of course there are moths. They are the dark moths, the ones whose wings look like bark, like stone, like the inside of a cave. They rest on the walls and they are invisible. They move and they are a soft blur in the deeper dark. When one lands on your arm — and one will — you feel only the lightest touch, the smallest warmth of a living thing, and then it is gone and you are not sure it was there.

There are things in the water film. You see them when the darkness cooperates — small, pale, many-legged things that move through the shallow moisture with a fluidity that seems impossible given how little water there is. They exist in the film. The film is their ocean. They are crustacean or insect or something between, and they are numerous, and they are busy in the way that things are busy when their entire world is a millimeter deep.

And there are the others. The ones you hear but do not see.

## What you hear

The sound landscape is almost entirely biological and almost entirely quiet.

The pipes pulse. That low, sub-audible rhythm. It is the closest thing to machinery that exists here, and it is not machinery, or not only machinery — the pipes have roots in them and the roots have expanded and contracted with moisture cycles and the pipes themselves have warped and what flows through them now may not be what flowed through them when they were installed. The pulse has a bio-rhythm. It is not mechanical-regular. It has the slight irregularity of a heartbeat. Of digestion. Of peristalsis.

The caterpillars eat. You can hear this if you are close to the wall, which you are, because the space is small and the walls are near. The sound is a tiny wet tearing, a rhythmic *shff shff shff* that is almost mechanical itself, like very small, very slow scissors cutting very soft, very damp paper. Dozens of them doing this simultaneously creates a texture of sound that is not loud but is constant, a background fizz of consumption.

The moss grows. You will not believe this, but you can hear it. Not the growth itself — that's absurd — but the sound of the moss's surface adjusting as it expands, the faint pop and creak of cells dividing and pushing against cells that divided minutes ago, the sound of a fiber stretching past its previous limit and settling into a new one. It sounds like the cooling of a house at night. Like something settling into a larger version of itself.

And then the other sounds. The ones that are harder to locate.

A clicking. Insectile, maybe, but too regular. Too rhythmic. It comes from the darkness above and it moves, and when it moves, the clicking stops and starts again from a new position, and the new position is always further than you would expect, as if the thing making the sound is either very fast or is not moving through space the way that things move through space.

A breathing. Not yours. Not the pipes. Something large, or something that sounds large, or something whose sound is amplified by the acoustics of the chamber in a way that makes you unable to determine its size. It is slow. It is patient. It does not change pace when you move, which means either it is not aware of you or it is aware of you and is not concerned.

And beneath everything, or perhaps above everything, or perhaps at a level that is not spatially located at all: a hum. A tone. Low, almost subsonic, felt in the chest more than heard in the ears. It is the sound of the place itself, the way a body has a sound if you are inside it, the way a seashell has the sound of the ocean because it is shaped to hold a particular resonance and the air inside it vibrates accordingly. The chamber is shaped to hold a particular resonance. It vibrates accordingly.

## What is wrong here

Let me be careful with this, because the wrongness is not dramatic. It is not the wrongness of a place that is broken or dangerous or haunted. It is the wrongness of a place where the rules have been *rewritten* and you have not yet been given a copy.

The space is smaller inside than it should be from outside. You did not measure, you could not measure, but there is a certainty about this that arrives not through evidence but through the body's own spatial sense, the proprioceptive awareness of how much room is around you. You walked a short distance to get here. You should be closer to the surface than you are. The space behind you, the way you came, is further than it should be, or rather it is exactly as far as it should be except that what it should be has changed.

Time does something. Not the obvious thing — not stopping, not reversing, not running fast or slow. Something else. Events happen out of order. Not dramatically. You hear the caterpillar chewing before the caterpillar arrives at the leaf. You feel the drop of water on your hand before the drop falls from the ceiling. The sound precedes the source. The effect precedes the cause. It is as if the space has a different relationship with sequence than the one you carry, and your senses are trying to reconcile two orders of happening and producing a kind of cognitive shimmer, a flicker, like a frame rate mismatch between you and the place.

The moths land on surfaces that are not there. You see a moth settle onto empty air — not hovering, not flying, *settling,* the way a moth settles when it has found a surface, folding its wings, committing its weight to something — and the surface is not visible. The moth sits on nothing. Or on something you cannot see. Or on something that has not arrived yet. After a moment, a shelf-fungus grows outward from the wall at that point, and the moth is resting on it, and has been resting on it, and was always resting on it.

The pale things in the water film move backward sometimes. Not in alarm — not the darting backward of a fleeing creature. They simply move in the wrong direction along the film, as if they had decided to un-travel a section of their path, and then they continue forward again from a point they had not previously reached. They do this without appearing to notice.

The pipes carry something that is not water. You know this because at one junction, where two pipes meet and the connection has been breached by root-growth, a substance leaks out. It is dark. It is viscous. It moves with a surface tension that is wrong — too high, too cohesive, too aware of itself. It does not fall so much as *assemble* at the breach, growing like a droplet of something that is deciding whether to become a liquid. When enough of it has gathered, it drops, and where it strikes the plant-matter floor, the floor *responds* — a brief ripple of growth, a pulse of acceleration, a moment where the living wall becomes more living, more active, more itself. Then it subsides.

The substance in the pipes is feeding the place.

Someone designed this. Someone built the pipes and laid the channels and established the grade and set the infrastructure in place, and then the infrastructure was colonized, and the colonization was not degradation — it was *continuation.* The plant and the pipe are not in conflict. They are in collaboration. The moss drinks from the channel. The roots drink from the pipes. The pipes deliver the dark substance that accelerates the growth that becomes the structure that holds the pipes. It is a system. It is a machine. It is a machine made of living things and dead things and things that are neither, and it is running, and it has been running for longer than the people who built it intended, and it will run for longer than they imagined, and it is not clear whether it is still doing what it was designed to do or whether it has evolved into something its designers would not recognize.

## The center

If you stand in the center of the chamber — and there is a center, the space has a geometry even if the geometry is not the geometry you entered with — you can feel the pulse of the whole system. The pipes. The moss. The growth. The dark substance. The caterpillars processing the walls into component matter. The pale things cycling through the water film. The moths moving between surfaces that exist and surfaces that do not yet exist. The clicking thing above you, whatever it is, moving through the dark in a pattern that is not random but is not legible.

You feel it in your chest. The hum. The resonance. The sound the chamber makes because it is the shape it is.

And you understand, standing there, that this place is not old. It is not ancient. It is *young.* The low erosion score is not because nothing has had time to wear the place down — it is because the place is still being built. It is embryonic. It is under construction. The walls are still thickening. The pipes are still being incorporated. The spatial wrongness is not corruption — it is growth. The place is outgrowing its own dimensions. It is adding rooms that do not yet connect to the outside. It is building internal volume that has no external analogue. It is becoming larger than its boundary, and the boundary is shifting to accommodate, and the boundary is also plant, and the plant is also growing, and the growth is fed by the dark substance in the pipes, and the pipes were put here by people, and the people are gone, and the place does not miss them, and the place does not need them, and the place is not finished.

It will not be finished for a long time.

The caterpillars chew. The moths settle on futures. The dark substance pulses through the pipes. The moss drinks. The space grows a room you have not entered yet, around a corner you have not found yet, and in that room, the same processes are beginning, and the walls are soft there, and the walls are damp there, and the walls are almost nothing yet, and they will be everything.

You leave. You go back up the short way, through the frame that might be a doorway or might be a gap between roots. The air changes. The coolness fades. You are outside, and outside is larger than you remember, and outside is brighter than you remember, and outside has a different relationship with sequence than the place you just left, and you are glad, and you are not sure why you are glad, and you are not sure the gladness is yours.

Behind you, a drop of water falls in the dark. It strikes a surface that was not there when you were.
