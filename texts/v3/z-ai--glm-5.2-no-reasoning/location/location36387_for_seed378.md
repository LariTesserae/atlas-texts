# The Condensaries of Vesh-Arran

## I. First Approach

The road rises. You notice the wetness before the heat — or rather, you notice them together, as a single sensation, the way a steam room announces itself. Water beads on the basalt slabs underfoot. Not from rain. There has been no rain. The stone itself is weeping, drawing moisture from air that cannot hold it, pulling condensation out of the bright, hot atmosphere like a cold glass in summer. Except the stone is not cold. Nothing here is cold. The water simply *is*, arriving on surfaces as if the world were sweating.

The road is old. The basalt slabs fit together without mortar, worn smooth by centuries of passage — feet, wheels, hooves, and things that do not travel on feet — and settled into a balance with the slope so complete that to walk it feels like being carried. Gullies have formed along its margins where runoff has found its preferred paths, and those paths have not changed in a long time. The stone is grooved. The grooves are home.

You are climbing a highland. The elevation announces itself not in cold — the heat refuses to release its grip — but in the quality of the air, which thins and clarifies until distance becomes architectural. You can see the shapes of things far away with a precision that feels almost hallucinatory. The horizon is not a blur. It is a line, drawn.

## II. The Settlement

Vesh-Arran sits in a shallow valley that is not really a valley — it is a wide fold in the highland, a place where the plateau dipped and stayed. The town is built of the same dark basalt as the road, and from a distance it looks geological. Roofs of dark slate. Walls of dark stone. Streets that are channels, cut narrow to manage the water that is always forming on every surface. The buildings are low and broad, flat-roofed, and their walls are thick in the way that walls built against heat become thick — not for insulation but for mass, for the slow absorption and release of temperature across the cycle of the day.

There are no plants. None. No trees line the streets. No gardens sit behind walls. No vines soften the corners. The town is mineral, entirely, and the only colors are the colors of stone: black basalt, grey slate, the rust-orange of iron fixtures, the pale yellow-white of salt deposits that bloom on every wall like frost.

The salt is not sodium chloride. It is something else — a mineral precipitate left behind when the condensation evaporates in the heat, and it forms crystalline traces on every surface that collects water. The buildings are patterned with it. Streaks descend from every roofline, every window ledge, every pipe exit, leaving white and pale-yellow traces that the residents do not remove because removing them would mean removing the evidence of water, and water here is wealth, is life, is the substance around which everything is organized.

The town is not large. A few thousand people, perhaps. It spreads across the fold in the highland in a pattern that looks organic but is not — it follows the water. Where condensation collects most efficiently, buildings cluster. Where the stone is drier, there are gaps, empty lots, the foundations of structures that were abandoned when their water stopped. The town is a map of its own hydrology.

## III. The Works

At the eastern edge of Vesh-Arran, where the fold deepens and the basalt gives way to a paler stone — a dense, chalky flint that the residents call *sahrim* — the industrial works begin. They are not hidden. They are not apologized for. They are the reason the town exists.

The works are condenseries.

Long, low structures of stone and iron, built to harvest water from the air. Their walls are pierced with thousands of small openings — vertical slits, finger-width, arranged in rows — that channel air through interior chambers cooled by deep-shaft ventilation. The air enters hot and wet. It meets cool stone. It gives up its moisture. The water runs down interior channels into collection basins, and from there into the cisterns beneath the town, and from there into the pipes that feed the foundries, the workshops, the homes.

The condenseries are always running. There is no off-switch. The heat of the highland drives the atmosphere to hold moisture; the elevation cools the deep stone; the differential does the work. Engines — real engines, iron and steam, built in the workshops of Vesh-Arran and maintained with a precision that borders on devotion — assist the flow, drawing air through the chambers faster, increasing yield. The engines are not loud. They are tuned to a low, rhythmic chuff that you feel more than hear, a pulse in the ground, in the stone, like a second heartbeat beneath the silence.

The foundries produce iron. The iron builds more condenseries and more engines and more pipes and more fixtures. The system is self-extending. It has been extending for centuries. The oldest condenseries are near the center of town, their stone walls darkened with age, their slits widened by erosion, their iron fixtures replaced so many times that no original component remains. They are like the road — settled into their function so deeply that they have become geological.

## IV. The Water

Water is everywhere and nowhere.

It forms on surfaces — on walls, on streets, on the stone faces of the highland, on the basalt slabs of the road — but it does not pool. It does not sit. It runs. The town is laced with channels, gutters cut into the stone at precise angles, carrying water from where it condenses to where it is needed. The channels are clean. They are maintained with a care that exceeds the care given to homes. A blocked channel is an emergency.

The air is wet. Not humid in the way of lowlands — this is a highland wetness, a thin, hot saturation that sits on the skin and does not evaporate because the body cannot cool itself in air that is already full. You are always damp. Your clothes are damp. The stone is damp. The iron is damp. Everything is covered in a film that is not quite water and not quite air.

At the edges of the town, where the channels empty into the fold's low point, there is a marsh. Not a marsh of plants — there are no plants — but a mineral marsh, a flat expanse of dark mud and pale salt and standing water that reflects the bright sky like a mirror laid on its side. The mud is fine, silty, the product of centuries of slow erosion, and it is alive in the way that mud can be alive when there is no root system to hold it. It moves. It shifts. It breathes when the temperature changes.

## V. The Fauna

There is no plant life on the highland of Vesh-Arran. No grass, no moss, no lichen. The stone is bare. But the place is not empty.

The fauna here does not depend on plants.

In the mineral marsh, the mud is thick with small organisms — flat, segmented things the color of the silt they live in, moving through the wet substrate with a motion like swimming, like burrowing, like both at once. They are not worms. They are not insects. They have no name in the common taxonomy because they belong to no common taxonomy. They are *ahzai* — a local word that means, roughly, "the things that are in the wet" — and they are the base of everything.

The ahzai feed on minerals. They process the silts and salts of the marsh through long, flat bodies that are essentially digestive tracts with a locomotion system attached. They extract energy from chemical gradients — sulfur, iron, the residual heat of the deep stone — and they excrete the pale salt that crusts the marsh's surface. They have been doing this for longer than the town has existed. The marsh is their work.

Above the ahzai, the ecosystem branches.

There are creatures in the condenseries — long-legged, narrow-bodied things that move across the interior chambers with a delicacy that seems impossible given their size. They are the color of wet stone. They feed on the ahzai when the ahzai are flushed from the marsh and pumped into the condensery channels with the intake water. They are not pests. They are tolerated. Their waste is mineral-rich and collects in the catchment basins where it is processed into a substance used in the foundries as a flux. The relationship is old. The condensery workers know which chambers the creatures prefer and leave them alone.

On the open highland, away from the town, there are larger things. You see them at a distance — tall, thin, moving across the bare stone with a slow, deliberate gait. They are not birds. They are not mammals. They are *tasharrin* — "the tall ones that walk dry" — and they range across the highland in small groups, feeding on the mineral deposits that form in the cracks and depressions of the basalt where condensation has been sitting long enough to leave its chemical trace. Their legs are long and jointed in ways that suggest they were built for uneven terrain. Their bodies are narrow, almost blade-like, and they move with a silence that is unsettling at a distance because the silence means they are aware of you and have chosen not to respond.

There are more. The sky has things in it — not birds, but gliding membranes that ride the thermal updrafts from the heated stone, spiraling in the bright air, their bodies translucent, catching light like oiled paper. They descend on the marsh in the late afternoon when the temperature differential between air and mud is greatest, and they feed on the surface ahzai in quick, precise strikes that leave no sound. The marsh erupts in a brief, silent chaos and then settles.

The ecosystem is mineral-based, chemical-driven, and entirely independent of sunlight for its primary energy. The sun provides heat. The heat drives the chemistry. The chemistry feeds the ahzai. The ahzai feed the rest. It has been running for a very long time.

## VI. The Atmosphere

The light is intense. The highland is above much of the haze that would soften the sun at lower elevations, and the air is clear enough that shadows have hard edges. The sky is pale — not blue but white, almost, bleached by the altitude and the heat. The stone reflects enough light that the ground is brighter than the sky in some directions, and walking the road into Vesh-Arran feels like walking into a lit room.

The heat is dry and wet at the same time. The air carries water but does not cool with it. The sensation is of being warmed and dampened simultaneously, as if the world were a greenhouse — which, in a sense, it is. The stone holds the day's heat long into the night. The night is not cold here. The night is merely less hot.

The silence is profound. The condenseries make their low, rhythmic pulse. The wind across the highland makes a thin, high sound in the channels and slits of the town. The ahzai in the marsh make no noise. The tasharrin make no noise. The flying membranes make no noise. The people of Vesh-Arran make very little noise. They speak at low volumes. They move with care. The town has the hush of a place where sound is not needed because the water does the communicating — its presence, its absence, its flow, its stoppage. You read the channels. You read the salt. You read the dampness on the walls. You do not need to be told.

## VII. The People

The residents of Vesh-Arran are few and spaced. The town feels emptier than it is because the people do not cluster. They move along the channels, between the condenseries, through the foundry yards, and they keep distance from one another as a matter of custom. Not hostility. Efficiency. The work is distributed. Each person tends a section of the system — a condensery, a channel, a stretch of road, a foundry, an engine. Their lives are spaced across the town like points on a graph, and they come together at the center, where the cisterns are, where the water collects, where the oldest buildings stand, and there they share what needs sharing and then return to their sections.

They are dark-skinned, dark-haired, and they wear clothing that is minimal and functional — wraps of woven mineral fiber, a material produced in the foundries from the processed waste of the marsh ecosystem. The fabric is grey, stiff when dry, soft when damp, which is always. It does not breathe because there is no point in breathing fabric when the air will not accept the moisture. The clothing is designed to hold water against the skin, not to wick it away, because the body's own condensation is its primary cooling mechanism in a place where the air cannot do that work.

They are not unhappy. They are not desperate. They are people who have found a way to live in a place that offers one thing — water, pulled from air — and have built everything around that one thing. The town is a machine for living in a wet, hot, empty, bright world, and it works.

## VIII. The Rules

The world here has rules that are not the rules of elsewhere.

First: water flows. It does not sit. If water sits, it is lost — to evaporation, to the salt, to the mud. Every surface in Vesh-Arran is angled. Every channel is graded. Every collection point is a waypoint, not a destination. The water moves from condensation to channel to cistern to pipe to use to marsh to ahzai to salt to stone to condensation. The cycle is closed. The cycle is everything.

Second: nothing rots. There is no organic matter to rot. The dead — human, animal, ahzai — are processed in the foundries. The mineral content is extracted. The rest is ash. Nothing is buried. Nothing is left. The highland is too hot and too wet for decay to work the way it works in plant-worlds. Instead, everything is reduced, chemically, and returned to the system.

Third: the silence is functional. Sound carries far in the thin air, and the fauna of the highland respond to it. The tasharrin come to investigate unusual sounds. The flying membranes descend. The marsh-things rise. The people of Vesh-Arran have learned, over centuries, that quiet is not just pleasant — it is survival. The condenseries pulse. The wind sighs. Everything else is still.

Fourth: the heat is not the enemy. The wet is not the enemy. The absence of plants is not the enemy. The enemy is stasis — the moment when the water stops moving, when the channels clog, when the engines fail, when the differential between air and stone equalizes and the condensation stops. The entire system depends on differential. Hot air, cool deep stone. Wet atmosphere, dry channels. The town exists in the gap between two states, and if that gap closes, the town dies. Everyone knows this. It is not spoken. It is the water in the walls, the salt on the stone, the pulse of the engines. It is the place itself.

---

## IX. Standing Here

You stand at the edge of the mineral marsh. The mud is dark and wet and reflects the white sky. Small movements beneath the surface tell you the ahzai are there, processing, living, feeding the chain that leads up through the condensery creatures and the tasharrin and the flying membranes to the people who tend the machines that pull water from air.

The town is behind you. The condenseries pulse. The salt crusts the walls. The channels run. The heat presses down. The light is bright enough to make you squint. The silence is deep enough to make you listen.

And what you hear, when you listen, is the sound of water forming on stone — not falling, not flowing, not raining — simply arriving, out of the hot, wet air, onto the cool, dark surfaces of a town that was built to receive it. A quiet sound. A sound like breathing. A sound like the world being alive in a way you had not previously recognized as life.
