# The Underneath

There is a place — you would not call it a town, exactly, though it was built with the intention of one — that sits below the lip of a spent limestone quarry. You enter through a cut in the rock face that someone, long ago, widened into a road grade. The road descends in switchbacks. By the second bend you are below surface grade. By the fourth, the sky has narrowed to a strip of grey above you, and the walls on either side have become the architect of your weather.

The quarry was worked out in the late industrial age. What remains is a bowl, roughly cathedral-sized in footprint, maybe slightly larger, with a level floor of compacted rubble and crushed stone, and walls that climb forty to sixty feet on three sides before opening to the terraced steps of the old excavation. When the quarrymen left, other people came. Not squatters, exactly. Settlers. They came with purposes.

---

The air is wet. Not rain-wet, though rain does come, sheeting down the quarry walls in drizzle-heavy curtains after weather passes overhead, and you can read the weather above by watching the walls weep. But the deeper wetness is ambient. The limestone holds water like a sponge. You can see it — dark patches high on the rock faces where the stone has drunk deeply, and lighter patches below where evaporation outpaces absorption. Everything that sits still too long accumulates a film of moisture. The bolt heads on the old pump housings. The canvas tarps stretched over the walkways. The handrails, which are iron and which have been iron for long enough that the rust has become a kind of patina, a surface that no longer progresses, that has reached an understanding with the damp.

The water table is near. You can hear it in the lowest corridor, where a steel pipe — laid by someone, for some reason, at some time — carries a trickle of groundwater from a sump to a drainage channel that follows the old quarry floor's gentle grade toward the southeast corner, where it disappears under a low stone wall into a culvert. The trickle is constant. It does not change with the weather above. It is the quarry's own tide.

---

The settlement — you might call it the Underneath, though the people who live here have several names for it, depending on which district you are asking about — is organized along the old infrastructure lines. There are three primary structures, all of them built into or against the quarry walls, and a network of covered walkways and gantries that connect them. The construction is layered: quarry-era stone walls at the base, then poured concrete from the first settlement period, then corrugated metal and timber framing from later additions. Pipes and cable runs thread along the gantries, some carrying water, some carrying electricity from the generator house, some apparently carrying nothing at all — dead lines that no one has removed because removal requires effort and the lines do not yet obstruct anything.

The generator house is the largest structure. It sits against the north wall, built into a natural alcove where the quarrymen had begun a new cut before finding the stone too fractured to be worth extracting. The alcove is perhaps twenty feet deep and forty feet wide, and the generator house fills most of it. Inside: a diesel generator, old but maintained, its block painted a green that has faded to something close to the colour of wet moss. It runs on a schedule — four hours in the morning, four hours in the evening — and when it runs, the settlement has electric light, and the pumps work, and a few other things work. When it is off, the settlement is lit by whatever people have: lanterns, battery lamps, the pale grey light that filters down from the sky above the quarry rim and reflects off the wet walls.

The diesel is brought in. How, from where, by whom — these are questions that have answers, but the answers are part of the settlement's logic, and the settlement's logic is not a public document. There is a road. The road connects to other roads. There is a supplier. The supplier is reliable enough.

---

Against the west wall: the habitation block. A two-storey structure, timber-framed, clad in a patchwork of materials — corrugated iron, plywood, salvaged quarry-office siding. It houses perhaps thirty people, in rooms of varying size and privacy. The ground floor is raised on short pilings — a precaution against the water that pools here after heavy rain, when the quarry floor's drainage is overwhelmed and shallow puddles spread across the compacted rubble. The pilings are stone, cut from the quarry itself, and they do not rot.

Against the south wall: the workshop. This is the strangest building, and the one that gives the settlement its reason. It is a long, low structure, half-buried in the quarry wall's talus slope, with a roof of corrugated steel that has been painted black, and a series of vent pipes that emerge from its roof and climb the quarry wall like mechanical vines, releasing thin streams of steam into the cool air. The steam is visible against the grey sky above the quarry rim. From outside, you can see it from the road, if you know where to look. It is the settlement's signature.

Inside the workshop: machinery. Lathes, drills, a small forge, a series of presses and rollers. The equipment is old — industrial-era, some of it possibly pre-war — but it is maintained, oiled, calibrated. The work that happens here is precise. The work that happens here is the reason the diesel supplier keeps supplying diesel, the reason the settlement persists, the reason thirty people live in a wet hole in the ground rather than somewhere drier and easier.

---

The work: they are making parts. Small parts. Parts for things that are themselves small, or parts for things that require a degree of precision that the workshop's particular configuration of old machines, skilled hands, and patient time can provide. The parts are machined, inspected, packed in oiled paper, and sent out by the same road the diesel comes in on. The parts are, let us say, not entirely conventional. They are not for cars. They are not for pumps or generators or standard industrial applications. They are for devices that exist somewhere else, devices that the people in the workshop understand in their specifics but do not necessarily understand in their purposes. A part is specified. The part is made. The part leaves. The specification for the next part arrives. This is the cycle.

---

The light is dim. Even when the generator runs and the electric lights are on — bare bulbs on cables strung along the gantries, work lamps in the workshop, a few fixtures in the habitation block — the light does not push back the gloom so much as inhabit it. The quarry walls absorb light. The wet stone eats it. What remains is a grey-green luminance, shadowless but also without warmth, like the light in a deep ravine on an overcast day. The settlement does not feel dark, exactly. It feels like a place where darkness has been negotiated with rather than defeated.

At night — and the settlement's night is largely a matter of consensus, since the sky above the quarry rim is the only clock, and it is often overcast — the light sources reduce to the lanterns in windows and the faint glow of the workshop's forge when it is in use. The generator shuts off. The quiet arrives.

---

The quiet. It is the settlement's most consistent quality. The generator, when it runs, is a distant, muffled throb — you feel it in the stone floor more than you hear it in the air. The workshop machines, when they run, produce a concentrated, enclosed sound that does not carry far beyond the workshop walls. The water — the trickle in the drainage channel, the drip from the quarry walls after rain, the slow seepage from the rock face — is the settlement's baseline sound. It is not silence. It is the sound of patience. The sound of a place that has been here for long enough that its noises have settled into a composition.

You hear, sometimes, the animals.

---

The fauna. This is the thing that surprises.

The quarry, despite its sparse vegetation — a few hardy ferns in the crevices of the rock walls, moss in the wet patches, a scattering of tough grasses along the quarry floor where the rubble has accumulated enough soil — is full of animal life. Not in the way a forest is full. In the way a cave is full. In the way a marsh is full. The animals here have found a niche, and the niche is the quarry, and the settlement is part of the niche.

There are birds. A species of swallow — you would need a guide to identify them precisely, and they are not quite right for any of the standard species — nests in the crevices of the quarry walls, high up, near the rim. They are small, dark-feathered, and they fly in fast arcs through the quarry's airspace, hunting insects that also live here, insects that breed in the standing water and the damp organic material that accumulates in the settlement's corners. The swallows are audible — a dry, clipped chirping — but they are not loud. They are part of the composition.

There are other things.

In the water — the drainage channel, the sumps, the shallow pools that form on the quarry floor after rain — there are creatures. You would call them newts, or salamanders, if you were being generous with your taxonomy. They are dark, sleek, six to eight inches long, and they live in the water and the wet stone. They are abundant. On a wet evening, after the generator has shut off and the lanterns are lit, you can see them at the edges of the pools, motionless, their eyes — which are large, and which are not quite the right shape for salamanders — catching the lantern light. They do not flee from people. They are accustomed to people. The people are accustomed to them.

The people in the settlement have a relationship with these creatures. The creatures are not farmed. They are not eaten. They are not, as far as anyone can determine, useful. But they are present, in numbers that vary with the water level and the season, and they are watched. The workshop has a logbook — one of several — in which someone, over years, has recorded the creatures' population, their behaviour, their patterns. The logbook is detailed. The logbook is not shared with outsiders. The logbook is, perhaps, part of the work, or part of something adjacent to the work, or part of something that is not the work at all but is the reason the work is done here rather than somewhere else.

There are also, in the darker corners of the quarry — the deep alcoves, the spaces behind the habitation block, the narrow gaps between boulders where the talus slope meets the floor — other animals. Larger. You see them as shapes, as movements at the edge of your vision. You hear them as soft, deliberate sounds: the scrape of something against stone, a slow exhalation that is not human. The settlers do not discuss them. The settlers do not appear to be afraid of them. The settlers, when asked, will say that the quarry is home to many things, and that the things have been here longer than the settlement, and that this is their arrangement.

---

The settlement has been here for — it depends on who you ask and how you count. The quarry was worked out perhaps sixty years ago. The first settlers came within a few years of the quarry's closure. The generator house was the first structure. The workshop was the second. The habitation block has been rebuilt twice. The current population has been stable for roughly fifteen years. The population is not growing. The population is not declining. The population is in a state of equilibrium with the settlement's capacity and the settlement's work and the settlement's arrangement with the things that live here.

This is a place that has settled into itself. The erosion parameter — the parameter that describes how established a landscape is, how long it has been in its current form — is high here, and it is visible in the way the structures have become part of the quarry. The stone walls of the generator house are stained with the same mineral seepage that marks the quarry walls. The iron gantries have rusted to the same colour as the iron-rich veins in the limestone. The habitation block's cladding has warped and faded until it appears to be a natural extension of the rock face it sits against. If you stood at the quarry's rim and looked down, you might not see the settlement at all. You might see only a quarry, wet, quiet, occupied by things that are not obviously human.

---

The atmosphere is cool. Not cold — the limestone provides thermal mass, and the quarry's position below the surrounding ground means it is sheltered from wind. But the dampness pulls the temperature down, and the lack of direct sun — the quarry walls shade the floor for most of the day, and the sky above the rim is more often overcast than not — means that the warmth that does arrive is thin and provisional. People wear layers. The habitation block has a heating system — a radiator, fed by the workshop's forge when it is running — but it is insufficient and supplemental. The real warmth comes from the habitation block's kitchen, which is always in use, and from the bodies of the people themselves, thirty of them in a structure that is not large, and from the animals, in their corners, which produce a heat that is difficult to account for by their size and number.

---

The sparse vegetation gives the place a mineral quality. There is no soil to speak of — only the crushed stone of the quarry floor and the thin organic layer that has accumulated in pockets and crevices. The ferns are the most visible: they emerge from the rock face in small clusters, their fronds dark green and wet, and they tremble in the air currents that move through the quarry when the wind above changes direction. The moss is everywhere, but it is thin, a film rather than a carpet, and it does not soften the stone so much as tint it. There are no trees. There are no shrubs. The quarry is a stone place, a water place, and the life that lives here lives in the stone and the water, not on the green growing surface of things.

---

The weirdness of this place is not in any single element. It is in the combination. A wet, dim, cool quarry, occupied by a small industrial settlement that makes parts for unspecified devices, inhabited by abundant animal life that includes creatures that are not quite standard, and larger things that are not discussed, and a logbook that records the creatures' population over years, and an arrangement — an arrangement between the settlers and the things that were here first — that is not written down and is not spoken of but that governs, in some way that an outsider cannot fully map, the settlement's rhythms, its work, its persistence.

The weirdness is in the sense, which accumulates slowly as you spend time here, that the settlement is not entirely the reason the settlement exists. That the work — the parts, the machining, the specifications — is a kind of maintenance, a kind of offering, a kind of rent. That the quarry is not empty. That the quarry was never empty. That the quarrymen left because the stone was fractured, yes, but also because they found, in the deeper cuts, things that were not stone, and the things were not hostile but were present, and presence was enough.

The weirdness is in the way the creatures in the water watch you, and the way the settlers watch the creatures, and the way the larger things in the dark corners are, perhaps, watching everything, and the way this watching — which is not threatening, which is not dramatic, which is slow and patient and old — is simply the condition of living here. The quarry is a place where attention is the fundamental currency. You pay attention to the water. You pay attention to the creatures. You pay attention to the specifications. You pay attention to the arrangement. And the quarry pays attention to you.

---

If you were to stand in the centre of the quarry floor, at the intersection of the three gantries that connect the generator house, the habitation block, and the workshop, you would feel the place's character most completely. The wet stone beneath your boots. The dim grey light from above, filtered by cloud and reflected by wet walls. The sound of water, everywhere, in the rock, in the channels, in the air. The faint throb of the generator, if it is running, or the deeper quiet if it is not. The sight of the swallows, high above, crossing the strip of sky. The knowledge, or the suspicion, that you are being watched — not with malice, not with hunger, but with the steady, unblinking attention of something that has been here for a long time and that regards your presence as temporary, and interesting, and not its concern.

The settlement will be here after you leave. The work will continue. The creatures will be counted. The logbook will be updated. The diesel will arrive. The parts will leave. The arrangement will hold. The quarry will weep when it rains, and the water will find its way to the drainage channel, and the channel will carry it to the culvert, and the culvert will carry it somewhere deeper, somewhere that the settlers do not go, somewhere that the larger things, perhaps, call home.

This is the place. It is small, and it is wet, and it is patient, and it is not entirely known.
