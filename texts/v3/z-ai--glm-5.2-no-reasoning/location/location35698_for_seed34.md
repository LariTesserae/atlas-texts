# The Weeping Plateau

## First Approach

You come up through a cut in the older rock — a pass that funnels wind and moisture alike — and the world opens. Not downward, as you'd expect from a high place, but *outward.* The plateau stretches in every direction further than the eye can settle. There is no single horizon. There are several, stacked one behind another in gradations of wet grey and pale ochre, each slightly more obscure than the last, until the farthest is just a guess the eye makes against the mist.

The ground is high. Not mountainous — there are no peaks here, no dramatic ascents — but *high* in the way a table is high: the whole landscape has been lifted, and you feel it in the thinness of air, in the way clouds come in not from above but from the sides, rolling across the plateau at head-height like they've lost their altitude and are looking for somewhere to rest.

It rains here. Not dramatically — rarely a storm — but with a persistence that has shaped everything. The rain comes in long, grey sheets that move across the plateau in visible fronts. You can stand in dry air and watch a wall of water approach from two kilometres away, see it darken the stone as it passes over, and then it reaches you and everything is wet and quiet and the world contracts to the space inside the rainfall, and then it passes and you stand in the aftermath and the stone steams faintly and the air smells of wet mineral and something older, something biological but faint, like the memory of soil.

The rock weeps. This is not metaphor — or not only metaphor. The plateau is layered: dense basaltic stone beneath, but above it a porous tuff, a pumice-like material shot through with voids and channels. Rain does not run off here. It sinks. It enters the stone and travels through it for hours, days, sometimes weeks, emerging finally from the faces of low cliffs and escarpments as perpetual seeps, as weeps, as slow trickles that never fully dry. The entire plateau is draped in these slow tears. Every cliff face runs with water. Every overhang drips. The stone is stained dark in vertical lines where the water has traced its path over centuries, and where the water exits the rock, it deposits minerals — iron-red, calcium-white, manganese-black — so that the cliff faces read like strange texts, mineral script written by water that has been reading the rock for longer than anything human has been reading anything.

## The City of Pools

And here is the strangeness, or the first strangeness: there is a city.

Not a city in the way that word usually operates — no towers, no walls, no streets in the grid sense. But organized construction. Intentional. Built over time, built by many hands, built and maintained and added to and repaired. It is a city of pools.

The people here — and there are people, though you encounter them rarely, a figure moving between structures in the middle distance, a curl of smoke from a vent-hole that might be a hearth — have built not against the water but *with* it and *into* it. The settlement follows the escarpment edge for perhaps four kilometres, a loose linear city built along the line where the plateau's cap of porous stone meets a lower layer of denser material and the water emerges.

The structures are low. Stone, mostly. The same tuff that the plateau is made from, cut into blocks and fitted without mortar — the wetness of the place would dissolve mortar, would wash it out over seasons, so they use friction and geometry and the weight of the stone itself. The buildings are rectangular, flat-roofed, and their roofs are slightly concave — designed to catch rain and channel it into cisterns built into the structures themselves. Every building is also a reservoir. Every roof is a watershed.

Between the buildings, channels have been carved into the stone of the plateau — not built, *carved*, the stone itself has been shaped into a water infrastructure that connects building to building, pool to pool, channel to channel. The water moves through the city in a network that is neither pipes nor streams but something between: open channels, narrow and precise, cut into the living rock, following the natural gradients that the plateau's ancient geology has provided. The channels are perhaps fifteen centimetres wide and ten deep. The water in them moves slowly but constantly. You could follow any channel and it would lead you to a pool, and the pool would overflow into another channel, and that channel would lead to another pool, and so on, the entire city linked by water the way other cities are linked by roads.

Some pools are large — communal, perhaps, gathering points. Others are small, private, set within the walls of individual compounds. Some are clearly functional: water for drinking, for cooking, for working. Others seem designed for sitting beside. The pools are not decorative in the way a fountain is decorative — they are functional in a different sense. They are for being near. The sound of them — the tiny sound of water entering water, of overflow, of the slow movement of a surface that is always being replenished — is the sound of the city.

And the city is quiet. That is the thing you notice. No machinery. No beasts of burden. No market sounds. The loudest sound is the water, and the water is quiet — it moves in small volumes through small channels, falls in small drops into small pools. The acoustic environment is the sound of water and the sound of wind and the sound of stone, and over all of this, the sound of rainfall when it comes, which is itself a kind of silence, a white noise that fills the space between sounds and makes them indistinct.

The people you do not see much of. You see evidence: a tool left on a ledge, a drying rack with something on it — fibers, perhaps, or thin sheets of something biological, a door-flap of woven material moved slightly by the wind as if someone has just passed through. The population is not large. The city has the feel of a place that was built for more people than currently inhabit it. Some of the structures have the particular look of buildings that are maintained but not used — roofs kept clear of debris, channels kept clear of sediment, but no wear on the thresholds, no smoke from the vents. The city is in a slow contraction. Not decay — nothing is decaying — but a drawing-in, a gathering of fewer people into fewer buildings while the rest are kept standing, kept functional, kept ready.

## The Ecology of Wet Stone

Vegetation is sparse. This is not a barren landscape — it is too wet for that, and the wetness prevents the absolute starkness of a desert — but what grows here grows in specific, opportunized ways. There are no trees. The elevation and the wind and the thin soil make trees impossible. What there is, is:

**Stone moss.** A dark, dense moss that grows exclusively on the wet faces of rock where water seeps. It forms vertical colonies on cliff faces and escarpments, following the water lines. In places it is thick enough to look like dark fur on the stone. It does not grow on horizontal surfaces — only where water moves downward across stone. The weeping cliffs are lined with it, so that the water that emerges from the rock doesn't run over bare stone but over moss, and the moss filters it, slows it, changes it. The water that drips from the bottom of a moss-covered cliff is not the same water that entered the stone at the top. Something has been exchanged.

**Basin algae.** In the pools — both the natural pools in the stone and the built pools of the city — there is a thin film of algae, blue-green, that forms on the surface of still water. It is not thick enough to obscure the water; it is more like a faint tint, a sheen. When the wind touches these pools, the algae film moves in slow, iridescent patterns. It is alive, clearly, but barely. It grows at the rate the water grows. The pools are never empty, so the algae is never stressed. It simply exists, a biological film on a perpetual surface.

**Crust lichen.** On the flat surfaces of the plateau, away from the water seeps, the stone is covered in places by a pale crust lichen — white-grey, sometimes with a faint yellow tint — that grows in circular patterns, spreading centimetre by centimetre over centuries. It is the slowest-growing thing here. Some of the lichen colonies near the city are perhaps a metre across, which means they are older than the city. Perhaps older than the idea of the city. They are not disturbed. The people here walk around them. There is a path that makes a clear detour to avoid a particularly large lichen colony, and the detour has been walked so many times that it has worn a shallow groove in the stone, a curve in the path that exists to protect something that grows a millimetre a year.

**Rush grass.** In the places where water collects on the surface — shallow depressions in the stone where the basalt has formed natural basins, or where the channels of the city empty into low ground before the escarpment edge — there grows a thin, tough rush. Not tall. Perhaps thirty centimetres at most. It grows in dense tufts from cracks in the stone where organic matter has accumulated. It is the closest thing to a plant community here. In the wettest depressions, the rush grows thick enough to form a mat, and in these mats, insects live — small, dark, flightless insects that move through the wet fiber and feed on the algae and the decaying rush material. These insects are the base of the food chain. They are the reason the birds come.

## The Birds

Fauna is rare, but it is present, and what is present is *specific.*

There are birds. One species, or perhaps two — it is difficult to tell because they are rarely seen close. They are large. Falcon-sized, but broader-winged. They ride the thermals that form along the escarpment edge, where the wet stone meets the warmer air below, and they circle in slow, patient patterns, watching the ground. They are not hunting the insects — the insects are too small. They are hunting something else.

There are lizards. You see them rarely, and only when the rain has just stopped and the stone begins to steam. They emerge from cracks in the rock — narrow, flattened lizards, dark grey, the colour of the stone, and they position themselves on the wet surfaces where the sun, when it breaks through, will warm them. They are the prey of the birds. The relationship is simple: the lizards eat the insects, the birds eat the lizards, and the insects eat the algae and the decaying matter, and the algae lives on the water, and the water comes from the rain, and the rain comes from — somewhere. The sky. The atmosphere. The systems that move across this vast plateau and deposit their moisture into the stone and let the stone distribute it.

There is nothing else. No mammals. No amphibians, despite the wetness — the temperature is too low, the nights too cold. No fish in the pools, even the large ones. The ecosystem is: stone, water, moss, algae, lichen, rush, insect, lizard, bird. Nine elements. And the people, who are outside it, or beside it, or within it in a way that is not ecological but something else.

## The Rules of the Place

Some things become clear through observation, through the slow accumulation of detail that a place like this requires:

**Water is the principle of organization.** The city is built where water emerges from stone. The infrastructure moves water. The buildings store water. The social life of the community, such as it is, takes place around water — the large pools are where people gather, not a market or a square but a pool, a place where water is present and moving and alive. The hierarchy of the city, if there is one, is read in water: the buildings closest to the primary seeps, where the water is freshest and most constant, are the best maintained and the most occupied. Further from the seeps, where the water has traveled through more channels and arrived more depleted, the buildings are less cared for. Distance from water is distance from centrality.

**The stone is the principle of permanence.** Everything that lasts here lasts because it is made of stone or rests on stone. Everything else — the woven door-flaps, the tools, the racks, whatever the people use that is not stone — is temporary. It will be replaced. The stone will not. The city has been here long enough that the stone it is made from has begun to weather in the same patterns as the plateau stone around it — the same mineral stains, the same moss colonization on the wet faces, the same lichen crust on the horizontal surfaces. The city is becoming part of the plateau. It is not a thing placed on the landscape but a thing made *of* the landscape, and time is making the distinction less relevant.

**Silence is the principle of social life.** The city is quiet because the people are quiet. They do not call to one another across distances. They do not speak loudly. Whether this is cultural or practical — whether the silence is chosen or imposed by the environment — is not something you can determine from outside. But the effect is clear: the city operates at the volume of its water. The channels make a sound like someone whispering continuously. The pools make a sound like someone breathing. The rain makes a sound like someone covering their face with their hands. The people move within this acoustic register. You see two figures meet at a pool and speak, and you cannot hear them from thirty metres away. They speak at the volume of the water.

**The weather is the principle of time.** The plateau is large enough — vast enough — to have its own weather. Not weather that arrives from elsewhere, but weather that *generates* here, that forms in the particular conditions of elevation and wetness and stone and wind. Rain comes in the afternoon, usually. Mornings are clear and cold. The mist sits in the late afternoon and overnight. The wind picks up in the middle of the day and drops in the evening. This cycle is reliable enough to structure life around — the people are active in the morning, before the rain, and in the evening, after it. During the rain, they are inside. The rain is not a disturbance; it is a punctuation. It divides the day the way a bell divides the hour. It is the clock.

## The Wide View

Step back. Step away from the city, from the escarpment, from the specific. Walk out onto the open plateau.

The stone stretches in every direction. It is not flat — it is gently undulating, swells and depressions that shift the surface by perhaps ten or twenty metres over kilometres, enough to create horizons that are near, that you can walk to in an hour, that resolve into more stone as you approach. The sky is large here. The clouds are low and move quickly, and their shadows race across the stone, patches of darkness and light that travel faster than you can walk. When the sun is out, the wet stone catches it and throws it back, a glare that is not hot — the temperature is cool, perhaps twelve degrees, the wind making it feel cooler — but bright.

The scale of the place becomes apparent only when you walk. You walk for an hour and the city has diminished behind you but the landscape has not changed. You walk for two hours and the city is a low smudge on the horizon, and the landscape has not changed. You walk for three hours and the city is gone, and the landscape has not changed. The plateau is a place that does not reward the eye — there is nothing to see at a distance, no landmarks, no features of note — but rewards the feet, the body, the experience of being *in* it. The stone under your feet is wet and slightly rough. The wind is constant. The air smells of wet mineral and cold. You are aware of the sky as a physical thing, a weight above you, and of the stone as a surface beneath you, and of yourself as a figure on that surface, small, moving, temporary.

There are pools out here too. Natural pools, formed where the stone has been eroded into basins. Some are no larger than a bathtub. Some are twenty metres across. They are filled with rainwater and the slow seep from the stone, and they are surrounded by the dark ring of stone moss and the thin tufts of rush grass. They are the oases of the plateau. Not because they are rare — they are frequent, perhaps one every few hundred metres in the wetter areas — but because they are where the sparse life of the place concentrates. You stop at one and you see the insects in the rush, and the algae on the water, and if you wait, perhaps a lizard on the stone nearby, and above, far above, the slow circle of a bird.

The plateau is not dramatic. It is not sublime. It is not terrifying or beautiful in the way that mountains or canyons are beautiful. It is a place of *persistence.* Of water and stone in a long, slow conversation. Of a city that has been here long enough to begin to look geological. Of people who have organized their lives around the movement of water through stone and have done so quietly, without monument, without event, without anything that would draw the attention of history. They are here. The water is here. The stone is here. The rain comes in the afternoon. The lizards come out after the rain. The birds circle above. The lichen grows a millimetre a year. The water enters the stone and emerges from the stone and the moss grows where it emerges and the pools fill where it collects and the city is built where it is most reliable and everything is connected to everything by the water, which is the language of this place, its syntax, its grammar, its voice.

And the voice is quiet. Almost silent. A whisper of water in stone channels. A breath of wind across wet rock. The slow, patient sound of a place that has been itself for a very long time and is in no hurry to be anything else.
