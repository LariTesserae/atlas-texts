# The Vertical City of Khar-Vost

## A Description

---

The first thing you notice is the silence. Not true silence — there is always something, here — but a silence relative to what a place of this complexity ought to produce. A city of this scale should roar. Instead it murmurs, as though listening to itself.

Khar-Vost rises from the salt plain like a wound in the earth's crust pushed inward and upward simultaneously, a geography that makes more sense once you understand it was never built from the ground up. It was carved from the ground *out*. The city is what remains.

---

## Terrain and the Lie of the Land

The plain is sodiumite — a pale mineral crust, alkaline, fine-grained as powder where the surface has been disturbed and elsewhere fused into a brittle glaze that rings underfoot like fired ceramic. It stretches in every direction to horizons made indistinct by haze and the sheer scale of the place. This is a continental landmass, or the dried bed of what was once something analogous to a sea on a world where water was never quite the point.

There is no water. There has not been water for a span of time that the geology records in patient, unconcerned strata. What remains is mineral: sodiumite crust above a deep compacted ash-layer above basaltic substrate above something older and stranger that the deepest bores of the city's original engineers never fully catalogued. The chemistry here is non-aqueous. Fluids exist — thick, slow chlorinated brines that seep through fractures at depth, sulfur compounds that sublime at certain temperatures — but nothing you would call water. Nothing that behaves as water behaves.

The elevation is extreme. The plain sits roughly four thousand meters above what the original surveyors designated as datum, and the city rises from the plain's western edge along a fault-block escarpment that climbs another three thousand in a series of stepped terraces and sheer faces. The air is thin but not unbreathably so — the atmosphere is dense at lower altitudes and the continental mass generates its own weather systems, feeble and slow, which push air upward along the escarpment's face in predictable diurnal cycles.

What this means, standing at the base of the city's lowest tier, is that you feel the height. Not as dizziness, but as a pressure differential — ears adjusting slowly, the body aware that it has climbed into a thinner regime. The sky above is a pale, washed-out amber during the long day, deepening to a dim rust at the cycle's nadir. The light source is diffuse — the atmosphere scatters it broadly — and there are no sharp shadows. Everything is lit evenly, like a stage flooded with work lights.

---

## The City: Form and Fabric

Khar-Vost is old. Not ancient in the romantic sense — not ruined, not fallen — but *settled*. It has existed in its current form for long enough that the processes of erosion and the processes of maintenance have reached a kind of equilibrium. Walls are worn smooth where wind funnels through corridors. Surfaces are patinated with mineral deposits laid down over centuries of slow chemical exchange between the stone and the air. Repairs blend into original construction so thoroughly that the distinction has become meaningless. The city is not deteriorating. It is not improving. It is *continuing*.

It is a city carved from the escarpment itself. The rock here is a dense, fine-grained tuff — volcanic ash compacted under pressure into something that holds detail like bone. The builders (whoever they were — the current inhabitants have origin stories that contradict each other and the archaeological record alike) worked *into* the stone rather than upon it. Streets are trenches. Plazas are excavations. Dwellings are chambers. The city's profile is the inverse of what you expect: rather than rising above the landscape, it descends into it. The highest points are the original escarpment surface, untouched. The lowest are the deep galleries, forty meters down, where the light from above reaches only as a faint ambient glow reflected off pale stone.

But this is not a cave system. The carving is organized — residential blocks, industrial quarters, processional ways wide enough for the movement of large equipment. There is infrastructure: conduits running along corridor ceilings for the transmission of power and communication signals, ventilation shafts bored at precise angles to catch the diurnal air cycles, drainage channels (for the brines, not water — nothing here is for water) cut into floors with the kind of precision that implies institutional engineering, not individual improvisation.

The construction is layered. The deepest levels are the oldest, cut with simpler tools in straighter geometries. As you ascend through the tiers, the work becomes more complex — curved walls, arched ceilings, decorative incisions that follow the tuff's natural bedding planes in patterns that might be aesthetic, might be structural, might be both. The upper levels show evidence of industrial expansion: broader passages, reinforced ceilings, the insertion of mechanical systems into spaces originally carved for other purposes. Factories have been built *into* the rock, their floors levelled with poured mineral aggregate, their ceilings pinned with iron rods where the tuff showed signs of stress.

The population density is low. The city could hold many more than it does — the residential galleries stretch on, block after block, most of them occupied but quiet, the inhabitants separated by empty chambers and disused corridors. You walk for minutes without encountering anyone. The emptiness is not decay. It is the result of a culture that values space, or has learned to, or has simply always had it. The city breathes because it is not full.

## Atmosphere and Light

The air is warm. Not hot — nothing here is hot in the way that implies danger — but consistently, uniformly warm, around thirty-two degrees, with a dryness that makes the warmth feel larger than it is. Your skin loses moisture quickly. The mineral content of the air gives it a faint taste — alkaline, metallic, like licking a coin. There is no humidity to speak of. The brines at depth do not evaporate into the atmosphere in any significant quantity; they are too deep, too slow, too chemically bound to their substrate.

Light enters the city from above — through the wide streets that function as slots in the escarpment's surface, through ventilation shafts, through the open courtyards that punctuate the residential blocks at irregular intervals. Because the light is diffuse, it does not create dramatic shafts or beams. It fills the upper levels evenly and fades gradually as you descend. The lower galleries are not dark — the stone itself is pale enough to reflect and scatter what light reaches it — but they are dim, amber-grey, the color of old bone in warm shadow.

Artificial light exists but is used sparingly. The infrastructure carries electrical power — generated somewhere deeper in the escarpment, by systems that hum and click in ways you can feel through the floor more than hear — and the inhabitants have access to lamps, but they are not lit during the day-cycle. The light from above is sufficient. At the cycle's nadir, when the sky dims to rust, lamps come on in doorways and corridor junctions, low-wattage, warm in color, giving the lower city a settled, domesticated quality. It feels like evening in a place that has no evening, only a gradual dimming.

---

## Industry and Technology

The city's industrial capacity is real and operational but not dominant. It exists in a specific quarter — the mid-levels on the escarpment's southern face, where the ventilation is best and where the tuff gives way to denser basalt that can bear the vibration of machinery. The sound here changes. Elsewhere, Khar-Vost is quiet; here, there is a low, persistent mechanical thrum, felt in the teeth and the bones of the skull more than heard.

The factories process mineral. Sodiumite is refined into industrial compounds. The deep brines are pumped, filtered, and separated into their component chemicals. Sulfur is extracted and reconstituted in forms that have trade value in networks that extend, presumably, beyond the escarpment — though the infrastructure of that trade (roads, vehicles, depots) is not visible from within the city. The industrial quarter smells of hot metal and alkaline dust and the sharp, electric tang of electrochemical processes. It is the only part of Khar-Vost that feels like it belongs to a world you might recognize.

The technology is industrial in character but not crude. The machinery is well-maintained, adapted to its environment over generations of incremental improvement. Pumps are driven by compressed air rather than electricity — the air-compression systems are the city's primary mechanical infrastructure, a network of pressurized conduits that run through the walls like a second nervous system. The air is compressed by wind turbines on the escarpment's upper face, turned by the diurnal cycles, stored in deep reservoirs carved from the basalt. When the wind is low, the reservoirs bleed slowly. When the wind is high, they refill. The system is elegant in the way that only things designed under constraint can be — no excess capacity, no waste, every joule accounted for.

Electrical power exists but is secondary. It runs the lamps, the communication conduits, certain precision instruments in the industrial quarter. The generators are deep, as mentioned — their exact nature is not visible from the inhabited levels, and the inhabitants speak of them with the kind of casual familiarity that suggests they are neither mysterious nor particularly interesting. They work. They have always worked. Someone maintains them. This is enough.

---

## Sound

Khar-Vost is quiet in the way that a library is quiet — not silent, but constrained. Sound exists, but it is dampened by the stone, absorbed by the mineral surfaces, scattered by the irregular geometry of the corridors. Voices carry but arrive softened, as though heard through a wall. Footsteps are muffled by the dust that covers every horizontal surface — a fine, pale powder, sodiumite and volcanic ash and the accumulated residue of centuries of slow erosion, never thick enough to sweep, never thin enough to ignore.

The dominant sound, if you stop and listen for it, is the wind. Not gusting, not howling — a slow, steady pressure of air moving through the city's channels in response to the diurnal temperature cycle. It sounds like breathing. Like the city is an organism with a respiration rate of one breath per day, inhaling through the upper corridors as the air warms and rises, exhaling through the lower galleries as it cools and sinks. The ventilation shafts were placed to amplify this effect, and the result is a place that feels, acoustically, alive. Not metaphorically. The air moves. The stone channels it. The sound it makes is the sound of a large system functioning as designed.

In the industrial quarter, the wind-sound is overlaid with the mechanical hum — a low frequency, maybe forty hertz, that you feel as much as hear. It does not intrude. It is part of the background, like the sound of your own heartbeat.

In the residential galleries, the silence is more complete. Here you hear small sounds: the scrape of a tool on stone, the whisper of fabric, the click of a door latch. The inhabitants are not loud. They move through their city with an ease that suggests long familiarity, and their movements produce the sounds of long familiarity — quiet, purposeful, minimal.

## Fauna

This is the strangeness.

For a world with no water, no vegetation, and a mineral-only substrate, Khar-Vost is densely populated by life. Not human life — the inhabitants of the city are something else, and will be addressed separately. *Animal* life. Or something that occupies the ecological role of animal life in a system that does not run on photosynthesis or aqueous biochemistry.

The dominant fauna are the lithovores — creatures that consume mineral substrate and metabolize it into energy through a process that the city's scholars understand in outline but not in detail. They are arthropodal in basic structure: segmented bodies, multiple pairs of legs, chitinous exoskeletons that incorporate the minerals they consume. They range in size from the length of a finger to the span of a hand, and they are everywhere.

In the upper levels, where light enters, the lithovores are active during the day-cycle, moving across the stone surfaces in slow, methodical patterns, grazing on the mineral deposits that form on exposed rock. They are pale — cream-colored, ochre, sometimes tinged with the pink of sodiumite — and they move with a deliberation that makes them easy to observe. They do not startle. They do not flee. If you place your hand near one, it will pause, wave its antennae (or the structures that function as antennae), and then continue feeding. They are not tame. They are simply unconcerned.

The larger species live deeper. In the lower galleries, where the light is dim and the air is warmer, the lithovores grow to the size of small dogs — heavy-bodied, slow-moving, their exoskeletons dark with the accumulated minerals of decades of feeding. They carve shallow grooves in the stone as they move, wearing paths in the tuff that the inhabitants do not repair because the grooves are shallow and the creatures are part of the city's ecology. They eat the stone. The stone regrows, slowly, through mineral deposition. The creatures eat it again. This cycle has been running for longer than the city has existed in its current form.

There are other animals. Flying things — not birds, but something analogous, with membranous wings and bodies the size of a fist — that roost in the ventilation shafts and emerge at the dimming of the light to feed on the smaller lithovores. They are silent in flight. You become aware of them only when one passes close enough to feel the air displacement. Burrowing things in the deep ash layers that you almost never see — only their trails, raised ridges in the powder, evidence of something that moves beneath the surface like a fish moves beneath water. Predators, somewhere in the system, because the populations of the smaller lithovores are stable and a stable population implies a limiting factor, but you do not see them. The inhabitants speak of them without alarm. They are part of the world. They belong here.

The abundance of life in a place so barren of what most ecologies require is the central strangeness of Khar-Vost. The system runs on mineral chemistry, not on sunlight converted to sugar. The energy enters at the bottom of the food chain through processes that are neither photosynthetic nor chemosynthetic in any familiar sense — the lithovores metabolize specific mineral compounds in ways that release usable energy, and the rest of the ecosystem is built on that foundation. It is a complete ecology. It has primary producers, primary consumers, predators, decomposers (the things in the ash, probably). It is alien only in its substrate.

## The Inhabitants

The people of Khar-Vost — they are people, or close enough that the distinction is a problem for philosophers — are tall, thin, and very still. They are not human. Their skin is the color of the stone they live in — pale, mineral-toned, slightly translucent at the temples and the backs of the hands, where the veins show as dark lines beneath the surface. Their eyes are large and dark and do not reflect light. They move slowly, speak rarely, and occupy their city with the same unhurried patience that the lithovores bring to their feeding.

They are the builders' descendants, or the builders themselves, or something that moved into the builders' space after the builders left — the question is unresolved and does not seem to trouble them. They maintain the city. They run the factories. They operate the air-compression systems and the electrical generators and the trade networks. They eat — but what they eat is not clear. Possibly the same mineral compounds the lithovores process, refined and prepared in ways that the industrial quarter facilitates. They do not eat in public. They do not discuss it.

Their number is small. The city holds perhaps eight thousand of them, in a space designed for many times that. They live in the carved galleries, one or two to a chamber, surrounded by emptiness. Their possessions are minimal. Their rooms contain sleeping-platforms carved from the tuff, storage niches, a lamp, sometimes a few objects whose function is not immediately apparent — tools, perhaps, or what we would call art, though the category does not map cleanly onto their aesthetic. The objects are geometric, incised with patterns that echo the natural bedding planes of the stone, and they are placed with care. Not displayed. Placed. There is a difference.

They are not unfriendly. They do not avoid contact. But they do not seek it, and their interactions with each other — observed from a distance, because they do not interact with visitors often — are spare. A word. A gesture. A shared task performed in silence. Their social fabric is not built on conversation but on proximity, on the shared experience of inhabiting the same space, on the slow accumulation of mutual presence over time.

## The Weirdness

The mineral regrows. This is the fact that underlies everything else and that the visitor must understand in order to understand Khar-Vost at all.

The tuff is not inert. Given time — long time, decades, centuries — the stone surfaces that are worn by the lithovores or eroded by wind or cut by the original builders are replenished by a process of mineral deposition that occurs spontaneously wherever the sodiumite crust is present. The material precipitates out of the air itself, drawing on the mineral content of the atmosphere, building layer by layer at a rate of perhaps a millimeter per decade. This is why the city does not deteriorate. This is why the erosion is in balance. The stone is being unmade and remade simultaneously, and the rate of unmaking and the rate of making have converged, over the long life of the place, into a steady state.

The inhabitants know this. It is not a mystery to them. It is the foundational fact of their world — the stone lives, in its way, and they live in it, and the arrangement is sustainable because the stone replenishes itself and they maintain what they build and the lithovores eat and are eaten and the system continues. The city is not built on a substrate. The city is built *within* a process.

This is why they are patient. This is why they are quiet. They live at the tempo of the stone.

---

## Rules of the World

The world of Khar-Vost operates on principles that are consistent and comprehensible but not always intuitive:

**Mineral deposition is constant.** Stone surfaces grow. This means that carved spaces slowly narrow over centuries unless they are maintained. The inhabitants spend a portion of their time re-cutting corridors and galleries that have constricted — not as a crisis, but as routine, the way another culture might sweep a floor. The marks of their tools are visible everywhere: fresh cuts in pale stone, the geometry crisp where the deposition has not yet begun to soften it.

**The diurnal cycle drives everything.** The temperature differential between day and the dimming creates the wind that powers the compression systems, the air circulation that keeps the lower levels habitable, and the behavioral rhythms of the fauna. The cycle is long — roughly thirty hours of light, twelve of dimming — and the city's operations are calibrated to it. Activity peaks in the first hours of light, when the air begins to move and the systems come up to full capacity. It slows during the dimming, when the wind dies and the lamps come on. The inhabitants sleep during the dimming. So does everything else.

**The brines at depth are the chemical engine.** They are not water. They are dense, chlorinated, and they carry the mineral compounds that the lithovores metabolize. They seep upward through fractures in the basalt, evaporate partially at the tuff layer, and deposit the minerals that the stone incorporates and the fauna consumes. They are the closest thing this world has to a circulatory system, and they are slow — moving at rates measured in centimeters per year, driving processes that operate on timescales that make human impatience irrelevant.

**Sound carries meaning.** The acoustics of the carved city are such that specific sounds — a tool striking stone, a door closing, the low hum of the compression system — are identifiable at a distance and carry information about what is happening where. The inhabitants use this. They do not need to communicate verbally to know what is occurring in their city. The city tells them, through its sounds, and they listen.

## Walking Through Khar-Vost

You enter from the plain. The sodiumite crust gives way to the first carved passage — a trench, three meters wide, two meters deep, cut into the escarpment's foot and rising at a gentle grade. The walls are smooth, worn by wind and the passage of bodies over a span of time you cannot estimate. The stone is pale, almost white, and the light from above fills the passage evenly. There are no shadows.

You walk for ten minutes before the passage widens into a plaza — an excavation roughly twenty meters across, open to the sky, ringed by doorways. The doorways are closed, their doors made of a dark mineral composite, fitted precisely into the tuff frames. One is ajar. Through it, you can see a chamber, dim, empty except for a sleeping-platform and a lamp that is not lit.

The plaza is quiet. A lithovore — the size of your hand, cream-colored, its legs moving with the slow precision of a creature that has never known predation pressure — crosses the floor in front of you, following a shallow groove in the stone that has been worn by generations of its ancestors. It does not look at you. It reaches the far wall and begins to feed, its mandibles rasping against the surface with a sound like fine sandpaper on wood.

You climb. The passages lead upward, branching, rejoining, forming a network that is complex but not labyrinthine — there is logic to it, a plan, though the plan has been modified so many times that the original intent is obscured. You pass through residential blocks where the doorways are spaced evenly and the corridors are wide enough for two people to pass without touching. You pass through an industrial area where the walls are reinforced and the air carries the smell of hot metal. You pass through a gallery, deep and dim, where the large lithovores move across the floor like slow, living stones, their exoskeletons dark and heavy with accumulated mineral.

You climb for an hour. The air thins. The light brightens. The sounds of the city — the wind, the hum, the occasional distant scrape of a tool — fall away until you are walking in near-silence, the only sound your own breathing and the soft crunch of dust under your feet.

You emerge at the top. The escarpment's edge. The plain stretches below you, sodiumite-white, featureless, enormous. The sky is amber. The wind is steady and warm and smells of mineral and distance. You can see the city's footprint from here — the grid of passages cut into the rock, the courtyards, the ventilation shafts, the whole carved complexity of Khar-Vost laid out like a diagram of itself, a plan view of a place that exists in three dimensions but is best understood from above.

It is very quiet. The wind moves. The stone grows. The lithovores feed. The inhabitants maintain what they have and let the rest be. The city continues.

You stand at the edge and the world is very large and very slow and very old and it does not need you to understand it. It will continue whether you understand it or not. This is not indifference. This is the nature of a place that has found its rhythm and intends to keep it.

---

*Khar-Vost. A city carved from living stone on a world without water, inhabited by the patient and the mineral, sustained by processes so slow they are indistinguishable from stillness until you look closely enough to see the motion. Go there if you can tolerate silence. Stay if the silence becomes comfortable. Leave before it becomes necessary.*
