# The Drowned Quarter

The water has been here long enough that no one remembers dry ground. Long enough that the city stopped fighting it and became something else.

---

What remains is submerged to the second story in most places, third in the low squares where the foundations gave way and the streets folded into canals. The buildings — sandstone and fired brick, old work, the kind that survives because it was made to survive — stand like elderly people wading in a current they have accepted. Their upper floors still breathe air. Their lower floors breathe water.

You move through this place by boat or by swimming, or by the narrow catwalks of salvaged stone and salvaged wood that bridge from rooftop to rooftop where the buildings cluster close enough to touch. These catwalks are old. Their surfaces are worn smooth by feet — human feet, and other feet — and they are the streets of this place now. The old streets below are for the water.

It is warm. The water holds warmth the way a body holds warmth, and it is never cold here, not in the way of deep places. The heat comes from below, from the volcanic shelf the city was built on, and it rises through stone and through water alike. On a still day, you can see the heat shimmering at the surface of the flooded squares, and the water at the edge of a rooftop is warmer than the water at the bottom of a doorway. The air, too, is warm and damp, thick with the smell of water that has been inside stone for a long time.

Light barely reaches here. What light exists comes as a kind of memory — a grey-blue glow that filters down from the surface during the day, never bright, never directional. It is diffuse, sourceless. You cannot tell where it comes from; it seems to exist in the water itself, as if the water produces it. At night, the only light is what rises from below: a pale luminance that comes in patches, moving, from the organisms that live in the volcanic cracks far beneath the city. On the darkest nights, the water glows, and the buildings above are lit from beneath.

This makes the upper stories darker than the water itself.

---

The buildings are dense. They were built close together, long ago, for defense or for community or for the simple logic of a settlement that grew along a single ridge of high ground before the water came. Now they form a chain of stone islands separated by canals that were once streets. The architecture is consistent — you can see the same hand in the doorways, the same proportion in the windows, the same style of arch over every entry — but it has been modified by habitation and by the water itself.

Wooden additions perch on the rooftops. Not much wood. Wood is rare here. Most structures are made from what the city itself provides: blocks prised from ruined walls, repurposed lintels, door-slabs laid flat to become floors. The construction is careful, considered, built to last. It lacks the desperation of the newly displaced. These are homes, not shelters.

Inside the upper rooms, you find evidence of long habitation. Smooth stones set into walls to hold lamps. Niches cut into brick to hold belongings. Rope lines strung across rooms to hang things from, to keep them dry. The floors are worn. The walls are worn. Everything is worn, and everything works.

Below the waterline, the buildings are different. The stone holds. But the mortar between the stones has softened in places, and the water has found its way in, and in the lower rooms — the ones you must swim to enter — the walls are furred with a soft growth, pale, that feeds on the mineral content of the dissolved mortar. It is not aggressive. It has taken decades to reach its current state. It is in balance.

---

The sparse vegetation exists only where it can exist. There is very little soil above the waterline, and what there is has been carried up in baskets and pots over generations. A few tough, waxy plants grow in shallow trays on the rooftops. They have small leaves and thick stems, and they need little water, which is a paradox because they are surrounded by it. But the water is not for them — it is too mineral, too warm, too full of what the stone gives it. They drink rain when it comes, which is not often, and they survive on the damp that rises from the wet stone beneath them.

Below the waterline, there is no vegetation at all. The light is too thin. What grows there is not plant but animal — or something between.

---

The fauna is what makes this place itself.

In the water, life is abundant. The warm mineral current that seeps up through the volcanic shelf feeds a complex community that has established itself in the drowned city. The pale growth on the submerged walls is part of it — a kind of filter-feeder that takes minerals from the water and is in turn eaten by small, shelled creatures that move slowly across the submerged walls, grazing. These are eaten in turn by the swimmers.

The swimmers are dark, flat, the size of a hand, and they move through the flooded streets in schools that follow the warmth. They have no eyes that you can see. They navigate by the heat and by the chemical signature of the water. They are the primary food source for everything above the waterline.

And above the waterline, the birds.

The birds are the dominant presence. They have long legs and long necks and they stand on the rooftops and the catwalks and the edges of the flooded buildings, still as the stone itself, watching the water. When they see a swimmer near the surface, they strike — fast, precise, neck like a whip — and take it. They are silent. They are patient. There are hundreds of them.

They build their nests on the upper ledges of the taller buildings, in the gaps where walls have partially collapsed, in the sheltered corners where two walls meet. The nests are large, built from stolen materials — cloth, cord, fragments of wood, bone. They are communal. A single building may hold thirty or forty nests, and the birds come and go in a pattern that follows the warmth of the water and the movement of the swimmers below.

They tolerate the human presence. Not warmly. But they do not attack, and they do not leave. They watch. They are always watching.

There are other things. Crabs — pale, long-legged — that scuttle across the wet stone at the waterline, too fast to catch, too numerous to worry about. They eat what the birds drop. Something larger lives in the deep canals, where the flooded streets open into wide squares and the water goes dark. You see it only in movement — a displacement, a shadow, a slow turning that is not the current. It does not come near the buildings. It does not need to.

---

The sound is minimal.

The water moves, but slowly. There is no tide here, or if there is, it is so small as to be unmeasurable. The water shifts with temperature and with the deep current that runs beneath the city, and this produces a low, constant, almost subsonic murmur that you feel more than hear. It is the sound of the city itself, vibrating.

The birds are silent. Even when they strike the water, they do so with a precision that produces almost no sound — a soft, wet plip, and then nothing. The swimmers are silent. The crabs are silent.

What you hear, when you stop and listen, is the sound of the buildings. Stone settling. Mortar shifting. The faint creak of the upper structures as the stone breathes with the heat. And, occasionally, from far off, a low, resonant tone that comes from the deep canals, as if something down there is calling across distances too great to see through.

Human sound is rare and therefore significant. A footstep on a catwalk carries. A voice carries farther. People speak softly, not from caution but from habit. The place imposes its own volume.

---

It rains perhaps once a month. When it does, it is an event.

The rain comes as a wall — a front of water that moves across the drowned city from the open water to the east, and it is heavy, and it is warm, and it turns the canals to foam for an hour. The birds do not fly from it. They stand in it. The people on the rooftops stand in it. The plants drink. And then it passes, and the air is briefly clear, and the light from the surface is briefly bright, and for an hour afterward you can see the swimmers in the water below you, silver and dark, reforming their schools.

Then the murmur returns, and the dim, and the quiet.

---

There is no technology here. Not even the memory of it. The people who live in the upper floors of the drowned buildings use stone and cord and bone and the materials the city provides. They catch the swimmers with nets of woven fiber. They collect the pale growth from the submerged walls and dry it and eat it. They use the birds — not as food, but as signal. The birds watch the water. When the birds move, something is coming. When the birds are still, the water is safe.

They have no metal. They have no glass. They have no machines. They have the stone and the water and the living things, and they have built a life from those alone.

The city is old. It was old before the water came. The stonework tells you this — the quality of the arches, the scale of the original doorways, the remnants of carved decoration on the higher walls that the water never reached. This was a place of consequence, once. Now it is a place of continuation.

---

The world has rules here, and they are simple.

The water is warm and it is always warm. It will not harm you, but it will not help you. You can swim in it. You can drink it, though it tastes of stone and will give you cramps if you drink too much. It is not hostile. It is simply the medium.

The dark is absolute at night. You do not move at night unless you must. The catwalks are narrow and the canals are deep and the sounds are different in the dark. You wait for the glow from below, and you move by that, or you wait for the grey of dawn.

The birds are not to be touched. Not because they will hurt you, but because they will leave. And if they leave, the water is unwatched.

The deep canals are not to be entered. Not because of what lives there — though that is reason enough — but because the current beneath them is unpredictable, and the buildings on their edges are less stable, and the water is darker and colder and does not glow.

You do not go below the third floor of any building that stands in the open squares. The growth there is different. Thicker. It moves, though the water does not. It is not dangerous, but it is not to be disturbed. It is the oldest thing here, older than the city, and it has its own purposes.

---

This is the place. A drowned city, warm and dark and quiet, inhabited by the descendants of whoever built it and by the birds that came after and by the things that live in the water that took the lower stories and made them its own. It is not dying. It is not ruined. It is in a state that has no word in any language that still exists here — the state of having been one thing and having become another and having been this other thing for so long that the first thing is barely a memory.

The water is patient. The stone is patient. The birds are watching. The dark is full of small lives. And the people move softly across the tops of the buildings, and they do not go down, and they do not leave, and this is their home.
