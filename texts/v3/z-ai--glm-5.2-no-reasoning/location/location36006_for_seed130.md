# The Inversion Fields

## I.

You come to understand, after a time, that you are standing inside something's throat.

Not literally. Not in any way a body would confirm. But the shape is here — the long vertical descent of the walls, the moisture that thickens as you go deeper, the way sound arrives from above as though swallowed. The Inversion Fields were built, and then built again, and then the building was forgotten, and then the forgetting settled into permanence, and now what remains is this: a manufactured basin three kilometers across, half a kilometer deep, its walls terraced with the corroding machinery of an industry that no longer has a name.

Everything here was made. There is no ground beneath you in the sense that your feet expect. What you walk on is the uppermost layer of a compaction lattice — a hexagonal grid of fired ceramic tiles laid over a substrate of crushed slag and vitrified industrial waste, laid over another lattice, laid over another substrate, descending in engineered strata for forty meters until you reach the original bedrock, which itself was cut and refinished and sealed before the first surface layer was ever laid. Someone did this. Someone decided that the earth here was insufficient and replaced it. That was long ago.

The basin collects rain. That is its primary function now, though it was not its original function, and the original function is debated or unknown or simply does not matter anymore. Rain falls — frequent, thin, persistent, the kind of rain that does not pour but rather materializes, as if the air itself were condensing into wetness rather than releasing it. The water arrives on the ceramic tiles and does not drain. Or rather: it drains slowly, through the hairline fractures between tiles, through the compaction layers, down into the lattice of pipes and cisterns that someone built into the body of the basin forty meters below the surface. The water moves through this hidden plumbing with a sound that you feel more than hear — a low, arterial thrum, like a building's circulation system running through walls.

The surface is wet. It is always wet. The rain does not stop so much as pause, and the pauses are brief enough that the tiles never fully dry. The ceramic is a dark burnt umber, almost black when saturated, and it reflects nothing because the light is too low for reflection. What light there is comes from two sources: the thin gray leak of sky visible when you look straight up through the basin's opening, and the bioluminescent deposits that have formed in the crevices between tiles — a pale, cold cyan that beads and pools in the fractures like something living, though nothing here is alive.

---

## II.

The scale reveals itself slowly. From the center of the basin — where the compaction lattice is thickest and the ceramic tiles have been replaced most recently, which means perhaps a century ago — you can see the walls rising on all sides. They are not vertical. They are terraced, each terrace perhaps fifteen meters deep, and each one houses a different stratum of the original industrial complex. The terraces step inward as they rise, like an inverted ziggurat, so that the opening at the top is perhaps two kilometers across while the floor where you stand is three. The walls are not smooth. They are thick with pipework, with the carcasses of furnaces, with conveyor housings and pressure tanks and the skeletal frames of assembly machines that were never removed because there was no reason to remove them and no one to do the removing.

The machinery is not dead. That is the thing that takes time to understand.

The power station on the seventh terrace — a squat, windowless block of riveted iron and refractory brick — still runs. No one feeds it. No one maintains it. But it runs. Its turbines turn at a speed that you can hear as a deep, shuddering oscillation that arrives through the floor tiles and enters your chest cavity and stays there, vibrating against your ribs. The fuel source is unknown. Some have theorized geothermal, drawn from the deep boreholes that puncture the basin floor at regular intervals. Others believe the power station feeds itself — that its output powers the pumps that circulate the water that cools the generators that produce the output. A closed loop. A perpetual motion machine that is not perpetual because it draws from somewhere, but the somewhere is hidden, and the hiddenness is itself a kind of answer.

This is why the basin is deafening. Not from one source. From all of it. The power station's turbines. The pumps in the sub-floor — thousands of them, each one a small electric heartbeat, together a vast arrhythmic chorus. The water moving through pipes. The rain striking ceramic. The wind that descends into the basin and strikes the terrace walls and organizes itself into eddies and currents that moan through the open frameworks of the assembly machines. The furnaces that still burn on the eleventh terrace, producing nothing, heating nothing, simply combusting in their sealed chambers with a low, constant roar that you feel in the wet tiles under your feet. The sound is not one sound. It is the accumulated noise of an entire industrial complex that was built and powered and then abandoned in place while still running. It is the sound of automation without purpose. It is very, very loud.

You cannot hold a conversation here. You shout and the sound is swallowed. You clap your hands and the basin does not echo — the sound is absorbed by the wet surfaces, the porous ceramic, the corrosion-softened metal. The noise is a fluid thing. It fills the space the way the water fills the hidden cisterns — completely, pressurized, finding every gap.

---

## III.

The vegetation is sparse. This word — vegetation — is used loosely. What grows here is not properly plant life. It is something that has emerged from the conditions, from the wetness and the ceramic and the residual chemical profile of the industrial waste that forms the substrate, and it resembles plant life the way a reflection resembles a person: from a distance, enough; up close, wrong.

On the lower terraces, where the water weeps from the walls in constant thin sheets, there are formations that might be colonies or might be single organisms. They are pale. They are the color of milk left too long in a glass. They grow in branching structures that reach outward from the terrace walls in horizontal shelves, and their surfaces are wet — not with the water that runs over them but with their own moisture, a dense, gelatinous sweating that catches the cyan light from the crevices and glows faintly, making the terraces look like shelves of lit from within. They are warm to the touch. Barely. A degree or two above the ambient cool, which suggests some metabolic process, some internal engine burning something — chemical, mineral, electrical — to maintain itself.

Nothing visits them. Nothing eats them. There are no animals here. There are no insects. There are no birds. The basin is a place where the biological has been replaced by the mechanical and the mechanical has been running so long that it has begun to produce something that resembles the biological again, not out of intention but out of entropy, out of the slow blurring of categories that happens when a system operates for millennia without interruption. The pale formations on the walls are not alive. They are not dead. They are a third thing, a thing that happens at the end of a long industrial process that no one turned off.

On the basin floor, the vegetation — such as it is — takes a different form. In the fractures between tiles, where the water collects and the cyan luminescence is strongest, there are growths that resemble root systems. They are not roots. They are crystalline, formed from the mineral content of the water and the chemical substrate, and they branch and bifurcate and follow the fracture lines with a regularity that looks deliberate. They are hard. They do not move. But they grow — slowly, over years, over decades, extending through the lattice like frost through a crack in old stone. Some have penetrated the ceramic tiles themselves, splitting them, and where the tiles are split, water pools on the surface in shallow pans that reflect the sky, which is the color of wet slate and offers nothing.

---

## IV.

The temperature is cool. Not cold — cool. The kind of cool that settles into your hands and feet and stays. The kind that makes the wetness on the ceramic feel colder than it is. The basin's microclimate is a product of its geometry: the walls block the wind except at the opening, where a constant slow draft descends and then rises and descends again in a cycle that the old engineers must have designed, because the draft corresponds to the thermal output of the still-running machinery. The hot terraces — the eleventh, where the furnaces burn — heat the air, which rises, which cools against the upper walls, which descends, which passes over the wet floor and picks up moisture and deposits it on the lower terraces where the pale formations grow. This is a weather system. Small, closed, artificial. It has been running for as long as the machinery has been running, which is to say: for an amount of time that no record captures. The erosion on the terrace walls suggests centuries. The compaction of the substrate suggests longer. The corrosion on the oldest pipework suggests longer still.

This is an ancient place. Not in the way that mountains are ancient. In the way that a machine becomes ancient — by running past the extinction of its makers, past the forgetting of its purpose, past the collapse of the civilization that built it, past the rise and fall of whatever came after, and continuing to run because no one told it to stop and it was built to last and it has lasted. The basin is in its final form. It has eroded and been repaired and eroded again and the repairs have eroded and the erosion has become a feature rather than a defect. The walls are not what they were. The terraces have shifted. The pipework has been replaced by mineral deposits that serve the same function. The compaction lattice has compressed and fractured and been infiltrated by the crystalline root-things and the infiltration has actually strengthened it, filling the fractures with a harder material than the original substrate. The basin has settled into itself. It has found the shape it will hold for the next stretch of deep time, and the shape is this: a wet, loud, dark, warm-in-places, cool-in-places, slowly growing, slowly corroding, permanently running machine-landscape that does not need anyone and has not needed anyone for a very long time.

## V.

The light. The light is almost nothing.

From the basin floor, the sky is a narrow oval of gray. On overcast days — which is most days — it is a uniform pale gray that provides enough illumination to see by, barely, if your eyes have adjusted. The ceramic tiles are visible as dark shapes on slightly less dark shapes. The terrace walls are visible as gradients of shadow. The pale formations on the lower terraces are visible because they glow — that faint, cold, internal luminescence that is not light exactly but is the absence of darkness, a thing that marks itself against the dark rather than dispelling it.

The cyan luminescence in the tile fractures is the basin's true lighting system. It is not electric. It is not chemical in any way that is understood. It appears in the fractures where water sits and where the crystalline root-things have grown, and it intensifies at night — or what would be night if you could see the sky, which you mostly cannot. The light pulses. Not regularly. Not randomly either. It follows a pattern that corresponds to the water flow through the sub-floor, to the pressure in the cisterns, to the rhythm of the hidden pumps. When the pumps cycle on — every forty seconds, or every eighty, or every two hundred, the intervals shift and drift like a heart with an arrhythmia — the fractures brighten. The water moves. The roots, if that is what they are, seem to pull something from the flow, and the pulling produces light. Or the light is a byproduct. Or the light is the point.

At night, the basin is a field of blue fire trapped in the earth's skin. The fractures run in every direction, illuminating the floor from below, and the pale formations on the terraces glow in their own muted white, and the furnaces on the eleventh terrace leak a dull orange through the seams in their housings, and the whole place is lit from beneath and from within and from the sides, and the sky above is black and offers nothing, and the basin is a cup of light held in the dark, and the light is not for you.

## VI.

The sound has structure. This becomes apparent after hours, after days. It is not noise. It is not music. It is a composition, though no one composed it. The elements are these:

**The turbines.** A low, sustained vibration in the 40-60 Hz range, felt as much as heard. It is the foundation. It does not change. It has not changed, perhaps, for centuries. It is the sound of the power station, and it is the sound of the basin's heartbeat, and when you are inside the basin you are inside the sound, the way you are inside the water when you swim.

**The pumps.** Higher — a buzzing, clicking, mechanical chatter that cycles. Each pump has its own pitch. Each cycle is slightly different from the last. The pumps are not synchronized. They have drifted over the years, over the decades, and their rhythms have become a polyrhythm that never repeats, that never resolves, that sounds sometimes like it is approaching a pattern and then diverges. This is the closest thing the basin has to a voice.

**The water.** Moving through pipes, moving through fractures, moving over the terrace walls in thin sheets that produce a sound like tearing — not the sound of water you know from rivers or rain, but a thinner, sharper, more metallic sound, because the water is moving over manufactured surfaces and has acquired a mineral signature that makes it denser, slightly more viscous, and the sound it produces is the sound of a liquid being forced through a geometry that was designed for it but that has degraded, so the water finds new paths, and the new paths produce new sounds, and the new sounds are added to the composition.

**The furnaces.** A roar, but sealed, muffled, coming through the brick and iron housings as a deep, uniform exhalation. It is the only warm sound. It is the sound of combustion that produces nothing, that goes nowhere, that simply happens because the fuel is there and the ignition is there and the chamber is there and no one has turned it off. It has been exhaling for longer than anyone can calculate.

**The wind.** The least constant, the most variable. It descends into the basin and strikes the terrace walls and the assembly frames and the pipework and produces a sound that is different every time because the wind is different every time, but the surfaces it strikes are the same, and the combination of the two produces a sound that is both familiar and strange — a moaning, a whistling, a low buffeting that comes and goes and that, when it is gone, leaves the other sounds more audible, more present, as if the wind were a kind of silence that occasionally arrives and then departs and leaves the noise in relief.

Together, these sounds are overwhelming. Not in volume alone — though the volume is considerable, 85-95 decibels on the basin floor, higher near the power station, higher still on the eleventh terrace — but in density. The sound fills the basin the way the water fills the cisterns. There is no space between sounds. There is no quiet. There is no pause. The sound is a medium. You move through it. It resists you. It pushes back.

---

## VII.

The weirdest thing about the basin — weirder than the still-running machinery, weirder than the cyan light, weirder than the pale formations that grow and glow and are not alive and are not dead — is the regulation.

The basin regulates itself. This is not metaphor. There are systems here — mechanical, hydraulic, perhaps chemical — that maintain the basin's conditions within narrow parameters. When the water level in the cisterns rises too high, pumps activate and divert it to overflow channels that drain into the deep boreholes. When the furnaces on the eleventh terrace overheat — and they do, periodically, the orange glow through the seams brightening to a harsh yellow — vents open and steam releases and the temperature drops. When the crystalline roots in the tile fractures grow too dense and begin to impede the water flow, the water pressure increases and the roots fracture and the fragments are carried into the cisterns and filtered out. The basin does not need anyone. It has not needed anyone for a long time. It is a machine that maintains a machine that maintains a landscape that maintains a microclimate that maintains the machine. The loop is closed. The loop has been closed for longer than the loop can remember, which is a strange thing to say about a loop, but the basin is a strange place, and the strangeness is not in any single element but in the totality — in the fact that all of this exists, and runs, and maintains itself, and produces light and sound and water and warmth and growth, and there is no one here to see it or hear it or feel it or know it.

Except you. Standing on the wet ceramic tiles in the near-dark, with the sound in your chest and the cool air on your skin and the cyan light pulsing under your feet, in a basin that was built by people who are gone, to serve a purpose that is gone, in a world that is gone or changed or simply moved on, and the basin does not care. The basin will continue. The turbines will turn. The pumps will cycle. The furnaces will burn. The water will fall and collect and drain and fall again. The roots will grow. The light will pulse. The sound will fill the air and the air will hold the sound and the sound will be the air, and the basin will be what it is, and it will be this, and it will be this, and it will be this.
