# The Croak

There is no sky here. Or rather, the sky exists the way a rumor exists — asserted by inference, never confirmed. What hangs above is a canopy so total and so many-layered that the word "canopy" is insufficient. It is a ceiling. A continent. A second ground grown downward and meeting the true ground halfway, so that everything between is a green and breathing corridor, warm and dark and soaked in a roar that never stops.

---

## The Sound First

You would hear it before you saw any of it. Hours before, if you were approaching. Not a sound — a sound implies a source, a moment, a beginning and end. This is a condition. A texture of the air. It accumulates as you near, a low vibration felt in the sternum, then a hum in the molars, then a pressure in the ears that resolves, as you close the final distance, into something words cannot manage but the body understands: ten million voices speaking at once, most of them not human, most of them not language, all of them urgent.

Frogs. That is the nearest analogy, and it is not near enough.

The amphibians here have no single name because no one has arrived to name them and leave. They are not the small, elegant jumpers of temperate ponds. They are dense-bodied, fist-sized and larger, with slick backs in greens and blacks and rust, and throats that inflate like bladders to a size nearly equal to their skulls, and when they call — when *all* of them call, which is most of the time — the collective noise is a physical thing. A wall of sound. A continuous churning roar composed of bass drones, mid-range rattles, staccato clicks that overlap and interleave and produce, in their aggregate, a kind of music that has no melody and no rhythm but possesses, unmistakably, a *structure*. A meaning. A conversation happening at a speed and in a register that the human ear can detect but the human mind cannot parse.

There are other sounds beneath and within the frog-roar. Insect drones — cicadas, katydids, things with translucent wings and bodies like polished seeds, their stridulations so rapid they register as tones rather than pulses. The creak and groan of living wood, branches and vines and aerial roots shifting against each other in air that moves, always moves, because everything here moves. Water dripping, somewhere, everywhere, a polyrhythmic percussion that neither begins nor ends. And beneath all of it, so low it is felt more than heard, the throb of the ground itself: the slow, deep resonance of a land that is still becoming, still settling, still deciding what shape it will hold.

The sound does not stop. It does not pause for night or day. It does not quiet when the rain comes — it intensifies, as if the rain were a conductor raising the tempo. The roar at its peak presses against the eardrums like water, like submersion, and the experience of standing in it is less like hearing and more like being held in a vibrating medium. Some travelers who have passed through places like this — and there are not many — report that after enough hours the sound ceases to be unpleasant and becomes something else: a womb-sound, a presence, something that holds you. Others report that it never becomes tolerable. Others do not report at all, because they did not stay long enough to form an opinion.

## The Land

This is a young place. Not young the way a meadow is young, succeeding a forest, succeeding a glacier. Young the way a wound is young: still open, still forming, still hot with the energy of its own creation.

The ground is low. Barely above the water table. In the wet season — and the distinction between wet and dry here is a matter of degree, not kind — much of it is underwater, a shallow, warm flood that moves slowly across the surface and retreats just as slowly, leaving behind pools and rivulets and long, sinuous channels that cut through the dark soil like veins through a leaf. The soil itself is new: dark, rich, almost black, volcanic in origin, the product of eruptions that occurred within the span of living memory, or what would be living memory if anything here had a memory long enough to contain them. The minerals are raw. The nutrients are abundant. Everything that falls here — leaf, branch, animal, fruit — rots quickly and is absorbed, and the richness of the soil is the richness of ongoing death, ongoing transformation, ongoing incorporation.

The terrain has no sharp relief. No cliffs, no summits, no dramatic drops. What it has is texture: hummocks and hollows, ridges of exposed root, depressions where water collects and stands, gradual slopes that lead nowhere in particular but redirect the flow of water and the movement of animals. The land is building itself, and it is building in the way that young land builds: not by carving grand features but by accumulating, grain by grain, layer by layer, the detritus of its own inhabitants. The frogs contribute. The insects contribute. The plants contribute. Each generation lives on the compost of the last, and the ground rises, imperceptibly, on the bodies of its residents.

There are places where the young stone shows through — basalt, dark and rough, with the ropy texture of recent cooling, the pahoehoe surfaces that look like frozen muscle, like the skin of something that was moving and stopped. These outcrops are the bones of the place, the only hard architecture in a landscape otherwise made of soft things: soft soil, soft water, soft rot, soft bodies. They are covered, even here, in moss and lichen and the clinging roots of epiphytes, but they persist, and they give the land a kind of spine, a reminder that beneath all this biological frenzy is a geology that is also active, also young, also still deciding what it wants to be.

---

## The Water

Water is not abundant here, but it is *present*, in the way that a neighbor is present: always nearby, occasionally at the door, sometimes inside. The humidity is absolute. The air carries water the way a sponge carries water — not as droplets but as a condition, a saturation, so that everything exposed to the air for more than a few minutes acquires a film of moisture, a slickness, a sheen. Leaves are wet. Bark is wet. Skin is wet. The frogs, who are always wet, are the most at home here, their skins designed for exactly this: a world where the boundary between air and water has been dissolved, where you breathe water and swim in air.

There are pools. Not many, and not large, but distributed across the terrain in every depression, every hollow, every place where the ground dips below the water table. They are warm — body temperature, almost, or warmer — and dark, stained with tannins from the leaves that fall into them and rot. They smell of iron and vegetation and something else, something organic and sharp, the smell of life processing itself. The pools are not stagnant, not exactly — there is movement, in and out, as the water table rises and falls with the rain — but they are still, and in their stillness they breed, and what they breed is mosquitoes, and midges, and the other small, winged, blood-seeking life that forms the next layer of this ecosystem's architecture.

Rain comes in bursts. Not storms, exactly — there is rarely wind — but sudden, dense downpours that arrive without preamble, dump their water in minutes, and depart, leaving the air temporarily cooler and the ground temporarily flooded and the frogs temporarily louder, if that is possible. The rain is warm. It does not refresh. It adds. It is another layer of the dampness, another contribution to the saturation, and the land absorbs it quickly, greedily, the dark soil drinking it down and holding it and offering it, over the following hours and days, back to the air through evaporation and transpiration, so that the water cycles here not in seasons but in hours, a tight, fast loop that keeps everything wet and everything growing and everything alive.

## The Vegetination

The plants here do not grow. They *erupt*.

This is the paradox of the young, warm, wet, dark: the conditions for growth are so favorable that the competition is not for resources but for *space*, for *time*, for the chance to exist before being overgrown, overshadowed, overtaken. Every surface is claimed. Every surface is contested. The ground is a dense tangle of herbaceous plants — ferns, principally, ferns of every size and form, from the tiny, delicate maidenhairs that carpet the soil between roots to the massive tree ferns that rise like columns, their trunks thick with the roots of other plants, their crowns of fronds spreading wide in the dim light. Between the ferns are broad-leaved herbs, plants with leaves the size of shields, leaves the size of tables, leaves so large they seem to be competing not for light but for sheer *area*, for the right to occupy the most space.

And above the ferns, the trees. Not the ancient, cathedral trees of old-growth forest — this is not an old-growth forest, this is a *first*-growth forest, the first forest, the forest that arrived when the lava cooled and the rain came and the spores landed and everything that could grow did grow, all at once, with the urgency of organisms that know, in some cellular way, that this opportunity is temporary, that this richness is a pulse, a surge, a moment in the life of a land that will eventually stabilize and calm and become something less frantic. The trees are tall but not enormous. They are fast-growing, soft-wooded, their trunks smooth and pale, their branches reaching up and out with a desperate, angular energy that makes them look less like trees and more like hands, fingers spread, grasping for light.

And on the trees, and on the ferns, and on each other, the epiphytes: orchids, bromeliads, mosses, liverworts, lichens, ferns upon ferns, plants growing on plants growing on plants in a recursive explosion of biomass that makes the concept of "ground" seem arbitrary. Everything is a surface. Everything is a substrate. The vertical structure of the forest is not layers but a *gradient*, a continuous transition from ground to canopy in which every centimeter is occupied, every gap is filled, every crack and crevice and surface is claimed by something green and growing and reaching for more.

The light that reaches the ground is minimal. The canopy above — that continental ceiling — filters the sun to a dim, green, underwater glow, and the ground-level plants survive not on direct light but on the faint, diffuse radiance that filters through, a light so reduced that the human eye reads it as dusk at noon. This is the source of the darkness here: not the absence of light but the *consumption* of light, the interception of every photon by the layered, stacked, compressed biomass that stands between the sun and the ground. The plants have made their own darkness. They have eaten the light and turned it into themselves, and the world they have created is the world they can survive in: warm, wet, dark, and roaring.

## The Fauna

The frogs are the sound, but they are not the only presence.

There are the insects, first: not a presence but a *medium*. The air is thick with them. Not swarming, not in the apocalyptic sense — but saturated, so that any volume of air contains within it dozens of flying, hovering, darting bodies, from the near-invisible gnats that hang in still clouds to the dragonflies that streak through with the precision of arrows, their iridescent bodies catching what little light there is and scattering it in brief, electric flashes. The mosquitoes are here, and they are legion, and they are the price of entry, the toll that the living world extracts from any warm body that enters its domain. They do not swarm in the theatrical way of northern mosquitos but in the persistent, exhausting way of something that has all the time in the world and all the numbers in the world and no reason to hurry. They find you. They always find you.

The spiders are here because the insects are here. Orb-weavers with webs the size of wagon wheels, strung between branches at face height, their silk gleaming with a faint, structural color in the dim light, and the spiders themselves sitting at the center, legs spread, patient, their bodies patterned in yellows and blacks that look like warning signs in a language you don't speak. Jumping spiders, small and curious and unsettlingly alert, watching from leaves with an intelligence that seems too large for their size. Huntsman spiders, flat and fast and the size of a spread hand, lurking under bark and behind leaves and in the kind of spaces you reach into without thinking and then think about for a long time afterward.

And the frogs. The frogs are everywhere. They are on the ground, in the pools, on the leaves, on the branches, on the tree trunks, on each other. They sit with the absolute stillness of animals that know they are both predator and prey, and then they move, and the movement is so fast and so precise that it seems to occur outside of time — a jump that is also a teleportation, a landing that is also a vanishing. Some are beautiful: the small, red-eyed tree frogs with their translucent skin and their vivid, painted faces, clinging to leaves with sticky pads that leave faint wet prints. Some are ugly: the large, warty, mud-colored toads that sit in the paths and do not move and look like they were carved from the soil itself. Some are strange: the glass frogs, whose bellies are transparent, whose hearts and livers and digestive systems are visible, beating and working, in a display of biological transparency that seems less like a feature and more like a failure to commit to being opaque.

There are larger things. Snakes, smooth and muscular, sliding through the leaf litter with a patience that makes the frogs' urgency seem performative. They are constrictors, mostly, and they eat the frogs, and the frogs eat the insects, and the insects eat the plants and each other and everything that dies, and the cycle is so tight and so fast that you can almost see it turning, almost see the energy flowing through the system like a current, like the water that cycles through the air and the soil and the pools, round and round, transforming as it goes, never lost, never still.

There are birds, but they are heard more than seen. Calls in the canopy — harsh, metallic cries that cut through the frog-roar like bright threads through dark cloth. They are fruit-eaters, mostly, or insect-eaters, and they exist in the upper layers, in the light, in the world above the ceiling that the ground-dwellers never see. Their sounds fall into the dark below like stones into water, and the dark absorbs them, and the roar continues.

There are mammals, but they are few, and they are shy, and they move at night, and their presence is inferred rather than observed: a rustling in the undergrowth, a shape that crosses a gap and is gone, a smell — musk, fur, warmth — that lingers in an air already thick with smells. They are the top of this system, or near it, but they are not its rulers. The frogs are its rulers. The insects are its rulers. The plants, in their slow, green, patient way, are its rulers. The mammals are visitors, late arrivals, tenants in a building they did not construct and do not control.

## The Darkness

And under all of this, the darkness.

Not the darkness of caves, which is the darkness of absence — the absence of light, of life, of warmth. This is the darkness of *presence*. The darkness of too much. The darkness that comes when life is so dense, so layered, so total that it blocks the sun and replaces it with itself. The light here is not gone. It is *used*. It is taken in by the leaves and the fronds and the canopy and turned into growth, into biomass, into the physical substance of the forest, so that what reaches the ground is not light but the *memory* of light, a dim, green, ambient glow that is less like illumination and more like the bioluminescence of a deep-sea environment, a light that the world makes for itself.

You would move through this darkness slowly. Your eyes would adjust, but the adjustment would never be complete — there is too little light for full human vision, and the world would remain a place of shapes and shadows, of forms inferred rather than seen. You would hear more than you see. You would feel more than you hear. The heat would press against your skin, the dampness would settle into your clothes, the sound would vibrate in your chest, and the darkness would be not an absence but a medium, a substance, something you moved through the way a diver moves through water, surrounded, supported, enclosed.

At night — and the distinction between day and night here is a matter of degree, a slight deepening of the darkness, a subtle shift in the temperature, a change in which frogs are calling — the darkness becomes total, and the world becomes sound and heat and wetness alone, a place that exists in the ears and on the skin and in the nose but not in the eyes, a place that has, for the duration of the night, no visual component at all. You could stand in it and close your eyes and see exactly what you would see with them open: nothing. And in that nothing, the roar, and the heat, and the wet, and the knowledge — the bodily, cellular, animal knowledge — that you are surrounded, that you are inside something, that you are not in a place but *within* a being, a system, a living thing that does not know you are here and would not care if it did.

## The Rules

This place has rules, but they are not human rules. They are the rules of wet things, warm things, growing things, and they are enforced not by authority but by physics, by chemistry, by the implacable logic of competition and saturation and time.

The first rule: *everything grows*. Everything. The soil, the plants, the animals, the populations. There is no equilibrium here. There is no climax community, no stable state, no balance. There is only growth, and the overflow of growth, and the consumption of that overflow by more growth, in a cascade that has not yet reached its limit because the land is young and the nutrients are fresh and the energy is abundant and nothing has had time to run out.

The second rule: *everything is wet*. This is not a rule that can be broken. The humidity is total, the rain is frequent, the water table is high, and there is no refuge from the damp. Things that cannot tolerate wetness do not survive here. Things that can — and the list is long, and varied, and strange — thrive.

The third rule: *everything is loud*. The sound is not a byproduct. It is a feature. It is how the frogs find each other, how the insects signal, how the birds mark their territory, how the system *talks to itself*, a continuous, deafening conversation that is the sound of life coordinating its own abundance. Silence here would mean death. Silence would mean absence. The roar is the sound of presence, of occupancy, of a world so full that it cannot be quiet.

The fourth rule: *nothing is permanent*. The land is young. The lava is recent. The soil is new. The trees are fast-growing and short-lived. The pools form and drain and reform. The populations boom and crash and boom again. This is not a place that has arrived at its final form. This is a place that is *in the middle of becoming*, and the becoming is violent and wet and loud and dark and warm, and it will continue, probably, for centuries, before the land settles and the nutrients slow and the growth decelerates and the system finds something like equilibrium and the roar — the immense, continuous, body-shaking roar — finally, gradually, begins to diminish.

But that is centuries from now. This is now. And now, the roar is here, and the dark is here, and the wet is here, and the frogs are calling, and the insects are humming, and the plants are growing, and the water is cycling, and the land is building itself, and everything is alive, and everything is loud, and everything is becoming, and you are standing in it, and it does not notice you, and it does not need to, and it does not stop.
