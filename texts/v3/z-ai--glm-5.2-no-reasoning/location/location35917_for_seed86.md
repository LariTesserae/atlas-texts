# The Underneath

## I.

You come down through a split in the basalt — not a cave entrance, not a shaft, just a place where the rock aged differently and pulled apart over some unimaginable stretch of time, leaving a diagonal seam wide enough to walk through if you turn your shoulders. The air changes before the light does. It gets heavier. It tastes like wet chalk and iron filings, and it carries sound in a way that makes your own breathing seem to arrive from somewhere else.

The passage is short — maybe forty meters — and then the walls fall away and there is nothing below you but more below.

## II.

The chamber is enormous. Not cathedral- enormous. Valley-enormous. The ceiling, when you can find it with your eyes, spans a sky of grey-veined stone perhaps sixty meters up, and it sweeps in a long, uneven arc that suggests the underside of a hill or a plateau. The floor, though — the floor is the real place. It drops away in a long, uneven descent, a slope of raw stone that looks like it was *peeled* open. That is the first word that comes to mind. The rock is fresh. Not ancient-smooth, not river-worn. It has the rough, granular surface of stone that was under pressure and recently relieved of it. Sharp edges. Grainy fractures. Striations running in sweeping lines across the walls and floor, as though something dragged across this stone and left deep grooves, and the stone is young enough that nothing has yet softened them. In places the rock is dark — a wet, glistening charcoal — and in others it lightens to a pale ash-grey where moisture has not reached.

The scale unfolds slowly. You think at first that you are in a large cavern. Then you walk for ten minutes and the walls do not approach. You walk for twenty and you realize you have crossed what might be the equivalent of several open fields, and the far walls are still just suggestions of darkness. The space is regional. It is the size of a small valley, a hamlet and its surroundings, all enclosed. And it is nearly empty.

## III.

Near — empty. That is the thing you must understand.

The stone floor stretches in all directions with almost nothing on it. Here and there, a ridge of darker rock rises a meter or two from the surface, running in linear paths that follow the strata. Occasionally a shallow basin depresses the floor by a few centimeters — wide, gentle, almost imperceptible until you are standing in one. There are no formations. No stalactites, no stalagmites, no columns, no flowstone curtains. This is not that kind of underground. The stone is too young, the space too new. Water has not had time to build. It has only had time to *arrive*.

And it has arrived, modestly. Not a river. Not a stream. Not even a proper pool. Water exists here as a presence distributed across the stone in thin, persistent ways. The basins I mentioned — some of them hold a film of water no deeper than the width of your finger. It does not splash. It does not flow. It sits, and it reflects the dim light, and it smells like cold minerals. In other places, the stone itself is simply *damp* — not wet, not running, just darkened by moisture that has seeped through the rock above and now clings to the surface in a layer so thin it is more a condition than a substance. If you press your palm flat against the floor and lift it, your skin comes away dark and cold, smelling of something below the mineral — something organic and ancient, like the ghost of soil that has not existed here for a very long time, if ever.

From the ceiling, condensation gathers at the joints between stone layers and falls. Not rain. Not a drizzle. A sparse, irregular percussion. A drop here. A silence. Three drops there, in quick succession. Then nothing for a long time, and then a single, heavy, resonant *tok* as a large drop hits an exposed shelf of stone twenty meters away. The sound carries. Everything carries.

## IV.

The light. You must understand the light.

There is no sun here, obviously. There is no entrance visible from most of the chamber — the seam you came through is a distant, pale grey suggestion on the far wall, barely brighter than the stone around it. No. The light here is generated by the stone itself, or rather by something *on* the stone, something that lives in the thin film of moisture that coats the darker patches of the floor and walls.

It is a bioluminescence — a cold, blue-white glow, faint but pervasive, produced by microorganisms in the damp. Not bacteria. Not fungi. Something simpler and older, something that has no name in the taxonomy of the surface world. Each individual point of light is barely visible — a faint smudge, a slight brightening of a wet patch. But there are millions of them. Billions. The entire floor, where it is damp, glows at a level just above perception, and the result is that the chamber is lit by its own ground. The light is dim. It is the light of deep twilight, of a heavy overcast evening, of the last minutes before full dark. You can see. You cannot see well. Shapes are resolved but not detailed. The ceiling is a pale suggestion. The far walls are more felt than seen.

The glow does not flicker. It does not pulse. It is perfectly, absolutely steady. It has been this bright for as long as anything here can remember, which is not long in one sense and eternal in another.

## V.

Now. The sound.

This is what stays with you.

The chamber is loud. Not the loudness of machinery or of wind or of surf. The loudness of life. Biological sound, layered and continuous, rising from every surface where moisture collects. It comes from the animals.

There are animals here. This is the first fact that strikes you as impossible, and then strikes you as the central fact of the place. The chamber is *full* of animals. Not dense in the sense of a forest floor or a coral reef — nothing is packed together, nothing is crowding — but in the sense that wherever you look, wherever the dim light reaches, there is something alive. And most of what is alive is moving, and most of what is moving is calling.

The sound is a sustained, layered chorus. It has no quiet moments. It has no crescendos. It is *exactly* as loud right now as it was an hour ago, and it will be exactly this loud tomorrow. It does not breathe. It does not rest.

## VI.

Let me describe what is making the sound, because the animals here are not strange — not in the way you might expect underground fauna to be strange. There are no translucent things, no eyeless horrors, no pale writhing things that should not be. The weirdness of this place is not in its deviation from the natural order. The weirdness is in its *commitment* to the natural order, in a place where you would not expect to find one.

The dominant animals are arthropods. Specifically, something very much like a sowbug — an isopod, flattened, segmented, many-legged — but large. The largest are the length of your hand. Their shells are dark, slate-grey, almost the same color as the wet stone, and they move in slow, deliberate paths across the floor, over the ridges, through the shallow basins of standing water. They do not hurry. They do not startle. They move as though they have always been moving and always will be, at this pace, in these directions, and this is nearly true.

There are thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Across the full extent of the chamber, their dark bodies dot the pale stone like a scattering of river pebbles. From a distance they are still. Up close, they are not still at all — their legs move in coordinated waves, their mouthparts work constantly at the thin film of microorganisms on the stone, and they produce sound.

It is a clicking. A dry, rhythmic, percussive clicking, produced by the striking of their mouthparts against stone and by the tapping of their legs on the rough substrate. Each click is small. Each click is nothing. But tens of thousands of clicking, tapping bodies produce a sound like a sustained, granular rattle — like heavy rain on a tin roof, but lower, dryer, more uniform. It is the floor of the chorus.

Above that, there is a keening. It comes from something else — something smaller, something that moves in the air, or rather in the space just above the stone, where the air is most damp. They are not insects. They are not birds. They are something like a winged arthropod — pale, translucent, with long, narrow wings that beat at a frequency you can hear but not see. They produce a high, thin, sustained note, and there are hundreds of them in the air at any moment, and their notes combine into a single, unwavering tone that sits above the clicking like a harmonic. It is the sound of a glass rim being rubbed, sustained endlessly, and it does not waver.

And beneath the clicking, low and resonant, there is a thrumming. This comes from the larger animals — and there are larger animals here, though they are not common. Something like a salamander. Dark, wet, heavy-bodied, with small limbs and a broad, flat head. They are the size of a large boot. They sit in the deeper basins, half-submerged in the thin water, and they produce a low, pulsing vibration that you feel in your feet before you hear it with your ears. It is not a call in any communicative sense. It is respiration. Their bodies vibrate as they breathe, and the stone conducts the vibration, and the basins of water amplify it, and the result is a subsonic pulse that underlies everything, that makes the air itself seem to throb.

Three layers. Clicking. Keening. Thrumming. All continuous. All unchanging. The sound of this place has the quality of a recording left playing in an empty room — it is so consistent, so invariant, that your mind begins to edit it out after a time, and then a moment of attention brings it back, and it is exactly as it was, and you realize it has not stopped. It will not stop.

## VII.

The ecology is simple, and because it is simple, it is robust.

The microorganisms in the moisture film are the foundation. They consume minerals from the stone — the stone is young, geologically fresh, still weathering, still releasing compounds from deep in its matrix. The microorganisms feed on those compounds and on the organic traces in the condensation water, which percolates through soil and rock above before arriving here. They grow in the thin, steady light that they themselves produce. They are grazed by the isopods. The isopods are eaten, occasionally, by the salamanders. The winged things feed on the microorganisms and on the moisture itself, and they are eaten, occasionally, by the salamanders when they descend low enough. The salamanders are the apex. Nothing eats them. They live long, slow lives in the cold water and the dim light, and they breathe, and the stone thrums.

There is no waste. Everything is recycled. The isopods produce waste that feeds the microorganisms. The salamanders produce waste that feeds the microorganisms. When something dies — an isopod on its back, a winged thing fallen to the floor — the microorganisms consume it within days, and the film grows thicker where the body lay, and the light is very slightly brighter there, and then it is not.

This has been happening for some time. Not geological time. Ecological time. The chamber is young. The ecosystem is younger. But it is established. It is in equilibrium. And the equilibrium is total.

## VIII.

The stillness of this place is not the stillness of a frozen moment. It is the stillness of a system that has found its balance and does not need to move from it. The isopods walk, but their walking does not change anything — they graze a path, and the path regrows. The winged things fly, but their flight is a circulation, not a journey — they rise and descend and rise again, and their numbers do not increase or decrease. The salamanders breathe, and their breathing is a pulse, and the pulse has a frequency, and the frequency has not changed.

If you sit on the stone floor — and it is cold, colder than you expected, perhaps ten or twelve degrees, the temperature of a refrigerator, the temperature of a deep cellar — if you sit and watch, you will see that nothing here is arriving. Nothing is leaving. The water seeps in at the same rate it seeps out. The light glows at the same intensity it glowed yesterday. The sound is the same sound. The animals are the same animals, in the same numbers, in the same places, doing the same things. You could leave and return in a year, and the only difference would be the specific isopod in front of you, the specific salamander in that specific basin. The pattern would be identical.

This is not death. This is not stasis. This is a living thing that has found its shape and holds it.

## IX.

There are no built structures here. No marks of human passage. No tools, no shelters, no paths, no fires, no bones, no artifacts. Nothing has been made. But — and this is worth noting — the stone itself, in its raw, unworked state, provides something that resembles, from a certain angle, the affordances of a built space. The ridges provide shelter from the condensation. The basins provide water. The chambers within chambers — for the main space is not uniform; there are alcoves, side passages, partial walls of natural stone that create something like rooms — these provide segmentation, differentiation of space. If a person were to live here, they would not need to build. They would only need to occupy.

And someone has. Not here — not in this chamber — but in the seam that leads down, in the upper passages, there are stones placed against walls in ways that suggest arrangement. There are places where the floor has been swept clear of debris. There are marks on stone that might be tool-marks, that might be the scratches of a sharp rock dragged across a surface to make a line, to make a border, to make a *mark*. The technology is of the most primitive sort — stone on stone, hand on stone, the body itself as the primary instrument. Whoever was here, or whatever was here, was here at the very beginning of making. At the threshold between using what exists and creating what does not.

They are not here now. The chamber is theirs in the way that a ruin is someone's, but there is no ruin. There is only the chamber, doing what it has always done, and the marks above, and the silence about them.

## X.

I said the chamber was nearly empty. Let me be more precise about what fills it.

When you stand in the center of the space — and there is a center, roughly, where the slope levels out and the floor extends most evenly in all directions — when you stand there and look outward, you see: stone, the pale blue-white glow of the damp floor, the dark shapes of isopods moving slowly, the faint pale tracing of winged things in the air above. You see ridges, low and linear. You see basins, shallow and still. You see, at the limit of the light, the suggestion of a wall, rising. You hear the chorus, constant. You feel the thrumming in your feet.

And you notice, after a time, that the emptiness is not absence. The emptiness is *space*. It is the space between the isopods, the space between the ridges, the space between the basins. It is the space that allows each thing to be what it is without collision, without compression. The density here is low — not because there is little, but because what is here requires room. The isopods graze at a certain density because that density is what the food source can sustain. The winged things fly at a certain density because that density is what the air can hold. The salamanders sit in their basins alone because a basin holds one salamander, and the basins are far apart, and that is the way of it.

The space is not wasted. The space is the medium. It is the thing the life moves through. It is the silence between the clicks, the gap between the drops, the pause between breaths. It is what makes the chorus audible, the light visible, the movement real. Without the emptiness, this would be a mass. With it, it is a *place*.

## XI.

The air moves, barely. There is a current — a slow, cold drift from the deeper passages toward the seam you entered through, following the gentle pressure differential between the underground and the surface. It is too slow to feel as wind. You feel it only as a tendency — the condensation on your skin evaporates slightly faster on one side, the thin fog near the floor drifts in a consistent direction. The air smells of stone and water and the particular, not-unpleasant mustiness of a healthy ecosystem enclosed — earthy, mineral, alive.

The temperature does not change. It will not change. This deep, this insulated, this stable, the temperature is a fact of the rock, and the rock does not change. The coolness is not seasonal. It is not diurnal. It is simply what it is — twelve degrees, perhaps, or ten, the exact temperature of the stone, which is the exact temperature of the water, which is the exact temperature of the air. No gradient. No variation. The isopods do not seek warmth. The salamanders do not seek cold. Everything here is at the temperature it prefers, because everything here evolved at this temperature, and this temperature has not changed in the time since the chamber opened and the life arrived.

## XII.

I want to tell you about the light one more time, because it is the thing that makes this place what it is, more than the sound, more than the animals, more than the space.

The light is even. It comes from below — from the floor, from the damp — and it rises. It illuminates the chamber from the ground up, which means that the floor is the brightest surface, and the ceiling is the darkest. This is the opposite of every surface experience you have ever had. In every other place, light comes from above. Here, it comes from beneath. Your shadow falls upward. The undersides of things are lit and their tops are dark. The isopods are bright on their bellies and dark on their shells. The ridges cast shadows that point toward the ceiling, not the floor. The salamanders in their basins are lit from below, and their eyes, set on the tops of their flat heads, are in shadow, which means they see the world in twilight even though the world is, in its own way, lit.

And the light does not move. It does not shift. It does not change color or intensity. It is the same blue-white, the same dimness, the same distribution, always. The floor glows. The floor has always glowed. The floor will always glow. This is the thing that the animals here know, if they know anything: the ground is bright, and the sky is dark, and the sound does not stop, and the water is always there, and the stone is always cold, and the world is this world, and it does not change.

It does not change.

## XIII.

You leave the way you came. Up through the seam, the air warming by degrees as you climb, the sound of the chorus fading — not stopping, never stopping, just fading, as you move away from it, as the distance and the stone absorb it, and you emerge into whatever weather the surface is having, and the light is wrong — too bright, too directional, too *high* — and the air is too warm, and the silence is shocking, and you realize that what you carry with you is not an image but a sound, a low, continuous, granular rattle with a thin keening above it and a throbbing below it, and it is still playing in the stone you just left, and it will be playing tomorrow, and the day after, and it will not have changed.

You carry the knowledge of a place that does not need you. That does not need anything. That found its answer and is living it, in the dark, in the cold, in the dim and steady light of its own ground, loud and alive and perfectly, perfectly still.
