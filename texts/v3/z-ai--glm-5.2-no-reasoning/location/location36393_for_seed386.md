# The Weeping Highlands

## The Ascent

The first thing you notice is the sound of breathing that isn't yours.

It comes from the rock itself.

You've been climbing for hours through forest so thick and wet it feels like moving through a green throat. The trees here — nobody has named them, because nobody lives here to name them — grow in spirals, their trunks twisted by some combination of heat and gravity and the slow mineral pressure of the mountain they cling to. Their bark is black, almost volcanic, and it weeps. Not sap. Water. Fresh, cold water that beads along the grain of the wood and runs down in continuous rivulets, pooling in the moss at their bases, feeding everything below.

By the time you break above the canopy, your clothes are soaked. Not from rain — there has been no rain, not today — but from the air itself, which is so saturated that it condenses on every surface. On your skin. On the leaves. On the stone. The highlands exist inside a cloud that never quite forms and never quite dissipates. You walk through the permanent almost-rain.

And then the forest falls away, and the plateau opens before you, and you understand where you are.

---

## The Plateau

It is vast. Not mountainous in the sharp, dramatic sense — nothing here is sharp. This is old country. The plateau stretches in every direction, kilometers of weathered stone worn into rolling undulations, shallow basins, broad shelves of exposed rock that have been eroding for longer than anything alive can remember. The stone is a deep oxidized red, streaked with black mineral veins and pale quartzite deposits that catch the flat, even light. It looks like the hide of some enormous sleeping animal.

And it is wet.

Not covered in water — that would be simpler. The water here exists in a state between groundwater and atmosphere that has no clean name. The stone is saturated. You can see it in the way the surface darkens in patches, in the way pools gather in the shallow depressions carved over millennia, in the way every surface has a faint sheen, as though the mountain is sweating. The water table sits just below the surface, maybe a meter down, maybe less. In places it breaks through entirely — a seep, a spring, a sudden wet wound in the rock where the mountain's internal water finds its way out.

These seeps are everywhere. They run down the gentle slopes in braided threads, merging into shallow channels that cut across the stone in patterns that look deliberate, almost calligraphic. Some of them have been flowing so long that they've carved channels a hand deep into the rock. Others are new, shifting, finding new paths after every season. The entire plateau is a slow, continuous redistribution of water from the interior of the mountain to its surface, and from its surface to the air.

The result is that you are standing in a landscape that is simultaneously desert and wetland. The bare stone stretches in every direction, open and treeless, but it is alive with water. And where the water gathers, even for a moment, life explodes.

## The Ecology of Wet Stone

The vegetation here has no patience. It takes what it can, where it can, as fast as it can.

Along every seep, every channel, every temporary pool, there is a riot of growth — low, dense, fiercely green. Mosses that form cushions a forearm's length thick, soaking water like sponges, their surfaces covered in tiny spore capsules that bend under the weight of condensation. Ferns — but not like the ferns lower in the forest. These are smaller, more compact, adapted to the exposed stone, their fronds curling tight during the brightest part of the day and unfurling in the morning when the condensation is heaviest. They grow in lines along the water channels, following the moisture like a living map of the mountain's plumbing.

In the deeper basins, where water sits longer, the vegetation thickens into something almost meadow-like — sedges and reeds and broad-leafed plants that have no common name, their roots tangled in the shallow soil that has accumulated in these natural bowls over centuries. Some of these basins are large enough to be called ponds, at least during the wettest part of the cycle. Most are just damp depressions, their surfaces green with algae or dotted with the small white flowers of something that looks like watercress but isn't, quite.

And everywhere, on every surface that stays wet for more than a few days, there is biofilm. A slippery, living scum that coats the rock and makes every step uncertain. It is olive-green, sometimes purple, sometimes a deep rust-red where the mineral content of the water is different. It is the base layer of everything here — the first thing to colonize bare wet stone, the thing that begins the slow process of turning rock into soil, the thing that everything else stands on.

You learn to watch for it. You learn to step where the stone is pale and dry. You learn quickly.

## The Heat

The temperature is the other presence here. It is not merely hot — it is the fundamental condition under which everything else operates.

The air sits on the plateau like a weight. It is thick, saturated, and genuinely difficult to breathe. Every breath you take carries visible moisture. Your lungs work harder than they should. After the first hour on the exposed stone, you understand why nothing large lives up here permanently. The effort of moving through this air, under this heat, is simply too expensive for anything that can't afford to lose water faster than it takes it in.

The stone itself radiates. By mid-afternoon — the light is always a flat, even gray-white, the cloud layer diffusing the sun into a directionless glow — the rock surface is warm enough that you can feel it through your boots. The water in the shallow pools is body-temperature. The biofilm thrives in this. The mosses curl inward. The ferns fold their fronds. Everything that can, retreats. The lizards — there are lizards, small dark ones with long legs and surprisingly bright eyes — retreat into the shade of the larger rocks and do not move.

And then, as the light begins to shift toward evening, the temperature drops. Not dramatically — it never gets cold here, not at this latitude, not at this scale — but enough. The stone stops radiating. The air, still saturated, begins to condense more heavily. And the plateau weeps.

This is the cycle. Every day. Heat, evaporation, cooling, condensation. The water rises from the stone during the day and falls back onto it at night. The mountain breathes it in and breathes it out. You can hear it, in the quiet — a faint, pervasive hiss of water finding its way through stone, emerging from seeps, running a few meters across a shelf, and evaporating again. A sound so constant and so low that it becomes indistinguishable from silence.

## The Industrial Ghost

You find the first pipe three days in.

You almost miss it. It emerges from a crack in the rock at the edge of a basin, a length of corroded metal — steel, maybe, or something close to it — about the width of your forearm. It has been here a long time. The rock has grown around its base. Moss has colonized its surface. Lichen has etched itself into the metal. It looks less like an artifact and more like a feature of the landscape, another mineral deposit, another vein in the stone.

But it is not natural. You can see the manufacturing marks, even through the corrosion. Flanges. Rivets. A valve handle, long since frozen in place by rust and mineral scale.

Over the next several hours of walking, you find more. A pumping station, or what is left of one — a concrete and steel structure, half-collapsed, its roof long since caved in, now serving as a foundation for a thick colony of ferns. A pipeline, mostly buried, occasionally surfacing in rusted metal segments that cross the stone like the spine of some buried animal. A drainage channel, cut into the rock and lined with brick, now so overgrown with sedge and moss that it looks like a natural watercourse.

This was a water extraction operation. Someone — a company, a government, it doesn't matter now — recognized the plateau's water resources and built the infrastructure to tap them. Pumps. Pipes. Holding tanks. They drilled into the rock and pulled water out of the mountain and sent it somewhere else, down the slope, to someplace that needed it.

And then, at some point, they left.

You can see why. The infrastructure is failing. The pipes have ruptured in a dozen places. The pumping station is useless. The water has reclaimed everything — it runs through the broken pipes now instead of being contained by them, it pools in the collapsed holding tanks, it follows the drainage channels but ignores the brick walls, spilling over the sides and finding its own paths down the stone. The mountain has absorbed the industrial apparatus and is slowly, patiently dismantling it. Another few decades and the pipes will be veins of rust in the rock. Another century and the concrete will be just another stratum.

But the ghost remains. You can feel it in the landscape — the sense that this place was used, was claimed, was made to serve a purpose that was not its own. And then was abandoned, and left to return to itself. Which it is doing, thoroughly, without hurry.

## The Quiet

It is the quiet that stays with you.

Not silence — the plateau is never silent. There is always the hiss of water, the distant dripping of condensation falling from fern to stone, the faint hum of insects in the vegetation around the basins. The occasional call of a bird — something raptor-like, high up in the cloud cover, unseen. The skitter of a lizard on rock. The low, almost subsonic groan of the mountain itself, adjusting, settling, its internal water shifting through fractures deep below.

But these are small sounds. They do not accumulate. They do not build into anything. The plateau absorbs them into its general hum the way it absorbs the industrial infrastructure into its geology. There is no echo — the landscape is too open, too flat, too soft with water and vegetation to bounce sound. Everything happens once and is gone.

In the morning, when the condensation is at its heaviest and the light is at its flattest and the heat has not yet begun to build, you stand on a shelf of red stone above a basin full of still water and green growth and you hear almost nothing. The world is holding its breath. The mountain is holding its water. Everything is waiting for the sun to climb high enough to start the cycle again — the evaporation, the condensation, the slow breathing in and out.

You stay for a long time. There is nothing to leave behind and nothing to take. The plateau is already in the process of forgetting you. Your footprints in the biofilm will be gone by tomorrow. The water will fill them. The moss will grow over them. The mountain will close around the fact of your presence like water closes around a thrown stone, and in a day, or a week, or a year, there will be no evidence that you were here at all.

The highlands continue. Wet. Hot. Open. Old. Patient.

Waiting for nothing.
