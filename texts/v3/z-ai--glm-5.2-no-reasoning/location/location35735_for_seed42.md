# The Drowned Cloister of Maren Vered

It is not an ocean. It is not a lake. It is the inside of a plant so old it has forgotten it was ever a seedling.

---

The first thing is the sound. Before light, before temperature registers, before any sense of place — the sound. A low, continuous, multilayered roar that is not violent but simply *present*, the way gravity is present. Water moves here, not in the chaotic tumble of a flood but in the slow, deliberate circulation of a body that has been breathing for millennia. The Maren Vered — the name is a convenience; it has no name for itself — is a sphere of living tissue roughly the size of a cathedral, and its circulatory fluid is this: a clear, cool, faintly luminous water that moves through enormous vascular channels the way blood moves through veins, pushed by peristaltic rhythms too slow to see but loud enough to feel in the chest.

You enter through one of the older root-gates, where the outer hull of the plant has thickened and split from its own growth, leaving a gap wide enough for a body to pass. The water carries you in. There is no air here. You realize this with a start, but the panic passes: the water itself is rich with dissolved gases, and something in its chemistry — the plant excretes it deliberately, or perhaps it is simply a property of the sap — allows lungs to draw from it as though it were air. The sensation is strange. Not breathing water. Breathing a place.

Inside, the architecture is immediate and undeniable. This is not a plant that grew into the shape of a building. This is a plant that *is* a building, and was always going to be one, the way a nautilus shell is always going to be a spiral. The central chamber rises above you — though "above" is approximate in a sphere of water — a vaulted space formed by the interlocking ribs of structural roots that have grown toward the center and then curved away, meeting a secondary canopy of finer branches that spread like fan-vaulting. The ribs are arm-thick near the walls, finger-thick where they nearly meet, and they are not dead wood. They pulse. A faint thickening and thinning, a tide of fluid passing through each one at slightly different intervals, creates a complex polyrhythm that underlies the great steady wash of the circulatory flow. You can hear individual threads in the sound if you listen: a low booming from the largest channels, a hiss from the narrow capillary networks that thread the walls like gold in crackled glaze, a rhythmic clicking — almost mechanical — from joints where two roots have fused and now flex against each other with the slow tidal force of the whole organism's growth.

The plant has been here, in this shape, for longer than anyone has recorded. Its settled form is its own: not growing rapidly, not decaying, not recovering from any wound. It maintains. The vascular walls have achieved a thickness and texture that suggests permanence — not stone, not wood exactly, but something laminated and fibrous, the surface slightly rough, like unglazed clay, dotted here and there with lenticel pores that open and close on cycles so long you might watch one for an hour and see no motion, yet across the day they visibly gape and shut. They are the plant's breath, or its sweat, or both.

---

Light comes from the water itself, and from the tissue. It is not bright. It is the light of a deep aquarium, a cool blue-green that strengthens toward the walls and fades toward the center, so that the heart of the chamber is the dimmest point and the periphery glows. The luminosity is biological — something in the sap, some phosphorescent protein or symbiotic microorganism — but it is not constant. It surges, very slowly, in waves that travel along the vascular channels like a blush moving across skin. When a surge passes through a wall, the entire chamber shifts color: blue, teal, a brief moment of almost yellow-green, then back to blue. This happens once every several minutes. The plant does not appear to control it. It is simply a property of the fluid's flow dynamics, the way certain channels compress and release, concentrating and diluting whatever produces the light.

There are no shadows here in the usual sense. The light is too diffuse, too omnidirectional. Objects are illuminated from below, above, and all sides simultaneously, which gives everything a quality of *presentness* — nothing recedes, nothing is hidden by darkness. This means the chamber feels smaller than it is, and also larger, because the eye cannot find a clear boundary where the wall is not. The walls are visible — textured, patterned, structurally complex — but they do not fall into shadow and so they do not give a strong sense of enclosure. You are inside something, but the something does not feel like a container. It feels like a medium.

---

The built elements are old. Very old. They are the work of hands, not of the plant, though the plant has grown around and through them with such thoroughness that distinguishing the two requires close attention. The first people to live inside the Maren Vered — and there were people, or something close enough — cut their dwellings into the vascular walls themselves, carving chambers and connecting passages from the living tissue. The cuts have healed. Each doorway, each window, each shelf cut into the wall is now lined with a thick callus of growth, smooth and pale, like scar tissue, raised slightly from the surrounding surface. The callus has its own texture — faintly lined, like the grain of a wood that grew under pressure — and its own color, lighter than the walls, almost white in the blue light.

There are perhaps thirty of these carved rooms, scattered through the chamber's periphery. They are not large. The largest might hold four people lying down; the smallest is barely a niche. They connect by doorways cut through the structural ribs — each one a tunnel a few meters long, smoothly callused, slightly constricted where the rib has tried to close the wound and only partially succeeded. The tunnels are the quietest spaces in the Maren Vered. The great vascular roar is muffled here, reduced to a low hum felt more in the bones than heard, and you can hear smaller sounds: the creak of the ribs under stress, the faint ticking of callus joints, the distant percussion of something knocking against the outer hull.

The rooms are empty of people. They have been empty for a long time. But they are not abandoned. The plant has repurposed them. In each carved chamber, the callus that lines the walls has grown inward, forming shelves, cups, partitions — not intentionally, not with design, but in the way that scar tissue fills a wound: filling the space the hands created, reclaiming it slowly. Some of the rooms are nearly full, the callus having expanded to leave only a narrow cavity. Others are still mostly open. The difference seems to be age — the oldest carved rooms are the most filled — and the process is ongoing, so that if you were to visit over years, you would see the spaces narrowing.

But the rooms are not empty of *everything*. There are tools. Stone tools, bone tools, a few of a material like hardened sap — stacked, arranged, some on the callus shelves as though placed there by hands. They were not. The water carried them. The circulation of the Maren Vered's fluid is not perfectly uniform; eddies and dead zones exist, particularly in the carved rooms, which project from the main chamber like cul-de-sacs. Objects caught in the flow tend to settle in these calmer spaces, and the residents — whoever they were, whenever they were — arranged the deposited objects deliberately. Or perhaps the plant did. The distinction is unclear. The tools are simple: scrapers, pounders, a few blades of remarkable thinness, hooks of bone that might be for catching something in the vascular flow.

---

The ecology is the part that takes longest to see, because the organisms here move on the plant's timescale, which is not yours.

The water is full of life. Not the darting, flickering life of fish in a reef, but a slower life, a patient life, suited to a medium that circulates on long cycles and a food supply that arrives in tides. The dominant animals — and they are animals, or something analogous — are filter-feeding things the size of a human hand, flat and roughly diamond-shaped, translucent, with a body plan that suggests a cross between a jellyfish and a leaf. They are called *veredai* by the few people who still come here, though "called" is generous; the word is passed among the occasional visitor and the hermits who still use the outer rooms. The *veredai* attach themselves to the vascular walls at the junctions where smaller channels feed into larger ones, and they feed on whatever the flow brings them: dissolved nutrients, microorganisms, fragments of organic matter shed by the plant itself. They are the color of the water, which makes them nearly invisible until they move, and they move rarely. Once every few minutes, a *veredai* will detach, drift with the current to a new position, and reattach. The motion is slow, unhurried, almost ceremonial. When one detaches near you, the displacement of water is a gentle push, and the sound of its reattachment — a soft, definitive *click* — is one of the small percussive notes in the chamber's acoustic texture.

There are others. Tiny shrimp-like things, translucent, red, a centimeter long, that swarm in the eddies behind the structural ribs, their collective motion creating faint shimmering clouds that catch the bioluminescent light. They are louder than they should be: their chitinous bodies click against the plant tissue when they land, and in groups they produce a sound like rain on a wooden roof, a dry rapid chatter that rises and falls with the currents. When the vascular flow surges, they scatter to quieter zones, and the sound drops to near-silence before rebuilding over the next several minutes.

Larger things live in the deeper channels, the ones that pierce the walls and connect to the plant's exterior. You see them only as shadows — long, sinuous, moving against the current with a muscular effort that suggests purpose. They do not enter the main chamber. They are not hostile; they are simply adapted to the channels, which are narrower, darker, faster, and they have no reason to come into the open. Occasionally one will rest at a channel mouth, its body half-visible, a dark shape against the glowing wall, and you can see it breathing — or the equivalent: a slow dilation and contraction of a structure along its flank, pumping the enriched water through whatever system it uses for respiration. The *veredai* do not avoid these channel mouths. The shrimp-things do. This tells you something about the food chain, though not enough.

And there are the symbionts. The plant's own tissue is not purely plant. At the microscopic scale — visible only as a faint cloudiness in the wall's tissue when the light surges strongly — there are colonies of something that lives inside the plant's cells, producing the phosphorescent compound in exchange for sugars or protection. This is not unusual. What is unusual is that the symbionts respond to pressure. When you touch the wall, the area around your hand brightens. Not dramatically, but visibly: the cells beneath your palm glow a fraction more intensely for several seconds after contact. This means the walls respond to touch. Not with sensation — the plant is not aware of you — but with a visible, physical response that makes the boundary between visitor and environment feel permeable, almost conversational.

---

The Maren Vered exists in a state of equilibrium that is not static but is extremely stable. The plant grows, but slowly — the callus in the carved rooms advances at perhaps a centimeter per decade. The vascular flow cycles, but the rhythm has not changed in any measurable way across the span of records that exist, which are not long, perhaps a few centuries, scratched into callus walls in a simple ideographic script by people who did not stay. The fauna populations rise and fall with the nutrient content of the water, which itself follows the plant's metabolic seasons, which are so long that a "summer" — a period of higher nutrient density, more active flow, brighter luminescence, more abundant animal life — might last decades. The current era is a mild one, closer to summer than winter, with robust flow and dense *veredai* populations, but the shift is imperceptible day to day. You would have to watch for years to notice that the light is very slowly getting brighter.

The few people who still come here — and there are a few, perhaps three or four hermits who occupy the outermost carved rooms on something like a seasonal basis, paddling through the root-gates on rafts of woven fiber, bringing stone tools and dried food that the water preserves almost indefinitely — do not worship the plant. They do not seem to think about it much at all. They live in it the way one lives in a cave: with practical regard for its properties and no particular reverence for its existence. They fish in the channels, using the bone hooks from the old collections. They sleep in the callus rooms, which are warm — the plant's metabolic heat keeps the interior a degree or two above the surrounding water. They move carefully, because the plant tissue is slippery and the currents in the central chamber are strong enough to carry an inattentive swimmer into a wall. They do not speak to each other. The sound is too loud for comfortable conversation, and they have developed a system of hand signals that is not a language so much as a set of shared habits: *this way*, *stop*, *look*, *good*. When they leave, they leave nothing. The callus will erase the evidence of their presence within a generation.

---

Standing in the central chamber — floating, hovering, held in place by the gentle tension between the inward current at the base and the outward current at the crown — you are inside something alive and something built and something abandoned and something inhabited, all at once, and none of these categories quite holds. The Maren Vered is a plant, but the rooms are architecture. The water is fluid, but it is also air. The light is generated by organisms you cannot see, in a body that does not know it is illuminated, for the benefit of creatures that have no word for any of it. The sound is the sound of a living system's mechanics: the pump of its fluids, the flex of its structure, the clicks and rattles and hisses of the organisms that have made their home in its infrastructure, all of it layered, all of it constant, all of it so loud that after a time it ceases to be sound and becomes something closer to weather — an acoustic climate you inhabit, that presses on you from all directions, that you stop hearing only in the sense that you stop feeling the temperature of the air.

The Maren Vered is not strange. That is the strange thing about it. There is nothing here that defies explanation, nothing that feels wrong or unnatural. It feels *settled*. It feels like a place that has found its form and is keeping it, slowly, patiently, with the confidence of something that has been itself for so long it has forgotten any other possibility. The carved rooms are being filled. The hermits will eventually stop coming. The tools will be absorbed into the callus. The *veredai* will continue to click against the walls. The light will continue to surge in its slow blue waves. The sound will continue.

You leave the way you came, through the root-gate, into whatever is outside, which you have already begun to forget.
