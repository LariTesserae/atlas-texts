# The Rootworks

## First Descent

The stairwell goes down. That's most of what can be said for certain at the beginning — a stairwell cut into living wood, each step worn to a shallow bowl by a century of feet, descending through tissue that smells like sawdust and overripe figs. The walls sweat. Not water exactly — a warm, faintly sweet sap that beads on the grain and runs in slow threads, collecting in the carved gutters someone channeled along the stairwell walls generations ago. The gutters are the first sign of habitation. They're worn smooth. They've been maintained.

The light down here is not darkness's absence so much as darkness's tolerance — a dull amber pervasion, as though the wood itself holds some faint old sunlight in storage and releases it grudgingly. You can see, but everything exists in a permanent late-dusk register: no shadows, no highlights, just amber on amber. Distance gets uncertain past twenty paces. Things at the edge of visibility are suspected rather than seen.

The sound arrives before the space does.

## The Chamber

The stairwell opens without warning into the Hollow — a cavity in the root-mass roughly the size of a cathedral nave, and shaped like one, though nothing built it. This is the heart of the place: a gap where a great ancestral tree's roots diverged and the space between them became, over centuries, a room. The ceiling is a woven vault of root and lateral runner, descending in columns that never quite touch the floor. The floor is peat and root-bark and the compressed remains of everything that has ever fallen here, springy underfoot, exhaling a mushroom-and-peat-smoke smell with each step.

It is loud here. Not a chaotic loudness — a layered, organized, industrial loudness, like standing in a factory where all the machines are alive. The sound comes from the animal mass, and the animal mass is the second thing you understand about the Hollow, after the light.

## The Fauna

There are, conservatively, a hundred thousand creatures in this chamber, and none of them are hiding.

The bulk of the volume is the Grinders — fist-sized beetles of a matte black that catches no light, moving in continuous processions along grooves worn an inch deep into the floor and the root columns. They carry things: grain, spore-packets, flakes of bark, each other's dead. Their carapaces click when they crowd, and a thousand beetles crowding at a junction produce a sound like rain on a tin roof, sustained and rhythmic and never stopping. The Grinders are the workers of this place. They maintain the gutters. They will, given weeks, disassemble anything left on the floor into transportable flakes.

Above them, the air holds the Whirrs — small flying things, moth-boned, in dense slow clouds that drift through the amber light like smoke with opinions. They make a wet electric hum, a sound halfway between a fly in a bottle and a transformer. They're harmless and everywhere, and you breathe carefully so as not to inhale them, and everyone who lives here has long since stopped noticing this concern. They gather at the sap-gutters to drink, and their clouds thicken visibly near any fresh wound in the wood, which is why the inhabitants are careful about wounds in the wood.

And threaded through everything, in the walls, in the floor, in the root-columns themselves: the Borers. Long pale creatures, thumb-thick, segmented, blind, patient as erosion, that tunnel through the living wood in networks the way blood moves through veins. Their tunnels ventilate the Hollow — you can feel the faint constant airflow, warm and damp and yeast-smelling, entering high on one wall and exiting low on the other, a breath the chamber takes every eleven minutes or so. The Borers don't work for anyone. But the whole place is arranged around the spaces they've made and the air they move.

## The Settlement

Humans live here too — some two hundred of them, and their town occupies the root-columns like rings on fingers.

The columns are hollow or made hollow, and each one is a building: floors cut in at intervals, connected by external stairways of peg and lashing, windows that are simply places where a borer tunnel exits and the residents widened it and hung a door. The town is vertical by necessity — the floor of the Hollow belongs to the processions and the peat and the beasts too large to be polite, so the people build upward along the columns, their houses stacked like bracket fungi, their walkways spiraling the roots in switchback stairs.

It's an industrial town, and this needs explaining, because the industry is strange: they farm the fauna.

The Grinders are their meat, their labor, and their freight system. Baiting a groove with fermented sap redirects a procession in a matter of hours; the town's whole freight network is lines of scent drawn on the floor with brushes. The Whirrs are rendered for oil — a thick amber grease that burns in lamps with a smell like frying insects, which is the only light source brighter than the wood's own glow, and the only light that makes proper shadows. The oil lamps are hung on poles and brackets all through the town, and at the height of the working day the Hollow's ceiling reflects a scatter of small flames like a low night sky.

The engine at the center of town is a Borer — a queen, gravid and enormous, thirty feet long, housed in a chamber the townspeople excavated for her decades ago and have been cultivating ever since. She lays continuously and the town's population of tunnel-network depends on her issue, and the town depends on the tunnels for air, for water, for the capillary transport of sap up from the deep root-wells. When she shifts her bulk — rarely, but the ground carries it — every conversation in the Hollow pauses mid-word, because a dead queen means three bad years while a successor matures, and everyone knows someone who died in the last interregnum.

So the engine is worshipped in the way engines get worshipped: fed, monitored, argued over. Technicians — rootwrights — live in the column nearest her chamber, and their tools are surgical, their records obsessive, their arguments with each other legendary and audible across the chamber floor.

## Water and Weather

It rains twice a day in the Hollow. Not from any sky.

The great tree above drinks from a river, and the water descends through the root-mass under pressure, and twice daily — when the tree's transpiration and the river's pulse align, at hours the town keeps by bells — the pressure exceeds what the wood can hold, and the roots sweat. The vaulted ceiling goes dark and glossy, then drips, then streams. Rain comes down in fat warm drops that hit the peat floor with a sound like slow applause, the Grinders' clicking swells to meet it, and everything in the Hollow — beasts, people, buildings — stands in the rain with the resigned adjustment of a town that has built under a leak and calls it weather.

The town harvests the rain. Every column has cisterns; every roof is a converging series of bark-shingles angled to channels; children's chore is walking the gutters with scrapers, clearing the beetle-traffic jams so the water moves. The water is sweet, faintly mineral, and tastes the way the whole place smells: fig, yeast, iron, peat.

Between rains, the damp persists — condensation on the cool inner faces of the outer root-walls, the floor peat staying black and springy, the air always just short of wet. Mold is the town's chronic war, the thing everyone's mother warned about: leave anything organic and unattended for a week and it comes back wearing a coat. Fabrics are oiled with Whirr-grease. Books are the town's rarest possessions and are kept in the driest column, in wax-sealed chests, and are read aloud on holidays because a book opened too often will not survive its own popularity.

## The Rules of the Place

There are rules here, and they're not arbitrary; each one has a body count behind it.

First: don't cut living wood. Wounded wood draws Whirrs by the ten-thousand, then Borer scouts, then things that eat what eats the wood. All construction uses deadfall, salvage, or the outer layers of already-hollowed columns, and the rootwrights can license a cut the way other towns license explosives. There's a sealed column near the north end of the Hollow, its stairways dead-ended, its people relocated a generation ago, that stands as the permanent sermon on this rule.

Second: the floor is a road. Don't stand in a groove, don't leave anything on the floor you aren't prepared to have processed, and if you need to cross the chamber you walk between the processions, not against them, and you move at the pace the beetles set. The town's children learn this before they learn letters. It's said that every family has a story involving someone who forgot.

Third: the queen's eleven-minute breath is time. Not metaphor — the airflow cycles are the clock. Contracts are written in breaths; work shifts are measured in them; the town's bells ring not on hours but on the thirty-fourth breath of each cycle, and a rootwright who says "meet me in two hundred breaths" means it exactly. Visitors find this maddening and then, within a week, find ordinary clocks unbearable.

Fourth: quiet things are dangerous things. The Hollow is loud as a baseline, and the fauna is loud as a baseline, and anything in this place that has gone silent has gone silent for a reason. A procession that stops clicking means a predator is near — the floor hunters, flat eyeless armored things that live in the deep peat and surface rarely, and the town's few actual weapons are kept for them alone. A Whirr-cloud that goes still means a sap-wound somewhere. Silence, here, is an alarm.

## The Life of It

Understand it as active, always. The processions never stop — the clicking is there when you sleep, a feature of sleep, what silence-in-the-Hollow actually consists of. The Whirr-clouds migrate through the day from column to column following the warmth of the lamps. The peat floor is being eaten and rebuilt continuously, a slow tide visible only if you mark a spot and come back in a month to find it inches displaced. The town's buildings settle and grow — because the columns they're built on are alive and thickening, the stairways need re-pegging every few years, doors need re-hanging, and there are men whose whole trade is the adjustment of a house to its living substrate. A house here isn't a place; it's a negotiation, ongoing.

And the ecology is a loop, closed and humming: the tree feeds the roots, the roots house the fauna, the fauna feeds and builds and airs the town, the town tends the fauna, the dead of both go to the peat, and the peat goes to the roots. Nothing here is wasted because nothing here is permitted to be.

## Standing in It, At the End

Stand at the mouth of the stairwell at the bottom of the descent, and take it in whole: the amber light lying evenly on everything like a memory of a memory of sunlight. The nave-scale emptiness of the middle distance, almost nothing in it — the processions hug the edges and the walls, and the open floor is bare peat crossed only by the freight-lines of drawn scent, visible as faint darker traces, like a diagram of the town's intentions. The columns rising through the gloom with their stacked houses, their lamp-scatter, their washing hung out on lines between shingles, stiff and warm and drying to the rhythm of the twice-daily rain. The noise: clicking, humming, the creak of living wood taking its eleven-minute breath, somewhere the sound of hammering from the rootwrights' column, somewhere the bells.

It's warm — warmer than any underground has a right to be, because the wood around you is alive and the peat is composting and ten thousand beetles are always moving. You sweat faintly and it never fully dries.

You are standing inside something's body, and it knows you're there, and it has decided you're fine.

That's the Hollow's character, in the end: a place that predates its people by whole ages, that tolerates and employs and is employed by them, that holds them the way a fist holds a seed — completely, and without malice, and for reasons of its own.
