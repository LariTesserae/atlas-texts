# The Custodian

She is not what you would expect.

That is the first thing to understand. You might imagine someone hardened, lean, weathered — a person sculpted by altitude and labor into something sinewy and spare. That person does not exist here. What exists is a woman in her middle sixties whose hands are broad and whose shoulders are rounded and whose body has acquired, over decades, the particular density of someone who lifts things that are always wet. Water weight. The weight of saturated mat. The weight of a pump housing when you've unbolted it and the water hasn't fully drained and you're holding it at chest height because there's nowhere to set it down that isn't alive. She is not lean. She is *compressed*. The way the atmosphere here is compressed, the way the light is compressed — everything at altitude, everything under pressure, everything denser than it would be lower down.

Her name is Maren. She has been here for — she is not certain. The tablet tells her. The tablet is how she keeps time, because her body cannot. Thirty-one years, the records show. She arrived when the workshop was three walls and a tarp. She is not sure that's right. The tablet's earliest entries are in a handwriting she recognizes as her own but does not remember producing, and they describe conditions she does not remember — a drier plateau, a thinner mat, the pumps running at different frequencies. She believes the records. She does not remember them. This is a distinction she has stopped worrying about.

---

She comes up from below.

There is a way. Not a path — paths imply permanence, imply feet, imply repetition wearing a groove. This is more like a permission. A route through the lower terraces that changes with each ascent because the vine-slope shifts, because the water finds new channels, because the mat grows over the footholds she used last time and she has to find new ones. The descent and return take a day and a half. She does this every — she does this regularly. The tablet reminds her. She has set it to pulse green when the interval has elapsed, and when she sees the pulse she packs her kit and goes.

The kit is specific. A machete with a blade wider than her hand, for clearing the vine-slope. A set of wrenches in a roll of oilcloth, for the pumps. A filtration mask, because the air on the lower terraces is thicker, wetter, full of particulate — plant matter and mist and the fine aerosol that the falls generate. A notebook, paper, waterproof paper, because she does not trust the tablet for everything. A thermos of something hot. She does not call it tea. It is not tea. It is whatever she has been steeping in the kitchen on the lower terrace, which is sometimes bark, sometimes leaf, sometimes the dried flowering tips of the reed-form, sometimes nothing — just hot water, which is its own comfort at this altitude.

The climb is hard. She does not talk about the climb. The climb is the tax she pays for living where she lives, and she pays it without accounting because accounting would make it heavier.

---

What she does, mostly, is listen.

You would think the work was mechanical. You would think it was the pumps, the valves, the gauges, the channels. You would think she walks her rounds and checks her equipment and adjusts her flows and logs her data and goes home. She does these things. She does them every day she is on the plateau, and she is on the plateau most days. But the work — the actual work, the work that matters, the work that no one has asked her to do because no one is asking — is listening.

She has been here long enough that her ears have disaggregated the sound. What you hear as a roar, she hears as — she once described it, in the notebook, as a *population*. Individual sounds, individual lives, each water-drop and each leaf-spill and each reed-vibration existing as itself within the aggregate. She can hear a leaf spilling on the eastern edge of the plateau from the western edge. Not because she has extraordinary hearing — she does not, or did not, once — but because her auditory cortex has been shaped by this place the way a riverbed is shaped by water. The sound has carved channels in her. She is, neurologically, an instrument tuned to this plateau.

She walks to the workshop and stands inside it and closes her eyes and she can hear which pump is pulling at which rate and which channel is flowing at which volume and which section of the mat is more saturated than it should be, which means the root-system has thickened upstream and is redirecting flow, which means she needs to go there and thin it, which means cutting living plant matter away from other living plant matter with her clippers, which is a specific kind of grief she has learned to perform without dwelling on.

She listens to the water the way a doctor listens to breath. She is not embarrassed by this comparison. She made it herself, years ago, in the notebook, in an entry she does not remember writing.

---

The plants have names. Not Latin names. Not common names. She named them, or they named themselves through her, or the names were always there and she found them.

The shelf-plant she calls *broadback*. The reed-form she calls *singer*. The mat-form she calls *lattice*. The vine-slope form she calls *draper*. These are not sentimental names. They are functional. She names things by what they do. She has no interest in what they *are* — in the taxonomic sense, in the genetic sense, in the sense that would require a lab and a scope and a certainty she does not possess and does not want. She is interested in what they do. What they do is hold water, move water, filter water, become water. She names them for their work, because she names herself for her work, because she learned long ago that naming by work is the only honest naming.

There is a fifth form. She has not named it yet. It appeared — she does not know when. She does not know if it *appeared* or if it was always there and she only recently distinguished it. It grows in the eastern denser growth, in the place where the shelf-plants give way to something taller, and it is not any of the other forms and it is not a hybrid of them and she does not know what it does. She has been watching it for — the notebook says four years. It does not seem to do anything. It grows. It is wet. It occupies space. She is patient with it. She has learned that patience in this place is not a virtue but a *method*. You wait and the thing reveals itself or it doesn't, and either way you have not wasted time because time is not a resource here. Time is a medium. Like water. It flows through you and you do not use it up.

She is slightly afraid of the fifth form. She does not write this in the notebook. But her hands tighten when she walks past it, and her breathing changes, and the sound of the place — the sound she carries in her body like a second heartbeat — shifts by a degree she cannot measure but can feel. Something is changing in the eastern growth. Something is coming into being. She does not know what. She does not know if she is its custodian or its catalyst or its witness or its predecessor.

She walks past it every day she is on the plateau and she listens and she does not go in.

---

Her hands are the part of her that is most legibly *of this place*.

She does not think about her hands. She uses them. But if you were to look at them — and she would let you, she has nothing to hide, she has not had anything to hide for decades — you would see something. The knuckles are thick. Not from arthritis but from use, from the repetitive torque of wrenches and the gripping of clippers and the pulling of cured stem-wood. The skin is rough but not cracked — the moisture here prevents cracking, keeps the skin pliable, so her hands are rough *and* soft, like the underside of a leaf. There is a scar on her left palm from when she taught herself to use the machete and did not yet know the angle the vine-slope required. The scar has healed clean and pale and is always slightly cooler than the skin around it, as though the scar tissue holds less blood or more water or is simply different in the way that healed things are different from unbroken things.

She uses her hands to read the mat. She kneels — her knees are fine, her knees are the part of her that has aged *least*, which she attributes to the cushioning of the substrate, which may or may not be true — and she presses her palms flat against the living ground and she feels for the water beneath. Not the surface water, not the seeps and the spills, but the water moving through the root-system, through the rhizome network, through the grafted and fused body of the whole plateau. She can feel the pressure. She can feel the direction of flow. She can feel, sometimes, a kind of *pulse* — a rhythmic variation in the pressure that is not mechanical, not the pumps, but something the plant itself is doing, something hydraulic, something that has to do with cells opening and closing, with osmosis, with the active transport of water from root to stem to leaf to air. She puts her hands on the ground and she feels the plateau *drinking*. She does not have words for what this is like. She has never tried to find words for it. It is enough that she can feel it. It is enough that she knows.

---

She was not always alone.

The notebook mentions others. Not by name — Maren does not name people the way she names plants, by function — but by presence. *The engineer*, early entries call someone, the person who built the first pump, who designed the filtration system, who taught Maren to read the gauges. *The engineer* appears in entries spanning the first three years and then disappears. No mention of departure. No mention of death. Just an absence, and then the entries continue, and the voice in the handwriting does not change, does not grieve, does not acknowledge the subtraction. Either Maren did not grieve or she grieved in a way that did not reach the page or she does not remember grieving. Any of these is possible.

Later: *the younger one*. Appears in entries from year seven or eight, stays for a span, goes. The handwriting in those entries is — she cannot read her own handwriting as emotional. She has tried. The entries are the same: flow rates, pump maintenance schedules, observations of the mat's expansion, notes on the eastern growth. But there is a page, between entries, that is blank. Not blank because nothing was written on it. Blank because something was written on it and then erased, or torn out, or washed away — the page is slightly rougher than the others, slightly different in texture, as though it has been wet and dried. She does not remember what was on it. She has stopped looking at it.

She is alone now. She is not lonely. She has thought about this distinction and she has decided that it is real, that aloneness and loneliness are different conditions, that the first is a fact and the second is a feeling, and that the feeling requires a memory of its opposite and the fact does not. She does not remember company well enough to miss it. The plateau does not miss company. The plateau is complete. She is part of its completeness. This is not a consolation. It is a description.

---

She sleeps on the plateau sometimes. Not in the workshop — the workshop is for work, and she maintains the boundary because without boundaries the work becomes everything and the everything becomes the work and the difference dissolves, and she has seen that dissolution and it is not peaceful, it is erasure. She sleeps in a structure she grew herself, twenty meters from the workshop, a small dome of trained shelf-plant, the leaves induced to curve rather than fan, the stems bent and buttressed into arches. Inside the dome: a bedroll, a small stove she does not use because the heat would harm the plant, a change of clothes in a sealed bag. The bedroll is on top of the living mat. She can feel the mat breathing — not literally, not lungs, but the slow expansion and contraction of saturated plant tissue as water moves through it, the hydraulic pulse she feels with her hands when she is awake and with her spine when she is asleep.

She dreams of water. Not the water here — not the falls, not the mat, not the plateau. Other water. Water she has known in other places, lower places, the places she came from before this, the places she does not remember well because the plateau has overwritten them the way the mat overwrites the ground beneath it, cell by cell, year by year. In the dreams she is in a river, or she is beside an ocean, or she is in a kitchen with a faucet running, and the water in the dream is doing something the water here does not do — it is *arriving*. It is coming toward her from somewhere else. It is not here already. It is not everywhere already. She wakes from these dreams with her hands outstretched, palms up, as though catching something.

She does not analyze this. She has more important things to do.

---

What she is doing, specifically, when you arrive — if you were to arrive, if you were the person arriving, which you are not, because no one arrives, because the route up is not a route and the plateau does not invite and the woman who tends it does not extend invitations — what she is doing is this:

She is kneeling at the edge of the western curtain, where the mat drapes over and the water falls, and she is measuring the flow. She has a cup. Not a measuring cup — a cup, a tin cup, dented, and she holds it in the falling water for a count of ten and she removes it and she pours it into a graduated cylinder she has bolted to a stake and she reads the volume and she writes it in the notebook and she does this every day she is on the plateau and she has done it every day she has been on the plateau for — thirty-one years, the tablet says. Thirty-one years of cups of water, counted to ten, poured, read, written. The data is in the tablet, digitized, graphable, trendable. She keeps the notebook because the notebook is physical and the tablet is not and she trusts physical things because the plateau is physical and the water is physical and the work is physical and she has learned, over decades, that the physical is what lasts.

The flow is changing. It has been changing for — she does not know how long. The volume is increasing. Not dramatically. Not alarmingly. A few milliliters per cup per year. The water is coming faster. She does not know why. She has checked the pumps. The pumps are fine. She has checked the channels. The channels are clear. She has checked the mat. The mat is healthy — too healthy, perhaps, the root-systems thickening, the lattice-form expanding, the whole substrate becoming denser and more complex and more *structured* in the way that living things become more structured as they mature. The plateau is maturing. It is becoming more itself. And the water is moving faster through it, and she does not know if that is a symptom or a cause or a consequence or simply what happens when a system like this one reaches a certain density.

She writes the number in the notebook. She closes the notebook. She puts the cup back in the waterproof bag she keeps it in. She stands up. Her knees do not hurt. She walks back toward the workshop, and the mat compresses under her boots and pushes back, and the leaves spill above her, and the reeds sing, and the pumps pulse, and the water falls, and the fifth form grows in the eastern growth, unnamed, unknown, and the light is white and close and does not change, and she is here, and she is tending, and she will be here tomorrow.
