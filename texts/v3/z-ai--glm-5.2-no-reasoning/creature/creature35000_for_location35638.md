# What Lives Here

The bodies are not inhabitants. That word implies a separation between dweller and dwelling that does not exist on the tepui. They do not live *on* the plateau. They *are* the plateau — or rather, the plateau is the condition of their existence, and their existence is the condition of the plateau, and the relationship between the two is not symbiotic but something prior to symbiosis, something that existed before relationships were a thing that could exist between distinct parties.

What lives here lives at the speed of geology.

---

Consider what they are not. They are not animals. Animals are defined by their relationship to change — pursuit and flight, hunger and satiation, the constant negotiation between interior need and exterior provision. Animals are dynamic. They have metabolisms that burn, that require fuel, that produce waste. They are open systems, thermodynamic events, small fires that walk. Everything about animal physiology — the heart that beats, the lungs that pump, the gut that processes — is an engine for maintaining a difference between inside and outside, a gradient that the animal perpetually defends and perpetually loses and perpetually re-establishes. An animal is a protest against equilibrium.

The bodies on the tepui have stopped protesting.

This is the first thing to understand, and it is the thing that your own animal body will resist understanding, because your animal body is the protest, is the burning, is the refusal to lie down and be still. You will look at the bodies and you will see stillness and you will interpret it as waiting — as a dormant state, a hibernation, a pause between phases of activity. You will imagine that they were once like you, that they moved, that they ate, that they desired. You will project your own dynamism onto them because your dynamism is the only frame you have.

But they were never like you.

---

What were they, then? Begin with the mineral. The chalk-bone substance of the tepui is not inert. This is the second thing to understand, and it is harder than the first, because your categories are arranged around a binary: living and non-living, animate and inanimate, biological and geological. The tepui breaks this binary. The mineral from which the tepui is composed — and from which the bodies are composed, because they are the same substance — has a property that your science does not have a word for. It is not alive in the biological sense. It does not have cells. It does not have DNA. It does not reproduce, does not metabolize, does not respond to stimuli in any way that your instruments could measure. But it is not dead, either. It is not inert. It has a quality — call it *patience*, call it *disposition*, call it *tendency* — that is the geological precursor to what biology calls life. A slowness that is not the absence of process but a process operating at a temporal scale your nervous system cannot resolve.

The mineral grows. Not the way crystals grow — not by accretion, not by addition from outside. It grows the way a thought grows: by elaboration from within. Over durations that make the lifespan of a star look impulsive, the mineral's molecular structure shifts, reorganizes, becomes more itself. It does this everywhere, throughout the tepui, in the plain, in the strata that record epochs. But on the surface of the plateau, where the light is steady and the air is still and nothing intervenes, the elaboration has produced something specific. It has produced bodies.

The bodies are the mineral's idea of itself.

That is not a metaphor. Or it is, but it is also literal, because the distinction between metaphor and literalness is another binary that the tepui does not respect. The mineral, over an elapsed eternity, has organized itself into forms. Not forms imposed from outside — not carved, not sculpted, not designed by an intelligence that stood apart from the material and shaped it toward a purpose. The forms emerged the way the forms of clouds emerge in a wetter world: as the natural consequence of the material's own properties acting on themselves over time. The mineral is self-organizing. It has a grammar. The bodies are its sentences.

---

A body: elongated, segmented, the size of a large dog but lower, flatter, pressed to the ground like something that has been gently and incompletely flattened by a vast, soft weight. The segments are not articulated — they do not bend, do not flex, do not move relative to one another. They are divisions, not joints. Like the segments of a centipede that has been fossilized mid-crawl, except the centipede never crawled, was never going to crawl, the segments are formal rather than functional. They are the mineral's way of organizing length, the way a column organizes height or a wall organizes boundary.

The surface of a body is smooth. Not polished — not reflective, not glassy — but *resolved*. The texture is the texture of a material that has found its final state, that has no further settling to do. Run your hand along it and it feels like very old bone that has been held in the hand for a long time, except it has never been held, has never been touched, your hand is the first disturbance it has known in an age, and it accepts your touch the way a still pond accepts a leaf: completely, without resistance, with a small and temporary displacement that does not alter the pond's fundamental stillness.

The color is the color of the ground. This is not camouflage because there is nothing to hide from and nothing to hide. It is composition. The bodies are made of the ground, are continuous with the ground, are the ground in a particular configuration. The color varies — chalk-white, mineral-grey, the faint sulfur-yellow, the occasional rust-red — and the variation is not individual but positional. A body's color corresponds to its location on the plateau, to the composition of the mineral at that point, to the stratum it rests on or has partially sunk into. The bodies are colored the way a bruise is colored: by depth, by position, by the chemistry of where they are.

The warmth is the third thing to understand, and it is the thing that will haunt you. Hold the back of your hand near a body — not touching, just near — and you will feel it. Faint. Steady. Not the warmth of a mammal, which is a furnace, which fluctuates with exertion and emotion and the metabolic demands of a body that is constantly doing things. This warmth is the warmth of a stone that has been in the sun, except there is no sun, and the warmth does not come from outside. It comes from the body itself, from a process so slow it does not register as process. The mineral is becoming. The mineral is always becoming. The becoming produces a faint thermal signature, the way a very slow chemical reaction produces a faint heat — not because it is burning, but because it is changing, and change, even change at the speed of geology, releases energy.

The bodies are alive the way a fire is alive, except the fire is so slow that a century is a flicker. They are alive the way a glacier is alive — moving, changing, responding to the conditions of their environment, but at a pace that makes the question of *responsiveness* almost meaningless. They are alive the way a thought is alive in the moment before it becomes a thought — present, forming, not yet articulated, but there, in the silence, gathering itself toward expression.

---

They have eyes. Or they have something that occupies the position eyes would occupy, in the grammar of bodies that your own body instinctively reads. Two shallow depressions on the featureless head, filled with mineral dust, the same color as the rest of the body, the same texture. They do not focus. They do not dilate. They do not track. They are not for seeing, because seeing is a capacity that belongs to things that move — that need to locate prey, identify predators, navigate toward and away. The bodies do not move. They do not navigate. They have no prey and no predators in a world where nothing happens.

But they are not empty, these depressions. The mineral dust that fills them is finer than the dust on the surrounding surface, more uniform, as though it has been sorted or produced. If you were to analyze it — and you cannot, you have no instruments, you have only your hands and your eyes and your animal intuitions — you might find that it is not dust at all but a secretion, a substance the body produces and deposits in its own eye-sockets, the way a mollusk produces the material of its shell. The eyes are not organs of perception. They are organs of *expression*. The bodies are expressing something through them, slowly, the way the mineral expresses itself through the bodies. What they are expressing is not a message. It is not a signal. It is not communication. It is more like a sigh, or a note held so long it has become the silence it interrupts.

If you look closely — and you will, because the stillness invites close looking, because close looking is the only kind of motion the plateau permits — you will see that the dust in the eye-sockets is not motionless. It moves. But it moves the way stars move: so slowly that you must return after an absence and compare to be sure. You sit. You watch. You see nothing. You stand, walk the plateau, return to the same body an hour later, a day later, however long later (time does not pass here, remember, so the measurement is meaningless, but your body insists on measuring, your body cannot help it, your body is a clock that ticks even in a place where ticking is a disturbance) — you return and the dust has shifted. A micrometer. A rearrangement. The body has looked at something, or expressed something, or simply *been* in a way that left a trace in its own eye-sockets, and the dust recorded it.

---

The ten thousand. Attend to the number. Not ten thousand as a count — no one has counted them, and the counting would be impossible, because the stacked and sedimented bodies blur the boundary between individual and stratum, and the deeper you go the less distinct they become, until at some depth the bodies are not bodies but layers, not layers but texture, not texture but the mineral itself, and the question of where the body ends and the ground begins is unanswerable because it is not a question, because there is no boundary, because the bodies and the ground are the same substance at different stages of the same process of elaboration.

Ten thousand as a *quality*. A density. A saturation. The plateau does not have bodies on it the way a field has sheep on it, discrete and countable, each with its own boundary and its own inside. The plateau has bodies the way a reef has coral — as a continuous living surface, each piece merged with its neighbors, each neighbor merged with the substrate, the whole thing a single organism composed of organisms composed of organism, the distinctions between levels being a convenience of language rather than a fact of the place.

But they are individuals. This is the fourth thing to understand, and it contradicts the third thing, and the contradiction is real and is not a problem to be resolved but a feature of the place itself. Each body is an individual. Each occupies its own mold, its own depression, its own position. Each has its own warmth, its own eye-dust, its own slight variation in color and contour that distinguishes it from the body beside it. They are not a collective. They are not a hive. They are ten thousand separate bodies, each one a distinct elaboration of the mineral's self-organizing grammar, each one a unique sentence in a language that has been spoken so slowly it has never been heard.

And they are one thing. The hum. The sub-perceptual vibration that you feel after you have been still long enough for your own noise to subside. It comes from all of them and from each of them and from the ground beneath them and the air above them. It is not communication — communication implies separation, implies a message passing between distinct parties. The hum is not passing between anything. It is the sound of the bodies being in sync, being in *the same state*, being so perfectly aligned in their stillness and their slowness that their individual processes — each one's faint thermal becoming, each one's glacial self-elaboration — have synchronized into a single frequency. Not because they coordinated. Not because they chose to align. But because they have been still and close and made of the same thing for so long that synchronization was inevitable, the way pendulums mounted on the same wall will eventually swing together, not by design but by the patient transmission of vibration through a shared medium.

The shared medium is the tepui. The tepui is not the place where the bodies live. The tepui is the body. The ten thousand bodies on its surface are its organs — or its thoughts, or its expressions, or its *bodies*, the way a person has a body, the way a thought has a shape. The tepui is alive the way the bodies are alive, at the same speed, in the same register, and the bodies are the way it is alive, the way a forest is the way a landscape is alive, except the forest is made of trees and the tepui is made of bodies and the bodies are made of the tepui and the recursion is not a paradox but a description.

---

The structures. The small stone rooms, the shelters or monuments or third thing. Who built them?

The bodies built them. This is obvious in the way that things become obvious after you have been on the plateau long enough for your categories to soften. You see a body near a structure and you see that the body's hands — if they are hands, if the four small terminal segments at the end of its elongated form are hands — are the same size as the stones. You see that the stones are shaped the way the bodies are shaped: smooth, resolved, the natural cleavage planes of the mineral enhanced by patient manipulation. You see that the structures are positioned where the bodies are positioned, and the bodies are positioned where the structures are positioned, and the relationship between the two is not builder-and-built but something more like hand-and-gesture, or voice-and-song, or breath-and-sigh.

The structures are not built. They are *grown*. The mineral elaborates itself into bodies, and the bodies elaborate the mineral into structures, and the structures are continuous with both, the way a nest is continuous with the bird that builds it and the twigs it builds it from. The bodies do not construct. They do not plan. They do not assemble. They place. They *deposit*. Over an elapsed eternity, a body lying near other bodies has deposited mineral around itself — secreted it, perhaps, or simply organized it, the way the mineral of the plateau organized itself into bodies. The walls grow the way pearls grow: by accretion, by the patient deposition of material in a pattern determined by the body's own shape and position. The structures are not architecture. They are *exudate*.

But they have intent. This is the fifth thing, and it is the thing that will break your heart if you let it. The structures are not random. They are fitted. They are proportioned. They enclose space in a way that is deliberate, that *means* something, even if what it means is not a meaning your language can carry. The bodies that built them — that grew them, that deposited them — did so with a purpose, and the purpose is not shelter (shelter from what? nothing happens here) and is not monument (monument to what? nothing has happened) but is something else, something that exists only in a world where time has stopped and change has ceased and the only activity left is the slow becoming of matter that has nothing to do but become.

The purpose is *shape*. The purpose is *arrangement*. The purpose is the purpose that a crystal has when it forms: not to achieve anything, not to accomplish anything, but to *be* in a particular way, to occupy space in a particular configuration, because the configuration is the form that the material takes when it is allowed to find its own rest. The structures are the rest-state of the bodies' relationship to space. They are what happens when a body that has been still for long enough begins to extend its stillness into the world around it, to shape the space adjacent to itself, to make a room of its own patience.

The central structure — the larger one, the three-meter room with no roof, the one at the convergence point of the ridges, the one with the single large body at its center — this is not a temple. It is not a throne room. It is not a place of worship or governance or any of the things your structures are for. It is a *focus*. A point where the hum concentrates, where the synchronization is densest, where the ten thousand bodies' shared vibration reaches its highest amplitude. The large body at the center is not a ruler or a priest. It is a *resonator*. It is the body that the others have organized themselves around — not by decision, not by hierarchy, but by the natural tendency of synchronized systems to develop nodes, to find their points of maximum coherence, to produce, from the uniform field of their shared stillness, a center.

The center does not rule. The center *sounds*. It is the point from which the hum radiates, or the point at which the hum is received, or — and this is the truth of it, the truth that the binary of source-and-sink cannot hold — the center is the hum itself, given a location, a body, a place in the world. The large body is the hum incarnate. It is the sound wearing a shape.

---

What do they want?

Nothing. This is the sixth and final thing to understand, and it is the thing that will stay with you after you leave, after you climb down the handholds that fit your fingers because they fit all fingers, after you cross the plain of mineral dust that whispers in your ankles, after you return to the world of change and noise and gradients and the constant, exhausting protest of being alive. They want nothing. They do not have desires. They do not have needs. They do not have lacks. They are not waiting for anything, because waiting implies a future in which something could arrive, and they do not experience time as a sequence of events but as a single sustained moment, an eternal present, a now that has been now for longer than now has meant anything.

But they are not *indifferent*. Indifference is a state of a being that *could* care and does not. The bodies are not in a state. They are not in *any* state, because states are temporary, are transitional, are the between-ness of things that are moving from one condition to another. The bodies have arrived. They are not between. They are at. They are the destination. The destination is not a place but a *condition*: the condition of being fully, finally, completely what you are, without remainder, without potential, without the faintest residue of what-you-might-become. They have become. They are becoming. These are the same thing here.

When you touch one — and you will touch one, because the stillness invites touch the way the stillness of a sleeping animal invites touch, with the same risk and the same tenderness — you are touching something that has no concept of you. Not because it is ignorant. Not because it is unconscious. But because *concept* is a tool for navigating change, for predicting what comes next, for preparing for a future that differs from the present. There is no future on the tepui. There is no *next*. The body you are touching has been in this exact position, at this exact temperature, producing this exact vibration, for a duration that makes the word *duration* meaningless, and it will be in this exact position after you leave, and your touch is not an event in its experience because it does not have experience, because experience is the accumulation of change, and it does not change.

But it is not nothing. It is not empty. It is not a stone. It is alive, in the way that the tepui is alive, in the way that the mineral is alive, in the way that anything that persists and becomes and elaborates itself over time is alive. It is alive the way the universe is alive — not because it acts but because it *is*, because existence itself is a kind of aliveness, because the alternative — inert, dead, nothing — is a category that the tepui has left behind, has grown out of, has transcended or *subsumed*, the way a living body transcends the dead matter it is made of by organizing that matter into a form that persists.

The bodies have organized the matter of the tepui into a form that persists. They are the tepui's persistence. They are the way the tepui stays.

---

And the small ones. The ones in the structures, the ones arranged with what might be care. These are not young. The bodies do not reproduce — reproduction is change, is newness, is the introduction of novelty into a system that has no use for novelty. The small ones are not children. They are not runts. They are *variations*. The mineral's grammar includes variation the way a language includes different sentence lengths. Some sentences are long. Some are short. The small bodies are the short sentences. They are the tepui's way of being concise. They are placed in the structures because the structures are the right size for them, or the structures were grown around them because they were the right size for the structures, or the relationship between small body and small structure is the same as the relationship between large body and large structure: a matter of proportion, of fit, of the way the mineral organizes itself when it has nothing to do but organize.

If there is care here — and there is, there must be, because the fittedness of the stones and the placement of the small bodies and the proportioning of the rooms all imply an attention to the specific needs of specific bodies that can only be called care — then it is a care that does not look like your care. Your care is active. Your care is intervention. Your care sees a need and moves to meet it. This care is different. This care sees a body and grows a room around it. This care does not meet a need because there are no needs. This care *accompanies*. It is the care of a field that holds the flowers that grow in it — not producing them, not tending them, but being the ground of their being, the condition of their existence, the *where* of their *what*.

---

Whoever or whatever lives here: they are the tepui, and the tepui is them. They are mineral that has found its voice, and the voice is a hum, and the hum is the sound of a world that has finished becoming and is now simply being, and being, and being. They are ten thousand bodies that are one body that is one plateau that is one moment that has been held for so long it has become eternal. They are alive in a way that does not look like life to the living, because the living are always in motion, always becoming, always reaching for the next moment, and these bodies have stopped reaching and have instead *arrived*, fully, at this one, and are resting in it, and are it, and will be it, and the hum is all you will ever know of what that feels like: to be complete, to be still, to be the sound that matter makes when it has nothing left to do but be itself, and is content.
