# What Lives at Braemoss

## The Workers, First

There are eleven of them, which is one more than the engines strictly need and two fewer than the place once supported. The number has settled there the way everything at Braemoss settles — not decided, just arrived at, through years of quiet attrition and the occasional departure of someone who could not bear the damp another winter. They do not replace people quickly. They do not replace people at all, sometimes. The Works runs whether there are eleven or nine or seven. The difference is only in how often the amber lamps stay lit.

They come up the track on the first day of their week carrying packs that are already heavy with the things they know they will need: dry socks they will never fully wear through, a flask of something distilled in the lowlands that burns warm and tastes of grain and iron, a book perhaps, though books at Braemoss swell with the humidity within days and by the end of the week their pages are soft and dark at the edges like old cloth. They do not bring much. There is no point bringing much. The place provides what it provides, and what it provides is warmth and water and the company of people who have chosen to be where conversation is nearly impossible and dryness is a memory.

Aldous is the one who has been here longest. Thirty-one years, though he does not count them and would not tell you the number if you asked; you learn it from the ledgers, where his handwriting changes over the years from the round careful script of a young man to the flattened, efficient hand of someone who has learned to write on damp paper in dim light. He moves through the engine hall the way the water moves through the peat — not quickly, not with evident purpose, but with a direction that is undeniable. He will stop at the fourth cylinder bank and put his hand flat against the housing and close his eyes and stay there for ten seconds, fifteen, feeling the cycle, and then move on without adjustment. Other times he will stop at the second cylinder and sign to whoever is near — a flat downward push of the palm, repeated twice: *this one is running ahead, check the tension on the upper fiber clamp.* He is not wrong. He has never been wrong about this, the other workers say, not in the way that matters, not in the way that causes damage or lost output. He is the Works the way the rowan is the chimney — not built into it, but growing from it, shaped by it, shaping it in return.

He is not tall. He is not short. He is the size of a man who has spent thirty-one years carrying wick bundles and bending into housing panels and eating dark bread and sleeping under wool that never dries. He is dense in the way that the peat is dense — not fat, not thin, but *compressed*, settled into himself, every extraneous thing pared away by the years until what remains is what the job requires and no more. His hands are broad and stained dark at the fingertips from the tannin water and from the iron that leaches through everything here, and the stain goes deep enough that it does not wash off in his weeks away. He carries the Works on his hands when he leaves. People must see it and not ask.

There are others. There is Maren, who weaves the wicks on the loom in the western shed and who has developed a rhythm with the waterwheel that is not mechanical but *musical* — she times her throws to the creak of the wheel, and the wicks she produces are measurably more uniform than those woven by anyone else, which matters because uniform wicks draw water at predictable rates and reduce the number of amber lamps in the hall. She is younger than Aldous by twenty years and has been here eight. She speaks in the hand-language fluently but also speaks aloud, in a low voice, when she is in the weaving shed alone, narrating her work to herself in words the others never hear. She does not know they sometimes hear it through the wall of the dormitory, very faintly, when the wind is right and the engines' drone shifts into a register that leaves a gap for her voice. They have never mentioned it.

There is Pirr, who tends the settling troughs and the outflow channel and who is the only worker who will wade into the peat water barefoot without hesitation. He says he can feel the rootlets under his feet and that they are not reading his weight but *greeting* him. He is not a mystical man. He is a practical man with a feeling in his feet. He catches the trout from the outflow with his hands, which the others consider unnecessary but do not discourage, because the trout are small and quick and the effort of catching them by hand occupies Pirr for long stretches of the afternoon when the settling troughs need nothing and the outflow runs clear and he would otherwise be idle, and idleness at Braemoss is not a sin but it is a kind of restlessness that the place does not easily absorb.

The others have names and histories and reasons for being here, but those are harder to find. They do not volunteer. They are not secretive; they are simply private in the way that people become when they live where no one can hear them speak. They have come from the lowlands, mostly, from the small towns in the valleys east and south where the water from Braemoss eventually arrives, and they have come for reasons that range from the practical — the work pays, modestly but reliably — to the personal, which they do not share, which is their right, and which the place absorbs without comment the way it absorbs everything.

## The Moths

The small brown moths in the engine hall are the only creatures here that the sound does not touch.

They are not remarkable to look at. Wings the color of the peat water — a dark amber-brown, slightly translucent at the edges, with a pale streak along the forewing that catches the green light from the lamps. They are perhaps two centimeters across at full spread. They sit on the running cylinders and on the iron beams and on the wick housings and on the damp brick and on the workers' aprons when the workers stand still long enough. They are not still. They move with the erratic, weaving flight of all moths, but slower here, as though the air's viscosity affects even their small bodies, and when they land they fold their wings quickly and press flat against whatever surface they have chosen and they *stay*, vibrating faintly with the engine's pulse, as if they are listening.

Their larvae eat the engine moss. Nothing else eats the engine moss — it is too thin, too mineral-rich, too adapted to the specific conditions of the hall. But the larvae burrow through it in small dark trails, leaving the pale moss paler where they have passed, and the moss grows back behind them in a week or two, and the cycle continues. There are perhaps three hundred moths in the hall at any given time. The workers do not count them. The workers do not think about them. The moths are as much a part of the machinery as the bakelite gaskets and the iron bolts.

What is remarkable about the moths is that they exist nowhere else. They are not a widespread species that colonized the hall. They *emerged* here. The ledgers do not record when they first appeared, but there is an entry in the maintenance log from forty-three years ago that reads: *small flies in the hall, larvae in the upper moss, no damage to wicks, no action taken.* The "small flies" were the first generation. By then they had probably already been there for years, unnoticed, the way everything at Braemoss is unnoticed until it becomes undeniable.

They have changed since then. The first moths, if they were like the first moths, were probably paler, probably less dense in the wing, probably more like the moths that live in the moss outside the hall. But forty-three generations — more, possibly, since the warm stable conditions of the hall allow multiple broods per year — have selected for something else. The moths that live here now are darker, thicker-bodied, slower in flight but more precise in landing. They can cling to a surface vibrating at forty-two cycles per offset rhythm without being thrown off. Their feet are different — the tarsi are longer, more hooked, adapted to grip iron and brick and the mineral-hardened surface of the engine moss. They are a new thing. They are still becoming a new thing. They are evolution at a rate visible to anyone who pays attention, though no one at Braemoss is paying attention to the moths.

They do not leave the hall. The doors are open during the day, the ventilation gaps in the walls let in air and let out the drone, and the moths could fly out into the bowl, into the moss and the sedum and the open sky. They do not. They stay in the hall, in the noise, in the green light, in the warm damp air that is exactly the temperature of their bodies. They are held here not by walls but by the fact that the hall is the world that made them. Outside is a different world. They do not go.

## The Mosses, Considered as Inhabitants

It is a mistake to think of the moss as decoration. It is a mistake to think of the moss as growth. The moss at Braemoss is not growing on the buildings. The moss is *living in* the buildings the way the workers live in the dormitory — as a resident, as a presence, as something that has claimed a space and maintains it.

The engine moss has already been described. It is pale, thin, unnamed. It lives nowhere else. It is the newest species here, or near-newest, and it is still finding its shape. Under the lamps, in the constant vibration, it spreads across the iron beams in a thin film that is almost not there — a faint pale haze, like breath on a cold window, but green. It does not build up into thick cushions the way the sphagnum does outside. It does not need to. The moisture here is constant; it does not need to store water against dry periods because there are no dry periods. It has traded the moss's usual strategy — hold everything, release slowly — for something leaner, something more like the factory's own logic: take what you need, pass the rest through, keep moving.

The sphagnum in the hollows is older. Sphagnum is always older, in the sense that it is the moss that builds the peat, and the peat is the substrate on which everything else stands. The sphagnum at Braemoss has been here since before the Works, since before the bowl was called a bowl, since before there was anyone to call it anything. It is the reason the water stays. It is the reason the peat exists. Every cellular layer of sphagnum that has lived and died in this bowl for the last ten thousand years is compressed beneath your feet, and the sphagnum that is alive now is genetically continuous with the sphagnum that was alive then, not the same plants but the same *colony*, the same organism extended in time, a single living thing that is ten thousand years old and still growing.

It does not think. It is not aware. But it is *persistent* in a way that resembles intention. It holds water. It acidifies its surroundings. It builds the ground it grows on. It does not do these things for the Works, but the Works benefits from them, and the Works in turn maintains the conditions the sphagnum requires — the wet, the warmth, the steady draw of water that keeps the water table high and the peat saturated and the surface perpetually damp. The sphagnum does not know about the engines. The engines do not know about the sphagnum. But they are partners. They have been partners for eighty-seven years, which is a short time in the life of a moss colony and a long time in the life of a machine, and they are growing toward each other, each year slightly more adapted, slightly more compatible, the engines settling into the peat and the peat rising around the engines and the sphagnum filling the gaps with itself.

The other mosses — the feather moss on the higher ground, the haircap near the foundations, the rust-orange iron-eater on the brickwork — are residents of shorter standing, or at least of less consequence. They fill niches. They do what mosses do. They hold. They persist. They do not drive the system the way the sphagnum does, but they are part of its texture, part of its sound, part of the way the place feels underfoot and in the air. Without them the bowl would be soggier, rawer, less complete. They are the difference between a place that works and a place that *is.*

## The Fungal Networks

And then there are the fungi, which are not mosses and not plants and not animals but something else, something that lives in the spaces between things and connects them.

The mycelium in the timber beams has already been described — the networks that have filled the cellular structure of the wood and replaced it with a mineral lattice that is stronger than the original. But the mycelium does not stop at the beams. It extends into the brick mortar, into the gaps between the foundations and the peat, into the peat itself, where it interlocks with the root systems of the moss and the squitch and the cotton grass and forms a network that is not a root system and not a fungal network and not a bacterial colony but a *composite*, a living matrix that is all of these things at once, woven together so thoroughly that separating them would kill them.

This network is the real infrastructure of Braemoss. The engines draw water, yes. The peat holds water, yes. But the network *distributes* water. It moves moisture from wet areas to drier areas. It moves nutrients from the composting wick-bundles to the growing plants. It moves chemical signals — the slow, chemical language of roots and fungi — from one side of the bowl to the other. When the workers lay the spent wicks in their long rows to compost, the network finds them within days. White threads appear at the edges of the dark fabric, fine as spider silk, and spread inward, and the wicks begin to break down, and the tannins and the iron and the oily residue move through the network into the peat and back into the moss and back into the engines. The cycle is not closed by the machinery. The cycle is closed by the fungi.

There is no single fungal organism here. There are many. But they have fused, over decades, into something that functions as a single system, the way a city functions as a single system despite being made of many people and many buildings and many overlapping networks. The fungal mat beneath Braemoss is the city. The moss is its streets. The engines are its industries. The workers are its — what? Its visitors. Its migrants. Its short-term residents who come and go and do not understand that the place they are tending is not a factory but an organism.

No one at Braemoss knows this. No one has dug into the peat and traced the networks and mapped the connections. They do not need to. They tend the Works. They tend the engines. They change the wicks and clear the channels and harvest the squitch and eat the trout and sleep in the damp and wake to the drone and do it again. The network beneath them does what it does. It does not require their understanding. It requires only that they keep the engines running and the wicks fresh and the outflow clear, and they do, and it does, and the place holds.

## The Rowan

The rowan tree on the chimney is the smallest living thing at Braemoss that the workers name individually. They do not call it "the rowan." They call it *the tree*, as though it is the only one, as though it has earned the definite article through sheer improbability.

It should not be there. The substrate in the chimney crack is shallow — perhaps thirty centimeters of accumulated debris, wind-blown dust, and composted moss, held in place by the brickwork and the constant moisture. The roots cannot go deep. They have gone sideways instead, down the outside of the chimney in a network of thin dark tendrils that grip the mortar lines and spread across the north face of the stack like veins on a hand. The tree is supported as much by the chimney as by its own roots. It leans slightly, always has, toward the south, toward the light, and the workers have twice installed a small iron brace to keep it from leaning further. The brace is rusted now and held in place more by the moss that has grown over it than by the bolts, which have loosened.

It berries every autumn. Small, bright, sour berries that the workers eat in the kitchen with the dark bread and the tea. They taste of the place — of tannin and iron and the warm sweetness of the peat smoke from the kitchen stove. The workers eat them and do not remark on them. The berries are part of the place the way the drone is part of the place, the way the damp is part of the place. You do not remark on what is always there.

The rowan is forty years old, or thereabouts. It will not live to be a hundred. Its substrate is too shallow, its roots too constrained, its growth too slow. But it has lived forty years in a chimney crack in a peat bog in a bowl where the air never dries and the sound never stops, and it has borne fruit every year, and it has become part of the Works in a way that no one planned and no one maintains and no one will mourn when it finally goes, because mourning is not how this place works. This place works by *incorporating.* When the rowan dies, its wood will be colonized. Its roots will become substrate. Its minerals will enter the network. Something else will grow in the crack, or nothing will, and the chimney will stand, and the engines will run, and the drone will continue.

## The Trout

The trout in the outflow channel are small and dark and not remarkable. They are brown trout — *Salmo trutta* — and they are there because the outflow channel is a slow, clear, oxygenated watercourse at a temperature that brown trout can tolerate, and they colonized it from the lowland streams that the outflow feeds into, swimming upstream through the moss and the sedum and the reeds until they found the settling pools and stayed.

They eat the insects that fall into the water. The insects are few — the sundew takes most of them, and the moths stay inside — but enough midges and gnats breed in the slower edges of the channel to support a small population. The trout do not grow large. The largest the workers have seen was perhaps twenty centimeters, and that was years ago. Most are twelve to fifteen. They are enough.

Pirr catches them with his hands, which is possible because the water is clear and shallow and the trout are not fast in the cold. He waits at the edge of the settling pool where the outflow begins and puts his hands into the water and stays still until the trout forget his hands are there, or until the trout accept his hands as part of the environment, which is a different thing, and then he closes his fingers around the fish and lifts it out and it is dinner. The other workers use a net. The net is faster. Pirr does not use the net. Pirr says the fish taste different when they are caught by hand. No one has tested this. No one argues.

## The Place Itself

And then there is the thing that lives here that is not any of these things but is all of them.

You could call it an ecosystem. That is the word for it, technically. A community of organisms and their physical environment functioning as a unit. But that word is too clean, too diagrams-and-arrows, too much like something you could draw on a whiteboard and understand by looking at it. What lives at Braemoss is not understandable by looking at it. You have to stand in it. You have to let the drone into your chest and the damp onto your skin and the warmth through your boot soles and the sweet rot into your sinuses and then you have to stand there long enough that you stop being a visitor and start being a presence, another small warm body in the system, another input and output, and then — not immediately, not in minutes, but in hours, in the slow time that the place runs on — you feel it.

It is alive. Not the way a tree is alive, not the way a man is alive. The way a reef is alive. The way a forest floor is alive. The way a coral reef is alive, which is to say: made of living things, built by living things, maintained by living things, and functioning as a single entity at a scale and a timescale that no individual living thing within it can perceive. The Works at Braemoss is not a factory in a bog. It is an organism that has a factory in it, the way your body has bones in it — structural, functional, integrated, necessary.

The engines are organs. The moss is tissue. The peat is memory. The fungal network is the circulatory system. The workers are — not the brain. The workers are the hands. They do the maintenance the body cannot do for itself. They change the wicks, which is to say they replace the cells that have worn out. They clear the channels, which is to say they keep the vessels open. They tend the loom, which is to say they build the new cells. They do not direct. They do not decide. They *tend.* And the body continues.

It does not know them. It does not know anything. It is not conscious. It is not aware that it is alive, if it is alive, which is a philosophical question that the workers do not ask because philosophy requires conversation and conversation requires silence and silence is the one thing Braemoss does not provide. But it *persists.* It maintains itself. It adjusts. When the rainfall drops, the peat holds. When the wicks degrade, the engines slow and the output drops and the outflow narrows and the moss at the eastern edge of the bowl grows drier and the peat compresses slightly and the water table rises fractionally and the engines speed up again and the output returns. It self-regulates. Not through feedback loops designed by engineers, though the engines were designed by engineers. Through feedback loops that have *emerged* from the interaction of engines and moss and peat and water and fungi and weather and time.

This is what lives at Braemoss. Not a creature. Not a community. A *process* that has become a place. A cycle that has become a body. A sound that has become a silence, in the sense that the workers who live in it no longer hear it, in the sense that a heartbeat is not heard by the heart that makes it.

The workers live in it and are part of it and do not know it and do not need to know it. The moths live in it and are adapted to it and are becoming something new within it. The moss lives in it and is the substance of it. The fungi live in it and are the connection of it. The trout live in it and are the proof that it reaches beyond itself, that the outflow goes somewhere, that the water drawn from the air and the soil and the peat becomes a stream that becomes a river that becomes part of the world that is not this bowl, that is dry and loud and fast and full of silence.

Everything here is slow. Everything here is wet. Everything here is part of everything else. And the engines run, and the drone continues, and the water rises, and the moss grows, and the moths fold their wings against the iron, and Aldous puts his hand on the fourth cylinder and closes his eyes and feels the cycle, and the rowan leans south, and the peat remembers, and the place lives.
