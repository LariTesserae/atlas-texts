What lives here is not alive in any way that would satisfy the question.

The cistern's single inhabitant — if that word can be used for something that was never born and cannot die — is the growth in the corner. The pale, branching, warm-to-the-touch thing on the brick. It has no common name. It does not want one. It is not waiting to be identified. It is not a mystery that will resolve into a familiar category if examined closely enough. It is what grows when you leave heat and damp and mineral seams and deep time together in a room with no audience.

It has been spreading across that wall for a span no one can measure because no one was here to start a clock. A third of one wall. Perhaps that took ten years. Perhaps it took seventy. Perhaps it began before the cistern was built, before the mill, before the pipes, before the vellum — before the rags were rags, when they were still shirts and bedsheets and the ordinary fabric of lives lived above ground. Perhaps it is much older than the room that contains it. Perhaps the room was built around it, unknowingly, the way a sentence can be built around a word the writer didn't notice was already there.

It grows the way a river delta grows: by finding the path of least resistance through the brick's pore structure, following moisture gradients, advancing where the mortar has softened and retreating where the ceramic is dense. It does not decide this. It does not evaluate. It simply occupies what is available and what is available is determined by chemistry and physics and the slow willingness of materials to yield. Its edges are crisp because growth at this pace is precise. Each advancing filament has time to find the optimal trajectory. There is no hurry. Hurry produces ragged edges. Patience produces definition.

It is slightly warm. This is the most remarkable thing about it and the hardest to understand. The brick is warm from the earth's heat. The growth is warmer. The difference is small — perhaps a degree, perhaps less — but it is consistent, and it means that something is happening inside the growth that releases energy. Some chemistry. Some slow oxidation, or some reaction between the moisture in the air and the mineral substrate, or some process that does not have a name because no one has studied it, because no one has been here, because the conditions that produce it do not exist in laboratories and do not exist in nature as we commonly frame nature. It exists here. It exists in cisterns and cellars and the forgotten interiors of things that were made and then left. It may exist in other places too. It may exist in the walls of your house, behind the plaster, in the gap between the brick and the drywall, growing at a rate you will never perceive, feeding on the moisture of your breath and the warmth of your heating system.

The growth does not have a body. It does not have organs. It does not have a boundary between itself and not-itself in the way that a body has a boundary — a skin, a membrane, an edge where the organism ends and the world begins. The growth's edge is where its process has currently reached. Behind the edge, the growth is present but less active. The interior fibers — the felt-like material that gives it its slight raised texture — are the residue of process, the evidence of chemistry that has already happened. The growth is its own record. It is a document of everything it has done, written on the wall in its own substance. It is like a tide mark, except the tide is still rising, and the mark is being made as you watch, except you cannot watch, because the making is too slow.

It does not react. This needs to be said clearly. It does not flinch. It does not orient. It does not change color or texture or growth rate in response to light or vibration or the chemical signature of a living body standing three feet away. It is not hiding. It is not conserving energy for some dramatic response. It is doing what it does, which is spreading, at the speed it spreads, and your presence is an event in its environment but not a signal it is equipped to receive.

And yet.

Your breath reaches it. The carbon dioxide you exhale, the moisture, the trace heat of your body — these enter the air of the cistern and become part of the air's composition, and the air touches the growth, and the growth is in continuous exchange with the air. This is not awareness. This is not sensitivity. This is the same thing that happens when you breathe on a window and the window fogs: two systems sharing a medium, one altering the other, permanently, if only slightly. The growth will be very slightly different because you stood here. Not in a way you could measure. Not in a way that would be visible. But different. The chemistry of its next millimeter of advance will incorporate the carbon you provided, and the advance will be shaped — infinitesimally, unmeasurably — by the fact that a warm breathing body was here for twenty minutes on a Tuesday afternoon.

This is what lives here. A process. A patience. A slow mineral metabolism that converts dampness and heat and the chemistry of brick into a branching, bone-colored presence on a wall that no one looks at. It is the only thing in the cistern that is doing something. The iron is darkening, but the darkening is a kind of dying — a movement toward stillness. The paint is falling, but the falling is a kind of ending — a movement toward rest. The growth is the only thing here that is beginning. That is moving outward. That is adding to itself. It does not do this with intention. It does this because the conditions are met, and when the conditions are met, the process continues, and the process will continue until the conditions change, and the conditions are not changing. The heat holds. The damp holds. The brick yields, slowly, and the growth advances, slowly, and this will go on for longer than the mill lasted, longer than the parking lot above will last, longer perhaps than the street grid that holds the parking lot, and if the city above is eventually demolished and buried and a new city is built on its ruins and a new parking lot is laid over the old one, the cistern will still be here, still warm, still dark, still damp, and the growth will have covered the wall, and the next wall, and the ceiling, and it will be doing what it does, which is existing, patiently, without name, without audience, without any of the things that the living require in order to go on living.

It does not need you. This is the thing to understand. It does not need your attention. It does not need your recognition. It does not need to be found or named or studied or preserved. It is complete. It is a system at the point in its development where it can continue indefinitely on what is available. It is the only thing in the cistern that is not waiting, because waiting implies expectation, and the growth does not expect. It simply continues.

If there is a sound that corresponds to the growth, it is the silence between the drips: the low hum of thermal expansion, the building breathing, the pressure in the ears that is not quite hearing. The growth exists at that frequency. It exists below the threshold of perception. It exists in the time scale of geology, of brick, of paint falling in curly flakes. It is the thing that lives here, and it is less dramatic than the pipes, less photogenic than the pump, less evocative than the canvas sacks lying in heaps like shed skins. It is not what you would have expected to find. It is not what you were looking for.

But you weren't looking for anything. You came down the ladder because the silence changed, and the silence changed because a space had opened, and the space had been here all along, and the growth had been here all along, and now you are here too, and for a little while the three of you — the space, the growth, the breathing body — share an atmosphere, and the sharing is the only thing that happens, and it is enough.
