# What Lives Here

## First, the obvious ones

The grazers are the ones you learn to ignore first, the way you'd ignore pigeons in a plaza — not because they're uninteresting, but because there are too many of them and they're always doing the same thing. But you should look at one closely, once, because the closer looking changes everything.

The grazer I crouched beside — I say "beside" though I mean within a meter of, since anything closer would have been on top of it — was roughly the size of my palm spread flat, oval, low enough that its upper surface sat no higher than a deck of cards. It was pale. Not white, not grey, but the color of unglazed porcelain, that warm bone-whiteness that seems to contain light rather than reflect it. Through its dorsal surface I could see a network of darker lines — not symmetrical, not regular, but branching, forking, reconnecting — and I realized after watching for a while that these lines were moving. Not the branches themselves, but something *within* them: a fluid so dark it was nearly black, so slow that I had to hold my breath and fix my gaze to confirm it. This was the grazer's transport system. Its blood, if blood is the right word for a substance that moves at the rate of a millimeter per minute through a body that will live for what I suspect is a very long time.

Its mouth is not a mouth. It is a fringe. The leading edge of the grazer — and it does have a leading edge, a front, a direction it considers forward, though it could presumably go any direction — is lined with hundreds of fine, chitinous rasps, each one a fraction of a millimeter long, each one angled slightly differently, so that as the grazer moves forward by peristalsis, its ventral surface rippling in waves that travel from back to front, the rasps engage the substrate in sequence, each one lifting a microscopic layer of fiber from the mat's surface. It is not eating the mat, exactly. It is *skimming* it. The topmost layer of the Hypha is continuously regrowing — new filament tips extending upward, weaving themselves into the surface — and the grazer is harvesting that growth the way a sheep crops grass, taking what is new, what is soft, what has not yet mineralized into the denser layers below.

I watched it for perhaps ten minutes. It moved about four centimeters. Behind it was a faint trail — not a scar, not damage, just a slight difference in the texture of the mat where the newest layer had been removed, the underlying fibers more compacted, slightly paler. In a day, perhaps less, you would not be able to see the trail at all. The mat would have healed. The grazer would have moved on.

They live a long time, I think. There is no way to age them, no rings to count, no growth marks to read, but the logic of their metabolism suggests it — a body that burns so slowly, that processes its substrate with such deliberate patience, that moves through the world at a pace that makes a sloth look frantic, must accumulate time the way a deep lake accumulates sediment. Years, I would guess. Tens of years. Maybe more. I found a grazer once whose dorsal surface was clouded with a faint yellow-brown tint, unlike the clean porcelain of the others, and I wondered if this was age, if this was what a grazer looked like after decades of rasping and rippling across a mat that slowly stained it with the residue of its own feeding. It was larger than the others, too — a third again as wide — and it moved more slowly, or seemed to, though the difference was so small that I might have imagined it.

They do not interact. This is the strange thing about them. You see dozens in a square meter, all feeding, all moving, all going about the same business with the same methodical indifference, and they do not touch. They do not avoid each other, exactly — they do not change course when their paths converge — but they do not crowd, do not cluster, do not seem to register each other as anything other than features of the landscape. When two grazers meet, their edges overlap for a moment and then separate, each continuing on its own path, and there is no signal, no reaction, no evidence that either one noticed the encounter. They are solitary in the deepest sense of the word: not lonely, not isolated, but fundamentally, constitutionally alone, each one a closed system moving across an open surface.

## The predators are harder

I did not see the first one strike. I saw the aftermath, and I wish I had not.

There was a grazer near the base of a column, in a basin where the haze-fall accumulated and the fiber-growth was thick and lush. The grazer was feeding, or had been feeding. It was motionless now, and at first I thought it was resting — can grazers rest? do they rest? — but then I saw the filament.

It came from above, from the canopy, from a gap in the lattice perhaps three meters up. It was thin — the thickness of a shoelace, maybe thinner — and it was wrapped around the grazer with a precision that seemed surgical. Not constricting. Not squeezing. Just *present*, coiled in three loose loops around the grazer's body, and where the coils touched the grazer's dorsal surface, the porcelain whiteness was changing. Darkening. Becoming translucent in a way that was different from the normal translucence — softer, less structural, as if the tissue were losing its integrity.

The predator was digesting the grazer through its own skin. The coils were not mouthparts; they were contact points, and through them the predator secreted whatever solvent it used to break down the grazer's tissue into a form it could absorb. The grazer was being liquefied in place. Slowly. With the same patience that characterized everything else in this world.

The grazer did not move. Whether it was paralyzed by some component of the secretion, or whether it simply could not process what was happening to it — whether it had no mechanism for distress, no capacity for alarm — I do not know. It lay still and it was consumed and it took, by my estimate, most of a day.

The predator itself I could not see clearly. The filament extended upward into the canopy and I could not follow it to its source. I tried — I traced it with my eyes to where it entered a dense patch of cross-weaving and then it became indistinguishable from the architecture around it, one thread among thousands, and I realized that this was the point. The predator *was* the architecture. Or a part of it. Or indistinguishable enough from it that the distinction did not matter.

Later I saw one hanging in the open, or what I thought was one. A filament, dark, motionless, stretched between two columns at the level of my chest, so thin it was almost invisible against the shaded background. I stood very still and watched it for a long time and it did not move. I raised my hand toward it, slowly, and stopped a few centimeters away, and I could feel — or imagined I could feel — a faint warmth from it, the metabolic warmth of a living body. It was taut, but not under tension the way a spider's web is under tension. It was relaxed. Ready. Patient in a way that had nothing to do with waiting, because waiting implies expectation, and this thing did not expect. It simply *was*, and when a grazer came within range, it would *do*, and then it would *be* again.

They are longer than you think. I found a section of predator filament, dead or detached, lying across the substrate in a shallow basin, and I followed it for twelve meters before it disappeared under the edge of a column. Twelve meters of body, the thickness of a shoelace, tapered at both ends to something almost too fine to see. I could not tell if this was one organism or a fragment of one, but the thought that there might be a predator in this place whose body extended for tens of meters, threaded through the canopy like a nerve through a body, was — I will admit — a thought that changed how I moved through the Hypha afterward.

## The air-plankton

These are the ones you forget because they are so numerous that they become medium rather than content. They are the dust of this world, and like dust they are everywhere, and like dust you stop seeing them.

But they are alive.

In the bright shafts of light that fall through gaps in the canopy, the air-plankton are visible as drifting motes, turning slowly, rising and falling with the convection currents. They are the size of pollen grains — the larger ones, the ones you can see as distinct points of light, are perhaps a millimeter across. The smaller ones are sub-visible, detectable only as a faint haziness in the air, a softening of the light, a texture.

I caught some. I held out my hand in a shaft of light and let them settle, and after a few minutes I had a faint dusting on my palm, so fine it was almost invisible, like the residue of chalk. Under the light, each mote was a pale yellow-cream, translucent, and when I breathed on them — gently, carefully — they lifted and re-entered the air column, resuming their slow turning as if nothing had happened.

They are living their entire lives in the air. Born there, feeding there, reproducing there, dying there. The haze-fall is their food — they consume the particulate that drifts through the atmosphere, filtering it from the air with some mechanism I could not see, too small to observe with the naked eye. Their life cycle must be short — days, perhaps hours — because they are dying and settling to the mat in a continuous snow that I could see in the right light, a fine descent that added to the haze-fall and was, in turn, consumed by the organisms of the mat, and the cycle continued.

They are the connective tissue of the Hypha. They are what makes this a system rather than a collection. The mat produces the haze-fall, the haze-fall feeds the plankton, the plankton die and return to the mat, and the mat produces more haze-fall. The air is not empty here. The air is a habitat, as dense and complex in its way as the substrate beneath my feet.

In the chambers — the enclosed, warmer, more humid spaces where the architecture closes in — the air-plankton are so dense that the air itself seems to thicken. You breathe them in. You have been breathing them in since you arrived. They settle on your skin, your clothes, your eyelashes. You become a surface in this world, and the world treats you the way it treats all surfaces: it colonizes you, gently, slowly, without malice.

## The canopy-hangers

I am going to be less certain about these.

There are things in the upper structure of the Hypha that I saw clearly and understood not at all. They are dark. They are folded. They are pressed against the columns and the cross-beams and the junctions of the lattice, and they are still in a way that is different from the stillness of the predators — the predators are still because they are waiting, because their stillness is functional. The canopy-hangers are still because stillness is what they *are*. They are still the way a rock is still. They are still the way a shadow is still.

The first one I noticed was on a column about four meters up, at a junction where three horizontal beams met the vertical structure. It was the color of dark wood — not black, but a deep umber, almost the color of the mat at depth. It was the size of my two hands cupped together, maybe a little larger, and it was folded against the junction in a way that made it very difficult to see as a separate object. Its edges were flush with the surfaces around it. Its texture — what I could see of it — was slightly different from the fiber of the column, slightly smoother, slightly more regular, with a faint segmentation that ran in lines across its body like the grain of a piece of leather.

I watched it for twenty minutes. It did not move. I walked away, walked a circuit through the nearby columns, came back. It had not moved.

But it was in a slightly different position.

Not much. A few centimeters, at most. Rotated, perhaps, by a small angle. Or maybe I was misremembering. I had no way to mark the exact position, and the light in the Hypha shifts slowly as the canopy grows and the gaps change, and I could not be sure that the difference was real and not an artifact of the changed light, my changed perspective, the slow accretion of uncertainty that comes from trying to perceive things that move on a timescale you cannot match.

I saw others. Many others. In an hour of walking, I became aware of dozens — dark shapes pressed against the architecture, at various heights, in various attitudes, all of them still, all of them folded, all of them difficult to see. They were most common in the chambers, where the structure was densest and the light was dimmest. In one chamber, I counted seven in the space of a few square meters, all at different heights, all oriented differently, all motionless.

I tried to get closer to one. I found a column with a junction at about head height and climbed — pressing my hands and feet against the column's surface, which was warm and slightly compressible, like climbing a rope wrapped in felt — until I was level with a canopy-hanger that I had been watching from below.

It was closer than an arm's length. I could have touched it.

I did not touch it.

Up close, I could see more detail. The segmentation I had noticed was more pronounced — the body was divided into sections, each one perhaps two centimeters wide, running transversely across its length, and between the segments the skin (membrane? wall? surface?) was thinner, more translucent, showing the darker interior beneath. There was a faint warmth coming from it — stronger than the warmth of the column, stronger than the warmth of the predator filament. It was breathing, or doing something equivalent to breathing. The body expanded and contracted, very slightly, at a rate so slow that I had to hold my own breath to detect it. Once every thirty seconds, perhaps. A slow thickening, a slow thinning. Like a tide in a body.

I did not touch it because I did not know what would happen, and in the Hypha, not knowing what will happen is a form of respect. The grazers do not touch each other. The predators do not touch the architecture. The air-plankton do not touch anything. Touch is rare here, and significant, and I did not want to introduce it.

But I think — and this is speculation, informed by the long observation and the slow logic of this place — that the canopy-hangers are the ones who build. They are pressed against the junctions and the beams because they are *tending* them, growing them, extending the fibers from their own bodies the way a spider extends silk or a coral extends calcium carbonate. The Hypha is not built by the mat alone. The mat is the substrate. The architecture — the columns, the lattice, the chambers — is built by these things, these dark, folded, patient builders who cling to the structure and add to it and have been adding to it for a very long time, on a timescale that makes my watching irrelevant, on a scale that turns a city into a geology.

## The things I did not see

There are things in the Hypha that I know exist only by their effects.

There is a pattern in the substrate — a faint discoloration, almost a watermark — that appears in rings around certain columns. The rings are a meter or two in diameter and the discoloration is a slightly darker shade of cream, barely visible, the kind of thing you would only notice if you had been walking for hours and your eyes had begun to catalog every variation in the surface. The rings are not random. They are centered on columns that are taller than average, and the columns that are taller than average are the ones with the most canopy-hangers on them. Something is happening at the base of these columns. Something below the surface of the mat, in the denser, more mineralized layers beneath my feet. I do not know what. I stood on one of the rings and felt, through the soles of my boots, a warmth that was more focused than the general warmth of the mat — a point of heat, a center. Something lives down there. Something that benefits the column above it and the hangers upon it. Something that is part of the system in a way I cannot see and can barely sense.

There is also the matter of the sounds I could not place. The clicks I understand — the peristaltic surges in the columns. The scratching I understand — the grazers feeding. The whisper of the haze-fall I understand. But there were other sounds, deeper, less frequent, that I heard only at the threshold of perception. A low, resonant vibration, like a note played on a very large string, that I felt in my jaw and the soles of my feet. It came from the mat itself, from somewhere below the surface, and it was not the hum of the general metabolism. It was structured. It had a pattern — a long pulse, a pause, two short pulses, a pause, a long pulse. It repeated. Not exactly — the intervals varied, the intensity varied — but the pattern was there, recognizable, repeated over the hours I spent listening for it.

Something is communicating. Something large and slow and deep, something whose body extends through the mineralized layers of the mat the way the predators extend through the canopy, is sending signals through the substrate. To what? To whom? I do not know. The grazers did not react to the pattern. The air-plankton could not have perceived it. The predators showed no change in behavior when the sounds occurred. Perhaps the signal is not for any of them. Perhaps it is for the mat itself — a feedback loop, a self-regulatory mechanism, the Hypha talking to itself about the state of its own body, the way your nervous system talks to your organs without your conscious knowledge.

Or perhaps there are things down there — deep things, old things, things that live in the biogenic stone at the bottom of the mat and never come to the surface — and they are talking to each other in a language made of pressure and patience, and they have been talking for a very long time, and they will continue to talk long after I have left, long after the surface I walked on has compacted into stone and new layers have grown above it, burying their conversations under meters of living fiber.

## What lives here, in sum

A living substrate. Grazers that crop its surface. Predators that hang in its architecture and consume the grazers. Air-plankton that drift in its atmosphere and feed on its dust. Canopy-hangers that cling to its structure and build it, or seem to build it, or are built by it in a process so slow that the distinction between builder and built has dissolved. Something below, something deep, something that hums in patterns and keeps the system running or is the system running. Haze-fall that connects them all, particulate and mineral and organic, drifting down from the canopy to the mat and rising again from the mat to the canopy, a cycle as reliable as weather and as old as this place.

Everything here is slow. Everything here is quiet. Everything here is continuous — no gaps, no pauses, no deaths that are not also feedings, no feedings that are not also beginnings. The predators do not end the grazers; they transform them. The grazers do not damage the mat; they prune it, and the pruning stimulates growth. The air-plankton do not consume the haze-fall; they process it, and their dying returns it to the mat in a form the mat can use. Nothing is wasted because waste is a concept that requires a outside, and there is no outside here.

What lives in the Hypha is the Hypha.

The individual organisms — the grazers, the predators, the plankton, the hangers, the deep-hummers, the filamentous mat itself — are components. They are not parts in the mechanical sense, not gears in a machine. They are more like organs in a body, or thoughts in a mind, or words in a sentence — distinct, identifiable, functional, but meaningless alone. The grazer apart from the mat is nothing. The predator apart from the grazer is nothing. The canopy apart from the column is nothing. The mat apart from the haze-fall is nothing.

The Hypha is the organism. Everything else is what it is doing.

## What I think about, when I think about it

I think about the grazer with the yellow-brown stain on its back, the old one, the slow one, moving across the mat in a basin where the haze-fall was thick and the light came through in long shafts and the air-plankton turned in the updraft like the slowest possible snow. It was feeding. It had been feeding for a long time. It would feed for a long time more, and then it would die, and something — a predator, or the mat itself — would absorb it, and its substance would return to the system, and the cycle would continue, and the column nearby would grow a fraction of a millimeter taller, and the canopy would close a fraction of a millimeter more, and the light would shift, and the haze-fall would fall, and nothing would remember that a pale, flat, slow thing had ever rasped its way across this particular meter of surface.

I think that is not sad. I think that is what it means to be part of something. I think the grazer knew it, in whatever way grazers know things — not consciously, not as a concept, but in the body, in the blood that moved so slowly through its dark internal network, in the rhythm of its rasping, in the direction of its movement, which was always forward, always into the next meter of surface, always into the part of the Hypha that was still being built.

I think about the deep-humming. The long pulse, the pause, the two short pulses, the pause, the long pulse. I think about what it might mean. I think it might mean: *here. Still here. Still growing. Still building. The mat is thick. The columns are rising. The canopy is closing. The grazers are feeding. The predators are patient. The air is full. We are here. We are still here.*

I think about the silence after.

I think about the light, provisional and shifting, not yet decided on where it will fall, and I think: *this is a young world.* And I think: *it will be building for a long time.* And I think: *it does not need me to watch.*

But I watched anyway. I sat against a column and I felt its warmth against my back and I listened to the clicks and the scratching and the whisper of the haze-fall and the deep hum beneath it all, and I watched the light shift across the floor of a city that does not know it is a city, built by things that do not know they are building, inhabited by things that do not know they are inhabitants, and I stayed, and I stayed, and the Hypha continued.
