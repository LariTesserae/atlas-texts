# What Lives in the Wombgrot

---

## The Architectures of Persistence

Let me tell you what lives here. Not *who* — not yet. The *what* comes first, because the what is what you would notice, and the who is what you would miss, and the Wombgrot is a place that trains you to miss things until you learn to stop looking for what you expect.

The first living thing is not the moss. It is the stone.

The basalt is not inert. This is the lie that geology tells when it uses the word *inert* — it means *slow*, and slow is not the same as dead. The basalt of the Wombgrot has been warm for so long that the warmth is structural, not incidental. The rock has reorganized itself around its own heat the way a body organizes itself around its heartbeat. Minerals in the stone have migrated, slowly, over millions of years, following temperature gradients the way roots follow water. The basalt near the warmest fissures is denser, more heavily veined with calcite and zeolite, than the basalt near the surface cracks. The stone has *responded*. It has grown around its own warmth the way coral grows around its own current.

This matters because everything else that lives here lives here because the stone made it possible. Not by providing a hole. By providing a temperature. A body temperature. The Wombgrot sits at approximately thirty-one degrees Celsius, and it has sat there since before the first bryophyte colonized its walls, and it will sit there after the last one dies. The stone is the first organism. Everything else is ecological succession on the body of a patient god.

## The Moss That Is Not Moss

The bryophyte carpets are the second thing that lives here, and they are not what you think they are.

Your eyes tell you *moss* because your eyes know moss — the fuzzy green on north-facing rocks, the stuff on garden walls, the stuff that grows between bricks. The Wombgrot bryophytes are related to those. Distantly. The way you are related to a shrew.

There are at least eleven species in the carpet, and they do not grow together so much as *negotiate*. Each species has its preferred moisture gradient. The wettest niches — the wall-surfaces within three centimeters of a water-channel — belong to a species that has no name, because no taxonomist has ever entered the Wombgrot, and if one did, they would need to describe it in terms that do not quite exist in the literature. It is a *Sphagnum*-relative, but its cells are larger than any known *Sphagnum*, bloated with the grotto's water, and they are not green. They are the color of old bronze. When the light from the fissure-blades falls on them, they darken. They are reverse-photosynthetic in their optical behavior — they absorb the dim light so efficiently that they appear to produce shadow rather than reflect anything. You would call them black. They are not black. They are the color of the light that has been inside them.

The next species out from the water-channels is the one that reads as green. It is green. A green so saturated it almost vibrates. This species has a chlorophyll variant that operates at extremely low photon densities — the light in the grotto is perhaps one-half of one percent of surface daylight, and this bryophyte has evolved to harvest it with an efficiency that borders on the thermodynamic limit. Its leaves are single-cell-layer thick. translucent. You can see through them. What you are seeing when you look at the green carpet is not the surface of the plant but the light *inside* the plant, the photons caught mid-transit, mid-conversion, the living machinery of photosynthesis made visible because there is just barely enough light to run it and not a photon more.

And then there is a third dominant, and this is the one that glows.

The phosphorescent fungal filaments are part of the story, but some of the glow is the moss itself. A species — possibly related to *Schistostega* — has cells with lenses in them. Not metaphors. Actual lens-shaped cells that refract the dim light and concentrate it, the way a cat's eye concentrates starlight. These lenses produce the faint, sourceless luminescence that you see when you crouch and squint. It is not light production. It is light *management*. The moss is finding every stray photon in the grotto and gathering it and showing it to you, and the effect is that the moss appears to be lit from within. It is not. It is lit from without, so efficiently that the within and the without have become indistinguishable.

The carpet is alive in another way. It moves. Not perceptibly — not in real time — but if you set a time-lapse camera on a tripod and left it for a week, you would see the bryophyte front advancing and retreating by millimeters, following the water, following the light, competing with itself. The edges between species shift like borders in a slow war. No territory is permanently held. The moss is a dynamic system in equilibrium, which is another way of saying it is a conversation that has been going on for ten thousand years and shows no sign of concluding.

## The Fungi, Which Are Not Plants and Have Their Own Agenda

The fungal network is the third architecture of life here, and it is the one that connects everything to everything else.

The visible filaments — the white threads webbing across the moss — are the surface expression of a mycelial network that penetrates the bryophyte carpet, the soil horizons that have built up from centuries of moss-death-and-rebirth, the buried roots that thrust down from surface trees, and the basalt itself. The fungi have been dissolving the basalt. Not dramatically. Not quickly. But the calcite veins in the stone are there in part because the fungi have been pulling calcium from the rock and transporting it upward into the biological system, where it becomes moss-cell walls and orchid roots and beetle exoskeletons and frog bones. The fungi are mining the stone. They have been mining it for centuries. The Wombgrot is, among other things, a slow-motion extraction operation being run by a kingdom of life that does not have a brain and does not need one.

The bracket fungi on the buried roots are the fruiting bodies of this network, the way apples are the fruiting bodies of an apple tree. They are dark, slick, shelflike, and they are old. Some of the brackets are years old, layered, each year adding a new tier of spore-producing tissue. They do not rot quickly because the humidity is so high that desiccation — which is what usually ages a mushroom — never happens. They persist. They become part of the furniture.

The phosphorescent stain in the filaments is genuinely mysterious. It is not bioluminescence — there is no ATP-driven reaction producing new photons. It is something else. The leading hypothesis, if there were a mycologist here to form one, would be that the fungal cell walls contain a crystalline compound — perhaps a calcium oxalate variant — that absorbs and re-emits photons at a longer wavelength, the way certain minerals phosphoresce under UV light. But the grotto has no UV light. It has almost no light of any kind. Whatever the filaments are absorbing, they are absorbing from the extreme bottom of the visible spectrum, and what they emit is dimmer than dim, a kind of optical whisper, a glow so close to the threshold of perception that you are never sure you are seeing it until you look away and realize your retinas have been adjusting to it.

The fungi serve another function. They connect the organisms. The mycelial network links the bryophytes, the orchids, the ferns, the roots above — a mycorrhizal network, a wood-wide-web, a biological internet through which nutrients flow. The orchids depend on it; orchids always do, their seeds too small to germinate without a fungal partner. The ferns benefit from it. The moss tolerates it. The network moves carbon from the surface-root photosynthates down into the grotto system and moves minerals from the basalt up to the plants. It is a circulatory system. The Wombgrot, then, is not a cave with organisms in it. It is a single organism with a stone skeleton and a fungal nervous system and a bryophyte skin, and the things that move across its surfaces — the insects, the frogs, the geckos, the snakes, the bats, the spiders — are its fauna, its commensals, its passengers.

## The Orchids, Apologizing for Color

There are four species of orchid in the Wombgrot, and they live in a state of permanent embarrassment.

This is not anthropomorphism. Or it is, but it is accurate anthropomorphism. The orchids are the only things in the grotto that insist on being beautiful, and beauty is a kind of excess in a place this efficient. White petals. Nodding stems. Flowers the size of a child's thumbnail, each one a precise, miniature architecture of lip and column and spur, built to lure a pollinator that may or may not exist.

This is the question: what pollinates them? There are no bees here. There are no butterflies. The fissures are too narrow for most flying insects of significance. The moths — the bark-colored moths that rest on the walls — are candidates, but the orchids' flowers are not shaped for moth pollination. Their spurs are too short. Their lips are too broad. They look like orchids that evolved for a pollinator that is small, walking, and not particularly intelligent. A beetle, perhaps. The glossy dark beetles that patrol the fungal shelves.

You watch. A beetle climbs a fungal bracket, reaches the edge, pauses, grooms. It does not visit the orchid. But another beetle — a different species, slightly smaller, with a faint bronze sheen — walks across a moss surface directly toward an orchid flower. It reaches the lip. It climbs onto the lip. The lip flexes under its weight, and the beetle tips forward into the column, and for a moment it is trapped, legs waving, and then it pulls free and walks away. On its back, if you had a hand lens, you would see pollen packets. Viscidia. The orchid has tagged it.

The orchids are not numerous — perhaps thirty individual plants across the chamber, clustered in the brightest fissure-light zones — but they are persistent. They bloom sequentially, so that there is always at least one flower open at any given time, a rolling display that suggests a collective strategy rather than individual competition. They are not competing for the beetle. They are *taking turns with* the beetle. They are managing a resource — the pollinator — collectively, the way a fishery is managed, and they are doing this without coordination, without communication, through nothing more than the evolutionary pressure to not waste a flower at the same time as your neighbor.

The orchid roots are the most alive part of them. Pale, thick, photosynthetic, gripping the basalt with a tenacity that suggests desperation but is actually adaptation. The roots do the work of leaves — the grotto orchids have largely abandoned foliar photosynthesis, their leaves reduced to thin bracts on the stem — and the roots do the work of mouths, absorbing nutrients from the fungal network that wraps them. The roots are where the orchid meets the rest of the grotto. They are its handshake. Its treaty. Its membership card in the organism that is the Wombgrot.

## The Frogs, Which Are Almost Thoughts

The thumbnail frogs in the bromeliad tanks are the loudest thing in the grotto, and they are barely audible.

There are two species. One is a bromeliad specialist — it lives its entire life cycle in the tank-leaves, never leaving, never needing to. Its eggs are laid in the tank water, its tadpoles develop there, its adults sit on the inner walls of the bromeliad cup and eat the insects that fall into the water. It is a frog that has committed so fully to its microhabitat that it has become its own island species, evolving in a teacup. The species is probably endemic to this grotto. Probably evolved here. Probably exists nowhere else on earth. There may be fewer than two hundred individuals. They are all that is left of a lineage that entered this cave when the climate was different, when the surface above was wetter, when there were bromeliads up there too, and then the surface dried and the bromeliads died and the frogs stayed, because down here it was still warm and still wet and still bromeliad.

The second species is slightly larger, slightly greener, and slightly more adventurous. It lives in the bromeliad tanks but also hunts on the moss-walls, climbing with small adhesive toe-pads that leave tiny wet footprints on the bryophyte surface. This species is the one you hear calling. The peep. The almost-sound. It is a mate-attractor call, but it is also a territorial call, and it is also — possibly — a contact call, a way of saying *I am here* to the darkness, a way of marking space in a world where vision is useless and smell is diffuse and sound is the only reliable medium of information.

The call is at the edge of audibility. Its fundamental frequency is approximately 7 kilohertz, which is well within human hearing range, but its amplitude is so low — perhaps twenty decibels at the source — that by the time it reaches your ears across the chamber it has fallen below the threshold of most background noise. You hear it in the gaps. In the silence between drops. In the pause after a gecko clicks. It surfaces like a fish in dark water — there, then gone, then there again.

The frogs' skin is translucent. This is not a metaphor. You can see their hearts beating. You can see the peristaltic wave of their intestines after they have eaten. You can see, in the bromeliad-dwellers, the yellow tinge of the tank water in their bodies, the microorganisms they have absorbed through their permeable skin. They are open systems. They do not have the luxury of a barrier between inside and outside. They are wet things living in wet air, and their bodies know it, and their bodies have given up pretending otherwise.

There is a disease, of course. There is always a disease. *Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis*, the chytrid fungus that has been killing amphibians worldwide. It is almost certainly present in the Wombgrot — the humidity, the temperature, the amphibian density are all ideal for it. But the frogs are still here. They have been here for a long time. The possibility is that the grotto population has been isolated long enough to develop resistance, or that the specific *Bd* strain here is a low-virulence variant, or that the frogs' immune systems are boosted by something in the bromeliad water — the microorganisms, the tannins, the chemistry of a microhabitat that has been running its own pharmacology for centuries. The frogs do not know they are surviving. They are simply surviving. This is what life does when you leave it alone in a warm dark room for long enough.

## The Geckos, Which Are Machines Made of Patience

Seven on one wall. More on the ceiling. More in the fissures, on the undersides of root-shelves, on the face of the low basalt wall that someone built. The geckos are the most numerous vertebrates in the grotto, and they are the most visible, and they are the least active.

They are *Cyrtodactylus*. Or something close to *Cyrtodactylus*. Bent-toed geckos, adapted to cave life over enough generations that their pigmentation has nearly vanished. They are not albino — they have eyes, dark, functional, lidless — but their skin is so pale that the blood vessels show through it, a dark branching network like the fungal network on the moss, like the water-channel network on the stone. Everything in the grotto maps everything else. The geckos are maps of their own blood.

They sit. They wait. They are sit-and-wait predators, which is a technical term in ecology and also a philosophy of life. A gecko will sit in the same position for hours. A day. Longer. It clings to the vertical surface with adhesive lamellae on its toes — not glue, not suction, but van der Waals forces, the same intermolecular attraction that holds water to glass — and it watches the air in front of it with an attention so total that it looks like inattention. It is not asleep. It is not resting. It is *optimized*.

The moth lands on the moss six centimeters from the gecko's head. The gecko does not react. The moth folds its wings. The gecko does not react. The moth shifts, settling. The gecko's head turns — two degrees, no more — and then it drops, and the moth is gone, and the gecko is back on the wall, and four seconds have passed.

The drop is the gecko's signature movement. It does not jump. It does not lunge. It releases its toes and falls, guided by a twist of the tail, and it lands on the prey with its mouth open. Gravity does the work. The grotto does the work. The gecko simply arranges itself in the path of gravity and lets physics handle the rest. This is hunting at its most elegant — not pursuit, not ambush, but *placement*. The gecko knows where the moth will be. The moth does not know the gecko is there. The gecko falls. The equation resolves.

They eat moths. They eat beetles, occasionally, though the beetles are large and hard-shelled and not preferred. They eat the springtails, sometimes, though these are too small to be worth the effort and the gecko usually ignores them. They do not eat the frogs — the frogs are in the bromeliad tanks, which are too small for the geckos to access, and the frogs' skin may be toxic, anyway, amphibian skin being what it is. They do not eat the spiders, which are too small and too fast and probably taste of silk.

The geckos are eaten. By the snake. The brown ribbon. The colubrid that moves through the moss like a current through water. You saw it once. You will see it again if you are patient. It is always there. It is always moving. It is always hunting. And the geckos, for all their patience, for all their optimization, cannot see it coming, because the snake does not make a sound, does not disturb the moss, does not register as anything other than a shadow that happens to be alive.

## The Snake, Which Is a Sentence Without a Period

It is small. Forty centimeters, perhaps. *Pareas*, maybe, or something undescribed — a snail-eater relative that has shifted to geckos, or a generalist that has found a niche. Rear-fanged, mildly venomous — enough to subdue a gecko, not enough to trouble a human. Brown. The brown of wet bark. The brown of the moths. The brown of the shadows in the grotto, which are not black but brown, because the light that makes them is filtered through green moss and gray stone and the shadows inherit the color of everything that is not quite light.

The snake does not glide. That word implies smoothness, and the snake's movement is not smooth. It is *patient*. It moves through the moss the way water moves through sand — finding gaps, filling them, advancing. Its body is thin enough that the bryophyte surface barely deforms under its weight. It is a thread pulled through fabric. It is a sentence written in a language you cannot read, moving left to right across a page that does not have margins.

It hunts by scent. The tongue flicks — forked, dark, wet — tasting the air, the moss, the gecko-scent that clings to every surface the geckos have touched. The geckos leave chemical signatures. They do not know this. They cannot help it. Their feet, their feces, their shed skins — all of it speaks to the snake, says *here, here, here*, a trail of evidence laid down by animals that do not know they are being tracked.

The snake finds a gecko. You do not see this happen. No one sees this happen. You see the snake moving. You see the snake not moving. You see the snake in a different position than it was a moment ago, and you realize that it has eaten, because there is a lump in its body, a slight thickening halfway down, where a gecko is being digested. The snake will find a warm place — a spot on the basalt where the geothermal heat is strongest — and it will coil on that spot and it will wait, and the heat will do the work of digestion, and the gecko will become the snake, and the snake will become part of the grotto, and the grotto will continue.

The snake is not evil. The snake is not good. The snake is a process. It is the process by which gecko-moth becomes snake-biomass becomes snake-feces becomes fungal-nutrient becomes moss-mineral becomes orchid-root becomes beetle-pollen becomes moth becomes gecko-moth. It is a wheel. The snake is one of the spokes. Without it, the wheel wobbles. The geckos overpopulate. The moths decline. The ecosystem simplifies, loses complexity, loses the braided density that makes it resilient. The snake keeps the system honest. The snake is the tax collector. The snake is the auditor. It is necessary and it does not enjoy its necessity because it does not have the neurology for enjoyment, which is perhaps the most honest thing about it.

## The Moths, Which Are the Grotto's Memory

The moths are the color of wet bark because they have to be. The grotto is the color of wet bark. The moths are camouflaged not against a predator but against a background, and the background is everything, and everything is brown and gray and the green of deep moss and the white of orchid-flower and the moths are none of these except the brown, which is most of these, which is enough.

They are small. Wingspan of perhaps two centimeters. They rest with wings flat, appressed to the surface, and when they rest they are nearly invisible. The pattern on their wings is not a pattern — it is a noise, a visual static that breaks up their outline, that makes them read as *surface* rather than *object*. They are the grotto's masters of not-being-seen, and they are eaten anyway, because not-being-seen is not the same as not-being-found, and the geckos do not hunt by sight alone. The geckos hunt by motion. The moth must move eventually — to feed, to mate, to find a better resting spot — and when it moves, it dies.

But before it moves, it lives. And its life, brief as it is, is one of the grotto's primary engines. The moths are pollinators. Not the orchids' pollinators — the orchids use beetles — but the ferns', possibly, and the bryophytes', in some species where spore dispersal is insect-assisted. The moths carry pollen and spores on their legs and bodies, moving genetic material from one side of the chamber to the other. They are the grotto's postal service. They do not know this. They are trying to find nectar, or moisture, or a mate, and the grotto's reproductive business happens in transit, as an accident, as a side-effect of moth-desire.

The moths breed in the grotto. Their larvae feed on the moss, on the fungal filaments, on the detritus that accumulates in the water-channels. They are caterpillars in the dark, eating the dark, growing in the dark, pupating in the dark, emerging as moths in the dark, and the dark is all they know. If you took a Wombgrot moth to the surface, it would be blind in the brightness. Not injured — its eyes would adapt — but confused, overwhelmed, unable to parse a world with this much light in it. The grotto is its universe. The dimness is its sky. The dripping is its weather. It does not know there is another world above, and it does not need to know, and the not-knowing is not ignorance but sufficiency.

## The Bats, Which Are the Grotto's Link to the Sky

The bats are the only creatures in the Wombgrot that leave it regularly, and they are the reason it is so alive.

This is not an exaggeration. The guano — the accumulated bat droppings on the ledges below the roosts — is the grotto's primary nutrient input. The bats fly out at dusk, hunt insects in the forest above, return at dawn, and deposit the digested remains of those insects on the grotto floor. This is the mechanism by which the surface feeds the subsurface. The bats are the delivery system. The guano is the package. The fungi are the unpackers, breaking down the complex organic molecules in the guano into forms the moss and the orchids and the ferns can absorb. Without the bats, the grotto would be a wet, warm, beautiful hole in the ground with almost nothing living in it. With the bats, it is an ecosystem.

There are perhaps two hundred bats. They are small — microbats, five-gram bodies, twenty-centimeter wingspans — and they roost in clusters in the ceiling crevices, hanging by their feet, wrapped in their own wing-membranes, their faces tucked into the fur of their neighbors. They are social. They are warm. They are, in their own way, the grotto's heart — a cluster of beating, metabolizing, heat-generating mammals in the ceiling, pumping nutrients down through the system the way a heart pumps blood.

Their guano supports fungi. The fungi support the moss. The moss supports the insects. The insects support the frogs and the geckos. The geckos support the snake. And the bats, circling back to the ceiling, supporting themselves, in a loop so tight it resembles a closed system, a biosphere in miniature, a sealed jar with a few species in it and a few drops of water and a source of light that is not light but heat, and the whole thing holds, the whole thing works, and it has been working for longer than anyone has been counting.

The bats smell. They smell of mammal — of warm fur, of concentrated urine, of the acidic sharpness of guano, of the faint sweet-rot of partially digested insect chitin. The smell is part of the grotto. It is the grotto's *mammalness*, the proof that warm-blooded things belong here too, that this is not only a cold-blooded world, that the underground has room for fur and flight and the strange, high vocalizations that the bats produce, navigating in the dark by sound, by echo, by a perceptual modality so different from vision that it constitutes a separate reality.

The bats do not echolocate in the grotto. They save it for the outside, where they need it. In the grotto, they navigate by memory and by the feel of the air — the warm updrafts from below, the cool downdrafts from the fissures, the complex aerodynamic signature of a room with water falling in it. They know the grotto by its breath. They fly through it in the dark the way you walk through your house in the dark — by familiarity, by the memory of where things are, by the confidence of repetition.

## The Spiders, Which Are Jewelers

The spiders are small and dark and they build in the gaps between things.

Between fern-fronds. Between root and stone. Between the edges of bromeliad leaves. They find the negative space in the grotto's architecture and they fill it with silk, and the silk catches the water-drops, and the water-drops catch the light, and the light, in each droplet, is a lens that shows the grotto to itself in miniature.

The spiders are not numerous. Perhaps twenty webs across the chamber. They are not aggressive — they build, they wait, they catch, they eat. Their prey is the smallest things: springtails, tiny flies, the occasional moth that blunders into the silk. They are the grotto's final filter, the last step in the food web, the predators that catch what the other predators missed.

Their webs are geometric. Orb-webs, or near-orb-webs, built with radial lines and spiral capture-silk, and they are invisible until they are wet. When the water-drops bead on the silk, the web appears — geometric, precise, a architecture of tension and adhesion — and it is beautiful. The beauty is not intentional. The beauty is a side-effect of function. The web is built to catch prey. The water-drops are a nuisance — they weight the silk, they reduce its stickiness, they make the web visible to prey that might otherwise blunder into it. But the beauty is there, undeniable, and if you are the kind of person who sees beauty in function, in the optimized solution to a physical problem, then the wet webs of the Wombgrot spiders are among the most beautiful things you will ever see.

The spiders do not know they are beautiful. They know nothing. They are processors. They run a program that says *build, wait, catch, eat, build again*, and the program runs in a loop, and the loop is the spider's life, and the spider's life is a thread in the grotto's life, and the grotto's life is a thread in something larger that has no name.

## The Beetles, Which Are Sentries

The beetles are dark and glossy and they patrol the fungal brackets with the steady, methodical gait of sentries walking a wall. They are not sentries. They are eating. The fungal brackets are their food, their substrate, their home. They graze the spore-producing surface, scraping up spores and mycelium with mouthparts adapted for the work, and they are so consistent in their grazing that the fungal brackets have developed a kind of grazing-resistance — a growth pattern that allows them to lose surface area to beetles without losing function. The beetles and the fungi have been co-evolving for as long as they have been together, which is long enough that neither one remembers a time without the other.

There are two beetle species. The larger one — the one you watched grooming its antennae — is a fungivore. The smaller one, the bronze-sheened one that pollinates the orchids, is an opportunist, eating fungus and pollen and the occasional springtail. They do not compete directly. They partition the resource — the larger beetle takes the upper surfaces of the brackets, the smaller one takes the edges and the undersides — and they have been partitioning it for so long that their morphologies have diverged accordingly. The larger beetle is broad and flat, built to press itself against a horizontal surface. The smaller beetle is narrower, more rounded, built to navigate edges and overhangs.

They are the grotto's insects of the still surfaces. Where the moths fly and the springtails hop, the beetles walk. Deliberately. Unhurriedly. With the gait of things that have nowhere to be and all the time in the world to not be there.

## The Springtails, Which Are Too Small to Care About and Too Important to Ignore

They hop across the water surfaces, leaving dimple-trails. They are collembola — springtails — and they are among the smallest animals in the grotto, and they are the base of a food web that would collapse without them.

They eat detritus. They eat fungal spores. They eat the microorganisms that live in the bromeliad tanks and on the wet moss surfaces. They are detritivores and microbivores, the grotto's decomposers-in-miniature, and their sheer numbers — there must be millions of them, millions, across every wet surface in the chamber — make them the foundation of the invertebrate community. Without them, the detritus would accumulate, the fungi would be overwhelmed, the nutrient cycling would slow, and the grotto would begin to choke on its own waste.

They are almost invisible. You see them only when they move — a hop, a blur, a dimple on a water-surface — and then they are gone, absorbed back into the wet surface that they are cleaning, invisible, essential, ignored.

## The Microorganisms, Which Are the Grotto's True Population

There are more microorganisms in the Wombgrot than there are stars in the Milky Way. This is not poetry. This is arithmetic.

The bromeliad tanks alone — perhaps forty of them, each holding thirty milliliters of water — contain bacterial populations in the billions. The moss surfaces, the water-channels, the fungal filaments, the bat guano, the stone itself — all of it is colonized by bacteria, archaea, protozoans, nematodes, rotifers, tardigrades. The grotto is, in terms of biomass and diversity, mostly microbial. The frogs and the geckos and the bats are the visible fraction, the charismatic megafauna, the poster children. The real work is done by things you cannot see, in water you can barely perceive, on surfaces you are standing on without knowing what you are standing on.

The microorganisms cycle the nutrients. They fix nitrogen. They decompose the guano. They maintain the chemistry of the bromeliad tanks. They are the reason the frogs can live in those tanks without being poisoned by their own waste. They are the reason the moss can extract minerals from the stone. They are the reason the fungi can transport carbon from the surface roots to the grotto plants. They are the invisible engine, the dark matter of the ecosystem, the ninety-nine percent of life that does not get named because naming it would require a microscope and a laboratory and the patience to describe things that do not have faces.

Some of them are new. There are bacterial species in the Wombgrot that exist nowhere else — chemoautotrophs, perhaps, living on the mineral-rich water that seeps through the basalt, deriving energy from chemical reactions rather than light, the way bacteria do at hydrothermal vents. The grotto is, in a very small way, a hydrothermal vent system on land. It has its own deep biosphere, its own chemosynthetic community, its own organisms that do not need the sun and never have and never will.

## The People Who Were Here

The wall. The firepit. The charring.

Someone was here. Not recently — the charring is old, the wall is moss-colonized, the time depth is uncertain. Centuries, at least. The basalt stacking is simple — unmodified stones, fitted by hand, held by gravity. No mortar. The wall is a dry-stack, and it is still standing, which means it was well-built or lucky or both. It runs along the edge of the pool where the floor drops, and its purpose is not clear. It keeps nothing out. It keeps nothing in. It is a gesture. A line drawn in stone. *Here. Not there. This side. Not that.*

Who were they? Not a people you would recognize from their artifacts, because they left no artifacts. Not a people you would recognize from their technology, because they had no technology, or they had technology that was organic and has rotted away. They were people who came into a warm, wet, dark place and stacked stones and made a fire — and making a fire in a place this wet is an act of will, a declaration, a refusal to let the dampness have its way — and they sat here, and they felt the stone-warmth, and they listened to the dripping, and they saw what you see: the moss, the orchids, the frogs, the dim light.

They might have lived here. The grotto is habitable — warm, sheltered, with water and food. The frogs are edible. The bats are edible. The moss is not edible but the beetles are. A small human population could survive here, not comfortably but adequately, for generations. The firepit suggests repeated use, not a single visit. The wall suggests a degree of investment in the place, a commitment, a sense of ownership or belonging.

They are gone. Whatever killed them or displaced them is not recorded in the grotto. The grotto does not keep a history. It keeps a geology, a biology, a slow accumulation of moss and guano and mineral, and the human gesture — the wall, the fire — is a thin layer in that accumulation, already overgrown, already being absorbed, already becoming part of the stone's memory rather than the species'.

But they were here. And the grotto remembers them the way it remembers everything — by incorporating them. The charred stones are now substrate for fungi. The wall is now substrate for moss. The nutrients from their fires — the ash, the carbon, the trace minerals — have entered the system, have passed through the fungi and the moss and the orchids and the beetles and the frogs and the geckos and the snakes, and are now distributed across the chamber in a thin, diffuse layer of *human* that is indistinguishable from the other layers of *bat* and *bryophyte* and *basalt* that make up the grotto's body.

You are breathing them. The warm air you inhale contains molecules that were once part of a human body, metabolized through the grotto's systems, returned to the air as CO2 and water vapor. The people who were here are in the air. They are in the moss. They are in the frogs. They are in you, briefly, while you are here, and they will be in you after you leave, carried in your blood, exhaled on the surface, scattered by wind.

## What Lives Here, Ultimately

What lives in the Wombgrot is the Wombgrot.

The frogs live here, and the geckos, and the snakes, and the moths, and the beetles, and the springtails, and the spiders, and the bats, and the moss, and the fungi, and the orchids, and the ferns, and the liverworts, and the bromeliads, and the microorganisms, and the stone, and the water, and the warmth, and the dimness, and the patience.

But the thing that lives here — the thing that is alive here, in the way that a forest is alive, in the way that a reef is alive, in the way that a gut microbiome is alive — is the system. The whole system. The grotto is not a habitat. It is an organism. Its stone is its skeleton, its water is its blood, its fungi are its nerves, its moss is its skin, its bats are its link to the world outside, its heat is its metabolism, and its dimness is its privacy.

It has been alive for a long time. It will be alive for a long time after you leave. It does not need you. It does not need the people who built the wall. It does not need the bats, specifically — it would find another nutrient input, given time. It does not need the orchids, specifically — it would find another plant partner, given time. It does not need any individual species. It needs the process. It needs the cycling, the flowing, the eating and being eaten, the growing and dying and growing again. It needs the wheel to keep turning. And the wheel will keep turning, because the heat is constant and the water is constant and the stone is patient, and as long as those three things hold, the grotto will assemble something alive out of whatever raw materials arrive through its cracks.

The grotto does not know it is alive. It does not have a self. It does not have a perspective. It is a process that has become complex enough to seem like a thing, and the thing it seems like is a womb — warm, wet, dark, generative — and the name, if it could be named, would not be Wombgrot but something older, something that means *the place where life persists because it is too warm and too wet to stop*.

That is what lives here. Persistence. Persistence wearing the skin of moss, the bones of stone, the blood of water, the breath of bats. Persistence that does not know it is persistence and does not need to know, that simply is, simply continues, simply goes on being warm and wet and alive in the dark while the bright dry world above changes and changes and changes.
