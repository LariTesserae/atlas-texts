# What Lives in the Mat

The first thing to understand is that "who" lives in the Mat is a difficult question, because the Mat's whole character is the dissolution of the boundary between resident and residence, between dweller and dwelling. The place does not contain inhabitants so much as it *is* its inhabitants — ten thousand species in a weave so thorough that the question "what lives here?" answers "everything, as one thing."

But that is the easy answer, and this place deserves better than easy answers. Because there are distinctions here, if you look, and the distinctions matter.

---

## The Mat Itself

The mat is not an organism. This must be said first, because the temptation is enormous. Standing on it, feeling it give beneath your feet like the skin of something patient, you want to believe you are standing on a single creature — some vast flat animal playing at being a landscape. But the mat is a community, a parliament, a negotiated settlement ten thousand signatories deep. The shingle-organism is not the velvet-organism. The velvet does not know the shingles exist. What looks like unity from above is, from within, an eternity of small arrangements: *you take the light side of this border, I'll take the dark; you make the sugar, I'll make the shade; you grow toward the seep, I'll grow away from it.* The centimeter-wide transitional zones — those diminished borders, those edges of resolved arguments — are the only proof you have that these are separate entities at all. And even that proof is ambiguous. Some of those borders may be where two growths of the same species meet and simply fail to recognize each other, each treating the other as foreign. Even the Mat does not always know where it ends and its neighbors begin.

What the mat has, that no individual organism has, is *time*. Or rather, the mat has solved time. It has achieved what no single living thing can achieve: zero net change. Each organism within it is still bound to the ordinary tragedies of living — growth, exhaustion, senescence, the composting of its own lower layers — but the *ensemble** has arranged these tragedies against each other so perfectly that they cancel. One organism's death is another's substrate, absorbed at exactly the rate it is offered. Nothing accumulates. Nothing is lost. The mat as a whole is not alive in the way its members are alive; it is something else, something for which we lack a good word. A *standing equilibrium*. A shape that time flows through without displacing.

And yet — and this is important — the mat is not wise. It did not *choose* this state. There was no conference of rhizomes, no treaty of rootlets. The equilibrium is an emergent accident of ten thousand organisms each greedily doing precisely what they are built to do, their greeds canceling into stillness. The Mat is what you get when competition is played out to its absolute end, when every strategy has been tried and every counter-strategy answered and what remains standing is not the winner but the *balance* — the configuration in which nobody can improve their position without destroying the whole, and the whole is the only thing keeping the ceiling from being just rock and the floor from being just stone.

The mat is the only thing in this world that has never been young.

---

## The Ceiling: The Light That Grows

The bioluminescent mat overhead deserves its own reckoning, because it is in some sense the Mat's *other* — its mirror, its complement, the thing it is not and cannot be without.

The floor mat builds. It accumulates tissue, thickens, threads downward, fractures stone, holds water, manufactures soil from raw geology. It is an accumulating organism, a hoarder of substance. The ceiling mat does the opposite. It produces nothing that lasts. Its entire output is light — light and a little warmth — and light cannot be stored, cannot be banked, cannot be woven into structure. Every photon the ceiling makes is spent the instant it is made. The ceiling mat is a *pure expenditure*, an organism whose whole existence is a continuous act of giving-away.

And the economy between them is perfect, which is the only kind of economy this place permits. The floor mat consumes light — the shingle-organism especially, each dinner-plate leaf oriented to catch the diffuse golden glow and convert it to sugar, which it secretes, which feeds the velvet, which feeds the deeper roots, which thread into stone and fracture it, which releases minerals, which feed everything, which sustains the ceiling, which makes the light. It is a circle drawn with no gap, and you, walking through it, are the only leak. Your body heat, your exhaled breath, the faint oils of your skin — you contribute a little, take a little, and are not woven in. You are a word outside the grammar.

The ceiling mat's relationship to the floor mat is not parasitism, not symbiosis in any directed sense. They are not *for* each other. But they have become each other's condition. If the ceiling went dark, the floor would have to learn a new economy or die. If the floor died, the ceiling would starve for the minerals the root-web delivers. They hold each other in existence the way two people falling lean against each other and neither can stand without the other's weight. It is not love. It is not even mutual benefit, exactly, because neither of them experiences benefit. It is just what remains when ten thousand generations of dying have left only the configurations that couldn't fall.

The translucent mite that lives on the bioluminescent panels — that single creature, alone of its kind, immune to the light, permanent — should be mentioned here. It has found the one niche in this entire place that nothing else wanted: the surface of an organism that radiates continuously and asks nothing in return. The mite does not eat the light. It cannot. Nothing eats light except through the alchemy of leaves. The mite eats something else — perhaps the faint organic film that condenses on the panels, perhaps nothing, perhaps it has slowed its own metabolism to a pace where the boundary between eating and not-eating has itself become a technicality. It is the closest thing this place has to a philosopher. It sits in permanent light and consumes almost nothing and moves almost never and will eventually die and be absorbed and its nutrients will join the slow circulation and some trace of its body will, in fifty thousand years, end up in a sugar bead on the tip of a velvet stem, and nothing will know, and this is the Mat's version of an afterlife.

## The Pale Larval Grazer

The segmented creature working the border zones — the soft, pale, too-many-segmented thing eating its way through the transitional strips — is the closest thing the Mat has to an animal citizen, and it deserves careful attention, because it reveals something about how this place handles *movement*.

It eats borders. That is its entire function, and it is a function the Mat needs. The border zones — those centimeter-wide strips where two organisms grow in diminished form — are the Mat's scar tissue, its compromise zones, its history of negotiation made physical. But scar tissue that is never remodeled becomes permanent, and permanence, in an equilibrium, is a kind of slow poison. A border that is never eaten never gets the chance to be renegotiated. The grazer keeps the borders alive by keeping them fresh — it consumes the compromised tissue, forcing both sides to regrow the boundary, to re-argue it, to reaffirm or revise the terms of their separation. The trail of bare substrate it leaves is re-colonized within minutes, and what grows back is not always what was there before. Sometimes the shingle-organism advances a fraction of a millimeter. Sometimes the velvet does. Sometimes a third species — one that had been waiting, suppressed, at the margin of the argument — takes the fresh ground and holds it.

The grazer is the Mat's editor. It is the only agent in the whole system that introduces change, and it does so by the smallest possible increment: a mouthful of border at a time. Without it, the Mat would still be in equilibrium, but it would be a *brittle* equilibrium — perfectly balanced, yes, but frozen in one configuration forever, unable to adapt if the thermal seep shifted, if the light dimmed, if anything at all disturbed the balance. The grazer keeps the Mat *practiced* at change. It is the reason the equilibrium is stable rather than merely static. It eats the peace in order to keep the peace renewable.

It is, of course, unaware of any of this. It is a creature of perhaps two dozen neurons, whose entire phenomenological world is: *edge here, food here, move forward.* That the Mat depends on its function does not mean it participates in the Mat's meaning. It is the only thing in this place that moves with purpose and the last thing in this place that could be called purposeful.

## The Thing on the Ceiling

The second creature — the flicker, the shape that might have wings, the movement that was there and then was not — cannot be described, only acknowledged. The Mat holds one secret, and this is it.

Understand what it means that you did not see where it went. The ceiling is fifteen meters of luminous growth with no shadow, no crevice, no direction to the light. Anything that moves there is visible from everywhere, always, unless it is moving in a way that the light conspires to hide — unless, perhaps, it *is* the light, or lives within the light, or moves along paths the bioluminescent growth itself conceals. Or unless it saw you first and is very good at not being seen, which raises the question of *why*. In a place where nothing hunts, where nothing is hunted, where the only threats are geological and operate on timescales of millennia, what pressure produced a creature that can vanish in a shadowless room?

Perhaps it does not vanish. Perhaps it *decides*. Perhaps it is the only thing in the Mat that can choose when to be perceived.

It might be a pollinator. It might be a relic — the last of whatever made the frames, or a descendant of it, reduced now to a flicker. It might be many, and you saw one. It might be the Mat's single concession to mystery, the one element that refuses the place's total legibility, the character in an otherwise finished sentence who will not sit still to be read.

Whatever it is, it is the only thing in the Mat that knows you are here and did not need to.

## The Frames, and Whoever Made Them

The frames are the Mat's only history, and they are a history almost entirely lost.

Black wood, smooth, skeletal — door frames with no doors, the bones of small sheds, standing at regular intervals in the broad chambers like the vertebrae of some large structure that has otherwise been digested. They are dead, and they are the only dead things here, which makes them precious in a way that has nothing to do with use. Everything else in the Mat is either alive or in the seamless process of being absorbed — the frames alone are *dead and persisting*, a state the Mat otherwise does not permit. The mat is growing over them at the same rate they are eroding, which means they are being incorporated at a pace so slow it constitutes a kind of respect.

Who made them? The text is almost silent, and this silence is itself information. Consider what the frames require: someone harvested structural stems from a larger plant species that no longer grows here — grown and cut in an earlier phase, before the mat closed over everything. Someone had tools, or at least edges. Someone stood these frames upright in places that do not correspond to dwelling, to shelter, to any use we can reconstruct. Someone did this *before the equilibrium*.

That is the crucial detail. The frames predate the Mat's finished state. They are artifacts of the *becoming*, not the *being* — remnants of a time when this place was still in process, when the ceiling was perhaps only partly luminous, when the floor was perhaps only partly matted, when there were still niches large enough for creatures with hands and intentions. Whoever made the frames lived in the Mat's childhood. They may have been the Mat's gardeners. They may have been its midwives, or its first casualties. The structural stems they cut — the large plant species that once grew here and grows no longer — suggest that the Mat's early phase included giants, and the frames' maker harvested them, and the giants are gone, and the maker is gone, and the frames remain, and the Mat has closed over the whole story like water healing after a stone pulled through.

The frames might be grave markers. They might be the supports for fabric that has long since been eaten. They might be the boundary posts of a territory whose meaning has been composted into the soil itself, so that the meaning persists as mineral and is taken up by roots and built into leaves and exists now only as chemistry. If so, then the frames' maker did not leave the Mat. The maker *became* it. Some fraction of every shingle-plate, some trace in every sugar bead, is the continued presence of the people who stood the frames — not as memory, not as spirit, as *nutrient*. The Mat does not remember them. But it is partly made of them.

And it is growing over the frames at the same rate they erode, holding them in a permanent half-state of being absorbed — which, if you wanted to be poetic about it, and this place makes you want to be poetic about it, is the Mat's way of keeping its makers close without letting them go: never fully consuming them, never fully releasing them, just *keeping* them, at the pace of a held breath.

## The Pools

The pools are the Mat's organs, in something close to the literal sense — not because they are alive, but because they are the points at which the Mat touches something outside itself and brings it in.

Each pool is a seep from below, a slow upwelling from the stone, held in a shallow depression where the mat has declined to grow. The pale collar of absorbent tissue around each pool drinks at exactly the rate the seep supplies. The pool never grows, never shrinks, never ripples — because nothing lands on it, because nothing moves it, because its surface has become a feature of the equilibrium as fixed as the frames. It is the only place in the Mat where the stone beneath is exposed — the only window down into the soft, warm, still-deciding rock — and it is the only place where the Mat's interior is available to the air. The pools are the Mat's *surfaces*, the places where it is thin enough to see through.

That they are perfectly still is not a defect but a design — not designed by anyone, but *settled into* by the same ten thousand years of cancellation that produced everything else here. The pools are where the Mat keeps its water open, and the reason may be circulatory: the collar tissue drinking continuously may be how the Mat moves minerals from the depths to the surface, the way a root system moves water, but here horizontal, distributed, each pool a node in a network of slow intake. The pools are not features *in* the Mat. They are features *of* it — as much its anatomy as the borders, the beads, the light.

And no creature drinks from them. Not the grazer. Not the mite. Not the thing on the ceiling, as far as anyone knows. The pools are perfectly, permanently untouched by everything except the collar that drinks them. They are the Mat's private wells, and the Mat does not share.

## The Strange Absence: The Missing Middle

Here is the thing that must finally be said about who lives in the Mat, and it is the hardest thing to see because it is an absence: *there is no one in between.*

The Mat contains the microscopic and the vast. It contains the bacterial and the geological. But between the mite on the ceiling panel and the mat itself — between creatures you can count on one hand and a system you can only walk across — there is nothing. No herd animals, no burrowers, no flocking things, no predators, no prey. The ecological middle of this world is *empty*.

This is not normal. A place this warm, this wet, this lit, this stable — on the surface, it would be a paradise for animals. It should be *crawling*. That it is not tells you something about how the Mat achieves its equilibrium, and what the equilibrium costs. The Mat's balance is not friendly to the in-between. Anything that moves at animal speed, eats at animal volume, reproduces at animal rates, becomes a destabilizing force, and the Mat — not consciously, but through the blind mechanics of its own balanced chemistry — does not tolerate destabilization. The air is sweet but not nourishing to animal lungs. The sugar beads are rich but positioned to be eaten by roots, not mouths. The fauna that persists is the fauna that found niches so narrow they function as hiding places from the equilibrium itself: one grazer in the borders, one mite on the light, one flicker in the impossible shadows of a shadowless room.

The middle of the food web is empty because the Mat has filled it. The Mat *is* the middle — the decomposers, the primary producers, the detritivores, the entire trophic stack, collapsed into a single horizontal weave. Animals would be redundant here. Animals would be competition. The Mat has, over ten thousand years of patient cancellation, quietly metabolized every ecological role an animal might play and folded it into its own chemistry. What lives here, in the end, is *the Mat, plus its edges*: the grazer at the border, the mite in the light, the flicker above, the makers below, and you, briefly, the largest animal this place has held in ten thousand years, walking across the top of a finished world like a word through a sentence already written, leaving footprints that close behind you in minutes, contributing nothing, taking nothing, remembered by nothing, and gone.
