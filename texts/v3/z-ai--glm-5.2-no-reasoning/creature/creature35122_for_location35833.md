# What Lives at High Sump

## The Small Ones

Begin with the carpet.

When you stand on the causeway, you are standing on them. You don't feel them — they are too light, too fluid in their collective motion — but you hear the dry pour of their bodies against stone, a sound like fine gravel shifting in a paper bag, and you look down, and the ground is *moving*. Not the stone. The layer on the stone. A living glisten, dark and wet, that parts around your boot and closes behind it, and the parting and closing are so quick and so complete that you wonder if you actually disturbed them at all, or if they flowed around the space your boot would occupy before it finished arriving.

They are the size of a thumbnail. Smaller, some of them. The very smallest are the size of a grain of rice, and they are nearly transparent — chitin so thin that light passes through it, and the dark mass beneath is not shell but organ, visible as a dark streak inside a translucent tube. You can see them better when one leaves the carpet and climbs the wall of a dividing barrier. Against the wet basalt, you see the legs — fourteen of them, or sixteen, you lose count, they move too fast, each one a blur, each one finding purchase on wet stone with a precision that seems mechanical, a tiny machine running on a surface it was built for. The body is segmented, three or four sections, and the head is — nothing. There is no head. The front of the body tapers to a point, and the point goes into the stone, into the joint between two basalt blocks, into the crack where the cement has crumbled, and it stays there, for a second, for two seconds, and when it moves on, the stone where it pressed its face is slightly lighter. Slightly cleaner. It took something.

They are feeding. All of them are feeding, all the time. The carpet is a feeding carpet. They feed on what, exactly, is the question, and the answer is: everything that can be reduced. The fog deposits minerals on the stone. The iron fixtures weep their oxidized streaks. The tar compound breaks down in the rain. The cement between the blocks is itself a compound, and compounds come apart, and the small ones take the pieces. They are not scavengers. Scavengers eat what was alive. These eat what was never alive, and they make of it — what? The question is not answered by watching them. They feed, and they grow, and some of them grow larger, and some of them grow differently, and the carpet is always the same density, which means some of them are leaving the carpet, becoming something else, while others are arriving, born from eggs or budded from bodies or assembled by some process that does not correspond to any reproductive strategy you have a framework for.

They do not click. They hiss. The hiss is the aggregate of their legs on wet stone, a million small dry sounds summed into a single texture of sound, and it is the lowest layer of the sound, the bed on which the clicking and the churning rest. If you crouch — and you can crouch, they do not react to you with fear, they flow around your knee, your hand, they climb onto your boot and explore the leather and find nothing reducible and leave — if you crouch and put your ear near the carpet, the hiss resolves into its components, and the components are not uniform. There are rhythms in it, micro-rhythms, surges and lulls, and the surges correspond, if you watch long enough, to the circling of the water in the nearest vat. When the water turns, the carpet surges. The turning feeds something into the stone, or into the fog, or into the air, and the small ones respond.

They have been doing this since before the iron was bolted. You believe this. You believe it because the carpet fits the stone too well, lives in the joints too precisely, has adapted to the architecture too completely to be a recent arrival. They are not in the city. They are of the city. They are its first residents, or its first substance, and everything built here was built on top of what they were already doing, which was: reducing the world to its components, slowly, patiently, a grain of rice at a time.

## The Mid-Sized Ones

They are the ones you think of when you think of the congregation.

The word comes unbidden, and then stays, because it fits. They gather. They stand. They face the same direction. They do not speak — or they speak only in the clicking, and the clicking is not language in any way you can parse, or it is a language so far from yours that parsing is the wrong approach, the way you would not parse birdsong, you would receive it, you would let it be what it is.

They are the height of a large dog at the shoulder, but the comparison is misleading, because nothing about them is dog-like. They are built low and wide, flat-bodied, their carapaces barely a foot above the causeway, and their legs spread outward from the body in a stance that reads as stable rather than sprawled. Six legs, most of them. Some have eight. The extra pair, when present, is smaller, set further back, and does not touch the ground — it hangs, slightly raised, twitching, and you cannot tell if it is a sensory organ or a vestigial structure or something in between, an adaptation mid-way through its arc, not yet committed to usefulness or obsolescence.

The body is a flat oval, wider than it is long, and the carapace is ridged — not heavily, not ornately, but in shallow parallel grooves that run front to back, and the grooves collect water, so that the animal looks ribbed with dark wet lines on a lighter dark. The color is not black. It is a dark olive, or a dark brown, or something between, and it shifts when the animal moves, not with iridescence but with the dull sheen of wet chitin catching flat light at a new angle. The surface is not smooth. At close range — and you can get close, they do not move when you approach — the surface is textured, microscopically rough, like very fine sandpaper, and this texture holds a film of water that does not run off, so that the animal is always wet, not from the fog but from itself, from its own surface, which seems to attract and retain moisture as a function of its structure.

The head. The head is the difficult part.

It is mostly eye. A band of lenses wraps the front of the head from one side to the other, a visor of compound eyes, and the lenses are not uniform — they range from the size of a shirt button to the size of a pinhead, packed tightly, each one a slightly different color, dark amber, dark red, dark grey, and each one wet. Each lens has its own droplet of condensation, and the droplet magnifies what is beneath it, and what is beneath it is a dark movement, a pupil, or the thing that passes for a pupil — a point of attention inside the lens, shifting, tracking. You are being watched by hundreds of eyes, and each eye is watching you slightly differently, from a slightly different angle, at a slightly different magnification, and the aggregate of their attention is not the attention of a predator. It is the attention of an instrument. You are being measured, not assessed. Catalogued, not considered. You are data, and the band of lenses is a sensor array, and the animal behind it is a platform for that array, and the platform is stationed at the rim of the vat because the vat is what it is calibrated for.

Because they watch the vats. This is what they do. They stand at the rim, their legs planted in the wet stone, their eye-band aimed at the surface of the turning water, and they click, and they watch, and they click, and they watch. The clicking is mandibular — two pairs of mandibles, one large, one small, overlapping, working against each other in a rhythm that is individual to each animal but that synchronizes, over minutes, with the animals nearest to it, so that a group of them at one vat will fall into a shared rhythm, a swelling and receding, a tide of sound that corresponds to nothing you can identify. Not to the water's turning. Not to the fog's movement. Not to the surging of the carpet. Something else. Something below, or inside, or distributed through the system in a way that the mid-sized ones can sense and you cannot.

They are monitoring. You become sure of this. Not in the way a human monitors — with intent, with a hypothesis, with a parameter list. In the way a gauge monitors, or a thermometer, or a seismograph. They are instruments pointed at the vats, and the clicking is their readout, and the readout is being heard by something, or processed by something, or at minimum recorded in the vibrations that run down their legs and into the stone and into the coils in the junctions and into whatever system lies below.

They do not eat. You watch one for an hour, and it does not eat. It stands. It watches. It clicks. The mandibles work, but nothing goes in. The mandibles are the mechanism of the clicking, not the mechanism of feeding. This is a repurposing, or an original use that was never feeding at all — mandibles that evolved, or were designed, to produce sound, to produce a specific sound, to produce a specific sound at a specific rhythm in response to specific conditions in the dark turning water. They are tuned. They are calibrated. They are stationed.

When one dies — and you see this, once, near the eastern end of the Sill, where the vats are spaced further apart and the fog is thinner — when one dies, it does not fall. It settles. Its legs fold, slowly, in sequence, and the body lowers, and the eye-band dims, the lenses clouding from the edges inward, and the clicking slows and stops, and the animal is a shell, wet, still, already being climbed on by the small ones, already being reduced. The carpet takes it. Over an hour, the shell is lighter. Over a day, it is fragments. Over two days, it is a stain on the stone, and then not even that. And the other mid-sized ones at the vat do not move. Do not react. Do not acknowledge the absence. Another one will come, climbing up from below, from the terraces, and take the empty station at the rim, and begin to click, and the rhythm will adjust to include it, and the congregation will be complete again.

They are not individuals. Or they are, but only in the way a cell is an individual — functional, replaceable, part of a larger body that does not notice its loss.

## The Large Ones

They are the boats, and they are the geology, and they are the part that makes the word "congregation" feel insufficient.

You do not see the first one arrive. You see it already on the Sill, and you think it is a rock formation, a low protrusion of basalt weathered into ridges and channels, and you walk past it, and an hour later you walk past the same place and it is closer to the vat, and you stop, and you look, and you see the legs. Four legs. Thick as the iron angle-stock of the lattice-towers, folded beneath the body in a compressed crouch, and moving — moving so slowly that the movement is not visible but is inferable, because the leg that was at angle A is now at angle A-plus-one-degree, and the body has shifted one degree forward, and this took sixty seconds.

The large one is the size of a small boat. A dinghy, or a tender. Three meters long, perhaps four, and two meters wide, and its carapace is a landscape — ridged, channeled, contoured, and the ridges are not shallow grooves like the mid-sized ones. They are deep, centimeters deep, cut into the chitin in a pattern that looks deliberate, that looks like the drainage channels on the causeway platforms, and they function the same way: water condenses on the carapace and flows in the channels and runs to the edges and drips off in steady lines, so that the animal is a self-draining surface, a body designed to shed water even as it absorbs it.

The carapace is not the same material as the smaller ones'. It is darker, denser, and it has a mineral quality — not just chitin but chitin suffused with something, silicate perhaps, or calcium, or something drawn from the basalt itself, so that the shell is part organic and part stone, and the seam between the two is invisible, a gradient from living tissue to geological substance. When you tap it — and you do, once, quickly, the way you would tap a wall you are not sure is solid — it sounds like stone. Not hollow. Not resonant. Dense and final, a sound that goes nowhere.

The head is eyeless. This is the thing that stays with you. The mid-sized ones are all eyes. The large ones have none. The head is a smooth, dark, wet surface, rounded, featureless except for a vertical slit at the front, a mouth or a pore, and from the slit, when the animal drinks, a proboscis extends — thick, dark, muscular, the color of the vat-lining material, and it lowers this into the water and the water moves around it, the slow circle parting and closing, and the drinking is not a gulp. It is an osmosis. It is a transfer. The animal is not taking water. It is taking what is in the water, and the water remains, and the circle continues, and the animal stays.

The large ones stay for days. They drink, and then they do not leave. They settle, their legs folding further, their bodies lowering until the carapace nearly touches the stone, and they become features. They become part of the Sill. The small ones climb on them and find nothing reducible — the mineral shell resists the carpet's work, or feeds it so slowly that the feeding is invisible. The mid-sized ones do not click differently when a large one is present. The large ones are not leaders. They are not authorities. They are something else.

They are the interface. You understand this slowly, over days, watching them. They drink from the vats, and the vats are warm, and the warmth is induced, and the water circles, and something in the water is being drawn through the coils and into the stone and into the work below. The large ones drink the same something. They take it into their bodies, and their bodies — their dense, mineral, half-stone bodies — process it, or store it, or distribute it. They climb down the terraces, back into the fog below, and they carry what they have drunk with them, and somewhere down there, in the wet dark, they deposit it, or excrete it, or simply hold it until it is needed, and the cycle continues.

They are pipes. They are living pipes, carrying the substance of the vats to wherever the substance of the vats needs to go. And the vats are not cisterns. The vats are wells, or organs, or glands, and the water is not water, and the large ones are not drinking. They are working. They are the slowest part of the machine, the part that moves at the speed of muscle and mineral, and they have been moving, and working, and climbing, and drinking, for a duration that the word "long" does not describe, because "long" implies a beginning, and you are not sure this had a beginning. You are not sure it has an end.

## The Ones Below

You do not see them. You hear them.

The churning. The wet friction, like cloth wrung by enormous hands. It comes from below the fog line, from the terraced wet stone that falls away on both sides of the Sill, and it is continuous, and it is layered, and the layers are not uniform. There are textures in it — a higher sound, thinner, like taut membrane slipping over taut membrane, and a lower sound, deeper, like heavy bodies in water, and between them a range of sounds that suggest not one kind of thing but many, a whole ecosystem of chitinous, multi-legged, wet-bodied things moving in and through and under water that is not rainwater and not the vat-water but something else, something that has its own source and its own temperature and its own current.

You think about the vats. The vats are lined with a material that is warm and yielding and pulses. The lining is organic, or was organic, or is the product of something organic. The vats are not containers. The vats are the surface expression of something that goes down, that extends into the Sill, into the rock, into the chambers below that you can feel through the soles of your boots as a low vibration. The vats are pores. The vats are the openings of a system that has its body in the stone and its breath in the fog and its blood in the dark turning water, and what lives below is the rest of it.

The churning is the sound of the rest of it working.

Not working in the sense of labor. Working in the sense of function. The way a heart works, the way a kidney works, the way a liver works — continuously, without pause, without awareness, without any sense of effort or complaint. The ones below are the body of the city, and the city is the body of whatever the vats are an organ of, and whatever the vats are an organ of is — you do not know. You cannot know. You are standing on its skin, breathing its breath, listening to its pulse, and it is so much larger than you that the word "large" is a category error. It is not large. It is a context. It is the context in which large and small have meaning, and it is the thing that the mid-sized ones monitor and the large ones serve and the small ones feed on and the fog condenses from and the iron was bolted to support and the stone was cut to house, and it has been here, in this shape, doing this work, for a time that does not correspond to any unit you carry.

The clicking swells.

The clicking recedes.

The water circles.

The large one at the nearest vat lowers its eyeless head and extends its dark proboscis into the turning water, and the turning water takes the proboscis in, and the fog condenses on the carapace and runs in channels to the edges and drips, and the small ones swarm under the drips, and the mid-sized ones click, and below, in the wet dark, the ones you cannot see continue their work, their endless, patient, purposeless-to-you work, and the Sill hums, and the sound is the sound, the only sound, the sound that replaces silence, and it does not stop.

## What They Are

They are not a congregation. A congregation implies a faith, and they have no faith. They have no doubt, either. They have no interior that requires either.

They are not a hive. A hive implies a queen, a center, a directive. There is no center here. The vats are not the center. The vats are a feature, like a spring is a feature of a landscape — an expression of something that has no location, that is distributed, that is the ground itself.

They are not a machine. A machine is built by something external to it, for a purpose external to it. This may have been built — the stone was cut, the iron was worked — but the builder, if there was a builder, is no longer external. The builder is in the stone, in the water, in the chitin, in the hum. The builder and the built are the same substance now, and the purpose, if there is a purpose, is the purpose of a body, not a device: to continue.

They are a continuity. They are a system that maintains itself through the labor of its parts, and its parts are the fauna and the stone and the water and the iron and the fog and the coils and the vibration and the dark material that lines the vats, and the system is the thing that lives here, and the things you can see — the small, the mid-sized, the large, the ones below — are its organs, its cells, its processes, seen at a scale where process looks like individual and individual looks like congregation.

They are alive. They are alive the way a forest is alive — not as a collective of separate lives but as a single living thing that is composed of lives, and the composition is the life, and the lives are the composition, and neither precedes the other.

They do not need you. They do not mind you. They do not mind.

The clicking swells.

The clicking recedes.

The water circles.
