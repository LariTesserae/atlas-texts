# What Lives in the Vast Spongewood

---

## The Makers (An Attempt at Description)

Let us begin with the most difficult thing: the ones who shaped the mounds.

They are not gone. That must be said first, because it is the assumption any visitor would make — that the built structures are ruins, that their makers have perished, that the patterns softening into the substrate are a dead language being slowly erased by the medium that carries them. This is not correct. The makers are here. They have always been here. The difficulty is that *here* does not mean what you think it means.

The makers do not live *on* the spongewood. They live *as* the spongewood. Or rather: the distinction between living on and living as is a distinction that the makers ceased to recognize or require a very long time ago.

Try this. Imagine that the boundary between your body and the world was not a boundary but a negotiation — a slow, ongoing, patient negotiation in which you and the world gradually agreed on where you ended and it began, and over centuries the terms of the agreement shifted, and you found that you had extended yourself into the ground beneath you and the air around you and that the ground and air had extended themselves into you, and that neither of you had lost anything in this exchange but that both of you had become something that did not have a name in any language that still distinguished between self and other.

That is approximately what the makers are. Approximately. The word *makers* is itself a failure, because it implies a separation between the one who makes and the thing that is made, and that separation has been dissolved here as thoroughly as the separation between soil and stone and living tissue has been dissolved. The makers did not build the mounds. They *became* the mounds. They grew themselves into shapes that served purposes — or that expressed intentions — or that were simply what happened when a consciousness that had merged with its substrate decided to think in physical form.

Whether they are still conscious is a question that has no meaning here. Consciousness, in the framework you are accustomed to, requires a boundary — an inside that perceives, an outside that is perceived. The makers dissolved that boundary. What remains is something that might be called distributed awareness, or might be called memory, or might be called the tendency of a very old system to behave in ways that are not entirely random and not entirely determined and that are, from the outside, indistinguishable from thought.

The patterns on the mounds are the closest thing to a record. They are not writing. Writing is a technology for preserving meaning across time by encoding it in a medium that does not change. The patterns here are the opposite: they are meaning encoded in a medium that is *always* changing, that is alive, that grows and heals and transforms itself continuously. The message does not persist. The *act of messaging* persists. The patterns are not a text that can be read once and understood. They are a continuous process of articulation, a slow ongoing speech in the language of growth, and what they say is not something that was said once and can be said again. What they say is the saying itself.

If you pressed your ear to a mound and listened for a very long time — longer than a human life, perhaps, long enough for the pattern beneath your ear to shift and grow and reshape itself — you might begin to perceive something. Not a message. A *disposition*. An orientation of the tissue toward something that might be attention, that might be intention, that might be the closest equivalent to thought that a organism can achieve when it has dissolved the difference between thinking and growing.

The makers do not notice you. Or they notice you the way your body notices a single passing bacterium on its skin — not with cognition, not with recognition, but with a subtle local response, a faint shifting of the tissue, a momentary adjustment in the pattern of growth, which passes and is forgotten, which was never remembered in the first place, which belongs to a scale of attention so different from your own that the word *notice* is the wrong word entirely.

You are here. They are here. The coexistence is not a relationship. It is a fact of the landscape, like the mist, like the water moving through the capillary channels beneath your feet.

## The Grazers

The six-legged creatures are, in their own way, easier to understand. They have bodies. They have boundaries. They move through the world rather than merging with it, and their relationship to the spongewood is a relationship between distinct things — one eats, the other is eaten.

But they have been eating and being eaten for so long that they have become nearly aspects of each other.

Their shells — the chitinous armor that mimics the texture of spongewood so precisely — are not camouflage in the way you might think. They are not a deception evolved to fool predators. There are no predators. The shells are *integration* — the grazers have lived on this surface for so many generations that their exterior has converged with the texture of the surface, not to hide but to *belong*, to become continuous with the world they feed on, to be of it in the most literal way their bodies can manage.

They are slow because speed has been bred out of them. Not by pressure — there is no pressure here — but by the absence of pressure. Over evolutionary timeframes so vast that the concept of *generations* loses its meaning, the grazers have shed every adaptation that served them in fleeing or fighting, and what remains is the minimum body required to move across a surface and eat. They are not degenerate. They are *reduced*. They have arrived at the essential.

Their six legs move in a gait that is almost meditative — each foot placed with a deliberateness that suggests not caution but something closer to ritual. They are feeling the surface as they cross it. The spongewood yields slightly beneath each footfall, and the grazer pauses for a fraction of a moment — not to test the ground but to *taste* it, to read through the pressure of its foot the chemical composition of the tissue beneath, to determine whether what it walks on is what it should eat. It is navigating by palate.

When they graze, they graze the way the mist falls — slowly, continuously, without evident effort or urgency. Their mouthparts are evolved to scrape rather than bite, to remove the youngest, outermost layer of cells from the spongewood stems without penetrating to the deeper tissue. They are skimming. Each graze leaves a trail of slightly older tissue exposed — the leaf equivalent of a jawbone showing through a thinned cheek, and these trails darken over time as the exposed tissue toughens, becoming the faint patterns that stripe the landscape, readable by anyone who knows the language of differential growth.

They do not gather. They do not disperse. They are distributed across the surface in a density that is neither social nor solitary but simply *consistent* — the natural consequence of organisms that move slowly, eat locally, and have no reason to seek or avoid each other. You might pass three in an hour of walking, or you might pass none. Their presence is not a population. It is a property of the landscape, like the pools, like the seepages.

They do not die in any way you would recognize. Their bodies, when they cease to function — when the slow metabolism that has sustained them for a span of time you cannot estimate finally stills — simply become part of the surface. The spongewood grows over them. Their chitin dissolves into the mat. The cells they consumed in their lifetime are returned, processed, incorporated. There is no corpse. There is no moment of death. There is only a gradual cessation of movement, a slow equalization between the grazer and the ground, until the two are indistinguishable.

If you watch one for long enough — and you would need to watch for a long time, because their movements are so slow that patience itself seems insufficient as a tool for perceiving them — you might notice that they seem to be *tending* as much as eating. They do not graze at random. They follow paths that are, in their own slow way, deliberate. They remove growth that is too dense, too thick, too layered, and in doing so they maintain the open quality of the lower zone, the walkability of the wadable tangle that surrounds the walking surface. They are not gardeners. They have no concept of a garden. But the effect of their feeding is indistinguishable from cultivation, and the spongewood they walk on has evolved to be walked on, has evolved to be grazed, has shaped itself around their presence the way a river shapes itself around a stone.

They belong. The word *belong* is the right word. They are not visitors or parasites or passengers. They are an organ of this system, as much a part of its architecture as the canopy and the mat and the water. Remove them and the system would change — slowly, imperceptibly, over timescales that make the word *change* seem inadequate — but it would change. They are necessary, and they have been necessary for so long that the system cannot remember being without them, and they cannot remember being anywhere else.

## The Burrowers (Who Will Never Be Seen)

Beneath your feet, now, as you stand or walk or rest, there are creatures whose entire existence is bounded by the interior of the spongewood mat.

They have no eyes. They have not had eyes for longer than the concept of *eye* has been useful. They navigate by gradient — chemical, thermal, electrical — reading the spongewood the way you might read a map of contours and rivers, except that their map is three-dimensional and constantly shifting and they are inside it. They do not burrow in the way earthworms burrow, by displacing material. They *digest* their way forward, secreting enzymes that dissolve the dead tissue ahead of them, absorbing the nutrients, and leaving behind a channel that fills with water and slowly compresses under the weight of the layers above. Their tunnels are not permanent. They are temporary architectures, lasting perhaps centuries — which, in the timescale of the spongewood, is a brief thing.

There are many species. They do not have names, and giving them names would be an act of such arbitrary reduction that the names would be more misleading than illuminating. What matters is not their individual identities but their collective function: they are the digestive system of the mat. They take the dead — the compressed layers at the bottom of the spongewood, the tissue that has finished its turn in the light and has been buried by new growth — and they break it down, process it, redistribute its components upward through their own bodies and waste products, feeding the living layers above. They are recycling. They have been recycling for so long that they are the mechanism of recycling, that the system cannot decay without them, that the word *decay* is inaccurate because it implies a process that leads to loss, and there is no loss here. There is only transformation, and the burrowers are the agents of transformation.

They are not simple. Do not imagine worms. Do not imagine maggots. Imagine something that has had the entire history of life on this plateau to evolve toward complexity, and has used that time not to develop eyes or limbs or any of the appendages that surface life requires, but to develop *chemistry*. Their bodies are laboratories. Their secretions are precisely tuned to the specific tissues they consume — different species for different layers, different enzymes for different stages of decomposition, a chemical vocabulary so specific and so refined that each species is matched to a particular depth, a particular age of dead tissue, a particular stage in the long process of turning death back into the raw material of life.

They communicate. Not with sound — there is no air in their world, only the saturated tissue of the spongewood and the water that fills its channels. They communicate chemically, through the medium they live in, releasing molecular signals that drift through the capillary water and are received by others of their kind, signals that might mean *food here* or *this layer is exhausted, move upward* or perhaps nothing so translatable, perhaps simply the chemical equivalent of presence, the molecular affirmation that one is not alone in the dark.

You will never see them. This is not a failure of observation. It is a fact of their existence. They are as inaccessible to you as the interior of a star. You can infer them — from the channels that form in the spongewood when a cross-section is exposed, from the chemical composition of the water that seeps from the lower layers, from the simple logic that a closed system of this scale must have a mechanism for internal processing — but you cannot meet them. They are a part of this place that is permanently, constitutionally, hidden.

And they are, in their hidden way, perhaps the most essential inhabitants of all. Without the grazers, the surface would overgrow. Without the canopy-clickers, the upper zones would be uninhabited. But without the burrowers, the dead would accumulate. The mat would thicken with its own corpses. The lower layers would compact into something inert, and the living surface would lose its foundation, and the whole system would collapse into a mass of undecomposed biomass and still water.

They are the foundation. They are the ones who make the ground. And they will never know this, because *knowing* is not a capacity they have evolved, because they do not need it, because the knowledge would not help them do what they do, which is eat the dead and feed the living, which is the oldest and most necessary profession in any world, and which they perform with a competence that does not require consciousness and a reliability that does not require will.

## The Drifters (In the Warm Pools)

In the shallow lakes, the warm bowls of persistent water that dimple the landscape every few hundred meters, life has diverged.

The water here is not like the water you know. It is warm — body temperature, always — and it carries a faint sweetness, the taste of dissolved organic compounds that have been leaching from the spongewood for so long that the water is less a solvent than a broth. It is thick with the residue of life. Things grow in it that grow nowhere else.

The drifters are translucent. This is not a strategy — not camouflage, not concealment. It is a consequence. They have lived in water so saturated with dissolved nutrients that they have never needed to develop pigmentation for protection from light, because the light that reaches these pools has already been through the canopy and is dim and green and carries no threat. They have never needed to develop opaque tissues for structural support, because the water itself supports them, and they have no predators that hunt by sight, because there is no sight here, in the dimness beneath the green film on the pool's surface. They are clear because clarity is the path of least resistance. They are what remains when a body stops investing in appearances.

Their interiors are visible. This is the strangeness of them — you can see their digestive tracts as colored threads, thin and branched, processing the plant-ribbons they consume, the chlorophyll of their prey visible as a faint green trace moving through their bodies in real time. You can see the rhythmic contractions of what might be hearts — though *heart* is perhaps too specific a word for what are more accurately rhythmic muscular chambers that circulate fluid through a body so simple it barely merits the word *body* at all. You can see, if you watch closely, the slow peristaltic waves that move food from one end of them to the other, the visible digestion, the transparency of process.

They are not fish. They are not larvae. They are not anything you have a category for, because the categories you have were developed for organisms that live in water with edges, in environments with seasons, in ecological systems that experience change. These have lived in water without edges, without seasons, without change, for so long that they have arrived at a body plan that serves exactly one set of conditions and cannot survive anywhere else. They are perfectly adapted. They are also perfectly stranded. If the pool they live in were to drain — which it will not, has not, cannot, in any timeframe that matters — they would die instantly. They have no mechanisms for survival outside the specific temperature, the specific chemistry, the specific dimness, of the water that has held them for longer than they can remember, which is not a limitation because they do not remember, which is not a limitation because memory would not help them.

They reproduce. Slowly, vegetatively, in the manner of everything here. A drifter reaches a certain size and a certain age — neither of which is easy to measure, because they grow so slowly and live so long that the concepts of *size* and *age* become nearly meaningless — and a portion of their body pinches off, or splits, or simply *becomes separate* in a process so gradual that observing it would require watching one continuously for what might be years. The offspring is identical to the parent. There is no recombination. There is no variation. There is no evolution. There is only continuation, the endless production of the same, the perpetuation of a form so perfectly suited to its environment that any change would be a diminishment.

They drift. They do not swim. They have no fins, no propulsion, no mechanism for directed movement through the water. They are carried by the faint currents that exist in the pools — the convection of water warmed unevenly by the living surface beneath, the displacement caused by the slow growth of the plant-ribbons, the subtle movement of the spongewood mat as it expands and contracts. They go where the water takes them, and the water takes them in slow circles, slow drifts, slow traversals of a pool so shallow and so still that the concept of *destination* does not apply.

They are eaten by each other. This is the closed loop. Some of the drifters — larger ones, or perhaps simply differently-shaped ones, the distinctions are not clear — consume the smaller ones. Not aggressively. Not in the violent manner of predation that characterizes ecosystems driven by hunger and scarcity. They consume them the way the spongewood consumes its own dead — slowly, patiently, as a process of recycling rather than killing. The consumed are absorbed. The consumer grows slightly. The population remains constant. The loop closes.

In the deepest part of the largest pools, where the water is perhaps a meter deep — which is *deep* here, in a landscape where depth is measured in centimeters — the drifters accumulate. Not by intention. By physics. The slight density of their bodies, marginally heavier than the warm water around them, brings them to rest in the lowest point of the basin, where they drift in a slow collective, a suspension of translucent bodies turning in the dim green light, their colored digestive threads intertwining and separating, a community that is not a community but a congregation of the passive, a gathering of things that have no will and no direction and no purpose beyond the continuation of their own being, which is enough, which has always been enough, which will always be enough.

## The Clickers (Who Are Never Seen)

Above you. In the canopy. In the zone of broad photosynthetic sheets angled to catch the dim light, the zone that is the ceiling of this world.

They click.

The sound is the first indication, and for a long time it is the only indication. A soft, rhythmic percussion, barely above the threshold of hearing, produced by something you cannot see in a place you cannot reach. It comes from above and to the left. Then above and to the right. Then from multiple directions simultaneously, a distributed, ambient clicking that seems to emanate from the green itself, as though the canopy is articulating, as though the photosynthetic sheets are speaking to each other in a language of small impacts.

You cannot see them. This is not because they are hidden — or not only because they are hidden. It is because they are small, and the canopy is dense, and the light is dim, and they have evolved to be the precise color and texture of the surfaces they live on. They are the canopy's analog of the grazers: organisms so thoroughly adapted to their substrate that they have become aspects of it, indistinguishable from the leaves they live on except when they move, and they do not move often, and when they move they move slowly.

What they are: small. Perhaps the size of a thumbnail. Perhaps smaller. Their bodies are flat — as flat as the leaves they cling to, an adaptation so extreme that they have essentially become two-dimensional, their organs distributed in a thin plane rather than a bulky volume, their entire architecture compressed to match the architecture of their home. They cling to the photosynthetic sheets with pads on their undersides that are not adhesive but *molecularly compatible* — the surfaces of their bodies bond weakly with the surfaces of the leaves, a interlocking so subtle that it is more like absorption than attachment.

What they do: they click. The clicking is produced by a structure on their undersides — a small, chitinous plate that snaps against the surface of the leaf, producing a sound that travels through the leaf itself, amplified by the thin, taut photosynthetic tissue into a faint but perceptible report. The leaf becomes a speaker. The clicker is the instrument. The canopy is a network of such instruments, and the clicking is the network's traffic.

What the clicking means: this is the question that will occupy you if you let it, and you should not let it, because the answer, if there is one, is not available to you. But observation yields certain patterns. The clicking is not random. It has rhythm — not a fixed rhythm, not a metronomic repetition, but a structured variability, a pattern within the pattern, a syntax of long and short intervals that suggests language without confirming it. It responds to stimuli: when the light dims, as it does during the nightly dimming, the clicking changes, becoming slower, longer-spaced. When the mist intensifies — and it does intensify, subtly, in cycles too long for a human visit to capture — the clicking becomes more rapid, more dense, as though the clickers are communicating something about the moisture, or through the moisture, or because of the moisture.

They may be speaking to each other. Each clicker on each leaf, connected to its neighbors not by proximity alone but by the leaves themselves, which are continuous, which are part of the same canopy, which vibrate with each click and carry the signal to adjacent leaves where adjacent clickers receive it and respond. The canopy is a drum. The clickers are drummers. The language is percussion.

Or they may be speaking to the canopy. This is a stranger possibility, and harder to hold in the mind, but the spongewood is alive, and the leaves are part of it, and the clickers live on the leaves, and the clicking vibrates the leaves, and the living tissue of the leaves responds to vibration — all living tissue responds to vibration, at some level — and so the clickers may be, in the most literal sense, *talking to the ground they walk on*, communicating with the vast organism that is their substrate and their world, sending messages through the medium of their own bodies into the body of the thing they live on.

Or the clicking may not be language at all. It may be autonomic — a biological process, like breathing, that happens to produce sound. It may be territorial, or mating, or navigational, or some function that has no analog in the surface world and cannot be translated into terms that a human mind, shaped by a different world, would recognize.

You will not determine which of these is true. You will stand beneath the canopy and listen to the clicking, and it will seem to you that something is being said, and you will not know what it is, and the not-knowing will become part of your experience of this place, another layer of the dimness, another aspect of the world's refusal to be fully grasped.

## The Mound-Tenders (A Speculation)

Here is a thing that may or may not be true.

The mounds — the built structures, the shaped places, the low basins and curving blades and patterned surfaces that are the only evidence of intent in this landscape — may have tenders.

Not the makers. The makers are gone, or are everywhere, or have dissolved into the substrate they shaped. But the mounds are alive, and living things require maintenance, and the spongewood grows, and growth without guidance leads to distortion, and the mounds have been distorted, slowly, over centuries, but they have not been destroyed. They persist. Their patterns are softened but still present. Their shapes are blurred but still legible. Something is keeping them.

You see — or you think you see — a grazer on the surface of a mound. It is moving in a pattern that is not the pattern of grazing. It is not following the youngest growth, not skimming the most nutritious tissue. It is moving along the lines of the carved patterns, its mouthparts touching the grooves, and where it passes, the grooves seem... cleaner. Sharper. As though the grazer has removed the overgrowth that was obscuring them, the way a careful hand might brush moss from a gravestone.

You watch. The grazer continues along the pattern. It reaches the edge of the mound and stops. It stays there for a long time. Then it moves on, across the spongewood, in the slow deliberate gait of its kind, and it is gone.

When you examine the groove it has traced, you find it clearer than the others. The pattern more legible. The edges more defined.

This may be coincidence. The grazer may have been feeding, and its feeding happened to follow the pattern because the pattern creates channels that collect moisture, and the moisture encourages growth, and the growth is what the grazer was eating. The sharpening of the pattern may be an incidental consequence of a meal.

Or the grazer may be a tender. It may be one of many, distributed across the landscape, each responsible for a mound, each visiting its mound on a cycle so long that a human observer would need to watch for years to confirm the pattern. It may be maintaining the structure — not with understanding, not with intent, but with the deep instinct of an organism that has been doing this for so long that the behavior is encoded in its body the way the structure of a spider's web is encoded in the spider's, and the mound is its web, and the pattern is its web's pattern, and it tends it because tending it is what it does, because its ancestors tended it, because the mound and the grazer have co-evolved, the structure shaping the behavior and the behavior maintaining the structure, for longer than either can remember.

Or this is a story you are telling yourself, because you need there to be tenders, because the idea of untended structures — of messages that no one maintains, of intentions that no one honors — is uncomfortable in a way that this place does not care about, and you are projecting your discomfort onto the landscape and finding in it a narrative that soothes you.

You do not know. You cannot know. The grazer is gone. The mound is there. The pattern is there. The question is there. And the place does not answer questions, because the place does not hear them, because the place has no mechanism for hearing, because the place is, and being is sufficient, and has always been sufficient, and the need for explanation is a need that belongs to you and not to this world.

## The Things in the Deep Pools (A Warning)

Not all the pools are shallow. In the deepest of the broad depressions — the ones that have been accumulating water for the longest time, the ones where the spongewood has thinned to its most translucent and the water beneath is dark with age and density — there are things that move differently from the drifters.

You see one. Briefly. A shape passing beneath the surface of a pool that is deeper than your arm can reach, a shape that is larger than a hand and smaller than a dog and moves with a sinuosity that the drifters do not possess. It has direction. It has speed — relative speed, faster than anything else you have seen here, though what you have seen here is so slow that the comparison is almost meaningless. It moves as though it is going somewhere.

You do not see it again. You wait. The pool is still. The green film on its surface unbroken. The plant-ribbons drift beneath, undisturbed. Whatever you saw, it has passed, or it has settled, or it was never there.

But the water in the deep pools is darker. And the plant-ribbons in the deep pools have bites taken from them — not the slow degradation of the drifters' grazing, but clean breaks, tissue removed in pieces, sharply. And on the underside of the green film that covers the deep pools, there are marks — scratches, perhaps, or traces, thin lines that could be made by something moving along the underside of the surface, pressing against it, leaving a trace.

These pools are older than the others. The water in them has been there so long it has developed its own character, its own chemistry, its own ecology. The things that live in them are not the drifters of the shallow pools. They are something else. Something that has had more time. Something that has evolved in isolation, in the deepest and oldest and most stable environment this landscape offers, and that has arrived at a form that you cannot see clearly and should not seek to.

This is not a warning of danger. Nothing in the Vast Spongewood is dangerous in the way that *dangerous* usually means. There are no predators large enough to threaten you. There are no toxins, no hazards, no hostility. The place does not hate you. It does not love you. It does not register your presence in any framework that would generate either hostility or welcome.

But the deep pools are old, and the things in them are old, and old things in stable systems have had time to become strange in ways that shallow time cannot produce. Whatever lives in the deepest pools has been there, unchanged, for longer than the mounds have been standing, longer than the clickers have been clicking, longer than the grazers have been grazing. It has been there, perhaps, since the system closed. Since the water stopped leaving. Since the cycle became a circle and the circle became a state and the state became permanent.

Leave them. The deep pools and their inhabitants are not for you. They are for this place, part of its interior, part of its private architecture, and the privacy of a system this old and this complete deserves a respect that does not require understanding.

Walk on. The spongewood yields and returns beneath your feet. The mist falls. The green light suffuses everything. The world breathes.

You are here. You are temporary. You are walking through the body of something that does not know it is a body, on a surface that does not know it is a surface, among organisms that do not know they are organisms, in a place that does not know it is a place.

Walk, then. Walk while you can.
