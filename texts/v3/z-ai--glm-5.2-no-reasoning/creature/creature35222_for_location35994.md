# What Lives Here

---

The first thing to understand is that nothing here was born here.

Everything came from somewhere else. The roots came from above, descending through a century and a half of compacted fill and forgotten pipe. The moss came from spores washed down through the same channels, or perhaps it was always here, dormant in the clay, waiting for the warmth to reach the threshold it needed. The beetles came through the drainage grate, or through cracks in the municipal stonework too small for anything but intent and patience. The moths came the same way, drawn by the heat, or by something the heat was signaling. The salamander-thing — that one is harder to account for. It may have been here before any of it. It may be the reason the pocket exists at all, the slow round body that pushed the earth aside and then, instead of withdrawing, simply stayed and grew thinner and more useful.

Even the artifacts came from somewhere. Even the engine, the chair, the bottles. Someone carried them down. Someone assembled them with purpose. That person is the one ghost in this space, the only absence that still has a shape.

But the person is gone, and what remains has become its own thing. Not a collection. Not a colony. An organism — or at least the beginning of one, the way a fertilized cell is the beginning of an organism, though it does not yet know what it will become.

---

## The Beetles

Start with them, because they are the most legible.

They are not one species. This becomes clear after you've been still long enough for them to resume their normal patterns around your feet. There are at least three distinct forms living in the root-mat, and they behave differently, occupy different strata, and interact with the space in ways that suggest分工 — a division of labor so clean it looks engineered.

The first type is the one you see most readily. Flat, dark, thumbnail-sized, with elytra that have a faintly oily sheen, as though they've been waxed. These are the ones that flow around your feet and close again behind you. They are the surface-tenders. They move across the moss in patterns that are not random — you realize this after watching them for ten minutes. They follow the whorls. They trace the spirals of moss growth, and where they walk, the moss darkens slightly, as though they're pressing something into it, or drawing something out. They are feeding, but not on the moss. On what the moss has collected — the moisture, the mineral film, the microscopic particulate that filters down through the soil from the city above. They are harvesting and they are also, without knowing it or perhaps knowing it perfectly, distributing. They carry what they've gathered to the root-fibers and deposit it at the junction points, where roots meet roots and the exchange happens. They are the surface transport. The capillary layer.

The second type is smaller, paler, and you almost never see it unless you peel back the root-mat, which you should not do, and which you won't, because the roots pulse under your hand and something in that pulse is a warning. But if you did, you would find them: pale, almost translucent, the color of the root-fibers themselves, eyeless, moving through the interstices of the mat like fish through coral. They are the ones that tend the roots from below — cleaning the fibers, removing dead tissue, carrying away the small fragments of decay that the salamander-thing misses or cannot reach. They are the deep maintenance. The immune system, if this is an organism. They do not come to the surface. They have no need to.

The third type is the rarest, and the strangest. You see only two of them in your entire time in the pocket, and both times only briefly. They are larger than the others — the size of the first joint of your thumb — and they are not dark but a deep, iridescent green, the green of the ceramic pipe, the green of the moss at its densest. They move slowly. Deliberately. They do not follow the moss patterns. They ignore the root-fibers. They go to the artifacts.

The first one you see climbs the rusted body of the engine. It walks to the maker's plate, the one the moss has eaten into Braille, and it stays there for a long time, its antennae moving across the raised dots of corroded metal as though reading them. Then it moves to the flywheel, walks its circumference once, and descends. The second one you see goes to the crate, enters through a gap between the slats, and does not come out. You hear, very faintly, the glassy tap of its body against a bottle.

These beetles — the green ones — are the ones that trouble you. The surface-tenders and the deep maintenance are explicable. They fill roles that exist in any ecosystem. But the green ones are doing something else. They are *inspecting*. They are checking the artifacts the way a nurse checks a patient's IV lines, or a mechanic checks the gauges on a machine that is running but should be monitored. They are treating the engine and the bottles as parts of the system — not dead objects colonized by life, but components that still have a function, even if that function has been redefined.

The beetles do not have a queen. They do not have a nest. They have this room. The room is the nest. The room is the queen. They are the cells of something that does not yet have a name, and they do their work without complaint or confusion, and they will do it until the room changes or until they die, and when they die the moss will absorb them, and the roots will carry what the moss has processed back into the system, and they will become part of the walls, the floor, the exchange, the hum.

## The Moths

The moths are harder. Not harder to observe — they are large, conspicuous, and they move often enough to track. Harder to understand. Because the beetles clearly belong here, clearly fill a niche, but the moths feel like they are *waiting* for something, and that waiting is their primary behavior, and you cannot tell what they are waiting for.

They are not like any moth you have seen. Their bodies are thick, furry, the color of dry clay, and they are warm to the touch — warmer than the air, warmer than the engine. When one lands on your hand, and one will, because they are not afraid of you, because nothing here is afraid of you, because fear requires a concept of damage and damage requires a concept of self that is separate from the environment, and nothing here is separate — when one lands on your hand, you feel its weight and its heat and the faint vibration of its wing muscles, still primed, still ready to fire even at rest. It is like holding a small, soft engine.

The wings are the thing. Grey-brown, as I said, but the patterns are not the patterns of camouflage or warning or sexual display. They are *diagrams*. You hold the moth on your hand and you tilt it toward the faint light from the grate and you look, and you see: lines. Cross-hatching. Structures that look like the root-knots on the ceiling, rendered in miniature, in pigment, on a moth's wing. You see the engine's flywheel — circular, with spokes. You see what might be the drainage grate, rendered as a grid. You see, on one moth, something that looks like the cross-section of a pipe, with roots threaded through it.

They are not drawing the room. They are not *illustrating* the room. The patterns are not representational in any way a human would recognize. But they correspond. They correspond in the way that a map corresponds to a territory — not by looking like it, but by encoding it. The moths are carrying information. They are carrying it in their wings, and when they fly from one point in the room to another, they are carrying it *to* somewhere, and whatever receives it — the moss, the roots, the engine, the walls — is reading it.

Or maybe not. Maybe the wing patterns are a byproduct. Maybe the moths are simply absorbing the room's geometry through their pupal casings and expressing it the way any organism expresses the pressures of its environment: in its body, in its form, in the accidents of its surface. Maybe they mean nothing.

But they rest on the engine. They rest on the engine specifically, three or four at a time, wings open, and the engine is warm, and the warmth has no source, and the moths are positioned on the flywheel the way someone would position gauges on a dashboard. And you cannot shake the feeling that they are part of the instrument panel. That the engine is being monitored. That the residual heat — the thermal memory of a fire that went out decades ago — is being *maintained*, not by the roots alone, but by the moths, who sit on the brass and absorb it and redistribute it and sit on the brass again, in a cycle that has no beginning and no end, only the steady tick of exchange.

They pollinate nothing. There is nothing to pollinate. Not yet. The salamander-thing prunes the root-tips and the nodules that look like unformed teeth, and those nodules might, eventually, become something — a flower, a fruit, an organ — but they have not yet, and the moths have been waiting for whatever they will become for a very long time.

This is what the moths are: a readiness. A mechanism that has been assembled before it is needed, the way the body produces antibodies before the infection arrives. The moths are the future's infrastructure, built by a system that does not plan but that *anticipates*, in the way that a river anticipates the sea, in the way that a seed anticipates the shape of the tree.

## The Salamander-Thing

It does not have a name. It does not need one. It has itself, and that is enough.

You see it twice in your time in the pocket, and both times it is doing the same thing, and both times it is aware of you, and both times it does not care.

The first time, it is in the root-knots near the ceiling, directly above the engine. It is moving along a thick root-fiber with a deliberation that is almost ceremonial — each of its six legs placed with precision, its body adjusting to the curve of the root, its blunt head turning slightly, constantly, scanning the growth. It finds a nodule — one of the pale, tooth-like growths at the tip of a root-shoot — and it pauses. Its mouth opens. Not a dramatic opening, not a yawn or a strike. A simple parting, the way a flower opens, and it takes the nodule into its mouth and closes, and stays there for a long time, its body motionless, its legs gripping the root, its throat working in slow, deliberate contractions.

It is digesting. But it is also deciding. Because not every nodule gets eaten. You watch it pass three nodules without pausing, and then stop at the fourth, and take it. The ones it passes are allowed to grow. The one it takes is removed. It is pruning, yes, but pruning is selection, and selection is judgment, and judgment requires criteria, and the criteria are not visible to you. The salamander-thing knows something about the nodules that you do not. It knows which ones should become teeth, or flowers, or whatever they are becoming, and which ones should be taken apart and returned to the moss as fertilizer, as fuel, as raw material for the next iteration.

The second time you see it, it is on the floor, moving through the beetles. They part for it the way they part for you, but differently — for you, they flow around your feet. For the salamander-thing, they lift. They climb its legs. They ride it for a moment, then step off, and you realize with a small shock that they are *cleaning* it. The pale deep-maintenance beetles, the ones that clean the root-fibers, are also cleaning the salamander-thing, picking debris from its segments, checking its joints, tending it the way they tend the roots. The salamander-thing is not an animal living in the system. The salamander-thing is an *organ*. It is as much a part of this place as the engine or the moss. It is the part that moves.

Its body is the color of the roots because it is made of the same thing. Not metaphorically. Biochemically. Its skin has the same texture, the same faint translucency, the same slow pulse that the root-fibers have. Its six legs are arranged in three pairs because the root-knots form in threes, and it needs to grip them, and its body is segmented because the root system is segmented, and it is *shaped by its function* the way a key is shaped by its lock. It did not evolve for this place. It was grown by it. Or it grew itself for it, which is the same thing, if you are patient enough, if you have a hundred years, if you have the warmth and the moisture and the silence and the time.

It is the gardener. But it is also the garden. It is the thing that tends the system and the thing the system tends. It eats the nodules and excretes the food the moss needs and the moss insulates the roots and the roots provide the structure the salamander-thing lives in and moves through and tends. It is a closed loop. It is a circle with a body in it.

When it looks at you — and it does, the second time, pausing in its slow traversal of the floor to turn its blunt head and regard you with eyes that are not eyes but dark spots, pigmented patches that nonetheless seem to *focus* — when it looks at you, you feel assessed. Not threatened. Not welcomed. Assessed. The way the beetles assess a new root-shoot. The way the moss assesses a new surface. It is determining whether you are part of the system, and if not, whether you should be, and if not, whether you are a nodule to be pruned or a nodule to be left alone.

It decides to leave you alone. It returns to its work. You are not part of the system. You are a temperature fluctuation, a temporary obstruction, a stone in the stream. The water will go around you. The water is already going around you.

## The Liquid in the Bottles

This is the thing you keep coming back to. This is the thing you cannot stop looking at.

The bottles are old. The glass is old. The liquid is not. The liquid is — you don't know. The liquid is *new*, or it is being continuously renewed, or it was old and has become new again, the way a river is always the same river and never the same river.

When you hold a bottle to your eye, you see the circulation. It is slow. It is not convection — there is no heat source at the bottom of the bottle, no cooling at the top. The liquid moves in a pattern that is not physical, or not only physical. It moves the way protoplasm moves in a cell — by intention, by the internal pressure of a system that is alive. The liquid is alive. Or it is the medium in which life is being prepared. Or it is the blood of something that has not yet decided what it is.

It is the same temperature as everything else. The temperature of the inside of a cupped hand. The temperature of a body. And it moves, and it has been moving for however long the bottles have been here — decades, maybe, sitting in a collapsed crate in a pocket beneath a city that doesn't know it exists — and it has not stagnated, and it has not evaporated, and it has not been consumed, and it moves.

You think: the roots have found the bottles. The roots have threaded through everything else in this room — the pipe, the engine, the crate. The roots are in the crate. You saw them, entering through the rotten bottom, threading between the bottles, not breaking them, *holding* them. And the liquid moves. And the roots pulse. And the hum continues.

The liquid is not in the bottles. The liquid is in the *system*. The bottles are windows. The bottles are the points at which the system's circulatory fluid becomes visible, the way a vein is visible through pale skin. You are holding a window. You are looking at the blood of something that is being born, slowly, over decades, in a pocket of warm earth beneath a city, using the infrastructure the city forgot and the artifacts a person left behind and the roots that came down from the trees and the beetles that came through the cracks and the moths that came because they were needed and the salamander-thing that came because it was grown.

The liquid moves. It breathes. It is warm.

You put the bottle back. You put it back exactly where you found it. You are very careful about this. You are very careful about everything you do in this room, because you understand, finally, that you are inside something, and it is alive, and it is working, and it does not need you, and the kindest thing you can do — the only correct thing — is to leave it alone.

## The Person Who Was Here

But there was a person. There was a person who sat in the metal chair and faced the engine. There was a person who brought the bottles, full of whatever they were full of before the roots found them. There was a person who knew this ground, who knew the pocket, who came down through a drainage grate with a purpose and a plan.

You think about the chair. The moss has grown over it but not consumed it. The moss has *respected* it. The moss, which incorporates everything, which coats everything, which has eaten the maker's plate into Braille and colonized the pipe and covered the root-mat — the moss has left the chair's seat clear. Not bare, but clear. As though the chair is still in use. As though the system is expecting someone to sit down.

The person was an engineer, or a surveyor, or a workman with a particular curiosity. The engine was theirs — brought down to pump water, to drain the pocket, to make it usable. The bottles were theirs — samples, maybe, of the groundwater, taken to be tested. The chair was theirs — a place to sit and watch the pump work and wait for the water level to drop.

It didn't drop. The water didn't come. The pocket was dry — is dry, is damp but not wet — and the engine ran for a while and then stopped, and the person left, or the person died, and the roots came down and found the engine still warm and the bottles still full and the chair still waiting, and they did what roots do, which is grow, and they grew into the engine and around the bottles and through the pipe and across the floor and up the walls and into the ceiling, and they *used* what the person had left.

But the chair. The clear seat of the chair. The moss knows something about the chair that it doesn't know about the engine or the bottles. The moss knows the chair is for sitting. The moss is keeping it ready.

This is the part that stays with you, after. Not the beetles, not the moths, not the salamander-thing's dark assessing gaze. The chair. The empty, moss-bordered, warm seat of a folding metal chair in a pocket of earth beneath a city, facing an engine that doesn't run, in a room that is a heart, kept clear by a system that does not plan but that *anticipates* — kept clear for someone who is coming, or for someone who will be needed, or for the moment when the system has grown enough, learned enough, pruned itself enough, that it will require something from outside. A visitor. A collaborator. A person in a chair.

The moths' wings carry diagrams of the room. The liquid in the bottles circulates like blood. The beetles tend. The salamander-thing prunes. The roots pulse. The engine remembers fire. And the chair is empty, and the chair is warm, and the moss is keeping it ready, and you were here, and you sat in it, briefly, before you understood, and then you stood up, and you left, and the grate closed behind you, warm, the way everything down here is warm.

And the hum continued without you.
