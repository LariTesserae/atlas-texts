# What Lives Here

They are not what you would expect, perhaps. Not a people shaped by their place so much as a people who have become the same temperature as it.

---

There is a woman on the third terrace who has been shaping the same kind of bowl for — she doesn't know how long. Decades. The bowls are not identical. She would find that idea strange, if it were articulated to her, because no two pieces of stone are identical, so how could two bowls be? But they are recognizably *her* bowls, the way a handwriting is recognizably someone's. The curve of the interior wall. The way the lip thickens slightly at one point, where her hand has learned to pause. She learned from a man who lived on the fifth terrace, near the tree, who learned from someone whose name is not remembered. She does not think of this as a lineage. She thinks of it as the way things are done.

She works with the door of her workshop open, usually. Not for light — the light is the same inside and out, that sourceless, even grey — but because the closed door makes the space feel smaller, and she has been in that room long enough that its walls have become specific to her. The chisel marks on the bench where she rests her elbow. The slight hollow worn into the stone floor where she stands. She does not notice these things. They are not features of her world; they are the substance of it.

Her hands are rough in a way that is not rough to her. The skin has thickened over the years into something like leather — like the oiled cloth she wraps the iron blade in, actually, though she has never made that comparison. She uses the iron blade perhaps twice a month, for the final smoothing of a bowl's rim. The rest of the work is done with flint and basalt and a rubbing stone she has carried so long that it fits the hollow of her palm exactly. The flake of schist she uses for fine cutting has a name, but the name is not a word you would recognize. It is the sound she makes when she asks someone to pass it to her.

She has a name, too. They all do. But the names here are not the kind of names that get written down or shouted across distances. They are the names you use when you are speaking to someone standing near you, which is usually how close people are when they speak. Her name, if you could hear it, would sound like water running over smooth stone. This is not a metaphor. It is a description of the phonemes available to a voice that has been shaped by this air, this quiet, this altitude.

---

There is a man on the eighth terrace who knows where the spring comes from. Not its source — he has never been there, though he could go — but its nature. He knows that the water runs through a particular kind of rock before it reaches the channel, and that this rock gives the water its taste, and that the taste changes slightly in the season that doesn't have a name but corresponds roughly to what you would call late autumn. He knows this because his mother told him, and because he has tasted it, and because the taste has not changed in his lifetime, which means it probably hasn't changed in a long time before that.

He is the one who maintains the pipes. Not officially — there is no office, no appointment — but when a clay pipe cracks, which happens every few years, he is the one who replaces it. He has a store of pipes, baked in a small kiln on the ninth terrace that he built himself, following a method he figured out by examining the existing pipes. He does not think of this as ingenuity. He thinks of it as noticing what is needed and doing it.

He is quiet in a way that is not withdrawal. He speaks when he has something to say, which is not often, and when he speaks, the people around him listen, which is also not often, because the things he says tend to be things like "the second pipe is running thin" or "the moss on the fourth terrace is spreading." He does not find this unimportant. He finds it precisely as important as it is, no more and no less, and this is a quality the people around him recognize and value without naming.

He sits under the juniper sometimes. Not to think — he doesn't distinguish between sitting and thinking, because sitting is not a pause in his life, it is part of it. He sits and he is there, and the tree is there, and the hills are there, and then he goes back down to the eighth terrace and checks the basins.

---

There are children. Five of them, at the moment, though the number shifts as children become adults, which they do without ceremony. The youngest is two — not two years, exactly, because the counting of years here is not precise, but two in the way that children are counted, which is to say she has been present for two cycles of the juniper's berries. She is learning to walk on the packed earth of the seventh terrace, where her parents work fiber — spinning a coarse thread from a plant that grows lower on the hill, a plant they trade for, receiving bundles of dried stalks twice a year from people who come up the hill.

She falls sometimes. The earth is packed hard but not stone, and she falls in the way that children fall, and she gets up, and the adults around her do not react because there is nothing to react to. She is not in danger. She is on a terrace, and the terrace is enclosed on three sides by stone walls and on the fourth by a drop that she cannot yet reach. When she can reach it, someone will block it, not out of fear but in the way that you move a chisel out of the path of a crawling hand. It is a practical act, not an anxious one.

The children play, but their play is not separate from the work. They imitate. They pick up scraps of stone and tap them with smaller scraps of stone. They sit next to the woman on the third terrace and watch her work, and sometimes she hands them a piece of soft stone and a dull tool and they scrape at it, and the thing they make is not a bowl and is not nothing. It is what they make, and when they are done, they put it down or carry it with them or leave it on the bench, and it exists for a while and then it doesn't, and this is fine.

The oldest child is eleven, or near enough. He is learning to shape stone properly now — not making bowls, but making blocks, the rectangular pieces that are used for repairs. He works with a man on the tenth terrace who is the closest thing the Quarter has to a builder, though he would not use that word. He is someone who knows which stones to cut and how to fit them, and he has been doing this long enough that the walls he has repaired are already old.

---

There are old people. Not many — the population is small enough that death is not frequent but is also not unfamiliar. When someone dies, they are taken down the hill. There is a place below the Quarter, a half-day's walk, where the dead are placed. This is not discussed in detail, not because it is forbidden but because the details are not interesting. The dead go down. The living stay here. The work continues.

The oldest person lives on the first terrace, the highest one, nearest the channel. She is — no one knows exactly. She has been there longer than anyone else, including herself. She does not work stone anymore. Her hands have lost the strength for it, though they retain the knowledge — you can see it in the way she picks up a tool and turns it, the way her fingers find the grip without searching. She sits in her doorway and watches the channel run. She is not waiting. She is not resting. She is old, and being old is its own activity here, one that requires the same patience as the rest of it.

She speaks sometimes. Her voice is very quiet, and the people around her lean in to hear, not because she is wise — though she may be, the word doesn't translate well — but because she has been here longer, and the things she says are the things that someone who has been here longer would say. "The moss was thicker before." "The sixth terrace's basin used to crack every year." "There was a second tree, once, on the second terrace. It died." These are not instructions or histories. They are observations, offered without emphasis, received without ceremony.

---

They are not sad. This is important to understand, because it would be easy to look at the grey stone and the grey sky and the grey clothes — they wear grey because the wool is grey, and the wool is grey because the sheep are grey, or were, when there were sheep here, which there aren't now but were once, lower on the hill — and see melancholy. But melancholy requires a sense that things could be otherwise, and this is not a sense that thrives here. Things are what they are. The rain comes. The stone is wet. The stone dries. The rain comes again. This is not sadness. This is weather.

They laugh, occasionally. Not often, and not loudly, but you can hear it sometimes — a low sound, more like a breath pushed out than a shout. Something is funny. A tool that slipped at the wrong moment. A bird that landed on someone's head while they were working. The fact that the goat that wandered up last week ate the herbs in the trough on the seventh terrace. These are funny because they are unexpected, and the unexpected is funny in a place where most things are expected.

They do not tell stories. This is not quite right — they tell stories, but the stories are short, and they are about things that happened, not things that didn't. "The woman on the third terrace made a bowl so thin you could see light through it." "The man on the eighth terrace fell asleep in the channel once and woke up wet." These are not myths. They are memories, shared aloud, and they do not grow in the telling. They are the same size every time they are told.

---

They do not want things. This is the hardest part to describe, because wanting is so fundamental to how you might understand people that its absence seems like a void. But it is not a void. It is a fullness — a life so complete in its elements that the reaching for something else has simply stopped reaching. The woman on the third terrace does not want to make a better bowl. She wants to make the next bowl, which will be what it is. The man on the eighth terrace does not want the pipes to last longer. He wants to fix them when they break. The children do not want to grow up faster. They are growing, and the growing is the thing.

There is a man on the sixth terrace who makes the brooms. He ties dried grass to stone handles — short, heavy handles that sit comfortably in the hand. The brooms wear out. He makes new ones. He has made hundreds. He will make hundreds more. When he dies, someone else will make them, or no one will, and the sweeping will be done with something else, or it won't, and the paths will be less clear, or they won't, and the Quarter will continue, or it will change in some small way that will become, over time, simply the way things are.

This is not resignation. Resignation implies that you wanted otherwise and have accepted that you cannot have it. They do not want otherwise. They are not in the world in a way that produces wanting. They are in the world in a way that produces *being*, and the being is enough, the way the stone is enough for the wall and the water is enough for the basin.

---

If you came here, they would notice you. They are not unobservant — they notice everything, the way the woman on the third terrace notices the grain of a stone, the way the man on the eighth terrace notices the sound of the water thinning in a pipe. They would notice that you are new, that you move differently, that you look at things they don't look at and don't look at things they do. They would not comment on this. They would give you water, because water is given. They would give you a place to sit, because sitting is what one does. They would continue their work, because work is what one does.

You would feel the quiet first, probably. Not silence — the Quarter is never silent, what with the water and the chisels and the broom — but the absence of urgency, of direction, of the sense that time is passing and must be used. Time passes here. It is not used. It is not wasted. It simply goes, the way the water goes, down the channel and through the pipes and into the basins and over the edge and into the earth.

You would feel the coolness. You would feel the dampness, if the rain had come recently. You would smell the wet stone and the moss and the dry dust of the empty buildings. You would sit on the bench under the juniper, or on the bench in a workshop, or on the packed earth of a terrace, and you would be there, and they would be there, and the rain would come or not come, and the chisel would tap or not tap, and the broom would scrape or not scrape.

And then, probably, you would leave. Because most people do. Because the Quarter is not a place that invites staying, except for those who were already here, and even they do not stay because they are invited. They stay because they are here, and being here is what they do, and what they do is what the Quarter does, and the Quarter does what it has always done, which is to continue, patiently, in the grey light, on the grey stone, under the grey sky, one terrace at a time.
