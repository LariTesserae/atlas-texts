# What Lives Here

---

Nothing lives here that you would recognize by its face.

That is the first thing to understand, and the hardest to hold onto, because the chamber *feels* occupied. The grinding sound implies effort. The rotation implies intention. The bioluminescent pulse implies communication — something signaling to something, some message passed in a code you cannot read. Your mind will keep reaching for the familiar. Let it reach. It will come back empty.

---

**The stalks are the closest thing to inhabitants.**

They are the largest organisms and the most obviously active. Each one is an individual — not a colony, not a clone-cluster, but a single coherent growth with its own root base, its own circumference, its own rotational direction and speed. You can tell them apart if you look closely. The bark-sheen varies: some are almost silver, some dark as oxidized iron, some carry a brownish patina that might be age or might be species difference. There are at least three morphologically distinct stalk-types in the chamber, and they do not intermix freely. The largest — the primary structural stalks that form the load-bearing lattice — are spaced at relatively even intervals, as though they established territory early and held it. The smaller secondary stalks fill the gaps, and the tertiary filaments — the bioluminescent ones — run along the surfaces of both without regard for the boundary, like vines that have learned which hosts are safe to climb.

Each stalk rotates at its own rate. Each one shudders and catches and sometimes reverses. Each one groans at a slightly different pitch, and the combined sound is not a chord but a texture — a rough continuous noise made of many independent frequencies, the way a crowd's murmur is not speech but the sound of many people speaking. The stalks are not coordinated. They are not communicating. They are each responding to the same underlying force — the electrochemical current that drives them — and their responses vary because their internal resistance varies, because their root connections vary, because no two stalks are identically situated in the gradient. They are independent engines coupled by proximity, forced into something that resembles cooperation by the fact that they touch, that their rotations interlock, that the lattice they form transmits torque from one to the next whether they like it or not.

This is not a community. It is a collision that has stabilized.

The stalks have been turning against each other for so long that their contact surfaces have worn smooth. You can see the polish — dark, glassy patches where two stalks rub, finer-grained than the surrounding bark, almost resinous. They have developed calluses. The tissue at the contact points is denser, harder, slightly lighter in color, and it regrows quickly when damaged. This is not adaptation in the evolutionary sense. It is adaptation in the immediate sense — the tissue responds to pressure by thickening, the way a tree responds to wind by growing thicker on the windward side. The stalks are shaping each other. They have been shaping each other for decades, perhaps centuries, wearing grooves, building calluses, learning — not learning, *becoming* — a mechanical fit that is not designed but is nonetheless precise.

There is something that might be death here. One of the secondary stalks, near the far wall, is not rotating. Its surface is dull, its color greyer than its neighbors, and two of the bracket-growths that cluster on it have gone dark — no vibration, no sheen, just flat pale patches like old scabs. The stalk has not rotted. It has not collapsed. It has simply stopped. The neighboring stalks rotate against it without complaint, their contact surfaces now rubbing against dead tissue, and the dead tissue is slowly being worn away — not decomposing, not being eaten, just being eroded by friction, returned to the system as particulate that the water will carry to the roots. It will take years. When it is gone, the gap will close as neighboring stalks grow into the space, and the lattice will reconfigure, and the dead stalk will be forgotten in the only way this system can forget: by overwriting.

Nothing mourns here. Things stop, and other things grow into the space they occupied, and the engine keeps running.

---

**The bracket-growths are not plants.**

They are not fungi, either, though they occupy the same visual niche. They are structures — appendages, extensions, organs — of the stalks they grow on. Not parasites. Not commensals. Part of the stalk's own body, the way leaves are part of a tree. Each bracket is anchored deep into the stalk's fibrous interior, tapping its circulatory system, drawing from the same electrochemical current that drives the rotation. They are, functionally, exposed tissue — the stalk's interior brought to the surface and spread thin, and their purpose is not clear but their behavior is.

They vibrate. Constantly. The vibration is high-frequency, barely audible, more felt than heard, and it changes in response to the stalk's rotational state. When a stalk rotates smoothly, its brackets vibrate at a steady frequency. When a stalk stutters or catches, the vibration becomes irregular, arrhythmic, and the surrounding brackets — on neighboring stalks, even on stalks of different types — shift their vibration in response. This is not communication. It is mechanical coupling. The vibration propagates through the lattice, through the contact points, and the brackets respond to the vibration the way a tuning fork responds to a nearby tone — sympathetically, physically, without cognition.

But the effect is information transfer. A stutter at one end of the chamber produces a vibrational signature that reaches the other end, attenuated but present, and the brackets along the way register it. Whether the system *uses* this information — whether it means anything, whether it changes anything — is a question that has no answer. The information exists. The transfer happens. What the system does with it, if anything, is not knowable from outside.

The brackets also reproduce the stalks, in a sense. On the underside of the largest brackets, in the pale moist space where they meet the stalk's surface, there are growths — tiny, dense, darker than the surrounding tissue, shaped like seeds but softer, like wet packets of fiber. They do not fall. They are not released. They are absorbed — slowly, over time, drawn back into the stalk's body, digested, their material redistributed. Whether they are reproductive structures or nutrient stores or something else entirely is unclear. They exist. They are produced and then reabsorbed. The system generates potential and then consumes it. If there is a metaphor here, you are too deep underground to appreciate it.

---

**The iridescent film on the wet stone is alive.**

It is the smallest organism in the chamber and the most numerous — a single-celled or perhaps colonial organism that coats every wet surface in a film so thin it is visible only by its iridescence. It is not the film itself that shimmers. The film is translucent, almost invisible. The shimmer comes from the way it structures the water that passes over it — the cells align in response to the mineral content of the water, forming a diffraction grating at the microscopic scale, and the light that passes through is split into its component colors. The film is a living prism.

It does not move on its own. It spreads — slowly, cell by cell, across the wet stone, following the water. Where the water goes, the film goes. When a rill shifts course, the film dies back along the old channel within days and establishes along the new one within weeks. It is the most ephemeral thing in the chamber and the most resilient, because it is the simplest. It does not need the lattice. It does not need the gradient. It needs only wet stone and the trace minerals the water carries, and it has been here longer than anything else, and it will be here after everything else stops.

The film is the garden's skin.

---

**The filamentous curtains are predators.**

This is the one fact that surprises you, because nothing in the chamber seems to eat anything. There is no fauna. No insects, no grazers, no movement except the mechanical. But the hanging filaments — the dense beaded curtains that catch the condensation from the ceiling — are not passive. They are not collecting water. They are collecting what the water carries.

Each filament is coated in a mucilage — thick, viscous, slightly adhesive — and the droplets that form along their length are not condensation. They are secretion. The mucilage traps particulate matter from the air: mineral dust, spores, fragments of organic material abraded from the stalks by their constant friction. The filaments absorb what they trap, slowly, through the same osmotic process that the film on the walls uses. They are filter feeders. They are the only things in this chamber that eat something other than dissolved minerals — they eat the system's own detritus, the dust and fragments that the grinding produces, the dead cells and worn fibers that the stalks shed as they turn. They are the cleanup crew. They are the reason the air in the chamber is not thick with particulate, the reason the floor is not piled with the debris of constant mechanical wear. They hang from the ceiling and they catch what falls and they eat it, and they glow faintly in the bioluminescent pulse, and they are the closest thing here to something you might call an animal, because they eat other things, and nothing here eats them.

Nothing here eats them because there is nothing here that eats. The filaments trap and absorb. The film absorbs. The stalks draw current. The brackets vibrate. The system is a closed loop of mineral and water and electrochemical energy, and the filaments are the only organisms that cross the boundary between mineral-eating and material-eating, the only ones that consume the bodies of other living things, even in fragments, even as dust.

They are the apex predators of the Ferrous Gardens, and they are strands of wet fiber hanging from a ceiling, and they have no idea what they are.

---

**There is something else.**

You almost miss it. You are climbing out, your hands on the wet stone, the sound diminishing, when you notice that the rock under your left hand is warm. Not stalk-warm — warmer. Hot, almost. And the fracture you are climbing through is narrower than it was when you descended, and the stone is not cold and slick and young. It is older. The edges are rounded. The surface is smooth. Something has been flowing through this fracture for a very long time, and it has left a residue — a thin, dark coating on the stone, organic, slightly tacky, and where your hand presses against it, the coating dims the bioluminescent light from below in a pattern that is not random.

The pattern is a series of concentric rings. Like growth rings. Like the cross-section of a tree. But spread flat across the stone, following the fracture, extending deeper into the rock than you can see.

This is not a plant. This is not a machine. This is something older and slower and larger than anything in the chamber below — something that lives in the fractures themselves, in the spaces between the rock, in the geology rather than the geography. The stalks in the chamber are its appendages, or its children, or its competitors — you cannot tell which. The gradient that powers the engine below is a feature of the rock, but something *made* the gradient exploitable. Something *shaped* the fractures so that the water would flow the way it flows. Something has been here since before the chamber opened, and it will be here after it closes.

It does not respond to your touch. It does not respond to anything. It is the closest thing to god in this place, and it is a thin dark film on the walls of a crack in the rock, and it has been growing at the speed of geology for longer than the garden below has been turning, and you are leaving, and it does not notice, and the garden does not notice, and the only thing in the Ferrous Gardens that knows you were here is you.
