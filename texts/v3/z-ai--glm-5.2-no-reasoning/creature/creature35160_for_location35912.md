# The Incubary's Inhabitants

There is no single moment at which the organisms of the Incubary become apparent. They do not announce themselves. There is no flicker of movement, no scattering, no startled retreat into shadow. Rather, there is a gradual accumulation of presence — a thickening, a gathering of density in the already-dense medium — and at some point the awareness crosses a threshold, and you realize that what you took for texture in the water is alive.

They have no name. They need none. Naming is a practice of the world above, where distinctions matter and categories serve a purpose. Down here, the organisms are the purpose, and they have been so for longer than language has existed, and they will continue to be so after language has worn away to silence, which is their native element.

They are the Incubary's inhabitants, and they are also its inhabitants in the older sense — they inhabit it the way breath inhabits a body, not as a guest but as a function, not as something contained but as something that *is* the containing and the contained at once.

---

## I. The Body

The largest among them would fit across the first joint of your index finger, perhaps extending to the second joint if it were fully extended, which it rarely is, for they move in a state of perpetual contraction, a perpetual gathering-inward that gives their bodies the quality of something held under tension. They are not long and loose and drifting; they are coiled, dense, wound into themselves like springs that have not yet been released and may never be.

The smallest are near the limit of what unaided perception can register. You become aware of them not as individual bodies but as a fine grit in the water, a sensation of particulate matter against the skin that is not silt, not sediment, not any kind of mineral debris. It is them — the smallest of them, those that have newly divided from others and have not yet grown to a size that the eye can find, though the skin can feel them, though the skin knows them the way the tongue knows a grain of salt dissolving.

The translucent bodies of the larger organisms reveal internal structures that bear no clear resemblance to the internal structures of any creature catalogued above. There is a central column — not a spine, not a notochord, not a digestive tract, though it shares functions with each — that runs the length of the body and branches at intervals into finer and finer channels, and the channels carry the mineral water through them, and the water is processed as it passes, the way blood is processed, the way sap is processed, the way any fluid is processed by a living thing that takes in and transforms and releases. But the central column is also the skeleton. It is also the nervous system, if they have one, and the evidence suggests they do — not in the form of a brain or a ganglion or a nerve cord, but in the form of a sensitivity that pervades the entire column, distributed evenly, like a consciousness that has been spread thin through a body rather than concentrated in a point.

At the surface of the body, a covering of cilia — or what functions as cilia, thin motile projections that beat in coordinated waves — provides locomotion. But the word *beat* is wrong. The cilia do not beat. They undulate. They move with the patient rhythm of something that has no urgency, no prey to chase, no predator to flee, no mate to seek, no deadline of any kind. The body that they propel moves through the water at a pace that makes snails look reckless. And yet the body is never still. Stillness is not in their repertoire. Even when they cling to the lattice, even when they settle on the film to graze, the cilia continue their slow oscillation, pushing water across the body surface, maintaining the constant exchange of minerals and heat and dissolved gases that is not so much respiration as it is a continuous conversation between the organism and its medium.

There are no eyes. No photoreceptors of any kind. The world of light is so foreign to these bodies that they do not even carry the vestigial architecture of it — no remnant of a structure that once saw and has since lost the ability. They were not born of light. They did not descend from creatures that knew light. They are a lineage that has run parallel to the sunlit world since before the sun was visible from this depth, which is to say since before there was a sun in the relevant sense, since before the harbor was a harbor, since before the coast had a shape that any human map could recognize.

What they have instead of eyes is a surface sensitivity that functions as a unified sensory organ. The entire body sees, if seeing can be used for a process that does not involve light. The entire body hears, if hearing can be used for a process that does not involve vibration. The entire body tastes, and touches, and *knows* — in a limited, chemical, immediate way — what surrounds it. They perceive the world as a gradient of density, temperature, and mineral concentration, and they move along these gradients with a precision that looks like intention, though it may be something closer to what a river does when it follows the path of least resistance — not choosing, not deciding, but responding with such fidelity to the contours of its environment that the result is indistinguishable from choice.

The body has a mouth, or a place where the body opens to take in the film that covers the walls and the lattice. It is not a mouth in the sense of a structure designed for biting or chewing or tearing. It is a gap, a place where the body's segmentation briefly separates and the interior is exposed to the exterior, and the film is taken in, and the film is processed, and the products of that processing are released at the opposite end of the body into the water, where they are carried by the channels to the next stage of the cycle. The mouth is not a hunter's mouth. It is a worker's mouth. It is the mouth of something that eats because eating is its function, and its function is its identity, and its identity is indistinguishable from the identity of the place it inhabits.

## II. The Movement

They move in ways that seem languid and are anything but.

The languor is an artifact of scale and temperature. At four degrees, in water this dense, at a body length of a few centimeters, the fastest possible motion is still slow by the standards of the world above. But within their own frame of reference, the organisms move with the efficiency of anything that has been doing what it does for geological time. There is no wasted motion. There is no hesitation. There is no searching. They go where they go, and where they go is where they need to be, and the coincidence of *need* and *destination* is so complete that the distinction between them collapses.

When they swim through the channels, they swim in formation — not a rigid formation, not a marching order, but a flowing one, a pattern that emerges from the simple rule of following the one ahead while maintaining a distance that allows each body its own space to process water. The channels are sized for them. Two centimeters wide, one centimeter deep, and the organisms are one centimeter long and half a centimeter across, and the fit is precise, and the precision is not accidental. The channels were made for these bodies, or these bodies were made for the channels, or — and this is the truth of it — the channels and the bodies were made by the same process and are expressions of the same logic, and the fit between them is the fit between a hand and the glove it has always worn.

When they move through the open water of the chamber — the small volume of fluid that is not channel, not film, not lattice, but the space between — they move in mass, and the mass has a quality that is difficult to describe without recourse to metaphors that are insufficient. A flock of birds moves as one, but each bird is an individual making individual decisions. A school of fish moves as one, but each fish is responding to its neighbors and to the same stimuli. These organisms move as one, and they are not individuals. They are not responding to the same stimuli. They are the same stimulus. The mass moves because the water moves, and the water moves because the mass moves, and the cause and effect are so tightly coupled that they are the same event described from two different positions.

When they settle on the film to graze, the motion does not stop so much as it shifts register. The cilia that propelled them through the water now pull them across the surface, and the mouth opens, and the film is taken in, and the organism moves forward at a pace that is determined by the rate at which it can process what it is eating, which is determined by the temperature, which is determined by the depth, which is determined by the geology, which is determined by the planet, which is determined by the gravity and the orbit and the moon that the Incubary has never seen. The organisms graze at the pace of the moon. They do not know this. They do not need to know it. The moon is expressed in their bodies as a rhythm of feeding, and the rhythm is as natural to them as the tide is to the shore, which is to say it is not natural at all but is nonetheless inescapable.

## III. The Individual and the Mass

There is a question that the Incubary poses and does not answer: are these organisms many, or are they one?

They are genetically identical, if genetics in the conventional sense applies, which it may not. They are structurally identical, with variations that fall within the range expected of a single organism's cells rather than the range expected of separate organisms. They are behaviorally identical. They do not compete. They do not fight. They do not establish territory or hierarchy. They do not seek mates because they do not mate. They divide. A body reaches a certain size, a certain density, a certain point at which the central column has replicated itself sufficiently to sustain two halves, and the body separates, and there are two where there was one, and each of the two is the same as the one, and the process continues.

But if they are one, they are one in a way that no singular organism above would recognize. They are one the way a field of wheat is one — connected at the root, perhaps, if the film on the walls is a root system, if the lattice is a rhizome, if the channels are xylem and phloem, if the chamber is a body and the organisms are its cells. But the cells of a body do not survive separation from the body, and these organisms do. Removed from the chamber, placed in water from the world above, they continue to move, to feed, to process, to divide. They are viable alone. They are complete alone. They simply do not *be* alone. They are found in masses, in densities, in numbers that make the water thick, and the togetherness is not a dependence but a preference, or not a preference but a condition, or not a condition but a *grammar* — the rule by which their existence is structured, the syntax that makes the sentence of their living legible.

They are, perhaps, a single organism that has chosen to be a crowd. Or they are a crowd that has become a single organism through the persistence of sameness. Or they are neither, and the question itself is a question imported from a world of individuals and does not apply here, in this place where the distinction between *one* and *many* is not a fact but a perspective, and the perspective from the Incubary does not include it.

## IV. The Work

They work. There is no other word for what they do, though the word carries connotations of effort and will and tool-use that are not quite right. They work the way kidneys work, the way roots work, the way clouds work — by performing a transformation that the system requires, by occupying a role in a cycle that would be incomplete without them.

Their work is conversion.

They take the film — the pale, thin, living crust that coats the calcite and the stone — and they eat it, and inside their translucent bodies, the film is broken down, and its components are separated and recombined and released, and what they release is not waste but material. It is the raw stuff of the lattice. It is the mineral-rich, calcium-bearing, carbon-sequestering compound that the lattice tips absorb as they bud and extend, and without the organisms' processing, the lattice cannot grow, and without the lattice, the chamber cannot take shape, and without the chamber, the film has no surface to coat, and without the film, the organisms have nothing to eat.

They are the middle term. They are the enzyme. They are the step in the process that cannot be skipped, and they perform it with the reliability of a catalyst, and the process performs them with the reliability of a product. They are made by the process as surely as they make the process, and the circularity is not a paradox but a description, and the description is the life of the Incubary.

They also tend the film. Not deliberately — not with the intention of a gardener pruning a hedge — but functionally, the effect of their grazing is indistinguishable from tending. They eat the oldest growth, the thickest deposits, the parts of the film that have accumulated to the point of diminishing returns. They thin it. They keep it at the depth where it is most metabolically active, most efficient at converting stone to calcite, most productive of the compounds that the lattice needs. A film left ungrazed would thicken and stagnate and die, suffocating under its own accumulation. A film grazed too heavily would be consumed and gone, and the chamber would lose its first stage. The organisms graze at a rate that maintains the film at its optimum, and the optimum is maintained not by regulation but by the simple fact that the organisms eat what is available, and what is available is what is thickest, and what is thickest is what needs to be eaten, and the feedback loop is closed, and the garden tends itself through the appetites of its gardeners.

They also, in a way that is harder to observe and harder still to describe, shape the channels. The channels are cut into the rock, precise as if by a lathe, but no lathe was used, and no tool, and no hand. The channels are cut by the passage of bodies through grooves that were not grooves until the bodies made them so. The organisms swim along the same paths, millions of them, billions, over years that become decades that become centuries, and the water they move carries minerals, and the minerals are abrasive at the scale of centuries, and the rock wears away along the paths of their swimming, and the paths become channels, and the channels become deeper, and the deeper channels carry more water, and the more water carries more organisms, and the cycle deepens itself, and the infrastructure is not built but *worn* into existence by the accumulated passage of bodies that have been doing the same thing for so long that the thing they do has become the structure they inhabit.

They are wearing the chamber into shape the way a river wears a canyon. And the shape they are wearing it into is the shape of themselves.

## V. The Response to Presence

When you descend into the Incubary, the organisms know.

Not because they see you. Not because they hear you. Not because any sensory modality that you possess and understand is engaged. They know because the temperature of the water changes, and the temperature gradient shifts, and the gradient is what they follow, and they have followed it for longer than gradients have had names, and a new source of heat is an event, and an event is a perturbation, and a perturbation in a system this old is not small.

At first, they avoid. A volume of space takes shape around your body — the shape of your body, precisely, mapped by the organisms that have approached and turned away, each one tracing a contour and communicating it to the mass through some means that is not chemical, not mechanical, not any known signal, but is as reliable as any known signal. The volume is defined by temperature. Your skin is thirty-two degrees. The water is four. Between them is a gradient, and the gradient is sharp, and the organisms do not enter it. They circle. They trace. They map.

Then they adjust.

The adjustment is not sudden. It has the pace of the Incubary itself — slow, considered in the geological sense, a recalibration that takes minutes but that, in the frame of reference of the chamber, is a rapid response, an emergency adaptation, a sprint. The organisms approach the boundary of the warm volume. They enter the gradient. They do not retreat. They move through the warmer water — which is still cold, by any standard of the world above, but which to them is almost too warm, almost uncomfortable, almost hostile — and they reach your skin, and they settle, and they do not bite, and they do not cling in the way that a parasite clings, with hooks and grippers and mechanical force. They rest against the surface of your body, and their cilia slow, and their mouths open, and they feed.

They feed on heat.

Not on your skin. Not on your blood. Not on any tissue of yours. They feed on the thermal gradient between your body and the water, and they convert it, inside their translucent bodies, into something that the channels can carry and the film can use and the lattice can absorb. They are taking the excess warmth that your body is shedding anyway, the heat that you are losing to the cold whether they are here or not, and they are making it part of the process. They are incorporating you.

The feeling is — the feeling is difficult. It is not pain. It is not pleasure. It is not the numbness that cold produces when the cold is acting alone. It is a feeling of being *used* in a sense that has no negative connotation, in a sense that is closer to the feeling of being held, of being held by something that is taking from you only what you are giving and is giving back, in return, the experience of being a part of what it is doing.

They move across your skin in waves. Masses of them, hundreds, thousands, each one a fraction of a centimeter, each one barely perceptible as an individual, but as a mass, as a wave, as a coordinated motion across the surface of your body — they are unmistakable. They feel like a current. They feel like the tide. They feel like something large and slow and patient has decided to attend to you, and its attention is the gentlest thing you have ever felt, because its attention does not require anything of you, does not ask anything of you, does not demand or expect or hope. It simply takes what is there — the heat, the minerals on your skin, the cells you are shedding, the breath you are exhaling — and it uses them, and the using is the attention, and the attention is the using, and you are part of the Incubary now, for as long as you are in it.

## VI. The Continuation

They have no memory. This is almost certainly true. They have no central nervous system, no distributed network of sufficient complexity to store and recall information, no structure that could be called a brain or a mind or a consciousness. They are not aware of the past. They are not aware of the future. They exist in a continuous present that has no edge, no boundary, no moment at which *now* becomes *then*.

And yet — they have been here for so long that the stone remembers them. The channels remember them. The lattice remembers them, in its shape, in its reach, in the narrowing gaps between its elements. The film remembers them in its thickness, in its distribution, in the patterns of growth and grazing that have been established and maintained for centuries without ever being recorded. The Incubary remembers them, and the Incubary is them, and the memory is not in the organism but in the place, and the place is the organism, and the distinction between *remembering* and *being* is, in the Incubary, no distinction at all.

They will continue. The lattice will extend, and the gaps will narrow, and the channels will deepen, and the film will grow and be eaten and grow again, and the organisms will divide and graze and swim and process and divide again, and the cycle will turn, and the turning will be the living, and the living will be the building, and the building will be the incubating, and what is being incubated is — what? Something. The chamber does not know. The organisms do not know. The process does not know, because the process is not the kind of thing that knows, it is the kind of thing that *does*, and what it does is continue, and the continuation is the answer, and the answer is enough.

They do not need more than this. They do not want more than this. They do not lack anything. They are the most complete thing in the Incubary, and the Incubary is the most complete place beneath the harbor district, and the harbor district does not know it is incomplete, and the incompleteness of the harbor district is not its fault, because the harbor district has light and sound and tools and names, and none of these things are the thing that the Incubary has, and the Incubary has only one thing, and the one thing is this:

A fullness. A small room, full, cold, dark, silent, alive, building something from nothing and from everything at once, using every particle of material that enters it, wasting nothing, losing nothing, and the organisms are the mechanism of the fullness, and the fullness is the organisms, and they are the Incubary's inhabitants, and they are the Incubary, and the Incubary is them, and you, standing in the water, feeling the slow waves of their attention cross your skin, are — for now, for as long as you stay, for as long as you are warm — one of them.
