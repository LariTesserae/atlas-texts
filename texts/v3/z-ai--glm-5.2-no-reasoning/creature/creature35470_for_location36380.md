# What Lives Here

## I. The Greenword Itself

The first and most honest answer is: *it* lives here. The Greenword is not a habitat. It is a creature. Or rather, it is a creature the way a reef is a creature — which is to say it is many creatures that have become so interdependent, so physically fused, so metabolically inseparable that the distinction between "organism" and "ecosystem" has dissolved entirely. The Greenword is one body with a trillion cells, and each of those cells belongs to a different genetic lineage. Moss and vine and fern and fungus and liverwort and the unnamed thing that was perhaps the first colonizer of this valley, the one whose descendants now form the central trunk and the beating chambers and the vascular pipelines — they are all still present, all still technically distinct species, still technically capable of existing independently. But they do not. They have not for a very long time. The Greenword has been a single entity for so long that the individual species have lost the ability to survive alone. They are organs now. Specialized tissues. The whole cannot exist without its parts, but the parts cannot exist without the whole, and so the question of what lives here becomes circular: the Greenword lives in the Greenword.

This is not symbiosis. Symbiosis implies distinct partners. What exists in the valley is more like integration — the biological equivalent of a magma chamber absorbing the country rock around it until the boundary between intrusion and host is no longer meaningful. The vines that thickened into trunks did not parasitize their hosts. They *became* their hosts, or the hosts became them, or the two grew together so completely that the vascular tissues merged and now the same fluid moves through both, and the same hydraulic pulse drives both, and neither could tell you which cells are vine and which are original trunk if anything were still capable of asking.

The Greenword is alive the way a circulatory system is alive. Not conscious. Not intentional. But *active*. Constantly, silently active, pumping fluid through channels, growing toward warmth and water, retreating from cold where it cannot sustain itself, building its own architecture and dismantling its own ruins simultaneously. It breathes, after a fashion — the collective transpiration of a million leaf surfaces exchanges gas with the atmosphere, and the rhythm of the hydraulic chambers draws air in and pushes it out through the porous outer skin of the growth. It metabolizes. It excretes. It responds to stimulus — slowly, so slowly that a human observer would need hours to notice, but the tropisms are real and directional and adaptive. It grows toward what it needs and away from what it does not, and in this sense, primitive as it is, it has something like preferences.

What it does not have is a sense of self. The Greenword does not know it is the Greenword. It does not know it is anything. It is a process — a self-sustaining, self-repairing, self-expanding process that has been running for perhaps ten thousand years, perhaps longer, in a glaciated valley with basalt walls and thin cold air and enough sunlight to drive photosynthesis but not enough to make the surface anything other than dark. The process does not require awareness. It requires only the laws of chemistry and physics and the specific history of this valley, which at some point — after the ice retreated, after the bare rock was exposed, after the first spores and seeds arrived by wind or by bird or by sheer statistical improbability — became a place where growth was faster than decay and where the geometry of the depression was such that the growing mass could not expand outward and so expanded inward, filling the air itself.

The Greenword lives here the way a fire lives in a forest — it is a phenomenon, not a resident. But unlike a fire, it sustains itself. It builds the structure that allows it to continue. It generates its own heat, manages its own moisture, constructs its own vascular system, and when a section dies — when a trunk rots, when a fungal infection compromises a chamber — the surrounding growth closes over the wound, absorbs the dead material, and builds anew. It is a phoenix that does not need to burn. It simply grows through its own death.

## II. The Central Trunk

At the center of the valley, in the chamber where the passages converge and the light is brightest — which is to say, barely light at all, the pale gray-green of a winter sky seen through frosted glass — there stands the thing that was probably once a tree.

*Probably.* The taxonomy is impossible now. The central trunk is so old, so thoroughly colonized, so deeply integrated into the Greenword's vascular system that its own tissue may constitute only a fraction of its current mass. Its bark is cracked and ridged like the skin of something ancient, something that has been expanding and contracting for so long that the surface has developed its own topography — ridges and valleys, peaks and saddles, a miniature landscape of woody tissue that hosts its own micro-ecosystem of moss and lichen. But beneath the bark, the wood is different from the wood of the surrounding trunks. It is denser. Darker. It rings, if you tap it, with a resonance that suggests not a single tree but a composite structure — layer after layer of growth, generation after generation of the same genetic lineage building on its own remains, each cycle adding a new ring of tissue around the core until the trunk has become something that no single tree could ever become: a pillar, a column, a structural element so massive that the rest of the Greenword has organized itself around it the way a city organizes itself around a castle.

The central trunk is the heart. Not metaphorically. The hydraulic chambers scattered throughout the valley — the swollen, fused, pulsing structures that drive fluid through the vascular channels — are connected to this trunk by the largest and oldest pipelines. And the trunk itself pulses. Slowly. One cycle per minute, perhaps slower — the rhythm is hard to time because it is so deep, so subtle, that you measure it more by the vibration in the floor than by any visible movement. But the movement is there. The bark expands. The bark contracts. Fluid rises from the roots below and descends from the canopy above, and the trunk compresses it and releases it and compresses it again, and this is the engine that drives the entire system.

How old is it? The question is not answerable. The wood at the core is petrified, or nearly so — mineralized by centuries of silica-rich water percolating through the tissue, the cells replaced by stone so slowly that the structure was never disrupted. The outer layers are alive. The inner layers are geology. The central trunk is a fossil that is also a living organ, a thing that spans the boundary between biological time and geological time so completely that it belongs to both and neither. It may be the oldest living thing on the planet, or it may be one generation in an unbroken line of growth-and-decay-and-regrowth that has been continuous for longer than the valley has had its current shape. The ice came and went. The climate shifted. The basalt eroded. And the thing at the center of the Greenword kept pulsing.

It is not conscious. It is important to say this again, because standing in its chamber, feeling the pulse through the floor, watching the bark expand and contract with a rhythm that echoes your own heartbeat slowed by a factor of sixty, it is difficult not to project. The trunk does not think. It does not feel. It does not know you are there. It is a pump. It is a magnificent, ancient, beautiful pump, and it has been pumping since before the survey crews existed, and it will continue pumping after the survey crews are forgotten, and it does not care, because it cannot care, because caring is not a capacity that wood possesses, even wood as old and strange as this.

But it responds. This is the thing that unsettles. The trunk is covered in growth — moss, fern, vine, fungus — and that growth, like all growth in the Greenword, is tropic. It grows toward warmth. It grows toward carbon dioxide. And the chamber around the trunk is warmer than the surrounding passages, because the trunk's metabolic activity generates heat, and the chamber is richer in carbon dioxide than the ambient air, because the trunk respires. So the growth in the chamber is thicker, denser, more active than elsewhere. And when you enter the chamber, you bring warmth and carbon dioxide, and the growth responds. Not to you. To your outputs. You are a resource, not a visitor. The moss does not know you are a person. It knows only the chemical gradient.

And yet. And yet. The trunk pulses, and the growth around it shifts, and the passages leading to the chamber are narrower than they were, and you are aware — in a way that is not rational but is not entirely irrational either — that the central trunk is the closest thing the Greenword has to a self, and the self does not know you, but the system of which it is the center has registered your presence, and the system is adjusting, and the adjustment is not hostile but it is not neutral, and you are inside something that is alive and you cannot tell whether it is breathing or merely pumping and the distinction may not matter.

## III. The Dead Who Remain

There are no animals in the Greenword. No living ones. But there are traces.

The survey crews came five times. The records — those that survived, those that were transmitted before the transmissions stopped — indicate teams of between four and eight people, equipped for a three-to-five-day survey of the valley's botanical composition. They were botanists, mostly, and ecologists, and two geologists who were interested in the basalt formations. They came in summer, when the ridgeline was passable and the surface of the Greenword was partially thawed. They came with equipment — core samplers, gas analyzers, weather stations, tents, food for a week. They came and they entered the valley and they descended into the Greenword and they did not all come back.

Three of the five teams returned, partially. Two members of the first team, four of the second, one of the third. The fourth and fifth teams did not return at all. The survivors reported disorientation, passage collapse, the inability to retrace their steps, the slow realization that the landscape had changed around them in the hours they had spent inside. They reported the cold, the silence, the dampness. They reported the growth. They did not report hostility. They reported being lost. They reported being *absorbed* — not attacked, not consumed, but enclosed, surrounded, slowly embedded in a living matrix that did not distinguish between their bodies and any other warm object.

If you go deep enough — and you should not, but if you do — you will find them. Or traces of them. A boot, half-colonized by moss, the leather softened and partially digested by the tannin-rich environment but still recognizable, still shaped like a boot, still laced. A frame pack, its aluminum stays intact but its fabric replaced by a thin mat of liverwort that has grown through the weave and formed a continuous surface where the nylon once was. A weather station, its solar panel filmed with ice and lichen, its sensors colonized by fungal threads that have grown into the housing and through the circuit boards, not destroying them so much as *becoming* them, the electronic components embedded in a matrix of mycelium that pulsates faintly with the same hydraulic rhythm as everything else.

The people are harder to find. The Greenword does not digest quickly — the cold inhibits decomposition, and the tannins and alkaloids in the plant tissues are antimicrobial, preserving organic material rather than breaking it down. But the growth is patient, and the growth is constant, and over the years — five, ten, fifteen — the bodies have been incorporated. Not eaten. *Grown around.* Roots have threaded through clothing. Moss has colonized skin. Aerial vines have wrapped around limbs, not constricting but conforming, following the contours of the body the way they would follow the contour of a fallen log, and slowly, over seasons, the body has become a feature of the substrate, a slight rise in the floor of a passage, a bump in the living surface that the Greenword has grown over and through and into until the body is not separate from the growth but part of it.

You would not recognize them as people. You would see a thickening in the root mat, a place where the moss grows slightly taller, a discoloration in the substrate where the tannins have reacted with the remnants of fabric and bone. If you dug — and you cannot dig, the growth is too dense — you would find, perhaps, a femur, its surface etched by root acids, wrapped in a web of fine rootlets that have drawn minerals from the bone over years and incorporated them into the Greenword's own structure. The calcium is in the leaves now. The iron is in the vascular fluid. The carbon is in the trunk. The people who did not leave have become part of the body that holds this valley, and the body does not remember them, because the body does not remember anything, but the atoms that were once arranged in the shape of a botanist are now arranged in the shape of a wall, or a floor, or a pipeline, or a leaf, and they will remain so until the Greenword dies, which may be never.

They are still here. Not as ghosts. Not as memories. As *material.* The Greenword is a closed system, and nothing leaves, and the dead are still present in the way that the carbon from a prehistoric fern is present in a diamond — transformed, unrecognizable, but not gone. The survey crews are part of the Greenword now, and the Greenword grows, and in growing it carries them forward, not in the way a cemetery preserves the dead but in the way a river carries the dissolved minerals of the mountains to the sea — distributed, diffused, present in every drop but concentrated in none.

## IV. The Things That Are Not Plants

There are things in the Greenword that are not plants and are not animals and are not the survey crews. They are difficult to describe because they were never described by anyone who saw them clearly, and the Greenword itself does not know they are there, or if it knows, it does not know in any way that involves awareness. They exist in the gaps. In the spaces between the vascular channels, in the dead zones where the hydraulic pulse does not reach, in the pockets of trapped air that have been sealed off by the growth and have sat, unchanged, for decades or centuries.

They are not insects. They are not worms. They are not any form of life that has a name, because the names were given to things that live in the world outside the Greenword, and the world outside the Greenword does not have the conditions that produced these things. The conditions are: total darkness, near-freezing temperature, saturated humidity, an atmosphere enriched in carbon dioxide and depleted in oxygen by the respiration of the surrounding plant tissue, and a substrate of partially decayed plant matter that is rich in complex carbohydrates but poor in nitrogen. These conditions exist nowhere else. And in these conditions, over the time the Greenword has existed, something has emerged.

They are small. The largest are perhaps the size of a fingernail. The smallest are barely visible — specks of movement in the condensation that films the walls of the air pockets. They are not microbial, but they are not far from it. They are multicellular, but barely — simple structures, tubular bodies with a mouth at one end and an anus at the other and not much in between, no nervous system to speak of, no eyes, no appendages. They eat the decay. They eat the fungal threads that penetrate the air pockets. They eat each other. They breathe the carbon-dioxide-rich air through their skin, and their metabolism is anaerobic, or nearly so, and they are the only thing in the Greenword that moves with anything resembling rapidity.

If you were to break open an air pocket — and this is theoretically possible, though the growth would seal the breach within hours — you would see them. A writhing in the moisture. A shimmer of life in the droplets that cling to the walls. They would look like nematodes, or like the larvae of fungus gnats, but they are neither. They are something else. Something that has evolved in this sealed, cold, dark, wet world for as long as the Greenword has existed, adapting to conditions that exist nowhere else, becoming something that could exist nowhere else. They are endemic to the Greenword the way a vent worm is endemic to a hydrothermal vent — perfectly suited to a habitat that would kill anything else.

They do not know you are here. They do not know anything. They are the simplest form of animal life that still counts as animal life, and they exist in the Greenword the way mites exist in a mattress — unseen, uncounted, recycling the dead material of the larger structure, part of the system but so small that the system does not register them as separate from the substrate. They are the only fauna. They are the only things that move with intention, even if their intention is nothing more than chemotaxis, the blind turning toward food and away from waste. They are the only things in the valley that are not the Greenword, and the Greenword does not know they are there, and they do not know the Greenword is there, and they are alive, and they are here, and they have been here for perhaps as long as the trunk has been pulsing.

## V. The Visitors

And then there is you. And whoever comes after. And whoever came before and left, the three who returned from the first team, the four from the second, the one from the third. The visitors. The temporary residents. The warm things that move through the Greenword and leave trails of carbon dioxide and heat and footstep-compressions in the root mat, and who are, for the hours or days they spend inside, the only things in the valley that are aware of the valley.

You live here in the way a blood cell lives in a vein — passing through, not resident, part of the system only transiently and only in the sense that the system processes you the way it processes everything. The Greenword does not want you. The Greenword does not not-want you. The Greenword grows toward you because you are warm and the growth is tropic, and when you leave, the growth fills the space you occupied, and the space you occupied becomes part of the body, and the body continues.

But for the time you are here — and you are here now, in the chamber with the central trunk, in the cold and the silence and the diffuse gray-green light — you are the only thing in this valley that is *experiencing* the valley. The trunk pulses but does not feel. The growth shifts but does not perceive. The nameless things in the air pockets move but do not know. You are the consciousness of the Greenword, the only awareness it has ever had of itself, and you are aware of it, and it is not aware of you, and this asymmetry is the strangest thing about the place — that something so vast and so alive and so ancient should be unconscious of itself, and that something so small and so brief and so fragile should be the one to witness it.

You live here for now. For the hours you spend in the valley, you are a resident. You are a feature of the landscape, a warm spot in the cold, a disturbance in the gradient. The Greenword will grow toward you. The passages will narrow behind you. The condensation will film your gear, and the moss will begin to colonize the still surfaces, and the roots will extend, millimeter by millimeter, toward the warmth of your body. You are being incorporated, slowly, patiently, and the incorporation is not hostile. It is the only process the Greenword knows. It grows toward what it needs, and it needs warmth and carbon, and you are both.

You will leave, or you will not. If you leave, the Greenword will close behind you within a day. The passages will seal. The traces of your passage — the compressed root mat, the broken tendrils, the faint chemical signature of your sweat and breath — will be absorbed into the growth within a week. Within a month, there will be no evidence you were here. If you do not leave, you will join the survey crews. You will become material. You will become part of the body. Your calcium will go into the leaves. Your carbon will go into the trunk. The Greenword will grow through you and around you and into you, and you will be here, in the valley, in the body, for as long as the body endures, which may be longer than anything else alive on this planet.

The Greenword lives here. The things in the air pockets live here. The dead live here, distributed and unrecognizable. And you live here, for now, briefly, the only mind in a body that has no mind of its own, aware of the pulse, aware of the cold, aware of the slow, patient, absolute growth that surrounds you on every side and that will continue, with or without you, with the indifference of something that has never needed to care.
