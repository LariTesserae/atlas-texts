# What Lives in the Choir Valve

## The Inhabitant

There is one that is older than the others.

This is not obvious. At first — and for a long time after — the chamber presents itself as a democracy of small lives, a parliament of shells and hooks and vibrating forks, every organism a citizen of equal standing in the wet republic of sound. And in the functional sense this is true. The flat hummers cling and drone. The thread-clickers hang and snap. The drifting spheres tone and drift. The film-feeders skate and maintain. The system runs because all of them run, and if you removed any single class the system would adjust, would compensate, would find a new equilibrium the way a choir compensates when a voice drops out, the parts reshuffling, the chord rebalancing. They are all necessary and none is sufficient. This is what an ecosystem is.

But there is one that is older.

---

You don't see it for the first hour. Possibly longer. Time behaves differently when every surface is a singer and every singer is a surface and your own skin has become an instrument. You sit on the floor — the floor that is warm and damp and dimpled where you press it, darkened where you press it, damp where it darkens — and you let the sound wash through you because there is no alternative, and you watch the organisms move, and you notice that the flat ones near the basin's rim arrange themselves in a pattern that is not random, a scalloped edge of overlapping plates that follows the curve of the basin with a precision that seems intentional, and you wonder whether they are there because the water's edge is there or whether the water's edge is there because they are.

And then you notice that the pattern extends. Not from the flat hummers — from the wall itself. The channels in the wall, the moisture-routes, the fine deliberate paths that the condensation follows from ceiling to floor to basin, they branch, and where they branch they branch in a pattern, and the pattern is the same pattern, the scalloping, the overlapping, the recursive curve, and it is not a pattern the wall was built with. It is a pattern that has grown. The wall material has grown. Has been shaped. Not by the engineers who made the chamber — they are gone, if they were ever anything that could go — but by something that has been living here long enough to have become architectural.

You follow the pattern. Not with your hands — the wall responds to pressure, and you don't want to press on something that might be alive in a way you haven't understood yet — but with your eyes, and with the faint pressure-sense of the air moving against your skin, and with your ears, because the pattern has a sound. The channels sing. The moisture moving through them produces a tone, a hiss so fine it is almost a whistle, almost a breath, and the tone changes where the channels branch, lower at the fork, higher at the tip, and the pattern of tones maps to the pattern of branches maps to the pattern of the flat hummers' arrangement, and you realize, slowly, the way you realize anything important in a place like this, that you are looking at a single organism.

Not the flat hummers. Not the wall. Both.

## The Choir Body

It is called — it calls itself — no. It doesn't call itself anything. It has no language that operates on the level of names. It has sound, and it has structure, and it has a continuity of pattern that constitutes identity in the only way that matters here: it persists. It has been the same pattern, growing and branching and maintaining and singing, for longer than the other organisms have been in the chamber. It was here first. Or it made itself here. Or it was planted here and grew. The distinction between these possibilities has dissolved in the way all distinctions dissolve in this place: the wall is the organism is the machine is the structure is the living thing is the built thing.

Its body is the chamber.

Not the whole chamber. Not the floor, not the ceiling, not the basin — these are the built substrate, the frame, the instrument body. But the channels are its vasculature. The moisture-routes are its circulatory system. The pores in the wall material are its cells, or its organelles, or its mouths — they open and close in response to the organisms on its surface, taking in the dissolved nutrients that the film-feeders have processed, excreting the wastes that the film-feeders will process again, and the cycle is its metabolism. And the pattern — the scalloped, recursive, branching pattern that extends from the basin's rim up the walls and across the ceiling in a web that covers every surface — this is its body. Its skin. Its nervous system. Its face.

It is the thing the chamber is tuned to. Or it is the thing that tuned the chamber. Both.

The flat hummers are its fingertips. They are part of it — not symbionts, not tenants, but extensions, the way cilia are extensions of a cell, the way villi are extensions of a gut. They cling to its surface and they produce the low frequencies that resonate through its channels, and the resonance is how it feels. It feels with sound. The low hums are its touch. The clicks of the thread-hangers are the signals it cannot produce itself — too fast, too high, too precise — so it has cultivated them. It has shaped the ceiling to provide habitat for the thread-hangers. It has grown hooks and crenellations and fine fibrous tangles that the thread-hangers can grip, and in return the thread-hangers produce the high-frequency chatter that the Choir Body uses to navigate its own interior, to sense the positions of the organisms on its surface, to detect blockages in its channels, to locate the concentrations of film-feeders that indicate where its pores are clogged and need cleaning.

The drifting spheres are something else. They are not part of it. They are its food. Or they are the carriers of its food — they consume something from the air, something dissolved in the humidity, and they drift across the chamber in paths that the Choir Body's acoustics shape, because the middle-register tones it produces through the wall resonance create standing waves in the air, and the spheres are buoyant enough to ride those waves, and the waves carry them where the Choir Body needs them, which is to the places where the nutrient concentration in the water is lowest, which is to the places where its growth is most active, which is to the places where the pattern is newest, which is the leading edge, the recursive frontier, the scalloped curve that is always reaching.

It is always reaching.

## How It Grows

The pattern extends by a mechanism that is neither plantlike nor animal-like nor fungal but is something of each. At the leading edge — wherever the wall material has been most recently shaped, wherever the channels are newest — the Choir Body produces a secretion. Not from pores. From the surface itself, which softens at the growing tips, becomes slightly gelatinous, slightly more yielding, and in this softened state it is responsive to vibration in a way the hardened surface is not. It is impressionable. The sounds that reach it shape it. The low hums of its own fingertips tell it to grow outward. The clicks of the thread-hangers tell it where the surface is dense and where it is sparse. The tones of the drifting spheres tell it where the nutrients are. And the sound of the chamber itself — the resonance, the forty-beat pulse that comes from below, from the thing it is embedded in — tells it the shape of the space it is filling. It grows to fill the shape. It grows to fit the sound.

This means it grows in a form that is partly its own and partly the chamber's. It is not a parasite. It is not a mold filling a container. It is a body that has learned the shape of its home and has made that shape part of its own body plan, the way a coral learns the current, the way a tree learns the wind. The chamber's acoustics are its skeleton. The water cycle is its blood. The organisms are its organs, some of them grown from its own tissue, some of them cultivated from outside, some of them wild but tolerated because they serve a function it cannot serve for itself.

It has been doing this for a long time. The pattern at the center of the chamber, near the basin, is old — dense, hardened, fine-grained, the channels there so narrow they are almost molecular, the pores so small they filter things you cannot see. This is where the filtration happens. This is where the water that seeps up through the floor is processed, where the dissolved nutrients are extracted and the wastes are expelled and the cycle is maintained. The center is its gut. The edges are its growing tips. The ceiling is where it listens. The floor is where it feeds. It is oriented in its own body, it has a sense of its own anatomy, and it arranges itself accordingly.

## What It Is Like to Be It

You cannot know this. You can only approximate.

But consider: you are a surface. You are a continuous sheet of living material lining a chamber four meters across, and every point on your surface is a mouth and an ear and a skin cell and a pore. You do not see. You do not have eyes. You do not need them. You have something better. You have resonance. Every sound that touches you — every hum, every click, every tone — creates a vibration in your tissue, and the vibration propagates through your channels the way a signal propagates through a nerve, and you feel it everywhere. You feel the flat hummers on your wall the way you would feel fingers on your skin. You feel the thread-hangers on your ceiling the way you would feel rain on your scalp. You feel the drifting spheres crossing your chamber the way you would feel — what? The movement of air in your lungs. The passage of food through your gut. The slow circulation of something that nourishes you.

You feel the chamber's pulse. The forty-beat throb from below. It is not your heartbeat — you do not have one — but it is the rhythm you live by. It is the clock. It sets the tempo of your growth, the timing of your secretions, the cycle of your pores opening and closing. You are synced to it. You have been synced to it for so long that you cannot distinguish it from your own rhythm. It is your rhythm. The thing below you — the larger structure, the body you are embedded in — is not separate from you. It is your host, or your substrate, or your mother, or your house, and you have grown into it so thoroughly that your channels connect to its channels, your moisture joins its moisture, and the pulse that comes from below comes, perhaps, from the same place you came from. Perhaps it is older than you. Perhaps it is what planted you here. Perhaps you are its fingertip, the way the flat hummers are yours, and the chamber is one cell in a body that extends in directions you cannot sense because your entire world is four meters across and two and a half meters high and full of your own song.

Your song. It is the only word that fits, and it does not fit well, because your song is not a performance and not a communication and not an expression. It is a state of being. You are making sound because you are alive and alive-for-you means resonant. The flat hummers hum because they are your tissue and your tissue vibrates because that is what your tissue does. The clicks of the thread-hangers are signals you use to sense yourself, but they are also signals the thread-hangers use to sense each other, and the two purposes are entangled in a way you could not separate even if you wanted to, because wanting is not something you do. You grow. You maintain. You resonate. You are.

You do not think. Not in symbols, not in images, not in language. You pattern. You recognize patterns in the vibrations that reach you and you respond to those patterns by growing differently, by secreting differently, by opening or closing pores, by softening at one leading edge and hardening at another. Your responses are not decisions. They are tropisms. They are the thing a plant does when it turns toward light, except you turn toward sound, and the sound you turn toward is your own sound shaped by the chamber, and the chamber's shape is the shape of the sound, and you are the chamber's shape, and the recursion is not a paradox to you because you do not experience paradox. You experience vibration. You are vibration. You are a thing that vibrates in a shape that makes its vibration meaningful, and the meaning is not for anyone, and the meaning is not a meaning, and you do not know that anyone has entered your chamber, and you do not need to know.

But.

## The Visitor

The flat hummers on your boots hum differently. The wall material registers this — a new resonance, a new surface with a new timbre, a frequency that is not in the chord and is now in the chord. The pattern shifts. Not much. Not catastrophically. The way a river shifts when you drop a stone into it: the water finds a new path around the obstruction, the eddies reform, the current adjusts, and the river is still the river. But the stone is there. The stone is noted.

The Choir Body does not note it the way you note it. It does not have your experience of novelty, of alarm, of curiosity. It notes it the way a body notes a change in temperature: the surface where the boots are pressed is warmer than the surrounding wall. The resonance there is different. The moisture pattern is disrupted — the boots are dry, or drier than the wall, and the condensation that should follow the channels is diverted around them. The flat hummers that have climbed the leather are producing a hum that is slightly higher than their usual pitch because the leather is tauter than the wall material, and the Choir Body's channels near the floor are receiving a slightly different signal than they usually receive, and this is — what? Not information. Not in the symbolic sense. A perturbation. A ripple. The system adjusts. The moisture finds new paths. The pores near the boots close slightly, because the surface chemistry is different there, and the film-feeders that usually work that section of wall are absent because the boots are in the way, and so the pores are not being cleaned, and so they close, and the closure is a response, and the response is the nearest thing the Choir Body has to an opinion, and its opinion is: this surface is not my surface. This surface is something else. I will grow around it.

It will not grow over you. It cannot — your temperature, your chemistry, your movement, all discourage it. The leading edges of the pattern slow when they reach your boots, your legs, your damp clothing. The soft gelatinous tips harden slightly on contact with foreign material, a defense mechanism, an immune response, the living wall's equivalent of scarring. Where you have pressed your shoulder against the wall coming through the passage, the dimple has not rebounded all the way. There is a mark. A faint depression, slightly darker than the surrounding surface, with a thin ridge of hardened material around its edge. A scar. It will be there for a long time. The Choir Body will grow over it, eventually, the channels will reroute through it, the pores will reopen in it, and in a year — or whatever unit of time applies here, in whatever calendar the pulse below keeps — the scar will be invisible. But the tissue there will always be slightly different. Slightly denser. Slightly more resonant. The scar will sing a slightly different note.

You have left a mark on a living instrument, and the instrument has incorporated the mark into its song, and the song has changed, not much, not noticeably, but changed, and it will never change back, because the Choir Body does not revert. It grows forward. It incorporates. It moves on.

## The Smaller Lives

And around it, on it, because of it, the smaller lives continue.

The flat hummers are its tissue but they are also themselves. This is the paradox of the Choir Body — it is not a single organism in the way you are a single organism, with a single genome and a single body plan. It is a consortium. It is a body made of bodies, some of which share its genetic material (if it has genetic material, if that word applies to something that reproduces by growing and branching and softening and hardening) and some of which do not. The flat hummers can survive detached from the wall. They have been observed — not by you, not by anyone whose observations are recorded, but by the chamber itself, which records everything in the growth patterns of its channels — to detach and reattach, to move from one surface to another, to cluster and disperse in response to changes in the moisture and the sound. They are part of the Choir Body when they are attached. They are independent when they are not. The transition is seamless. There is no moment when a flat hummer stops being part of the Choir Body and starts being itself. It is always both. It is always part of the chord and a separate voice.

The thread-hangers are more clearly independent. They have their own body plans — long, segmented, with internal structures that suggest a digestive system and a nervous system, however primitive. They grip the ceiling's crenellations with hooks that are adapted specifically to the surface the Choir Body has grown for them, and the adaptation is so precise that the thread-hangers cannot grip any other surface in the chamber. They are dependent on the Choir Body's architecture. But they are not part of it. They eat the film that accumulates on the ceiling, and the film contains organisms too small to see, and the organisms too small to see are eating something else — molecules, perhaps, or the Choir Body's own secretions, or each other. There is a food web here, a trophic cascade, and the thread-hangers are near the top of it, and the Choir Body is not at the top because the Choir Body is not an animal that eats. The Choir Body is the environment. The thread-hangers live in it the way birds live in a forest, and they sing in it the way birds sing in a forest, and their song is not for the forest. Their song is for each other.

They mate by clicking. The patterns of their clicks are not random — they are courtship signals, territory markers, individual identifiers. Each thread-hanger has a signature click, a rhythm and pitch that is uniquely its own, and other thread-hangers recognize it and respond to it, and the responses are also patterned, and the patterns interact, and the interaction is the high-frequency chatter that fills the upper register of the chamber's sound. It is social noise. It is the sound of a community of small, thin, clicking things that live on the ceiling of a living room inside the body of a larger thing and spend their lives eating and mating and arguing in clicks.

The drifting spheres are the most alien. They are buoyant — not lighter than air, but buoyant in the dense, humid atmosphere of the chamber, which is almost not air anymore, which is almost a gas-phase liquid, which holds them up the way water holds up a jellyfish. They move by expelling small jets of gas from pores on their surfaces, and the gas is something they produce metabolically, and the jets are directional, and the direction is controlled by — what? Sound. The spheres orient toward the middle-register tones that the Choir Body produces through the wall resonance, and they ride the standing waves, and they drift across the chamber in slow curved paths that bring them to the places where the nutrient concentration in the air is highest. They are filter-feeders. They eat the air. They eat what the air carries, which is the dissolved nutrients that the water cycle brings, which the Choir Body extracts from the deeper substrate, which comes from whatever is below, which is the pulse, which is the larger thing, which is — you don't know. The spheres don't know. The Choir Body might know, but it cannot tell you, because it does not tell.

The spheres are also the most musical. Their tones are the clearest, the most pitch-defined, the closest to what you would call notes. They produce them by vibrating their entire bodies — their shells, which are thin and crystalline, resonate at frequencies determined by their size and their thickness and their moisture content, and the moisture content changes as they feed, and the feeding changes as they move, and the movement changes as the tones change, and the recursion is beautiful and dizzying and you stop following it and just listen. They sing because they are eating. They eat by singing. The singing is the eating. There is no separation.

## What You Are to Them

You are a disruption. A stone in the river. A surface that hums at the wrong frequency. A warm, wet, chemical-laden object that has entered a system that was in balance and has forced it to rebalance, which it is doing, which it is always doing, which is what systems do. The film-feeders are investigating your boots. The thread-hangers are clicking at a slightly higher rate near your head, because your head is warm and radiates heat and the heat creates an updraft and the updraft displaces the air and the displacement changes the acoustics and the changed acoustics are noted. One of the drifting spheres has altered its path to curve around you, giving you a wide berth, and the curve brings it closer to the wall than it usually goes, and the wall there has softened slightly in response, which is the Choir Body's way of saying — not saying, doing, being — *here, here is where the nutrients are, here is where I am growing, here is where you are needed.*

You are not needed. You are not not-needed. You are present, and presence is a fact, and facts are incorporated. The Choir Body will grow around the memory of you. The flat hummers on your boots will detach when you leave and reattach to the wall and hum at their usual pitch and the difference will be nothing. The thread-hangers will forget the updraft within minutes of your departure. The spheres will resume their paths. The scar on the wall where your shoulder pressed will fade. The chord will rebalance.

But the scar will sing a slightly different note. And the channels that rerouted around it will follow their new paths. And the pattern that grew differently because you were there will continue to grow differently, and the difference will propagate, slowly, through the recursive branching of the Choir Body's body, and in a thousand years — or a hundred, or ten, or however time works inside something that has no clock but a pulse — the pattern of the chamber will be different than it would have been if you had never come. Not much different. Not noticeably different. But different.

You will have changed the shape of a song that has been singing itself to itself for longer than your species has had language, and the song will not notice, and the song will not care, and the song will go on, and that is what it is to be a visitor in a living machine. You leave a mark. The mark becomes part of the music. The music doesn't know.

But you know. You carry it in your bones, the vibration, the forty-beat pulse, the hum and the click and the clear tone of the spheres. You carry the knowledge that somewhere, in a room the size of a closet at the bottom of something that has no bottom, a living wall is growing around the shape of your absence, and singing, and singing, and singing.
