# What Lives Here

---

The *veredai* come first to mind because they are the most visible, but they are not the most important. They are the surface text. They attach and detach and reattach with that soft definitive click, and they are so perfectly the color of the water that you begin to suspect they did not evolve this way for hiding but because the water is what they are made of, slightly compressed, given a shape and a will to hold it. A *veredai* is a leaf-thin diamond the size of a palm, translucent, and its translucency is not the delicate kind. It is the translucency of wax paper, of a lampshade, of something designed to diffuse light rather than transmit it cleanly. When one passes across a glowing wall, the light behind it does not sharpen; it smears. The *veredai* becomes a moving blur of slightly different blue, and then it settles, and the blur resolves, and the wall is as it was.

They have no eyes. They have no features. They are not faceless the way a worm is faceless — they are faceless the way a page is faceless, in a way that implies the face was never the point. Their undersides, where they grip the vascular walls, are covered in something that is not suction and not adhesion but a kind of gentle fusion: the tissue of the *veredai* and the tissue of the plant meet and partially interlock at the cellular level, so that detaching is not a peel but a slow unstitching, a deliberate loosening of connection that takes several seconds. You can feel the effort of it if you are close enough. The water shifts. There is a small tension, then a release, then the *veredai* drifts, tumbling slowly, adjusting its orientation with micro-contractions of its entire body — not swimming, not steering, just *leaning*, the way a leaf in a stream leans toward one bank — until it finds a new surface and begins to stitch itself down again.

What they feed on is not fully understood. The dissolved nutrients in the vascular flow are part of it. The microorganisms — the bacteria-analogues, the single-celled things that cloud the water when the light hits it right — are part of it. But there is a third component that the hermits know about and that the *veredai* do not advertise: they feed on the plant's own waste. The Maren Vered excretes through its lenticel pores and through its vascular walls, shedding cellular debris, spent proteins, fragments of tissue that have reached the end of their metabolic usefulness. This material is invisible in the water — too fine, too diffuse — but it is there, and the *veredai* collect it, and in collecting it they perform a service for the plant that the plant does not know it needs. They are its kidneys. Its liver. Its slow, patient filtration system, and they have been doing this work for so long that their body plan has forgotten any other way of being. They are not parasites. They are not guests. They are an organ that the plant grew externally, or that grew itself externally in response to the plant, and the distinction between these two origins has been irrelevant for millennia.

---

The shrimp-things are louder than they should be, and this is not an accident. They are a centimeter long, translucent, red — a red so vivid against the blue-green water that it seems aggressive, a tiny flag of difference planted in a world of sameness. They are red because they carry a compound in their chitinous shells that binds to oxygen, or whatever the local equivalent of oxygen is, and this compound is red for the same reason hemoglobin is red: it is doing work, and the work is visible. They cluster in the eddies behind the structural ribs where the current slows and the small particles they eat settle out of the flow. They eat constantly. Each one is a mouth on legs, or a mouth on something that functions as legs, and they move with a jerky, frantic energy that is utterly unlike the *veredai*'s patience or the plant's slowness. They are the fastest things in the Maren Vered, and this makes them the most alive, in the way that humans use the word *alive* — busy, urgent, going somewhere.

Their clicking is a byproduct of their feeding. They land on the plant tissue, feed, take off, land again, and each landing produces a click — a tiny, dry, percussive sound that is individually insignificant but collectively forms that rain-on-wood chatter that rises and falls with the currents. When the flow surges and scatters them, the silence they leave behind is striking. It is the sound of a thing missing, and you realize only then that their clicking has become part of your baseline, part of what the Maren Vered *is*, and without it the chamber sounds incomplete, like a sentence with a word removed.

They are food. The sinuous things in the deeper channels eat them — the shrimp-things' avoidance of channel mouths is learned, or instinctive, or simply the kind of knowledge that bodies carry without minds. A shrimp-thing that drifts too close to a channel mouth is caught in the faster current and carried inward, and the sinuous things take it with a motion that you feel as a disturbance in the water rather than see as an event. The shrimp-things do not seem to mind this in any way you can identify. Their populations are enormous, their reproduction is rapid, and the losses to predation are a rounding error in their arithmetic. They exist in such numbers that the word *individual* feels wrong for them. They are a population that happens to be broken into pieces, the way a beach is broken into grains.

---

The sinuous things in the deeper channels are the ones you think about latest and longest, because they are the ones you see least.

They are long. This is the first thing you can determine from the shadows that rest at the channel mouths — the partial bodies, the dark shapes against the glowing walls. A body visible for a meter or more, and then it curves into the channel and is gone, and the curve implies more body behind the curve. Three meters? Five? The channels are not large, and the things within them must be flexible in the way that a tube is flexible, bending without joints, or with joints so numerous they function as continuous flexibility. They move against the current, which is strong in the channels — the vascular flow is fastest where it is most constrained — and they move with a steady muscular effort that suggests not struggle but habit. They are built for the channels. The channels are their world, and the main chamber is not, and they do not enter it the way a fish does not enter the air.

They breathe through the structures along their flanks — the slow dilation and contraction, the pumping. It is not gills, exactly. It is more like the lenticel pores on the plant's walls: an opening that exchanges gas with the medium directly, without intermediary organs. This means they are simple, internally. They do not have the complexity of the *veredai*, which at least have a feeding mechanism and a locomotion mechanism and a mechanism for attachment. The sinuous things seem to have only two functions: moving and breathing. They eat, presumably — the shrimp-things disappear into the channels and do not come back — but you do not see them eat. You see them move, and you see them breathe, and that is nearly all.

But there is a third behavior, and it is the one that stays with you. Occasionally, two of them will rest at the same channel mouth, their bodies overlapping in the slow current, and they will remain there for a long time — an hour, more — not moving, not feeding, not doing anything that has a name. Their flanking structures pulse in synchrony. The water between them is still. And then one of them slides back into the channel, and the other follows, and the channel mouth is empty. You can call this mating if you want. You can call it nothing if you prefer. What it looks like is two long dark things resting together in the only open space they know, in the only light they will ever see, before returning to the dark they live in. It looks like the closest thing to tenderness that a body without a face can manage.

---

The symbionts are the least visible and the most essential. They are the light, and without them the Maren Vered would be dark, and if it were dark it would be a different place entirely — not a cathedral but a cave, not a medium but a void. They live inside the plant's cells, and they are too small to see, and their existence can only be inferred from the consequences: the glow, the response to pressure, the slow waves of brightness that travel the vascular channels like a blush. They are not the plant. They are not separate from the plant. They are the third thing — the thing that exists in the space between *self* and *other*, between *organism* and *environment*, and the Maren Vered is built from this third thing as much as it is built from its own tissue.

When you touch the wall and it brightens, it is the symbionts responding. They do not know you are there. They do not know they are responding. The pressure changes the shape of the cells they live in, and the change in shape triggers a metabolic shift, and the metabolic shift produces more of the phosphorescent compound, and the compound glows. It is mechanical in the strict sense: cause and effect, no intention. But it does not feel mechanical. It feels like the wall noticed you. It feels like the wall said something small and unimportant — *yes*, or *here*, or just *hm* — and then went back to what it was doing. This is anthropomorphism. This is the error. But the error is easy to make here, inside something alive, where every surface is warm and every surface responds to touch and the light moves in you like blood moves in skin.

---

The hermits are what lives here that is most like you.

There are not many of them. Three, perhaps four. They come and go through the root-gates on rafts that are barely rafts — woven fiber stretched over a frame of callused wood, the wood harvested from the outer hull where the plant has split and shed pieces of itself. The rafts are not for floating on the water. They are for carrying things: dried food, tools, small bundles of fiber that might be clothing or might be netting. The hermits swim. They are strong swimmers, adapted to the currents, and they know the rhythms of the Maren Vered's circulation the way a sailor knows tides — not intellectually but bodily, in the timing of the legs and the angle of the body against the flow. They enter when the current is moving inward through the root-gates, and they leave when it is moving outward, and they have no clocks and no calendars and do not need them.

They are not what you expected. They are not ascetics. They are not seekers. They do not come here for transcendence or purification or contact with something ancient and strange. They come here because the rooms are warm and the water is clean and the channels have food in them and nobody else comes here. They are practical people making a practical choice about where to be, and the strangeness of the Maren Vered is, for them, not strangeness but home — the way a house is not strange to the person who lives in it, even if the house is unusual, even if the house is a plant.

They do not speak. This is not a vow of silence. It is a simple response to the acoustic environment: the Maren Vered is too loud for speech, and they have been here long enough that speech does not occur to them as an option. Their hand signals are few and sufficient. *This way. Stop. Look. Good.* They do not have a signal for *bad* or for *danger*; the Maren Vered does not present dangers that require such signals. The currents are strong but predictable. The channel-things do not bite. The water is breathable. The plant does not care about them one way or the other. It is the safest place they know, which is why they are here.

They fish in the channels with the bone hooks from the old collections. They catch the shrimp-things, gathering them in handfuls, and they eat them raw, crunching the chitinous shells between their teeth. The shells are red, and their teeth are red after eating, and they wipe their mouths on the backs of their hands and the red smears and fades. They also catch the sinuous things occasionally — or rather, they hook them and pull them partway out of the channels, and the sinuous things thrash, and the hermits hold them and kill them with a stone and eat them too. The sinuous things are not common food. They are too large for one meal, and their flesh is dark and dense and has a texture that the hermits do not prefer. But they do not waste what they catch, and a single sinuous thing feeds a hermit for days.

They sleep in the callus rooms. The callus is warm and slightly yielding, not soft but not hard — the surface has a quality like thick leather, and it gives a fraction under weight and then holds. They curl on their sides. The rooms are small enough that they can brace themselves against opposite walls with hands and feet, which they do when the current shifts, and they sleep through the shifts, waking only enough to adjust and then dropping back. They sleep deeply. The sound of the Maren Vered is not noise to them; it is the sound of their environment being itself, and a environment that is being itself is a safe environment, and a safe environment is one you can sleep in.

They do not think about the plant. Or rather, they think about it the way you think about your own bones — with a distant, structural awareness that does not require attention. They know it is alive because they can feel the pulse in the walls and they know the callus is growing because they have to clear it back from the doorways of their rooms every few years, scraping it with stone tools, and they know the water is the plant's fluid because they have tasted it and found it different from the water outside, richer, warmer, faintly sweet. But they do not think about what the plant is, or what it means, or what it has done by becoming what it is. These are not questions that occur to them. The plant is. They are in it. The questions that occupy them are: where are the shrimp-things densest today, is the current shifting early or late, does the callus need clearing, is the dried food running low. These are enough questions. These are all the questions there are.

---

And there were others. Before the hermits, there were the carvers — the ones who cut the rooms from the vascular walls, who made the tools, who scratched the ideographic records into the callus. They are gone. The callus is erasing them. Their rooms are narrowing, their records are softening, their tools are settling into the scar tissue like stones settling into mud, and in another few centuries there will be nothing left of them but the shapes of the rooms themselves, and even those will be filling, closing, the way a wound closes when there is nothing in it.

The carvers were not the hermits. This is clear from the rooms — the carvers needed more space, more rooms, more connection. They carved thirty rooms and connected them with tunnels, and they kept tools and stored things and made records. They were a community, or at least a group. They lived here the way people live in a town — with proximity, with shared infrastructure, with some version of the knowledge that others are near. They are gone now, and the hermits are not their descendants. The hermits came later, found the rooms, found the tools, and use them without knowing who made them or why. The ideographic records on the callus walls are unreadable to the hermits, who look at them the way you might look at the grain of a piece of wood: as texture, not text.

Who were the carvers? The Maren Vered does not remember them. The callus that grew over their cuts does not preserve their intention, only their actions: *here a wall was cut, here a doorway was made, here a shelf was carved*. The plant responded to their presence the way it responds to everything — by growing, by filling, by slowly reclaiming the space they opened. It did not resist them. It did not welcome them. It did not know them. And they, in turn — did they know it? Did they understand that they were cutting into a living thing, that the walls they carved were vascular tissue, that the warmth they relied on was metabolic heat? Or did they simply find a material that could be carved, a space that could be occupied, and use it?

The records, unread, cannot say. But the rooms suggest something. They are not placed randomly. They follow the vascular architecture, clustering where the channels are smallest and the tissue is densest — the places where the structural integrity of the wall is highest, where a cut does not compromise the plant's circulation, where the healing callus will grow fastest and strongest. The carvers knew where to cut. They understood the plant's body well enough to work with it, not against it. They were not parasites. They were — and the word is inadequate but it is the word that comes — *gardeners*. They shaped the plant's growth by creating wounds that the plant would heal in specific ways, and the rooms they lived in were as much the plant's creation as theirs. A collaboration, though neither party knew the other was collaborating.

---

The things that live in the Maren Vered are not a community. They do not know each other. The *veredai* do not know the shrimp-things. The shrimp-things do not know the sinuous channel-dwellers. The sinuous channel-dwellers do not know the symbionts that light their rest at the channel mouths. The hermits do not know the carvers. The carvers did not know the plant. The plant does not know any of them.

And yet: the *veredai* clean the plant's waste. The shrimp-things feed the channel-dwellers and eat the microorganisms that might otherwise clog the vascular channels. The symbionts light the interior, which makes it possible for the hermits to see, which makes it possible for them to fish, which makes it possible for them to live here, which makes it possible for the carvers' rooms to be occupied, which makes it possible for the carvers' work to be maintained, however inadvertently, however temporarily. The hermits clear the callus from the doorways, which keeps the rooms open, which keeps the architecture intact, which — though no one intends this — preserves the record of the carvers' presence for a little longer than the plant would have on its own.

It is not a community. It is an ecology, which is a different thing and in some ways a better one. A community implies awareness, intention, the knowledge that others exist and matter. An ecology implies only this: that the actions of each organism affect the conditions under which the others live, and that the system as a whole maintains itself because the effects balance, because the waste of one is the food of another, because the wound heals and the scar becomes a room and the room becomes a home and the home is warm because the walls are alive.

The Maren Vered does not know it is any of this. The Maren Vered is a plant, doing what plants do: growing, circulating, exchanging gas and fluid and mineral with its environment, maintaining its form against the forces that would deform it. It is not a host, though things live in it. It is not a builder, though it is built. It is not a god, though it is vast and old and lit from within and patient beyond any human patience. It is a living thing, and living things do what they do, and what they do is make room — not intentionally, not with welcome, but simply by being large and warm and stable enough that other things find their way in and find what they need and stay.

---

What lives here is: a plant that does not know it is a place, and the things that have made themselves at home in the not-knowing, and the slow accumulation of their presence, which is also the plant's presence, which is also the light, which is also the sound, which is also the silence that the sound makes when you stop hearing it and start inhabiting it, which is what you do, eventually, if you stay long enough. You stop hearing the roar. You stop seeing the glow. You stop feeling the warmth of the walls. You become part of the ecology in the only way a transient thing can: by being affected, by affecting, by breathing the place and being breathed by it, and then by leaving, and by being forgotten, completely and without malice, by the thing that held you.
