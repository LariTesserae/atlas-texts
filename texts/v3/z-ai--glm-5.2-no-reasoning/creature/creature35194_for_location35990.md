# What Lives Here

---

The first thing to understand is that nothing here was born here. Not originally. Everything that lives in the Cistern of Weeping Iron is a descendant of something that came from outside — something that fell through a crack, washed in with a seep, was carried on the boots of the person who built the dome, or on the boots of the person after them, or the one after that. The place was sterile once, or near enough. New stone, new iron, clean water. That time is so far gone it has become geological. The life here has forgotten outside. Outside is a rumor. Outside is a direction that no longer exists.

What lives here now is the seventh or eighteenth or thousandth generation of things that have never known sunlight, never known dry air, never known a surface that was not iron or aggregate or water or another living thing. They have been shaped by this place the way water shapes stone — not quickly, not dramatically, but completely, and without leaving any record of the original form.

---

## The Film

Start with the walls, because the walls are where everything starts.

The biofilm that coats the aggregate is not one organism. It is a community — a fabric, a quilt, a parliament of organisms so intertwined that separating them kills most of them. There are iron-oxidizing bacteria at the base, the founders, the ones that first colonized the rust and made it edible. They are the reason the streaks on the walls move. They graze. They reproduce. They die and their remains feed the layer above them, which is a community of organisms that cannot eat iron directly but can eat the bacteria that eat iron, and above them a layer that eats the remains of those, and above them a layer of fungi — or something like fungi, something that grows in filaments and spreads in threads and produces spores that drift in the saturated air and settle on every available surface and become the next generation of film.

The film is thin. A few millimeters at its thickest, a fraction of a millimeter where it is new. But it covers everything. Every surface that holds moisture holds film, and every surface in the cistern holds moisture. The film is the skin of the place. It is what the cistern has grown over itself the way a wound grows a scab, except the wound was not a wound, and the scab is alive, and it does not want to heal because it is not broken. It is just becoming.

The bioluminescence comes from the middle layers of the film. Not all of it — some of the free-floating organisms in the water also produce light — but most of it. The light is produced by organisms that have no reason to produce light. There is nothing here that sees. The slugs are eyeless. The smaller organisms are too small to have eyes. You are the only thing that has ever entered this space and been able to perceive the light, and the light was here before you and will be here after you. It is not for you. It is not for anything. It is a byproduct of a metabolic process that has no name because the organisms that perform it have no name, and the light they produce is a consequence of chemistry that serves some other purpose — energy release, stress response, the management of a reaction that would otherwise damage the cell — and the fact that it is beautiful is irrelevant to everything except you.

It is beautiful. In the dark, in the wet, in the sound, the light pulses and the walls seem to breathe and the whole room clarifies for a moment and you see that the film is not uniform. It has texture. It has topography. There are ridges where the film has thickened over particularly rich deposits of iron, valleys where the water runs faster and the film cannot establish itself, channels and basins and small raised platforms that look — and this is your mind, not the film, your mind doing what it does — like streets and buildings and the layout of a city seen from above. The film has architecture. It is not architecture. It is growth. But the difference, in this place, has narrowed.

The film moves. Over the course of hours, you can see it move. The ridges shift. The valleys deepen or fill. A patch that was dark becomes lighter as new growth covers old growth, and a patch that was light becomes dark as the old growth dies and is consumed by the new. The film is a landscape in time-lapse, except the time-lapse is happening at a speed you can perceive if you are patient, and you are patient, because you are still here, and the room is teaching you patience whether you want to learn or not.

## The Slugs

They do not have a name. Call them slugs because that is the closest shape, the nearest analogy, the word that fails least badly. They are not slugs. They are not larvae. They are not anything that has been classified, because no one has ever taken one out of this place, and if someone did, it would die. It would die the way a deep-sea fish dies when brought to the surface — not from the pressure change, though there is that, but from the absence of the thing it is made of. The slugs are made of this place. Their bodies are saturated with iron. Their tissues are dense with minerals that they have absorbed from the water and the film and the iron beams they graze on. They are, in a sense, made of the cistern. They are the cistern eating itself and walking around.

They are pale. Translucent. You can see their internal structures if the light is right, and the light is occasionally right — when the bioluminescence pulses and the water is still enough to see through, you can see the dark thread of their gut, the slow pulse of whatever serves them as a heart, the branching gills that extend from their flanks like the pages of wet books. The gills are how they breathe. They filter the iron-rich water through structures that are not gills in the vertebrate sense but serve the same function — gas exchange, mineral exchange, the slow transaction between inside and outside that defines being alive. The gills are beautiful. They fan and fold and catch the pale light and for a moment, in the pulse, the slugs look like something lit from within.

There are perhaps thirty of them. Perhaps forty. It is hard to count because they move and because some are always beneath the surface, in the deeper water, in the parts of the cistern you cannot see. The largest is the length of your longest finger. The smallest is the length of your smallest fingernail. They are not the same species — or rather, they are not the same kind. There are at least three forms: a large, slow form that grazes the iron beams; a smaller, faster form that grazes the film on the walls; and a third form, rarely seen, that lives in the deep water and surfaces only when the light pulses and the water is still. The third form is the most translucent. You can see almost everything inside it. It looks like a ghost of a slug, a suggestion of a slug, a slug that is almost but not quite committed to being solid.

They move with a confidence that is either admirable or terrifying, depending on how you think about it. They have never known a predator. They have never known a threat. The cistern is stable — it has been stable for longer than they have been alive, longer than their species has been a species, longer than the concept of species has applied. They exist in a world without danger, without change, without anything that would require them to be afraid. They are not afraid. They do not know what fear is. They move through the dark water and across the iron beams and along the walls with the unhurried calm of things that have never been hurt, and watching them, you realize that this is what it looks like to live without threat, and it looks like the way water moves — inevitable, purposeless, and completely at peace.

They do not react to you. You are too large to be food. You are too large to be a threat, in their experience, because they have no experience of threats. You are a presence. You are a shadow that falls across them when the light from the entrance shifts. You are a vibration in the lattice that they feel through their bodies and do not interpret. You are, to them, a feature of the landscape, a new pillar, a temporary beam. They graze around your feet. They pass over your boots if you stand still long enough, and you stand still long enough, because standing still is what this place does to you. A slug crosses your boot. It is warm. You did not expect that. It is warmer than the water, warmer than the air, warmer than the iron. It is alive and it is metabolizing and it is producing heat and you can feel it through the leather, a small warm weight moving with small warm confidence across the surface of something it does not recognize as foreign because it does not have the concept of foreign.

## The Smaller Things

Between the film and the slugs, there is a world of things too small to see as individuals but too large to ignore as a collective. The water is not clear. It has a texture, a density, a visible weight. Hold your hand in it — and you have held your hand in it, because you are still here and curiosity has not killed you yet — and you can feel the life in it. Not as a sensation. As a resistance. The water is thicker than water. It moves differently. It carries things.

There are clouds in the water. Not sediment — the water is too still for sediment to remain suspended. These clouds are alive. They are colonies, swarms, aggregations of organisms that exist at the boundary of visibility — visible as movement, as texture, as a shift in the water's density that drifts and recombines and disperses and reforms. They are eating. They are being eaten. They are the intermediate layer of the ecosystem, the things that eat the film and are eaten by the slugs, the things that eat the dead slugs and are eaten by the film. They are the middle of the food web, the connective tissue, the thing that makes the cistern an ecosystem rather than a collection of organisms.

Some of them glow. The bioluminescence in the water comes from these — small, drifting, pulsing points of pale blue-green light that move with the water and do not move with the water, that have their own motion, their own direction, their own intent. They are not coordinated. Each one pulses on its own schedule, its own rhythm, its own private logic. But when enough of them pulse near-simultaneously — and this happens, and this is what clarifies the room — the effect is not of individual lights but of a single light seen through a prism, a single breath exhaled by a single room.

There are things that live on the slugs. You see them when a slug passes near the entrance light — small, dense specks on the slug's surface, moving, repositioning, feeding on the slug's mucus or its dead skin or the minerals that accumulate on its body. The slugs do not seem to notice. The things on the slugs have things on them — smaller things, barely visible, existing at a scale where the boundary between organism and molecule becomes a matter of opinion. The cistern has layers of life the way it has layers of rust, and each layer feeds the next, and the deepest layers are too small to see and too important to ignore and are, in their way, the foundation of everything that happens here.

## The Things on the Beams

The crust on the iron beams is its own ecosystem, separate from the film on the walls and the life in the water, though connected to both. The crust is thicker than the film — in places, an inch or more, built up over centuries of deposition and growth. It is layered. The lowest layer is iron oxide, rust, the product of the beams' slow reaction with water. Above that, calcium carbonate deposited by the water as it evaporates and recondenses — except it does not evaporate here, not truly, not in the traditional sense; the humidity is too high, the air too saturated. What happens is more subtle: the water cycles through the crust, through the organisms, and in the process of being metabolized and excreted, it deposits minerals. The crust is built by life. It is a biological structure. It is a reef.

The organisms in the crust are different from the organisms in the film. They are denser, slower, more mineralized. They live in the spaces between the iron oxide and the calcium carbonate, in pores and channels that they have created by their own activity, and they process the water as it moves through. They are the filters. They are the reason the water on the beams is warm. They are the reason the water on the beams tastes different from the water on the walls — more processed, more biological, more like the inside of a mouth. The water on the beams has been through something. It has been inside something. It has been metabolized and excreted and metabolized again, and by the time it drips from the end of a beam into the pool below, it is not the same water that arrived on the beam. It has been changed. It carries the signature of the organisms that processed it — their waste products, their minerals, the chemical traces of their living — and this signature is what the slugs taste when they graze the beams, and what you taste when you touch it to your tongue, and what the room tastes when it tastes itself.

The crust is warm. This is the strangest thing about it, the thing that takes the longest to accept. The beams are warm. Not hot. Not even comfortably warm. But warmer than the air, warmer than the water, warmer than the stone. The organisms in the crust are metabolizing iron — eating it, breaking it down, extracting energy from the chemical bonds — and the byproduct of this metabolism is heat. A tiny amount of heat from each organism, multiplied by uncountable organisms, multiplied by centuries of activity, added up and accumulated in the crust and the iron beneath it. The cistern is, in a small and accidental way, heated by its own life. The warmth is the life. The warmth is the sum of all the eating and breathing and excreting that has happened here for longer than anyone has been counting, and it rises from the beams like a breath, and the air carries it, and the dome holds it, and the room is warm the way a body is warm — not from a furnace, not from a fire, but from the accumulated business of being alive.

## The Deep Water

No one has measured the depth of the water beneath the lattice. The lattice extends to the walls on all sides, and the gaps between the beams are narrow — too narrow for a human hand, too narrow for a slug, wide enough only for water and the smallest organisms. What is beneath the lattice is unknown. The water is dark. The light does not reach it. The bioluminescence comes from within it, from organisms suspended at various depths, but the light they produce illuminates only their immediate surroundings, and the depths remain dark.

There is something down there. You can hear it. Not with your ears — the sound of the water is too loud, too constant, too layered for you to hear anything below it. But you can hear it with your body, with your feet on the lattice, with the vibrations that come through the iron. There is a movement in the deep water. A large movement. Not the movement of the slugs, which you can feel when they cross a beam near your foot — a small, specific vibration, a tapping. This is different. This is a slow, massive displacement, a shifting of volume, a thing that moves water the way a boat moves water, by being in it and by being large enough that its presence is felt as pressure rather than seen as shape.

You do not know what it is. You will not know what it is. The lattice will not let you see, and the water will not clarify, and the thing — if it is a thing, if it is not just the water moving in patterns you are misinterpreting, the way you misinterpret the sound as speech — the thing does not surface. It does not interact with the life above the lattice. It exists, if it exists, in a separate zone, a deeper cistern beneath the cistern, a space that is part of this space but not accessible from this space, and whatever lives there lives there without reference to anything above.

Unless it is the slugs. Unless the third form — the translucent one, the one that surfaces rarely, the one that looks like a ghost — unless that form lives in the deep water and is the large movement you feel. A slug larger than the others, larger than your finger, larger than your hand, living in the dark beneath the lattice, surfacing only when the light pulses and the water is still. You do not know. You cannot know. The cistern has a secret, and the secret is its depth, and the depth is where the largest thing lives, and the largest thing is the thing that you will think about later, when you have left, when you are dry, when the sound of the water has faded and the taste of iron has left your tongue. You will think about the thing in the deep water. You will wonder if it was there. You will wonder what it was. You will not wonder if it was alive, because everything here is alive, everything here has been alive for longer than you have been alive, and the deep water is the deepest part of the alive, and the thing in it — if it is a thing — is the most alive of all.

## What Lives Here

What lives here is a room. Not the organisms — not the film, not the slugs, not the clouds in the water, not the crust on the beams, not the thing in the deep. The room. The cistern itself is alive, in the way that a reef is alive, in the way that a forest is alive, in the way that any system of interdependent organisms and mineral substrate and water and time is alive. The room breathes. The room metabolizes. The room excretes. The room has a temperature and a rhythm and a sound and a light, and all of these are products of its life, and all of these are the life, and the life is the room.

The person who built the dome — who shaped the ceiling so that the sound would collect and return — did not know they were building a body. They were building a cistern. They were building a place to hold water. But water is the medium of life, and iron is the substrate of life, and time is the condition of life, and they built a place with all three, and life came, and life stayed, and life became the place.

What lives here is a process. A cycle. Water falls on the walls and picks up iron and feeds the film and the film feeds the clouds and the clouds feed the slugs and the slugs feed the things on the slugs and the things on the slugs die and feed the film and the film processes the iron and the iron feeds the crust and the crust processes the water and the water falls again. It has been falling for centuries. It will fall for centuries more. The cycle does not need you. The cycle does not know you. The cycle is the room, and the room is the cycle, and you are standing in it, and you are the first thing in centuries to stand in it and understand that it is standing too.

The cistern weeps. The walls cry rust. The beams excrete processed water. The slugs graze with the calm of things that have never known fear. The film moves in its century-long migration. The light pulses at intervals that suggest breathing. The dome returns the sound of all of it, slightly changed, as though considering it, as though the room is listening to itself the way you are listening to the room, and the thing in the deep water moves, and the water cycles, and the iron is eaten and deposited and eaten again, and the room is alive, and the room does not need you, and the room receives you, and you are still here, and you are small, and the room is complete.
