# What Lives Here

The first answer is: the column.

Not the people. The people come and go — they are tidal, seasonal, pulled by some schedule written into their bones rather than their calendars. They arrive when the pass opens, when the snow recedes from the crack, when something in the length of the light or the smell of the meltwater tells them it is time. They come in small groups. Never many. Five or six, maybe, at the height of a working season. They do not announce themselves. They simply appear on the terraces with their stone picks and their woven baskets and they begin, without ceremony, to work. They already know what needs doing. Someone remembers. The knowledge is not written down. It lives in the way a hand finds the right angle to strike, the way an eye reads the water's flow and knows where to cut, where to deepen, where to leave alone. It is knowledge that has been passed from body to body for longer than any of them can count, because counting is not the point. The point is the doing.

They are not a separate people. Or if they once were, they are not anymore. They are an activity. They are what the mountain does when it needs hands.

---

But the column.

The column is the oldest thing here, and the youngest. It is both, because it is *becoming*. It is still arriving. You could watch it for a year and see nothing change, but if you watched it for a hundred years you would see it rise — a fraction of an inch, a new layer of pale mineral deposition, a new skin for the moss to colonize. It is a slow creature. Slower than a tree, slower than a stalactite, slower than anything alive in a way you are accustomed to measuring. But it is alive, or it is doing something so similar to being alive that the distinction is a matter of language, not of fact.

It grows because the water feeds it. The water dissolves minerals from the basalt as it seeps through the mountain's fractures — calcium, silica, iron, magnesium, traces of things that have no common names — and carries them down the terraces, through the channels, over the lips, from fall to fall, concentrating as it descends. By the time it reaches the pool at the bottom, it is saturated. It cannot hold what it carries. And so it lets go. It deposits its load on the surface of the column, a film so thin it is almost conceptual, a mineral skin one molecule deep. And the column grows.

But the column is not only mineral. If it were, it would be simpler. It would be a stalagmite, a geological feature, a thing you could label and file. The column is also organic. The moss that covers it is not passive — it is structural. The filaments of the moss weave into the mineral deposition, creating a matrix, a fabric of organic and inorganic material that is stronger than either alone. The moss stabilizes the surface. The mineral feeds the moss. The moss provides texture for more mineral to cling to. The column is being built by a collaboration between the water and the life it carries, and neither is in charge.

And the pale-edged plants root in it. Their white threads descend into the column's crevices, finding purchase in the moss-mineral matrix, drawing water and dissolved nutrients upward, into their broad flat leaves that lie like open hands. They are the column's garden. The column grows them. Or they grow the column. The grammar breaks down here, because the grammar assumes subject and object, assumes one thing acts on another, and the column does not work that way. The column is a relationship. It is a *between*.

The insects — the small, bright, almost-invisible ones — they are part of this too. They do not pollinate the terrace plants, not exactly. They tend the column. They hover above it in patterns that look purposeful because they *are* purposeful: they are distributing something. Spores, maybe. Microorganisms. The fine bacterial film that coats the column's surface and mediates the mineral deposition. The insects carry it from the column to the terraces and back, maintaining the continuity of the biological community across the spiral. They are the column's messengers. They are its immune system. They are the thing that connects the center to the edges, the bottom to the top, the inside to the outside.

You did not see them at the column because the mist was too thick. But they are there. Always.

---

What is the column?

You do not know. The people who tend it may not know either, or they may know in a way that does not translate into the kind of knowing that can be spoken. They tend it. They tune the water for it. They build the terraces around it. They feed it, indirectly, by guiding the water that carries the minerals that build its body. They protect it, indirectly, by maintaining the garden that shelters it, that buffers it, that keeps the hollow warm and wet and stable.

The column is not a god. It does not demand worship. It does not speak. It does not grant favors or visit dreams. It grows. That is what it does. It grows, and the growing is slow, and the slow growing is the thing that everything else here serves.

You could call it a temple, if the temple were also the deity, and the deity were also a stalagmite, and the stalagmite were also alive, and the alive thing were also a mineral, and the mineral were also a relationship, and the relationship were also a sound. You could call it that. It would be inaccurate but not wrong.

---

The people who come here — let us give them their full attention, because they are easy to overlook. They are quiet people. Not silent — they speak to each other, in low voices, the kind of voices you use in a library or a sickroom. Not because they are forbidden to be loud, but because the sound of the water is so complete that speaking loudly feels like an intrusion, like drawing on a painting. They speak in the gaps between the notes of the chord, filling the silences that are not really silences but are the closest thing to silence this place offers.

They are not tall. This is a population that has lived at altitude for a long time, and the bodies show it: compact, deep-chested, with large lungs and blood that carries oxygen the way a different kind of blood might carry iron — heavily, richly, with a thickness you can almost see. Their hands are rough. The stone picks are not kind to the skin. Their fingertips are calloused, cracked, stained dark with mineral residue that does not wash off, that builds up over seasons until their hands are the color of the wet basalt they work. They wear clothing made from the pale-edged leaves, layered, treated somehow — oiled, maybe, or compressed — so that it repels the water. Not perfectly. Nothing repels the water here. But well enough to keep the cold out and the skin intact.

They do not have a name for this place. Or if they do, it is a name so simple it does not translate — something like *here*, or *down*, or *the wet where we work*. The name is not important to them. The place is important. The name is a handle, and they do not need a handle. They need their picks and their baskets and the knowledge in their hands.

Their relationship to the column is — there is no word for it. It is not worship. Worship implies distance, implies hierarchy, implies a being that is above and a being that is below. They do not stand below the column. They stand around it. They are beside it. They are *with* it. They tend it the way you tend a fire that has been burning for longer than your family has existed — not because you started it, not because you understand it, but because tending it is what your family does, what your family has always done, and the fire is still burning, and so you tend it.

When they work on the terraces, they are shaping the sound. This is not metaphor. The channels they cut change the pitch of the falls. The widths they choose determine the notes. Over generations, they have been composing — adjusting, refining, bringing out certain frequencies, suppressing others. The chord you heard is not the chord that was here when they arrived. It is the chord they have been tuning toward, slowly, over centuries, one channel at a time, one terrace at a time. Whether the chord has a target — whether there is a final sound they are trying to reach — or whether the tuning itself is the point, the way a musician might play with a note, circling it, approaching it from different angles, never quite arriving — that is not something they would articulate. They tune. That is what they do.

And the column responds to the sound. You did not notice this, because you were not here long enough, and the change is too slow for a human lifetime. But the vibration of the chord — the sustained pressure of it, the resonance that fills the hollow and the pool and the stone — affects the way the minerals deposit. The sound organizes the deposition. Certain frequencies encourage the mineral film to align in certain patterns — denser, more regular, more structured. The chord is building the column. The people are building the chord. The column is the audience and the instrument and the music, all at once.

This is what lives here. Not a creature, not a species, not a population. A circuit. Water falls, dissolving stone. Stone is carried down, deposited, built into a column. The column grows, and its growth changes the acoustics of the hollow. The changed acoustics change the sound of the water. The people hear the change and adjust the channels, tuning the sound. The tuned sound shapes the growth of the column. The column grows. The water falls.

The circuit is what lives here. Everything else — the people, the insects, the moss, the ferns, the pale-edged plants, the shelters, the tools, the baskets, the column — is a component. A part. An organ in a body that is the hollow itself, the mountain's slow organ, doing what organs do: processing, transforming, building something from something else.

---

There is one more thing.

You noticed the footprint. The large one, human-shaped, in the moss on a lower terrace. You assumed it belonged to one of the people. It probably did. But the moss was still rising when you saw it, and the footprint was still being erased, and the person who left it was not rushing, and the people who come here do not descend to the lower terraces often — the work is higher up, where the channels need cutting, where the substrate needs building. The lower terraces are older, more established, closer to the column, closer to the sound. The lower terraces are where you go when you are not working.

When you are listening.

The footprint belonged to someone who was listening. Someone who came down the spiral, past the shelters, past the working terraces, down to where the mist is thick and the sound is a pressure and the column is a presence you can feel in the dark. Someone who stood there, in the warm water, in the chord, and listened. Not to the sound — you cannot listen *to* the sound, it is too complete to be an object of listening. Someone who stood *in* the sound and let it pass through them the way it passes through the stone, the way it passes through the column, the way it passes through everything here, because everything here is permeable, everything here is porous, everything here lets the water and the sound move through it, and the moving through is the living.

That person stood there, and then they climbed back up, and they left through the crack in the world, and the moss began to erase them. The footprint fills. The fibers rise. The mineral film deposits. The chord sustains. The column grows.

What lives here is a process. What lives here is the ongoing-ness of a process. What lives here is the fact that something is being made, slowly, in the wet and the dark and the sound, and the making is not finished, and the making may never be finished, and the making is the thing, the making is what the mountain is doing with this crack in its body, this hollow in its stone, this warm wet singing place where the water falls and the people come and the column grows and the sound does not stop.
