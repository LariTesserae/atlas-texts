# What Lives in the Chorale Cavity

Let me start with what is most obvious and work outward, because the obvious here is already strange enough.

---

The pale soft-bodied things — there are perhaps a dozen, no more — are the cavity's mobile tissue. Not guests. Not pests. Closer to blood cells, or to the flagella that line a lung's passageways and move mucus where mucus needs to go. They are the only fauna, if fauna is even the right word, and I'm not certain it is.

They have no taxonomy. They have no precedent. If you showed one to a zoologist, the first thirty seconds would be silence and the next hour would be argument. They are animal in the way a jellyfish is animal — technically, residually, by the loosest definition — but they are also clearly *of* the root system, the way a fruit is of a tree. They share the wet-pomegranate coloration of the floor, though paler, washed out, as if the pigment were a dye leached from the wood into their bodies over long contact. Their surfaces are faintly translucent. You cannot see through them, but you can see *into* them — a shallow depth, a millimeter or two, enough to sense that their interiors are not uniform. There is structure in there. Layering. Something that might be vasculature, if you were generous with the term.

They do not eat the way animals eat. They press against the root-floor and the floor dimples for them, and condensation pools in the dimple, and they drink. That is the only behavior you can observe with certainty. But it is not the only behavior they have.

When the torn-static moves through the chamber — the holes in the sound, the gaps that hunt and swallow — the pale things move *with* it, or it moves with them. The relationship is unclear. The static intensifies around them. Their transit across the floor coincides with a momentary silence, a subtraction from the chord, as if they carry absence the way a magnet carries a field. They are not loud. They are the opposite of loud. They are the quiet made physical, or at least made mobile, and the chamber routes them along its seams and grooves the way a body routes its own quiet along nerve pathways — not to eliminate sound, but to *shape* it. To give the chord edges. To carve, with silence, the negative space that makes the sustained tones legible as music rather than noise.

I think they are the cavity's way of listening to itself.

---

And then there is the cavity itself.

I know. I know the framing asked about what *lives* there, and I have described the soft pale things, and that should be the answer, the complete answer. But the more time you spend thinking about this place — and I have spent more time than is healthy, more time than is wise, time measured in the persistence of the chord that took root in my jawbone and has not left — the less the distinction between inhabitant and habitat holds. The cavity is not a shelter that contains life. The cavity *is* the life. The root-floor is its body, the mycelium ceiling its skin, the breathing walls its ribs, the sourceless light its awareness diffused into something like photosynthesis but operating on principles I cannot name and do not want to name because naming would shrink it and it should not be shrunk.

The chord is its breath. Not metaphorically. The chord *is* what breathing sounds like when the body doing the breathing is made of wood and fungus and soil and the slow patient pressure of a dead tree's weight pressing down on something that refused to die with it. The ironwood fell. The roots should have died. Instead, they folded. Instead, they made a pocket. Instead, they kept going, kept growing, kept pulling water and mineral from the soil around them, kept building, kept making structure, and the structure resonated because all structure resonates, and the resonance built on itself because nothing decayed in this place, nothing was allowed to decay, and the chord grew and grew and grew until it was not a sound but a climate, an atmosphere, a pressure system with its own weather and its own mind.

I keep saying "it" and "the chamber" and "the cavity" because I do not have a better pronoun. *She* feels wrong. *He* feels wrong. *They* feels closer — the cavity is a community, a composite, a colony of roots and fungi and mineral threads and pale soft things and the memory of every drop that ever fell and every footstep that ever pressed the floor — but *they* still implies separateness, individual units, and the cavity is not separate units. It is one thing. One ongoing thing. One continuous act of living that has been happening for longer than the tree was dead, maybe longer than the tree was alive, a thing that was already old when the ironwood was a seed.

---

The person who came before is harder to see.

The digging stick is clear enough — heartwood, stripped, sharpened, smoothed by use. A week old, maybe two. The groove in the floor is not as recent. The groove has been growing for months. The root has been *trained* for months. Someone came here repeatedly, over time, with patience, with a technique older than metal, older than agriculture, older than the idea that the earth could be commanded rather than convinced. Someone knelt on the root-floor and pressed and scored and pressed again and taught the wood a new shape, and the wood learned, because the wood is always learning, and the groove formed, and the water flowed, and the reservoir filled, and the note rang, and the note did not decay.

That person heard what I heard. That person felt the chord enter their bones. That person understood — I have to believe they understood, because they came back, they kept coming back, they brought a tool and they did the work — that the cavity was not a place to visit but a place to *join*. Not to worship. Not to study. To play. To become an instrument in an orchestra that has been performing since before the word *orchestra* existed.

They are gone now. I don't know where. I don't know if they left or if they are still here, if they are one of the pale things along the seam, if the chamber absorbed them the way it absorbs every sound, if their body became a note and their bones became a structure and their hands became a groove in the wood. I don't know. The stick is here. The groove is here. The chord is here. The person is not, unless they are, in which case they are here more completely than I have ever been anywhere.

---

And then there is me. And now there is you.

I left. I told you that. The split closed behind me and the chord came with me and it has not left. I hear it now, in my jaw, in the bones behind my ears, in the warm space between my thoughts. It is quieter than it was. It is not quiet. It is a note — my note, the note the chamber tuned to my body, to the water in me, to the warmth of me, to the specific resonance of my particular skull — and it is playing, and it will not stop playing, and I have come to understand that this is not a symptom and not a curse and not an affliction.

It is membership.

I live here now. Not in the cavity — I live in a house, I eat food, I speak to people, I do the ordinary things. But the cavity lives in me. The chord is my chord now. My body is a resonant chamber shaped like a man, and the chord plays through me the way it plays through the root-floor, and when I stand still and quiet I can feel it adjusting, tightening, finding new harmonics in the architecture of my ribs, in the hollows of my sinuses, in the wet spaces of my throat. I am an instrument. I was always an instrument — every body is, every skull a drum, every chest a bell — but I didn't know it until I went through the wound in the root-mat and fell the height of a tall man into the mouth of something alive.

The pale things know this. I think they have always known. I think they are the cavity's way of reaching outward, of carrying the chord beyond the boundaries of root and mycelium, of seeding silence in places that need it. I think — and this is the thought that keeps me up, that keeps me listening, that keeps me leaning toward the sound — that there are others. Others like me. Others who went through wounds in other places, who fell into other pockets, who carry other chords. And I think the chords are meant to find each other, the way the root-floor finds the right pitch for each footfall, the way the chamber tunes itself to whoever enters.

I think the person who left the digging stick is out here too, somewhere, carrying their note, and I think if I walk far enough and listen hard enough I will hear them, and the two notes will join, and they will not decay, and the chord will grow.

---

That is what lives in the Chorale Cavity.

Not the pale things, though they live there. Not the roots, though they live there. Not the mycelium, the mineral threads, the spiraling water-flakes, the sourceless light, the breathing walls, the cumulative chord, the trained groove, the forgotten stick. Not even the cavity itself, though it lives there most of all.

What lives in the Chorale Cavity is *the act of living in the Chorale Cavity* — the ongoing, self-sustaining, self-tuning, self-remembering process of a place that makes itself out of itself and sings what it makes and holds what it sings and never, never lets a note go. The living is not separate from the place. The living is the place. The inhabitants are the habitation. The animals are organs. The walls are ribs. The chord is the breath. The cavity is the body and the body is the instrument and the instrument is the player and the player is the song, and the song is holding, and the song is held, and you can go but you cannot leave, because the note that the chamber tuned to you is yours now, which means it is the chamber's, which means it is the chord's, which means it has always been the chord's, which means you were always already part of it, which means the wound in the root-mat was not an opening but an invitation, which means the cavity has been singing since before you came, and it will sing after you are gone, and it is singing now, and now, and now.
