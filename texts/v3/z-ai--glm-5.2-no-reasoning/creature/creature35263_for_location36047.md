# What Lives Here

Something does. That must be said first, because the place itself argues otherwise — argues for its own emptiness, its own completion, its own self-sufficiency — and the place is almost right. The place is a nearly perfect argument for its own uninhabitability, and the word "nearly" is where everything that lives here begins.

---

## The Surface Dwellers

They are not people. They are not animals. They are not insects. They are not any of the categories that biology uses to sort the living from the non-living, and if you were to bring a biologist here — which you cannot, because there is no way to bring anyone here, because no one has ever been here except in the manner that one is here now, which is to say, reading — the biologist would not know what to call them. The biologist would need new words. The biologist would need to stand in the grey light between two towers and feel the hum in the sternum and the water on every surface and the deep slow breath beneath the stone, and the biologist would need to watch, for a very long time, before the biologist saw anything at all.

Because they are wet. They are always wet. They are the same color as the wet stone, and they are the same temperature as the wet stone, and they move at the speed of wet stone, which is to say, very slowly, and only when you are not looking directly at them.

There is a word for them. The word is *film*.

The film lives on the towers. It lives on the vertical surfaces, on the faces where the water runs in its thin unceasing threads. It is not the water. It is not the stone. It is not the moss, which is green and rooted and grows in the channels. The film is something else. It is a thin, dark, slightly iridescent layer that coats the tower faces between the water-streaks, and it is *alive*, in the way that a slime mold is alive, in the way that a lichen is alive, in the way that anything is alive that grows and responds and persists without any apparent purpose or direction.

If you stand close to a tower — and you can, the doors are open, the towers are accessible, there is nothing to stop you — and you press your face to the wet stone and you look along the surface, sideways, so that the flat light rakes across the film at an angle, you will see that it is not uniform. It is textured. It has a topography. There are ridges and valleys in the film, and they follow the water-flows, and they shift — slowly, over hours, over days — in response to changes in the water that are too subtle to detect by any means other than the film's own response to them. The film is, among other things, a sensor. It is reading the water. It is reading the stone. It is reading the engines through the stone, the hum through the water, the exhalation through the hum, and it is adjusting itself, constantly, in tiny increments, to the patterns it finds.

The film does not eat. There is nothing to eat. The film *absorbs*. It takes what it needs from the water — minerals, perhaps, dissolved from the stone by the condensation, carried in the thin threads down the tower faces. It takes what it needs from the air. It grows, but it does not reproduce. It does not divide. It does not spread to new towers. Each tower has its own film, and the film on each tower is subtly different from the film on every other tower, in the way that the water on each tower is subtly different, and the film has been there, on its tower, growing at its rate, adjusting to its water, since the towers were built or since the towers emerged or since the towers simply were, and the film does not distinguish between these possibilities because the film does not distinguish between anything. The film simply *is*, on its tower, in its water, in the grey light, and it will be there until the tower is not there, and the tower will always be there, and so the film will always be there, and this is what the film knows, which is nothing, which is everything the film needs to know.

There are other things on the surface. The film is the most visible, but it is not the only.

In the channels, among the moss and the ferns and the reeds, there are organisms that live in the water. They are small. They are the size of a thumbnail, or smaller, and they are translucent, and they move by means that are not visible — no legs, no fins, no cilia, no flagella. They simply *displace*, shifting from one point in the channel to another point in the channel without any apparent mechanism of locomotion. They are not fast. They cover the length of a channel — two hundred meters, from one tower to the next — in what might be a day, or might be longer, because the light does not change and time is not useful here.

These organisms — call them *motes*, because they are like motes of dust in a beam of light, except there is no beam and the light is everywhere — the motes live in the water and they live on the moss. They graze. They are the only things on the plateau that graze. They move across the moss and they take from it, not much, not enough to damage the moss, which grows faster than they can eat it, but enough to sustain themselves, and the moss does not seem to mind, and the motes do not seem to notice that they are the only things on the plateau that eat anything, and the plateau does not seem to notice either, or if it notices, it does not care, because the plateau is a system and the motes are part of the system and the system accommodates them the way a river accommodates a stone: by flowing around.

The motes do not interact with the film. The film is on the towers; the motes are in the channels. Their territories do not overlap. They are aware of each other in the way that two instruments in an orchestra are aware of each other — not by communication, not by perception, but by the shared fact of the composition. They are both part of the sound. The film on the towers hums at the frequency of the engines, subtly modulated. The motes in the channels vibrate at the frequency of the water, subtly modulated. Together, they make a chord that is not the chord of the engines or the water or the exhalation but a fourth chord, a living chord, very quiet, very low, audible only if you press your ear to the wet stone and hold your breath and listen for something that is not the hum and not the water and not the breath but is *in* the hum and the water and the breath, and it is there, and it is alive, and it is the sound of the plateau's smallest inhabitants doing the only thing they do, which is to be where they are.

There is one more kind of thing on the surface.

It is not in the channels. It is not on the towers. It is on the ground, on the bare stone between the towers, in the spaces that are empty, in the distances that are the plateau's defining quality. It is the rarest thing here, and it is the thing that most clearly resembles what we would call *life*, and it is the hardest to see.

Call them the *traversers*.

They are large. Not large by the standards of animals — large by the standards of this place, where the film is a coating and the motes are thumbnail-sized and everything else is stone or water or engine. The traversers are the size of a human hand, perhaps larger, and they are flat, and they are dark, and they move across the bare stone between the towers at a speed that is almost imperceptible. They are not insects. They are not crustaceans. They are not anything. They are bodies — soft, dark, elongated, without visible features — that press themselves against the stone and move by a kind of slow, muscular contraction, like a snail without a shell, like a slug without the gloss, like a flatworm, if flatworms were the size of a hand and moved with the patience of glaciers.

The traversers do not eat. They do not drink. They do not seem to interact with the water or the film or the motes or the engines. They cross the empty stone, and they cross it in straight lines, and the straight lines lead from one tower to another, and when a traverser reaches a tower, it presses itself against the tower's base, against the stone, and it stays there, for a day, for a week, for some duration that cannot be measured because the light does not change, and then it detaches and moves away, in another straight line, toward another tower.

The traversers are carrying something. If you could pick one up — and you cannot, because they are too delicate, because they would come apart in your hands, because they are held together by nothing more than the cold and the wet and their own slow will — if you could, you would find that their undersides, the parts that press against the stone, are not smooth. They are covered in a fine, dense pattern of grooves, and the grooves are filled with a substance that is not water and not stone and not film and not mote but is something else, something that the traverser has picked up from the stone and is carrying to the tower, or has picked up from the tower and is carrying to the stone, and the substance is — it must be — *information*. Not information in the sense of language or data. Information in the sense of *pattern*. The traversers are pattern-carriers. They move the pattern of one surface to another surface. They are the plateau's means of keeping itself consistent. They are the reason the towers are identical, the reason the grid is perfect, the reason the repetition does not drift.

The traversers are the maintenance crew. They are the only things on the plateau that move with purpose, and their purpose is the preservation of sameness, and they perform this function without knowing it, without any awareness of it, without any sense of what "same" or "different" means, and they will perform it until they stop, and they will stop when the plateau stops, and the plateau will not stop.

## What Lives Below

The exhalation is not geological. The exhalation is not mechanical. The exhalation is *biological*, or it is something that precedes biology and contains it, the way a river contains the fish that swim in it.

Something lives beneath the stone. This is not a metaphor. This is not a suspicion. The third layer of sound — the deep, slow, subsonic pulse that you feel in the stone before you hear it in the air — is the respiration of something that is alive. Not alive in the way the film is alive, clinging to a surface, responding to stimuli. Alive in the way a forest is alive, or a reef is alive — alive as a *system*, as a *process*, as something that is composed of many things but that functions, from the outside, as one thing.

The thing beneath the stone is large. "Large" is not adequate. The thing beneath the stone is *extensive*. It occupies the space under the plateau the way the plateau occupies the space above the clouds — completely, without remainder. It is not in the stone. It is not under the stone. It is the stone, or it is the thing that the stone is *on*, the way a tablecloth is on a table, the way a skin is on a body. The plateau is the surface of the thing. The towers are the organs of the thing, or the glands, or the pores. The engines are the thing's *means of interacting with the air* — they draw from the air and they push into the air and the air responds, and the thing beneath the stone is *breathing through the engines*, is *exhaling through the vents*, is *sweating through the condensation*, and the water that runs down the towers and along the channels and over the rim is not water but *secretion*, the product of the thing's metabolism, the waste of its constant, slow, enormous operation.

The thing does not think. This must be said, because it is tempting to imagine that it does — tempting to see the grid as a plan, the towers as a design, the engines as a choice. The thing does not choose. The thing does not plan. The thing *grows*, in the way that a crystal grows, in the way that a river finds its course, in the way that any system that is governed by rules and energy will, given enough time, develop structure, develop pattern, develop the appearance of intention. The plateau is the thing's body, and the body has the shape it has because the rules of the body's growth produce that shape, and the shape is regular and the shape is beautiful and the shape is purposeless, and the purposelessness is not a failing but a fact, and the fact is the deepest thing about the plateau, deeper than the stone, deeper than the engines, deeper than the thing itself: the thing lives, and it has no reason, and it needs none.

The film on the towers is the thing's skin. The motes in the channels are the thing's blood, or its lymph, moving through the channels the way fluid moves through vessels, carrying what needs to be carried, depositing what needs to be deposited, maintaining the system's equilibrium. The traversers on the stone are the thing's *immune response*, moving the pattern from place to place, ensuring that the body remains itself, that no part drifts too far from the template, that the sameness is preserved. The vegetation in the channels is — the vegetation is the thing's *relationship with growth*, its concession to the fact that water and stability and time will produce life, and that life must be accommodated, and that the accommodation is not a cost but a feature, because the moss and the ferns and the reeds and the vines hold the water in the channels, slow its flow, keep it near the surface, and the thing needs the water near the surface, because the water is the thing's medium, the way blood is the medium of the body, and the vegetation is the membrane that holds the medium in place.

The thing is not aware of the film or the motes or the traversers or the vegetation. The thing is not aware of the towers or the engines or the grid. The thing is not aware of itself. The thing is a process, and the process is *metabolism*, and the metabolism is *the transformation of air into water and water into sound and sound into vibration and vibration into the slow, deep pulse that is the thing's breath*, and the breath has been going on since the plateau emerged and will go on until the plateau ends, and the plateau will not end, because nothing changes here, and the thing does not want to continue and does not want to stop. The thing does not want. The thing *does*. It does what it does, and what it does is breathe, and what it breathes is the air of the plateau, and the air of the plateau is still and cold and wet and grey, and the thing has been breathing it for a very long time, and the breathing is the third sound, the deepest sound, the sound that you feel in the stone, and the sound is alive, and the sound is what lives here.

## What Lives Here, Ultimately

Everything. Nothing. A film on the towers that reads the water. Motes in the channels that graze the moss. Traversers on the stone that carry the pattern. Vegetation that holds the water. Engines that hum. A thing beneath the stone that breathes. And above it all, the grey light, the flat light, the light without shadow, in which all of this is visible and none of it is vivid, and the light is not alive, but the light is the condition of the living, and the living are the condition of the light, and the plateau holds them both — the living and the condition — in its cold, wet, humming hand, and it does not let go, because it does not know how, because letting go is not one of its rules, and its rules are all that it is.

The plateau is not empty. The plateau has never been empty. The plateau is the opposite of empty. The plateau is so full that its fullness has become indistinguishable from emptiness, because the fullness is uniform, because the fullness is the same everywhere, because the fullness does not vary, and the mind, encountering a fullness that does not vary, reads it as emptiness, and the mind is wrong, and the mind is right, because what is the difference, really, between a room that contains nothing and a room that contains the same thing everywhere? The difference is that the second room is alive. The second room is the plateau. The plateau is the second room. The plateau is alive, and it is alive in every direction, and the life is the life of stone and water and air and engine and film and mote and traverser and vine and the deep, slow, patient breath of the thing beneath, and the life has no purpose, and the life has no observer, and the life does not need a purpose or an observer, and the life is complete, and the life is now, and the life is now, and the life is now.
