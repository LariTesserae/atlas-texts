What lives in the Brinecellars lives without the burden of being noticed.

I want to be careful here. I want to resist the easy summary — the inventory, the headcount, the ecological breakdown that would turn this place into a problem set. Forty to sixty thousand creatures, the old survey says, and that number is already a kind of violence done to the reality. Not because it is inaccurate, but because it implies that what matters about life in the Brinecellars is its quantity. What matters is the way all of this life fits together — or perhaps more accurately, the way it all coexists without any need to fit, without any governing arrangement, without any principle of organization beyond the simple, stupid, radiant fact that conditions are good and bodies will do what bodies do when conditions are good.

---

Let me start with what I find most difficult to describe, which is the springtails.

They are the true inhabitants. I want to say that plainly, even though it will sound strange, even though the infrastructure was built by human hands for human reasons. The springtails do not know about the infrastructure. They do not know about the municipal authority or the records or the century or two of abandoned intention. They know the film. They know the moisture. They know the warmth that rises through the stone like a slow, patient breath from the earth's chest. And they are here, in the millions, because what they need is here in abundance.

The difficulty in describing them is that they resist the individual. A single springtail is barely a creature at all — a pale fleck, a mote with legs, smaller than a grain of rice and less substantial than a grain of rice. You could hold one on the tip of your finger and not be certain you were holding anything. It would not fill the surface area of your fingernail. It has, I suppose, a life — a sequence of hours or days or weeks during which it moves and feeds and perhaps mates and eventually dies — but this life is not a thing you can narrate. There is no protagonist among the springtails. There is no story to tell about a single one of them.

But together — together they are something I have never adequately found language for. They are a presence. They are the way every surface in this place has a quality of motion that the surface itself does not own. The stone shelves appear to breathe. The walkway planks appear to think, slowly, dimly, the way stone might think if stone could think — and it is not the stone, it is the springtails, moving in their constant, silent, seething multitude across the surface, and the surface moves with them because they are so many and so small that their aggregate motion becomes indistinguishable from the motion of the thing they move across.

I have watched them scatter from beneath a boot. I have watched the ripple — that radiating wave of tiny bodies flowing outward from the point of disturbance, reaching the edges, pausing, and then flowing back, as though the surface were a pool and the footstep a stone dropped into it. The metaphor is too clean. What actually happens is messier, slower, less symmetric. The springtails do not flow back as a unified body. They return in pieces — a few here, a few there, tentatively, the way water finds its way back into a depression after being displaced. The surface does not recover all at once. It recovers the way a wound heals, from the edges inward, and when it is fully recovered, it looks exactly as it did before, and you cannot tell where the disturbance was.

They are silent. I need to say this again because it is the thing I return to, the thing I cannot reconcile. That many living things, moving at once, over a surface, in all directions, feeding, mating, dying, being born — and there is no sound. Not a hum. Not a click. Not a whisper. If you close your eyes, you know they are there only because the air has a weight to it, a density, a feeling of being occupied at a scale below perception. The silence where sound should be is a specific, locatable pressure. It sits behind your ears. It sits in your temples. It is the sensation of your auditory system expecting input and receiving nothing where nothing should not be.

What lives here, in the most honest sense, is that silence. That silence is alive.

---

The cave crickets live on the walls, and they are different. They are visible. They are countable, in the way that individual things are countable even when you do not bother to count them. They cling to the wet limestone in their clusters, pale and humpbacked, their legs folded beneath them in configurations that seem anatomically unlikely — too long, too angular, too much limb for the body they serve. When the lamplight catches them, their translucent abdomens glow faintly, and the wall becomes a kind of constellation, a map of small, cold, pale stars in the dark.

They watch. I do not want to anthropomorphize this, but I do not know another word. They are still, and they are oriented toward you, and their eyes are black and flat and do not reflect, and you feel seen. Not in the way a dog sees you or a bird sees you — with interest, with assessment — but in the way a stone might see you if a stone could see, which is to say with a patience that does not include you in its calculations. They watch the way the cave watches. They are part of the architecture. When you pass, they do not move. They wait for the disturbance to go away, because disturbances always go away, because the Brinecellars are permanent and the things that pass through them are not.

There is a comfort in this, though I am not sure it is a comfort you would want. It is the comfort of mattering very little. The crickets have been here longer than the infrastructure. They will be here after the iron brackets rust through and the walkways collapse and the lead linings crack and the last bulb fails. They will cling to the wet stone and they will glow faintly in whatever light remains and they will watch, with those flat black eyes, whatever comes next, and they will wait for it to go away too.

---

The spiders I think of as accountants. This is not fair to them. They are not cold or mechanical or indifferent in the way that word implies. But they are here because the math works. Flies in, flies caught, silk spent, silk replenished, eggs laid, eggs hatched, more silk, more catching, more flies. The math is simple and it has been working for as long as there have been flies in the Brinecellars, which is to say for as long as there has been organic residue in the basins, which is to say for as long as the basins have held brine, which is to say for as long as the water has seeped in from above and the heat has risen from below.

Their webs are not beautiful. I want to be clear about this because the temptation is to find beauty in everything that lives in a place like this, to aestheticize the ecosystem, to make it a poem. The webs are not poems. They are hammocks, strung in corners, in junctions, in the gaps between pipe and wall, in every angle the infrastructure provides. They collect moisture until they sag. They collect prey until they are heavy with it — the tiny, dark bodies of flies tangled in the silk, still twitching or long still, a larder that the spider visits and revisits and draws from in its methodical, unhurried way.

You will walk through them. This is inevitable. The webs are everywhere, and the light does not reach far enough to reveal them all, and you will feel the silk on your face before you see it — fine, almost weightless, clinging to the small hairs of your skin, and you will taste the salt and the damp and that faint bitterness, which I believe is the adhesive the spider applies to the threads, the compound that makes them sticky, that makes them catch, and you will wipe your face and your hand will come away with nothing on it, because the silk is too fine to see on your skin, but you will feel it for minutes afterward, a phantom sensation, the ghost of a web that is still intact somewhere behind you, already being repaired, the spider already at work closing the gap you made.

What lives here, in the webs, is industry. Not human industry — nothing like the evaporation basins, nothing like the municipal project with its records and its reasons. Spider industry. The kind that does not require a reason. The kind that proceeds because the silk is available and the flies are available and the corners are available and the work is simply what the body does when the conditions are right.

---

The amphipods in the brine channel are the creatures I understand least. They are pale, small, sideways-swimming, and they move across the shallow bottom with a constancy that seems to have no object. They forage. For what, I am not always sure. The bacterial mats, perhaps. The organic particulate that settles into the channel from above. Each one is a half-inch of translucent body and relentless, purposeless motion, and there are thousands of them, and together they make the bottom of the channel look like a street in a city you cannot quite identify — busy, directional, every pedestrian going somewhere specific, but you cannot tell where, and you cannot tell whether the destinations are real or merely the momentum of bodies that have been moving for so long they have forgotten how to stop.

When the light catches them, you see the dark thread of the gut — the shadow of the last meal, processing. This is a detail I find I cannot leave out, though I do not know why it matters to me. Perhaps because it is the one thing that makes them legible as individuals. The gut is a record. It says: this body ate something, recently, and it is here, inside, being made use of. Every amphipod carries the evidence of its last meal as a dark line through its translucent body, and when two pass each other, you can see, for a moment, two dark lines crossing, two records overlapping, two separate lives intersecting in the shallow water without acknowledgment or interest, and then separating, and the channel goes back to being a surface in motion, and you are watching it from above, and you are not in it, and the amphipods do not know you are there.

The beetles are different. The beetles are solitary in a way that reads as intention. Dark, oval, smooth — they row through the brine with those oarlike legs, and they make the smallest possible disturbance on the surface, a faint bow wave that spreads and dissolves within an inch or two of the body. When two beetles meet, they circle. The circling is brief — a second or two — and then they separate, moving in different directions, and the surface stills behind them. When a beetle meets an amphipod, there is no circling. There is no reaction at all. The beetle rows past. The amphipod continues its foraging. They occupy the same water and exist in different countries, and neither country has a border that the other can perceive.

I have thought about this a great deal — more, probably, than it deserves. The beetles and the amphipods share a habitat, share a food source, share the same shallow amber brine and the same dim light and the same slow warmth from below, and they do not interact. They do not compete. They do not acknowledge. They are, to each other, furniture. This is not indifference, because indifference implies the possibility of interest. It is something prior to indifference — a kind of radical, constitutional separation, as though they are not in the same place at all but in two places that happen to overlap in the coordinates you can measure but not in the coordinates that matter.

---

The flies are the ones I cannot hold.

I have tried. I have stood in the Brinecellars and watched the columns rise — those columns that look like smoke, then like rain, then like something your mind refuses to process because the number of individual things moving together as one exceeds whatever threshold the visual system has for the concept of multitude. They breed in the basins. They breed in the mineral film, in the organic residue, in the shallow, warm, nutrient-rich margins where the brine meets the stone. They are the foundation of the spider economy. They are the reason the webs exist. They are, in a sense, the reason the spiders exist — not the individual spiders, which would exist somewhere regardless, but the density of spiders, the abundance of webs, the felt-everywhere presence of silk in this place.

The flies are not individual. I know they are — I know that each one is a separate organism with its own wings and its own body and its own brief, probably unremarkable life — but in the Brinecellars, they are a phenomenon. They are weather. They rise in columns when disturbed, and the columns are dense enough to feel against your face — a soft pressure, a warmth, a breath of bodies — and then they settle, and they are invisible again, and you are left with the memory of something you could not quite see and cannot quite describe and that was, for the seconds it lasted, as close to the experience of standing inside a living cloud as anything I have encountered.

What lives here, in the flies, is generative chaos. Not the chaos of disorder, but the chaos of production — constant, relentless, mindless production of more bodies, more wings, more dark tiny forms rising and settling and rising again, feeding the spiders, feeding the film, feeding the springtails that feed on the film that the flies breed in, the whole system turning over and over on itself like a wheel with no axle, no center, no point of stillness, just motion transferred from one form to another to another in the dark and the heat and the damp.

---

And the bats. I want to end with the bats, because they are the ones who leave.

Two hundred of them, approximately. Small, brown, unremarkable. They roost in the higher crevices where the stone has cracked — narrow, warm, dark spaces just large enough for a body to fit, and the bodies fit, and the colony fits, and the ceiling of the Brinecellars holds them the way a shelf holds books — loosely, unintentionally, with no particular attention to the contents.

They are asleep when you are there. Or they seem to be. The bats in the Brinecellars do not stir during the day. They are folded into their crevices, their wings tight against their bodies, their faces tucked into the dark, and from below they are barely visible — small brown shapes in the upper dark, motionless, silent, and if you did not know to look, you would not know they were there. The Brinecellars, during the day, are a place without bats. The bats are in the stone, and the stone is not a place.

At dusk they leave. Through a fissure in the ceiling that opens somewhere above onto the coastal scrub, they pour out — not all at once, not in a dramatic column, but in a stream that thickens and thins and thickens again over the course of several minutes, until the crevices are empty and the ceiling is just a ceiling, and the Brinecellars are, for the first time since dawn, a place without any large bodies in it at all. Just the springtails, the crickets, the spiders, the flies, the amphipods, the beetles, and the dark, and the heat, and the water, and the hum.

They return before dawn. This is the event I want to describe, because it is the loudest thing in the Brinecellars, and the Brinecellars are a quiet place, and the contrast is something you feel in your sternum.

The return takes about three minutes. It is not organized. It is not graceful. It is a brief, fluttering, chittering chaos — two hundred small bodies pouring back through the fissure, filling the upper air of the chamber with the sound of wings and the high, thin sound of bat-voices, the sound that is not quite a squeak and not quite a click and not quite anything you have a word for, and the bats are circling, finding their crevices, folding themselves back in, and the sound is everywhere at once, bouncing off the wet walls, amplified by the low ceiling, and for three minutes the Brinecellars are not a quiet place. They are a place full of sound, full of bodies, full of the particular energy of creatures returning to the place they sleep, and the sound is not peaceful and it is not frightening and it is not beautiful. It is simply full. It is the fullness of two hundred lives compressed into a space that was, moments ago, empty of them, and the fullness is a physical thing — you feel it in the air, in the pressure, in the way the dim orange lamplight is broken and scattered by the passage of wings.

And then it is over. The last bat finds its crevice. The last wings fold. The last chitter subsides into the particular silence of a bat at rest, which is the same as the silence of the stone, which is the same as the silence of the Brinecellars themselves, which is the silence of a place that does not know it is being observed and does not care and does not change when the observation ends.

The dripping resumes. The generator holds its note. The springtails move across the surfaces. The flies settle. The spiders wait. The amphipods forage. The beetles row through the shallow brine in their separate countries. The salt forms, grain by grain, in the basins no one is harvesting. The water seeps in from above. The heat rises from below.

And what lives here keeps living, in the dark and the damp and the warmth, without purpose, without oversight, without any need for either, the way life lives wherever it finds the conditions for living, which is to say: everywhere, eventually, given enough time, given enough water, given enough warmth rising through the stone like a slow, patient breath from a body that has been sleeping for a very long time and is not done.
