# The Keepers of the Weep

They are not what you expect, and they are not what you would call inhabitants, and yet the place is theirs the way a word is the throat's.

They exist at the pace of the place.

---

You will not see them at first. This is not concealment — nothing here conceals; the light forbids it — but a matter of *register*. Your eyes are built for bodies. Bodies move at the speed of intent: a step, a glance, a breath. What lives in the Terraced Weep moves at the speed of water crossing a tilted stone floor, and your eyes will slide over it a dozen times before they admit what they are seeing.

Look at the retaining walls. Look at the dark, damp faces, the grids of shallow indentations, the weep-channels with their clear threads of water. Look at where the wall meets the floor, where the bright groove runs and the sheeting film converges and the stone is darkest and wettest.

There. Where the wall's weeping has, over a long time, deposited a slight mineral thickening — a pale lip, like the tide-line of a drying pool — the thickening is not mineral. Or rather, it is, but it is *organized* mineral, mineral with an inside.

They are low. That is the first thing your eyes finally accept. Low and flattened and close to the stone, closer than a shadow, and of a piece with the wall in a way that makes the phrase "of a piece" feel newly literal. Their bodies — if bodies — are long, horizontal, tapered things, the thickness of a forearm at the deepest point, lying along the base of the walls in the wettest margin, and their color is the color of the wet stone with a difference: where the wall is merely dark, they are dark *with grain*, a fine dark stippling, and this stippling, you realize at some point, is not pattern but *structure* — the surface of them is dense with tiny, close-set apertures, each one the width of a pin's head, each one weeping, each one the source of a film of water that runs down their flanks and merges with the film on the floor, so that they are ringed in a minute halo of flow, continuously rinsed, continuously clean, continuously *part of the water's passage*.

They drink the way the walls drink. Through the skin of them, slowly, always. The water comes to them; they do not go to the water. This is the whole posture of their existence.

---

Count them and you will be wrong, and be wrong again, and eventually understand why you cannot count them.

The terraces are dozens. The walls are kilometers. A keeper lies along a stretch of wall-base — some a few meters, some a long quarter of the terrace's edge — and where one ends, the next begins, but the transition is not visible. Their tapers overlap. Their stippled surfaces match. The pale mineral lip of one keeper's territory shades into the pale mineral lip of the next with no seam, because each keeper *makes* its own lip — excretes it, perhaps, or builds it, or grows it, a slow crust of filtered mineral laid down at the boundary as a fence is laid down at a field's edge — and the crusts of adjacent keepers meet and merge. The territory of each is a length of wall, and the boundary between territories is a boundary only in the sense that a property line is a boundary: real to them, invisible to you.

You crouch at a boundary — you find one eventually, by noticing where the wall's grid of indentations changes its spacing, coarser on one side, finer on the other — and you watch for a long time, and you see the two keepers *maintain* it. Each extends itself, very slowly, toward the meeting point. Not fighting. Not competing. Adjusting. If the water's flow along the groove shifts seasonally — and it does shift, subtly, with some rhythm of the upper source you cannot perceive — the boundaries migrate, and the keepers migrate with them, keeping pace with their own water, a slow cartography happening at the speed of sediment.

---

They are not animals. It is important that you understand this, because the word "animal" will keep trying to attach itself and will keep being wrong. Animals *do* things. Animals decide. What these keepers do is closer to what a river does, or what the liver does: they *process*.

Here is their function, and you work it out over days, sitting on the walls, watching, and it is simpler and stranger than you guessed:

**They keep the channels clear.**

The weep-channels in the walls are conical, tapered wider on the inside, and the water that comes through them carries what water always carries at this altitude: a fine mineral load, dissolved and suspended, the faint sediment the description of this place mentions in its patient, geologic voice. Over years, this sediment accretes. A weep-channel narrows. The flow through it drops. And a channel that narrows is a channel that will clog, and a clogged channel is a wall that stops weeping, and a wall that stops weeping is the beginning of the end of the entire instrument — for the whole Terraced Weep is a single hydraulic argument, and every one of its small bored holes is a premise that must hold.

So: the keepers.

They lie along the bases of the walls where the channels discharge, and their apertured skin takes in the flow, and in taking it in they take in the sediment, and somewhere in their dark, flattened, tapering length the mineral is sorted — kept, passed, bound — and what emerges from them is water cleaner than what entered, plus that pale lip-crust at their edges, plus, at very long intervals, a small dense pellet of consolidated mineral, dark as the stone, which they void from a posterior aperture and which lies in the groove until the water has worn it smooth and it becomes indistinguishable from a pebble. The terraces have pebbles. You assumed they were geological. Some fraction of them are keepers' work, keepers' leavings, keepers' *sentences*.

They tend the channels closest to their stretch of wall. A keeper whose lip-crust has grown up around a channel's outlet will, over months, shift itself — a slow glacial rearrangement of its length along the wall-base — so that the crust does not block the discharge but cups it, channels it, shapes itself into a *spout*. Look for this and you find it everywhere: the little mineral lips that guide the weep-water into the grooves are not features of the original construction. They are the keepers' oldest work, laid down and re-laid, generation over generation, a slow masonry of excretion maintaining the great masonry of stone. The hands that built the Weep made the walls weep. The keepers keep the weeping weeping.

And on the terrace with the shallow basins — the mirror-bright pools — the keepers there are different in form and identical in function. They live *in* the basins, flattened against the floors of them, wider and thinner than the wall-keepers, almost perfectly flat, their stippled apertures facing upward through the still water, and their work is the basin's clarity. The pools do not cloud. The pools do not silt. You looked at them and thought *eternal*, and the truth is better: they are *maintained*, continuously, by flat dark patient things that eat the cloud out of the water, and the mirror you marveled at is their accomplishment, polished from the inside.

---

Do they perceive? The honest answer is that they perceive *water*.

Not light — the great even sourceless brilliance of this place means nothing to them; they do not have eyes, and if they have light-sensitivity it is buried so deep in their grain as to be an heirloom of some other existence. Not sound. Not you. You can stand over a keeper for an hour, boots in the groove, and it will do nothing, change nothing, be a length of wall to you. You are not a predator to be wary of and not a companion to be aware of. You are weather. You are a very brief weather.

What they perceive is the *flow*. Each of them, along its length, is a continuous gauge of hydraulic condition: the pressure in the film, the temperature of the water, the rate of the seep, the mineral signature of the discharge from each channel they attend. When a weep-channel upstream begins to narrow, the water through it changes before it visibly diminishes — faster, thinner, fractionally different in what it carries — and the keeper's stretch of wall *knows* this the way your skin knows a draft: not as information but as condition. The response is not decision. It is the response of a system to its input, a gland responding to chemistry, a river responding to rain: the keeper's apertures shift their intake along its length, its lip-crust is laid down with a slightly different geometry, its slow body drifts a few centimeters toward the weakening channel to cup its failing discharge more closely. Over months. Over the duration of your entire visit it will have accomplished an adjustment you would need time-lapse photography to confirm.

This is what lives here: not minds, but *tunings*.

---

And now the question the place has been quietly asking you the whole time, the question you finally sit with on the retaining wall with your boots in the bright groove and the shadowless light on the wet stone:

**Who made them?**

Because here is the thing: the Terraced Weep is a machine, a hydrological instrument of staggering, generational, stone-on-stone labor — and every machine of that kind of ambition has, built into its deepest design, the problem of its own maintenance. The hands that ground those polygonal blocks together, the hands that bored the conical channels by abrasion and patience, those hands knew what water does to a channel over a century. They knew the sediment would come. They could have made the channels replaceable — they did not; the channels are the stone. They could have made the walls scrub-able by hand — they built kilometers of them, at the pace of a lifetime, and the lifetimes were needed elsewhere, grinding the next terrace's stones.

Instead, they made — or found, and shaped, and placed — the keepers.

The keepers are the maintenance schedule made flesh. Or made *something* — made whatever they are made of, which is mostly water and mineral and a dark stippled grain that might be heritage of stone or heritage of something older. The builders' final and greatest hand-tool was a living filter, bred or built or bargained-for, installed along every wall-base, in every basin, at every lip and groove and conical eye of the whole vast instrument. And then — this is the part that gives you the vertigo — the builders could leave. And did. The machine runs because the water runs, and the water is tended because the tenders run on the water, and the circle closes with no one inside it. You have been walking through an automated waterworks, and its automation is the low, flat, apertured, slow, dark length of the walls' living base, and it has been in continuous operation for longer than any of the names you have for durations.

Perhaps they are still being *made*. Perhaps, somewhere in the hidden upper source, the water passes over keepers of a different order, ones whose processed leavings are not pellets but *seeds* — small dense nodules of organized mineral that wash down the cascades, lodge in a groove, and in a decade or a century begin to stipple, begin to drink, begin to keep. You find, on your last day, in the quietest corner of the lowest terrace, a pellet that is subtly wrong: not smooth-worn like the geological ones, but faintly patterned, faintly grained, faintly *apertured*, as though a pin's-head hole had been started and abandoned. You carry it a while. You put it back in the groove, where the water takes it.

---

So this is who lives in the Terraced Weep:

Not a people. Not even, in the way you mean it, a fauna. A population of slow, dark, flattened tenders lying along the wet bases of the walls, drinking the discharge, eating the silt, laying down their little mineral fences and spouts, keeping the great instrument of which they are the only moving parts in continuous, quiet, self-sustaining function. Hundreds of them. Perhaps thousands, in the deep terraces you did not reach. They will never know you were here. They will never know anything. They will keep the weep- channels weeping and the basins mirrored and the grooves bright for as long as the water comes down from the source, and when a keeper finally fails — and they do fail, after durations; you find one, once, a length of wall-base where the stippled grain has gone smooth and the apertures have closed like eyes, and the pale lip-crust around it is cracking, untended — the stretch of wall goes quiet for a while, and the adjacent keepers creep into the gap across the boundary-crusts at their glacial pace, and the boundary-crusts merge, and the vacancy is filled, and the instrument does not notice.

You come up through the narrow corridor at last and you look back down the stepped, gleaming, saturated cascade of it, and now that you know what to look for you can see them everywhere: the long dark slightly-wrong lines along the bases of the walls, each one a keeper, each one a length of living wall among the built walls, all of them drinking, all of them tending, all of them holding the whole place together at the pace of a pulse, under the even light, in the permanent cool.

The water runs. The stone weeps. The keepers keep.

Nothing else happens. That is their work: that nothing else happens.
