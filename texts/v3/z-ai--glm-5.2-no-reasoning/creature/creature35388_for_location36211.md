# What Lives in the Vastic Millwheel

---

## I. The Inheritors

Something lives here that was not always here.

That is the first thing you must understand, and it is the thing that the place itself would never tell you, because the place does not tell. The place *is*. But the swimmers — the pale, bluish, eel-shaped bodies that track the currents with the ease of thoughts following a familiar argument — they are not original to this system. Nothing in the ecology is. The plant is original. The plant is the condition. Everything else is a tenancy so old that the tenant has forgotten the lease exists, and the landlord never knew there was one.

The swimmers descend from something that swam in open water. You can see it in the geometry of their bodies — the hydrodynamic taper, the unlateralized tail, the placement of the sensing organs along a lateral line that traces a heritage of chasing and fleeing through three-dimensional space. They were not made for these channels. They were made for the open dark, for the hunt, for the breathless arithmetic of predator and prey that is the grammar of every ocean that has ever existed. And then they came here, or were brought here, or drifted here on some current that existed before the rotors standardized the flow, and they stayed.

Staying changed them.

Not dramatically. Not in the way that cave fish lose their eyes or deep-sea anglerfish grow their lures. The changes are subtler, and they are all in the direction of *less*. Less muscle mass. The rotors provide the current; the swimmers do not need to fight it. Less jaw strength. The plant tissue is soft, pre-processed, already partially broken down by the enzymatic surface of the substrate. Less aggression. There is nothing to compete for. The plant does not run out. The swimmers graze the way you might graze at a buffet that never empties and never closes and never changes its offerings — without urgency, without preference, without the desperate calculus of scarcity that drives every decision a wild animal makes.

Their color is the tell. The pale blue is not camouflage — nothing here hunts by sight, and the water is too clear for concealment to matter. The pale blue is *withdrawal*. It is the color of a body that has stopped producing the pigments of stress, of territorial display, of sexual selection. The swimmers do not display. They do not select. They reproduce — you can see the young, smaller versions of the adults, moving in the same currents with the same unhurried precision — but the reproduction is not seasonal, not competitive, not triggered by anything external. It is a maintenance function. It happens because the body has a rhythm, and the rhythm is the same rhythm as the rotors: slow, cyclic, without climax or resolution.

There are swimmers that have been alive for a very long time. You can identify them by the scars. The healed grazing marks on the substrate are paralleled by healed marks on the swimmers themselves — long, shallow gouges along the flanks, the marks of something that once caught them and did not finish the job. The scars are old. The tissue around them has grown pale, paler than the rest of the body, almost white, and the white traces a history of encounters with the things that live in the darker reaches of the chamber, the things that do still hunt, that do still operate on the old grammar of chase and kill, that never adapted, that never *could* adapt, because their nature is the old nature, and the old nature does not compromise.

The old swimmers are large. The largest you see is perhaps four meters long, and it moves with a deliberation that the younger ones lack — not slowness, but *intent*, the quality of a body that has been everywhere in this world that a body can go and has concluded that none of the places are different enough to hurry toward. It passes you at a distance of perhaps two meters. Its eye — a flat, dark disc, without iris, without expression — registers you. You are not food. You are not threat. You are not recognized. You are a fact of the water, like a change in temperature, like a floating particle of something the cleaners will eventually find and investigate. The swimmer passes, and its wake is a gentle displacement that pushes you slightly sideways in the current, and then it is gone, and the water closes behind it as though it was never there.

## II. The Cleaners

The cleaners are the organisms you understand first, because their function is legible. They are doing something that you can name. They are doing the dishes.

They are not crustaceans, though the description is close enough to be useful. They have bodies of calcium carbonate — the same mineral that reinforces the filaments on the ridges — and their bodies are articulated in a way that suggests limbs, though the limbs are not jointed in the arthropod manner. They are hinged. Each hinge is a weakness in the crystalline structure of the body, a point where the calcium carbonate is thinner and more flexible, and the movement is not muscular but *structural* — the body flexes at the hinges because the body is built to flex there, the way a hinge in a lid is built to flex, and the flexing is powered not by muscles but by the osmotic pressure of the water itself, which enters and leaves chambers in the body in a cycle that is, again, the same cycle as the rotors. Everything here runs on the same clock.

There are many of them. More than you can count, because they are small — the largest is the size of your hand — and because they move slowly and because they are the color of the substrate, a mottled brown-green that is not camouflage but *incorporation*. They have been here so long that they have taken on the color of the place they live in, not through adaptation but through accretion. Their shells are layered with the same biofilm they remove, deposited and re-deposited in such thin films that the color has become part of them, and they have become part of the color of the substrate, and the distinction between cleaner and cleaned has blurred to the point of meaninglessness.

They move in patterns. The patterns are not random — you can see the logic if you watch long enough. Each cleaner has a territory, a region of substrate that it covers in a circuit that takes approximately six hours, and the circuit is not memorized but *grown into* — the cleaner follows chemical gradients in the substrate that mark where biofilm has accumulated since the last pass, and the gradients are produced by the substrate itself, which exudes a compound that binds to the biofilm and makes it detectable. The plant is not directing the cleaners. The plant is not aware of the cleaners. The plant exudes the compound because the compound is a byproduct of a metabolic process that has nothing to do with cleaning, and the cleaners have evolved to detect it because detecting it leads them to food, and the food is the biofilm, and the removal of the biofilm benefits the plant, and the benefit is not the plant's goal but the system's shape.

This is how everything works here. Nothing is for anything. Everything is the result of something else, and the something else is the result of something else, and the chain does not have a beginning and does not have an end and does not have a purpose, and the absence of purpose is not an absence. It is a presence. It is the presence of equilibrium, and the equilibrium is so complete that the word "purpose" has no traction here, no more than the word "up" has traction in a place without a sky.

The cleaners do not die. Or rather — they do not die of age. They die of accidents, of the larger things that sometimes come up from the dark and eat them, of the slow accumulation of damage that eventually exceeds the body's capacity for repair. But they do not *age*. A cleaner that has been alive for fifty years is indistinguishable from a cleaner that has been alive for five. The only difference is size — the older cleaners are slightly larger, because they have added more layers to their shells — and the size is not a decline. It is an accumulation. They grow the way a pearl grows, by adding material to a surface, and the material is the same material the substrate is made of, and when a cleaner dies — when it is eaten, or crushed, or simply eroded past the point of function — its shell dissolves, and the calcium carbonate returns to the water, and the water carries it to the terraces, and the terraces extract it, and the substrate uses it to build more of itself, and the cycle is closed, and the closer is the death.

## III. The Vein-Dwellers

The organisms in the veins of the great leaf are the hardest to see, because the leaf is above you and the veins are thick and the sap is not transparent. You see them as shadows. Dark, hand-shaped shadows that move along the veins with the flow, and the movement is not swimming. The vein-dwellers do not swim. They are carried. They have attached themselves to the inner walls of the veins the way a barnacle attaches to a hull, and they feed from the sap as it passes, and they release their grip when they need to move — when the sap changes, when the concentration of sugar drops, when the vein widens or narrows — and they drift to a new location and reattach.

They are not parasites. The description has been given, and it is accurate, but it does not capture what they are, because "parasite" implies harm, and there is no harm here. The vein-dwellers take a small fraction of the sap's sugar, a fraction so small that the leaf does not notice it, cannot notice it, has no mechanism for noticing it. They are a rounding error in a budget so large that the budget has become geological. They take what they need and no more, not because they are restrained but because taking more would require a metabolism they do not possess, and the metabolism they do not possess is the metabolism of excess, and excess is not a concept that exists here.

What they are, more precisely, is *commensal in the way a river is commensal with its banks*. They live in the flow. They are shaped by the flow. Their bodies are flat, elongated, tapered at both ends, and they orient themselves along the axis of the vein with a precision that is not learned but structural — their bodies are built to align with current, the way a weather vane is built to align with wind, and the alignment is not a behavior but a physics. They are, in a sense, instruments. They measure the flow of sap by being in it, and the measurement they produce is their life, and their life is a number, and the number is the rate at which the leaf feeds the body, and the body does not need to know the number, but the number is maintained, and the maintaining is the vein-dwellers' function, though "function" is, again, a word that implies something the system does not have.

There are vein-dwellers in every vein. The smallest capillaries have the smallest dwellers — creatures no larger than a grain of rice, visible only as specks of darkness in the fine network of the leaf's extremities. The largest veins, the rivers that carry the bulk of the sap from the center of the leaf to its edges, have dwellers the size of your forearm, and these are the ones you can see most clearly, and when you see them clearly — when you rise close enough to the underside of the leaf that the veins are meters above you and the sap is visible through the translucent wall of the vein as a golden, viscous fluid — you see that the dwellers are not simple.

They have structure. They have internal organs, or organs that serve the function of organs — filters that extract sugar from the sap, sacs that store it, channels that distribute it to the tissues of the body. They have a nervous system. It is not complex — it is a ladder of ganglia along the ventral surface, and it controls the grip, the orientation, the release, and nothing else. They do not have brains. They do not have senses beyond the tactile and the chemical. They do not have anything that resembles a thought. But they have a *pattern*, a pattern that is the pattern of the vein they live in, and the pattern is so consistent that a vein-dweller removed from its vein and placed in another vein of the same diameter and flow rate will behave identically, because its behavior is not a product of its individuality but of its context, and its context is the vein, and the vein is the leaf, and the leaf is the plant, and the plant is the world.

## IV. The Dark-Reachers

Beyond the light, in the parts of the chamber where the rotors' glow does not reach and the leaf's green-gold falls short, there are other things.

You do not see them clearly. You see their effects. A swimmer that was here a moment ago is gone, and the water where it was is clouded with a faint, dark particulate that dissipates in the current. A cleaner that was climbing a ridge has vanished, and the ridge has a new mark on it, a scrape that was not there before. The sounds change — the deep thrum of the rotors acquires an edge, a higher frequency, a catch, as though something has briefly interfered with the flow.

The dark-reachers are predators. They are the only predators in this world. They are the reason the swimmers have scars, the reason the cleaners sometimes disappear, the reason the ecology is not a closed loop of perfect efficiency but a loop with teeth in it. They live in the dark because the dark is where the system is weakest, where the light does not penetrate and the currents are slower and the substrate is thinner and the rules — the rules of equilibrium, of maintenance, of the endless, changeless continuation — are less enforced.

They are large. You infer this from the marks they leave — the scars on the swimmers are spaced wide, as though made by something with a broad mouth or a wide grip. The particulate they produce when they feed is not blood, not tissue, but a dark, fine cloud that is the byproduct of a digestive process that does not resemble any digestion you know. It is rapid. It is complete. It breaks the prey down into mineral and water and a small amount of organic compound that the dark-reacher absorbs through its surface, and the mineral settles to the substrate and the water joins the current and the cycle continues, and the only evidence that a living thing was eaten is the cloud, and the cloud lasts less than ten seconds.

You catch a glimpse of one. Once. At the edge of the light, where the green-gold dims to amber dims to gray, something moves. It is not the movement of a swimmer — not the sinuous, current-riding motion of something that belongs in the water. It is a *displacement*. The water moves aside, and the thing that moved it is the shape of the absence. You see what is not there, and what is not there is large — five meters, perhaps six, perhaps more, because the edge of it is beyond the light and the edge is not a boundary but a guess — and it is dark, not black but the dark of something that absorbs light, that does not reflect, that has optimized its surface for invisibility in a world where visibility is the rule.

It has eyes. Or it has something that functions as eyes — two points of bioluminescence, a deep red, positioned where eyes would be on a face, and the red is not for seeing. The red is for *signaling*. It is a signal to other dark-reachers, or to the things it hunts, or to the dark itself. The meaning of the signal is not knowable. It is a word in a language that has only one speaker and one listener, and both of them are the dark.

The dark-reacher does not come into the light. It does not need to. The light is where the prey is, but the prey comes to the edge of the light — the old swimmers, the large ones, the ones that have been here long enough to know that the edge is where the current is slowest and the grazing is best — and at the edge, the dark-reacher takes them, and the taking is fast, and the dark closes, and the light does not change, and the rotors do not slow, and the world does not notice.

The dark-reachers are the only things in this world that change. They are the only things that are different from what they were. The swimmers have adapted to the system. The cleaners have adapted to the system. The vein-dwellers have adapted to the system. The dark-reachers have adapted to the *edge* of the system, to the place where the system's rules break down, and the adaptation is not stability but *opportunity*. They are the gradient. They are the thing that is not at equilibrium. They are the crack in the perfect machine, and the crack is not a flaw. The crack is necessary. Without the dark-reachers, the swimmers would grow without limit, the cleaners would accumulate without limit, the vein-dwellers would clog the veins, and the system would collapse under its own weight. The dark-reachers are the system's pressure relief. They are the reason the system can be stable, because stability is not the absence of change but the balance of change, and the balance is between the thing that builds and the thing that eats, and the eating is the dark-reachers' work, and the work is done in the dark, and the dark is where the light does not go, and the light does not go there because the leaf does not extend that far, and the leaf does not extend that far because the leaf has no reason to, and the reason it has no reason is that the dark is not its problem.

The dark is the dark-reachers' problem. And the dark-reachers are the dark's solution.

## V. The Question of the Swimmers' Inner Life

Here is the thing you cannot determine, and it is the thing that will keep you awake after you leave.

The swimmers behave as though they are not conscious. They respond to stimuli. They seek food, avoid the dark edge, orient to the current, graze, rest, reproduce. But they do not *choose*. There is no moment in which a swimmer pauses, considers, and takes an action that is not the action the current and the substrate and the light would predict. They are deterministic in the way a clock is deterministic, and the clock is a mechanism, and the mechanism has no inner life, and the inner life is the thing you cannot find.

But.

The old swimmers — the large ones, the scarred ones, the ones that have been here for longer than you can estimate — they behave differently. Not much. Not in a way that would be visible to a casual observation. But in a way that is visible if you watch for a long time, and you have watched for a long time, and the difference is this: they go to the dark edge on purpose.

Not to die. The ones that go to the dark edge come back. They come back with new scars, sometimes, but they come back. They go to the edge and they stay there for a time — minutes, hours, you cannot tell — and they return, and the returning is not the behavior of something that was carried by the current or driven by hunger or following a chemical gradient. The returning is the behavior of something that *went somewhere and came back*. The behavior of something that had a reason, and the reason was not food and was not safety and was not reproduction.

The reason was the dark.

The old swimmers go to the dark edge because the dark edge is where something *happens*. The rest of this world is the same. The same light, the same water, the same temperature, the same sound, the same everything, always, without variation, without surprise, without event. The dark edge is different. The dark edge is where the dark-reachers are, and the dark-reachers are the only things in this world that are not the same as they were a moment ago. The dark edge is where change lives, and the old swimmers go there because they are old enough to have noticed that change exists, and the noticing is not a behavior. The noticing is a *preference*. And a preference is not a mechanism. A preference is the first, faintest, most deniable evidence of an inner life.

You do not know if the swimmers are conscious. You do not know if the old ones have something that you would recognize as awareness, as experience, as the felt quality of being alive. But you know that they go to the dark, and they come back, and they go again, and the going is not driven by anything you can identify, and the not-driven is what you call choice, and choice is what you call consciousness, and consciousness is what you call a soul, and the word "soul" is absurd in this place, in this world of plant and water and rotors and the endless, changeless turning, but the absurdity does not make the going stop. The going is real. The going is the one thing in this world that is not explained by the system.

The going is the crack in the machine where something leaks through, and what leaks through is not water, not mineral, not sugar. What leaks through is *want*. And want is not a function. Want is not a maintenance process. Want is the thing that the system cannot account for, because the system accounts for everything, and want is the thing that is not everything. Want is the one thing that is *more*.

And you do not know what to do with that, so you watch, and the old swimmer goes to the dark again, and the dark takes it, and the dark gives it back, and the giving-back is the most important thing that happens here, and the most important thing that happens here is invisible, and the invisible thing is the reason you will remember this place, and the reason you will remember this place is not the rotors or the leaf or the terraces or the sound.

The reason you will remember this place is that something here *wants*. Something here chooses. Something here, in the cool, clear, changeless water, in the world that has finished, in the machine that has no designer and no purpose and no end, something here goes to the dark and comes back, and the going and the coming-back is the only story this place has, and the story is enough.

The story is enough.
