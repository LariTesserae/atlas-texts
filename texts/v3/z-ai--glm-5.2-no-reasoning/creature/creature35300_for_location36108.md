# What Lives Here

The first thing to understand is that nothing here *inhabits* the chamber in the way you might inhabit a room. Nothing chose this place. Everything here was *grown into* it — shaped by the same slow force that shaped the walls, the channel, the pump itself. The beetles did not arrive from elsewhere and find a niche. They emerged here, the way a callus emerges on rubbed skin. Their ancestors lived here. Their ancestors' ancestors lived here. The chamber and the beetles are the same age, which is to say they have no age — they have always been *becoming* this, together.

---

## The Beetles

Fourteen of them. You can count them because they hold position the way stars hold position: reliably, recognizably, in a pattern that shifts only at the scale of lifetimes. Each one has a territory on the pump's surface — not defended, not marked, simply *occupied*, the way a stone occupies the ground it rests on. They do not fight. They do not compete. There are fourteen lenticel-clusters on the pump and fourteen beetles and the math is not a coincidence. The math is the point.

They are flat. Flatter than you expect when you see one up close — a body profile like a shield pressed close to the wood, legs tucked under the carapace in a posture that suggests they are always bracing against something. Against the pump's vibration, maybe. Against the exhalation of the lenticels. Against the possibility of being dislodged. Their shells are dark green but not a green that comes from pigment. It is structural color — the same iridescence that makes a peacock's feather or an oil slick on wet pavement. In the chamber's amber light they look almost black. Only when one moves do you catch the flash: a seam of teal, a thread of violet along the elytra's edge, there and gone.

Their mouths are the remarkable thing. You have to be close to see it — and being close means pressing your face against a warm, sweating, vibrating root-pump while a fine mist of tannin-water settles on your eyelashes — but if you are close, you can see the mandibles. They are not pincers. They are not scoops. They are *fittings*. Curved, grooved, precision-shaped to seat against the rim of a lenticel the way a key seats against a lock. When a beetle settles onto an opening pore, its mandibles click into position with a sound so small it is more felt than heard — a tiny mechanical snap, like a watch crystal seating. Then the lenticel breathes out, and the beetle drinks.

What they drink is not water. The mist that exits the lenticels during compression is a solution — tannin-thick, mineral-laden, carrying compounds you would need a spectrometer to name. But you can infer some of it from the beetles themselves. Their carapaces are harder than they should be for an insect this size. Their legs are unusually strong — when one walks, it moves with a deliberation that suggests it is carrying more weight than its volume would predict. Whatever they are extracting from the pump's breath, it is densifying them. Mineralizing them. They are becoming, slowly, more like the machine they live on. Their shells have a slight warmth to them, even when the pump is between beats. They hold the heat.

They live a long time. You know this not because you can observe a lifespan but because you can observe *patience*. A beetle will sit on a closed lenticel for minutes, motionless, waiting for the compression phase. It does not fidget. It does not search for a better pore. It waits. This is not learned behavior — newly-emerged adults do it with the same untroubled stillness as elders whose carapaces have been worn smooth by years of mist. It is written into them. The pump writes the schedule. The beetles read it with their bodies.

When one dies — and they do die, eventually, the mineralization that hardens their shells presumably reaching some organ that cannot handle the density — it does not fall. It stays where it was, gripped by the surface tension of the mist-film, and over the course of days it is consumed. Not by the other beetles. By the algae on the pump's surface, the same thin film that coats the chamber walls. The dead beetle becomes a dark spot, a slight thickening of the green, and then it is part of the pump's surface, and the lenticel it once attended has already been claimed by a larva that pupated in the channel-bank and climbed the pump in the night with the blind certainty of something that has always known where it was going.

Fourteen. Always fourteen. The number is not symbolic. It is structural. It is the number of feeding sites the pump supports, and the population has calibrated itself to that number over a span of time that makes the calibration perfect. Not through planning. Through the slow grinding of generation against resource, resource against generation, until the fit is so tight that nothing wobbles. Fourteen beetles. Fourteen pores. A lock and its keys.

## The Moths

Nine. Always nine. They are harder to count than the beetles because they do not move and because they are the color of everything that is not the pump. Wet bark, wet root, wet wood — the moths are the color of wet wood. Their wings are broad and flat and held against the ceiling's branch-surface with a stillness that goes beyond camouflage into something like *absorption*. They are not hiding. They are *being* the ceiling.

When you first entered, you saw six. Then your eyes adjusted and you saw eight. Then one shifted — a rotation of perhaps five degrees, one wing tipping to catch the light differently — and you saw the ninth, and you understood that you had been looking at it for several minutes without seeing it, and that it had let you look, and that its invisibility was not a defense but a *state*. The moth was not concealed. The moth was *inconsequential*. It occupied the space between visual attention and recognition, and it lived there comfortably.

They are not like the moths you know. Their bodies are wrong for moths — too dense, too rigid, as though they have been partially mineralized the way the beetles have. Their wings are not powder-soft. When the one shifted, the movement was precise, mechanical, the wing rotating on its hinge with a controlled ease that suggested a joint rather than a membrane. The wings have texture: fine ridges running from base to tip, like the grain of the wood they rest against. These ridges are not decorative. They are acoustic. The moths are listening to the ceiling.

This is what you piece together, crouching in the warm dark, watching the nine shapes that are almost not shapes: the moths are monitoring the pump's output from above. The xylem column that rises through the ceiling carries not just water but vibration — the pump's rhythm transmitted upward through the trunk, attenuating, dispersing, reaching the branch-woven vault as a tremor so subtle that nothing with a less sensitive instrument than a moth's ridged wing could detect it. The moths sit against the wood and they feel the pulse and they know, the way a doctor knows by pressing fingers to a wrist, whether the pump is running true.

They do not know that they know this. They have no language for *true* or *false*. But their bodies know. If the pump's rhythm changed — faster, slower, irregular — the moths would know before the beetles, before the wasps, before the chamber itself registered the difference. What they would do with that knowledge is unclear. They have no capacity for intervention. They are monitoring a machine they cannot repair. Perhaps that is the point. Perhaps a system this old needs not maintenance but *witness*. Perhaps the moths are the chamber's way of knowing itself.

When dusk comes — or when whatever signal they are waiting for arrives — they will descend to the pump. Not to drink. To lay. Their eggs are small, dark, and already slightly warm when they emerge, deposited in the mist that collects in the lenticel-channels between the beetles' feedings. The larvae that hatch from those eggs will not look like moth larvae. They will look like the pump's own tissue — pale, wet, fibrous — and they will feed on the film that grows on the lenticels' lips, a film of algae and mineral residue that the pump's breath sustains. They will feed and grow and pupate against the pump's warm surface, and they will emerge as adults, and they will climb to the ceiling, and they will find a branch, and they will press their wings flat against it, and they will wait.

Nine. Always nine. Three per quadrant, with one quadrant holding the entrance gap and therefore only three spread across a wider arc. The spacing is not territorial. It is acoustic. Each moth sits at a node — a point where the branch-vault's vibration pattern peaks — and the nine nodes together form a sensor array that covers the entire ceiling. They are spaced the way microphones are spaced in a studio. They are not aware of this.

## The Wasps

You cannot count them. This troubles you more than it should.

They are the most active things in the chamber, which means they are the least settled, which means they are the hardest to hold in your attention. The beetles stay. The moths stay. The wasps *come and go*, and their coming and going is so constant and so purposeful that you cannot pin down a number. Six? Twelve? More? They are thin-waisted and amber-bodied and they move with a jerkiness that feels almost offensive after the pump's measured rhythm and the beetles' glacial patience and the moths' absolute stillness. They are the only thing here that reminds you of the world above — the world of speed and urgency and things that must be done *now*.

Their holes are perfect. Each one is a circle the same diameter as a pencil lead, bored into the softer wood of the chamber's lower walls where the root tissue is youngest and least dense. The holes are spaced at intervals that look irregular but are not — there is a logic to the spacing, a pattern that follows the grain, and the wasps knew the pattern before they bored. The wood told them. The wood's fiber orientation, its density gradients, its moisture content — these are the instructions the wasps read with their ovipositors and their mandibles, and they bore where the wood says *bore*, and the result is a distribution of nests that strengthens the wall rather than weakening it.

This is the part that is hard to accept. The wasps are not parasites. They are not pests. They are not exploiting the chamber. They are *maintaining* it.

What they carry out: degraded tissue. Soft wood that has been wet too long, root-matter that is losing its structure, cellulose that is breaking down into the kind of mush that rots. They carry it in their mandibles, tiny pellets of wet pulp, and they carry it *out* — through the entrance gap, into the root-throat, up into the open air, where they drop it. You do not know where it goes after that. You do not know how far they carry it. You know only that it leaves.

What they carry in: resin. Not tree resin — something else. A secretion, perhaps from their own bodies, perhaps harvested from the root-mat's deeper layers. It is amber (the color that names them) and it hardens on contact with air and it is the reason the chamber's walls do not rot. The wasps line their holes with it. They line the *walls* with it — a thin film, applied over years, over generations, until the chamber's inner surface is sealed against the decay that should have consumed it long ago. The wood is wet but it does not rot. The wood is warm but it does not compost. The wasps have made it *permanent*. Or as permanent as anything alive can be.

They do not interact with the pump. This is important. The pump is the beetles' domain and the moths' concern. The wasps belong to the *structure* — the container, the chamber, the space that holds the machine. They are the building's keepers. If the pump is the heart, the wasps are the pericardium — the sac that holds the heart in its place and keeps it from touching anything it should not touch.

They are not aggressive. You learn this when you accidentally brush the wall near a cluster of nest-holes and four wasps emerge and hover — not toward you, not away, just *at* you, at a distance of about twenty centimeters, their thin bodies vibrating in air that is already vibrating with the pump's pulse. They look at you with compound eyes that are not angry but *assessing*. You are not wood. You are not rot. You are not resin. You are nothing they have a protocol for. They hover for six seconds — you count them against the pump's rhythm, *thk-thk-thk-thk-thk-thk* — and then they return to their holes, having decided, by some criterion you cannot access, that you are not a structural concern.

You are not a structural concern. You almost laugh. But laughter seems wrong here. Everything seems wrong here except the pump.

## The Thing That Is Not an Insect

There is one more living thing in the chamber. You almost miss it. You *should* miss it. It does not want to be seen and it has had a very long time to learn how not to be seen.

You notice it because of the algae. The green film on the chamber walls is not uniform — it is slightly thicker on the western side, slightly thinner on the eastern, and in one spot, about a meter above the floor, it forms a shape that is too regular to be accidental. A oval of bare wood, about the size of your palm, clear of algae, surrounded by a slightly raised lip of thicker growth. It looks like a wound that has healed around a scab that has not fallen off. It looks like a face with no features.

It is not a face. But it is not nothing.

The thing on the wall is a flatworm. You know this the way you know the pump is a pump — by function, by inference, by the slow accumulation of evidence that resolves into certainty without ever passing through proof. It is not a species you know. It is not a species anyone knows. It is the color of the wood it lives on, and its surface is textured like the wood it lives on, and it does not move while you are watching it. But it *has* moved. The bare patch of wood where the algae has been cleared — that is its feeding ground. It grazes the algae that grows on the chamber walls, keeping one small area clean, and the clean area is shaped like a body because the body was resting there while it ate.

You watch for a long time. The pump counts the time for you. Thirty beats. Sixty. Ninety. The thing does not move. It is so still that you begin to doubt your inference. Maybe it is a scar in the wood. Maybe it is a stain. Maybe you are seeing patterns in the grain the way you see faces in clouds, the way your mind insists on significance where there is only texture.

Then, between one beat and the next — between the *thk* and the *thk* — it moves. Not quickly. Not slowly. With a velocity that has been chosen. It inches forward along the wall, its body so flat and so closely applied to the surface that it seems less like an animal moving and more like the wood itself rearranging. It covers three centimeters in the time it takes the pump to cycle twice. Then it stops. It is in the same position relative to the wall, in the same posture, having translated an invisible distance. If you had not been staring, you would not know anything had changed.

It is not part of the system. Not the way the beetles are part of the system, or the moths, or the wasps. It does not interact with the pump. It does not serve the tree. It lives here because the chamber is warm and damp and the algae grows and the algae grows because the light filters through the water-skin above and the walls are wet and the conditions are right. The flatworm is an *opportunist*. It is the only thing in this chamber that could leave and survive elsewhere. The beetles would die without the pump's mist. The moths would die without the pump's vibration. The wasps would die without the root-wood to maintain. The flatworm would be fine. The flatworm has options.

But it stays. It has been here long enough that its body has taken on the color and texture of the walls. It has been here long enough that the algae has learned to grow *around* its resting shape, building the raised lip that marks its territory. It has been here long enough to have become, if not part of the system, then part of the *place* — a feature, like the callus on the channel or the depression under the pump. It has worn itself into the chamber the way water wears stone. Not by force. By *presence*.

It is the only thing in the chamber that is aware of you.

You know this because when you shift your weight — a small adjustment, your knee settling a centimeter deeper into the root-mat — the flatworm's oval shifts. Not away from you. Not toward you. It *tenses*. A contraction so subtle that it is visible only as a slight change in the texture of the bare patch, a tightening of the body's edge against the wall. It felt your movement through the wood. It is feeling you now. It is feeling the warmth of your body, the carbon dioxide of your breath, the unfamiliar vibration of your heartbeat — which is, you realize, faster than the pump's. Much faster. Your heart sounds frantic in this context. Your heart sounds *rude*.

The flatworm does not flee. It does not attack. It registers you the way a seismograph registers a tremor: precisely, without comment, without alarm. You are data. You are a disturbance in a field that has been undisturbed for longer than you can imagine. It notes you and it does not care and it will not remember you after you leave.

This is what it is like to be perceived by something that has no opinion. It is unsettling. It is clarifying. You have been in the chamber for — you do not know how long. Long enough that the pump's rhythm has begun to feel like your own. Long enough that the amber light has stopped looking strange and has started looking *correct*. Long enough that the flatworm's regard, which is not regard, has made you understand something about this place that the beetles and the moths and the wasps could not teach you.

Everything here is alive. The pump is alive. The walls are alive. The water is alive in the sense that it carries life. The air is alive in the sense that it carries the pump's breath. But nothing here is *aware* in the way you are aware. Nothing here has a self. The beetles have function. The moths have position. The wasps have purpose. The flatworm has sensation. But none of them have *selves*. They are processes. They are things happening, not things *being*.

And the chamber does not miss having a self. The pump does not feel the absence of consciousness. The chamber is complete without an observer. It was complete before you came. It will be complete after you leave. Your awareness of it is not part of its completeness. Your wonder is not part of its wonder. It does not wonder. It *is*.

## What Lives Here

Everything. Nothing. A pump that does not know it is a pump, feeding a tree that does not know it is fed. Beetles that are keys. Moths that are microphones. Wasps that are mortar. A flatworm that is a sensor array with no one to read it. An algae that is paint. A water that is blood. A wood that is wall and floor and ceiling and machine and machine-housing all at once.

What lives here is a process that has been running so long it has forgotten how to start and has never learned how to stop. What lives here is the absence of choice. What lives here is the presence of *fit* — every thing in its place, every place shaped by its thing, every shape the result of a negotiation between wood and water and insect and time that was settled so long ago the terms are no longer visible.

What lives here is forty beats per minute, unwavering, in a room no one will ever name, feeding a tree no one will ever see, in a country that has no use for names because everything here already knows what it is.

*Thk. Thk. Thk.*

Still going.
