# What Lives in the Sump

The first answer is: everyone. Everything. The place is so densely inhabited that the question is not *what* lives here but *what doesn't*, and the answer to that is nothing, nothing doesn't live here, there is no unoccupied volume, no unused molecule, no gap between organisms that is not itself an organism. Life in the Sump does not exist *in* the environment so much as the environment *is* life, compressed to the point of becoming substance, becoming terrain. You are not walking on ground. You are walking on the living and the recently dead and the not-yet-dead and the in-between, and all of them are at home.

But that is the ecological answer, the systems answer, the answer that describes the Sump the way a census describes a city. It is accurate and it is insufficient. What lives *here*, in *this* place, in the specific sense of *whose home is this* — that is a different question, and it has a slower answer.

---

## The Chord-Makers

Start with the smallest and most numerous, because they are the foundation and because they are the closest thing this place has to a definitive presence.

They are pale. They are soft. They have not yet decided to be hard.

The insect-analogs of the Sump are not insects in the way you would recognize — no chitinous armor, no polished carapace reflecting light. Their exoskeletons are leathery, gristly, still somewhat translucent at the joints, and through the membrane you can see the darker threads of whatever serves them as gut, as nerve, as the internal scaffolding that will eventually, in some distant descendant, harden into something more permanent. For now they are unfinished. They are drafts. They are the first pencil-sketch of what an insect might be, and they have been left in pencil because the place they inhabit does not require permanence. Nothing here requires permanence. Permanence is a technology for places that have winters, that have dry seasons, that have *change*. The Sump has no change. The Sump has only continuation, and continuation does not demand armor.

They ride the convection columns — the warm, wet columns of air that rise from the decomposing mesh where the heat is generated — and they ride them the way plankton ride the ocean currents, passively but not randomly, oriented by some sense you cannot identify toward the zones where the vapor is thickest and the suspended organic particles are densest. They feed in the air. The air is their ocean and their pasture simultaneously. They filter it. Their mouths — if they are mouths — are open all the time, and the moisture that condenses on the fine hairs of their bodies carries with it the microscopic organic matter that the Sump sheds from every surface, and they eat what the Sump excretes, which is also what the Sump is made of, which is also what the Sump will become. They eat the future and the past with equal indifference.

Their sound is the first thing you learn to hear as music rather than noise, and this takes time. At first it is a drone, a whine, a vibration in the bones of your skull. But stand in it long enough and the drone resolves, and you begin to hear the structure within it — the way certain frequencies cluster, the way certain individuals or certain species produce tones that interlock with the tones of others, not by intention but by the brute mathematics of proximity and population. It is not a chorus. A chorus implies coordination. It is a *chord* — the accidental harmony of ten thousand instruments that happen to be in tune because they were built from the same materials by the same process in the same conditions, and sameness produces consonance, and consonance produces something that is not far from beauty.

They do not know they are making sound. They do not know they are making anything. They are breathing, or they are stridulating, or they are performing some function that is both and neither, and the sound is the byproduct, the way heat is the byproduct of a body at work. They are the Sump's respiration made audible. When they are densest — in the vapor columns, in the still air between the reed-stands where the moisture hangs heaviest — their collective sound is almost a single tone, a held note, and it vibrates the stalks around them, and the stalks vibrate the water on their surfaces, and the water falls in tiny droplets back to the mesh, and the cycle completes, and the note holds.

They do not die individually. They die constantly. Their lifespans are measured in hours, perhaps, or in some unit for which you have no equivalent — some fraction of a biological cycle that is governed not by the rotation of the planet or the tilt of its axis but by the rate at which the mesh decomposes and reconstitutes and decomposes again. They are born from eggs laid in the condensation on the underside of leaves, they mature in the time it takes for the condensation to evaporate and reform, they mate in the columns of vapor, they lay their eggs on the next available wet surface, and they cease. They do not cease dramatically. They simply slow, and stop, and settle, and become part of the mesh, and the mesh feeds the next generation, and the next generation stridulates at the same frequency, and the note holds.

This is their home. The entire Sump is their home, every cubic centimeter of it, because they are small enough to inhabit the air itself, and the air is everywhere, and the air is always warm, and the air is always wet, and the air is always full of the food that the Sump provides by the simple act of being alive and shedding. They do not leave because there is nowhere to go that is not this. They do not arrive because they have never been anywhere else. They are the closest thing the Sump has to a permanent resident, and they have been here, in one form or another, since the first protein decided to fold itself into something that could stridulate.

## The Complainers

Beneath the insect-layer, occupying the pools and the hollows and the wet depressions in the mesh, the amphibian-things have made their homes, and their homes are puddles, and they are territorial about them in a way that is both absurd and entirely characteristic of this place.

They are larger than the insects — palm-sized, some of them, or fist-sized, or the size of a spread hand — and they are wet, always wet, their skin glistening with the moisture that is their medium and their necessity and their obsession. They have the general shape of frogs, if frogs were still in the conceptual phase, if frogs were a proposal rather than a finished product. Their bodies are soft, their limbs are thick and blunt, their heads are broad and flat and nearly featureless — two bumps that might be eyes, a slit that might be a mouth, and elsewhere on the head, in patterns that vary from individual to individual, a scattering of pits and ridges that are clearly sensory but whose sense you cannot identify. They are reading the water. They are tasting the air. They are doing something with the chemical information that floods their environment, something more complex and more detailed than anything your own senses can achieve.

Their calls are the second layer of the Sump's sound, and they are — there is no other word for it — complaints.

A thrum. A honk. A long, descending moan that starts at one frequency and slides down to another, passing through frequencies that are felt rather than heard, that vibrate in the chest, that make the water in the pools around them ripple with sympathetic resonance. They call from their pools, and other individuals in other pools call back, and the exchange is not dialogue — it is not information transfer — it is simply volume, simply insistence, simply the refusal to be silent in a place where silence does not exist.

They are complaining about territory. Each pool — each depression, each hollow, each transient accumulation of water — is a home, and each home is defended, not by violence but by volume. The louder the call, the larger the claimed territory. The deeper the moan, the more serious the claim. When two individuals are too close, their calls accelerate, intensify, overlap, until the two sounds become a single churning noise that resolves only when one of them moves, shifting its bulk to another pool, another hollow, another few centimeters of the Sump's endless wet surface.

They are not fighting. Fighting implies investment, implies a future in which the outcome matters. They are negotiating, and the negotiation is conducted entirely in the present tense, and it is resolved the moment one of them shifts position, and then it is forgotten, and then it begins again, because there is always another individual too close, there is always another voice too near, there is always a reason to complain.

They feed in their pools. The pools are not empty — they are, like everything else in the Sump, saturated with life, with larvae, with microorganisms, with the organic sediment that settles out of the saturated air and collects in the low places. The amphibian-things sit in this soup and absorb it, filter it, pass it through their bodies and out again, and the waste feeds the pool, and the pool feeds the mesh, and the mesh feeds the insects, and the insects feed the air, and the air feeds the pools. They are a node in a circuit, and the circuit is closed, and they do not know it, and they do not need to know it.

Some of them have young, and their young are not what you would expect. They are not tadpoles, not exactly — they are smaller, softer, more formless, transparent almost to the point of invisibility, little ghosts of organisms that drift in the pools and grow by accretion, by the accumulation of the same organic matter their parents are filtering, until they are large enough to settle onto the mesh and claim a pool of their own and begin to complain. The transition from drift to claim, from transparent to opaque, from juvenile to adult, is gradual, unmarked, unceremonious. There is no metamorphosis here. Metamorphosis is a technology for organisms that need to change shape in order to survive a change in environment, and the environment here does not change. They simply grow, and as they grow they solidify, and as they solidify they find their voice, and as they find their voice they begin to complain, and the complaint is the beginning of their adult life, and it will not end until they cease, which they will, and which will be unmarked, and which will not matter, because there are ten thousand more exactly like them, and ten thousand more after that.

They are at home. They have always been at home. They will always be at home, in the warm dark pools of the Sump, in the water that is always there, in the air that is always wet, in the sound that is always complaint. They are the Sump's id, its ongoing grumble, its refusal to be silent, its insistence that the conditions, while adequate, while survivable, while literally the only conditions they have ever known, are somehow not quite right, and that someone should be told.

## The Waders

The larger animals move through the Sump the way ships move through fog — you know they are there by the sound of their displacement, by the creaking of stalks, by the sigh of water pushed aside, by the subtle change in the insect-chord as the insects adjust their positions to accommodate a body passing through their column. You hear them before you see them, and when you see them, you see them only partially — a flank, a limb, a broad head, a section of mottled back — and then they are gone, or not gone but reabsorbed into the visual noise of the Sump, which is designed, by the logic of its dim light and its dense growth, to make everything partially visible.

There are several kinds. You learn this slowly, over time, by the sound of their movement and the quality of their glimpses.

The first kind is the wader. You encounter it — or it encounters you, or neither of you encounters the other because encounter implies recognition and what is happening is simpler than that — in the lower ground between two reed-stands, where the water is more open and the mesh is thinner. You hear it before you see it: a slow, heavy, rhythmic displacement, the sound of something substantial moving through shallow water with deliberation. Splash. Pause. Splash. Pause. The pause is as long as the splash, and the rhythm is patient, and it comes from your left and moves to your right and does not alter its pace when it passes the place where you stand.

It is the size of a large dog, low to the ground, with a body that is broad and flat and supported by four legs that are thick and short and seem to have the texture of wet clay. Its skin — if it is skin — is mottled in patterns of gray and green and the brown of old water, and the patterns shift as it moves, not with the rapid color-change of an octopus but with a slow, tide-like movement, as though the pigmentation is suspended in a medium that is slightly fluid, that responds to the animal's position and posture and the light falling on it by rearranging itself at a pace that is visible only if you watch for a long time, which you cannot, because it is already past.

Its head is broad and flat and carried low, nearly at the level of the water, and on the head are two structures that you eventually identify as eyes — not because they look like eyes, but because they are positioned where eyes would be, and because they orient toward changes in the Sump's sound-pattern in a way that suggests they are gathering information, though whether the information is visual or acoustic or something else entirely is beyond your ability to determine. They are not bright. They are not focused. They are dark patches on a darker head, and they may be functional or they may be vestigial or they may be in the process of becoming one or the other, and the animal does not seem to care which.

It navigates by touch. Its legs, as they move, brush the surface of the mesh, and the brushing is not incidental — it is sensory, it is a way of reading the terrain by contact, by vibration, by the texture and tension of the surface beneath it. It knows where it is the way a blind person knows a room, by the feel of the walls and the furniture and the spaces between. It does not need to see. Seeing is not useful here. The light is too dim, the vapor too thick, the visual range too short. Touch is the primary sense in the Sump, and the wader is fluent in it.

It is looking for something. You cannot tell what. It moves from pool to pool, lowering its head to the surface of each one, staying for a moment, then moving on. It may be eating. It may be drinking. It may be sampling the chemical content of each pool, reading the Sump's information the way the amphibian-things read it, but at a different scale, with a different appetite. It is at home here, in the lower ground, in the open water, in the warm shallow navigation from one pool to another, and its home is large — it covers a range that you cannot estimate, a territory that is defined not by boundaries but by the consistency of its movement, by the regularity of its rounds, by the path it walks each day, which is every day, which is the same day, because the Sump does not have different days.

## The Climbers

The second kind of large animal lives above. Not in trees — there are no trees, not yet, nothing tall enough to qualify — but in the reed-stands, in the dense clusters of stalks that rise to heights of three or four meters, their upper reaches thick enough to support weight, their lower reaches dense enough to provide a kind of vertical architecture. The climbers occupy this architecture the way monkeys occupy a canopy, but they are not monkeys. They are not anything you have a word for.

You become aware of them by sound: a creaking, a bending, a slow elastic strain of plant-fiber under a distributed load. You look up — which is a mistake, because the condensation falls into your eyes — and you see movement in the stalks, a shifting of pale columns, a flexing of the reed-joints. Something is up there. Several somethings.

The first one you see clearly enough to describe is descending. It moves headfirst down a stalk, and its movement is not the movement of an animal that has limbs adapted for climbing — no grasping hands, no prehensile tail, no claws. Its body is tubular, elongated, and it moves the way a snake moves up a tree, by wrapping, by constriction, by the sequential gripping of its body against the stalk in a series of loops that shift and release and grip again. Its skin is dark — dark green, almost black — and smooth, and wet, and it has the look of something that has not yet decided to be a snake, that is still considering whether a tube is the optimal shape for an animal that lives in vertical vegetation, and the jury is still out.

It is not large. Perhaps a meter long, perhaps less, and as thick as your wrist at its widest, tapering to a head that is barely distinct from its body — a slight thickening, a slight change in the texture of the skin, two dark spots that might be sensory organs and a slit that opens and closes with its movement, breathing, tasting the air that flows down the stalk, air that is slightly drier up here, slightly cooler, but only slightly, because the Sump's saturation reaches even this high.

It reaches the junction where the stalk meets another stalk, and it pauses, and it orients, and you realize that it is not alone. There are others — three, four, more — moving through the upper structure of the reed-stand, their bodies looped around stalks, their heads oriented toward the junctions where leaves emerge and where the small organisms of the upper canopy cluster to feed on the weaker light that filters through. They are hunting. Or grazing. Or doing something that is between the two, something that does not distinguish between predation and browsing because the organisms they are eating are small enough that the distinction is academic.

They are at home in a way that the wader is not. The wader moves through the Sump; the climbers live *in* it, inhabit it the way a fish inhabits water, their bodies continuous with the vegetation, their movement an extension of the vegetation's movement, their presence in the stalks as natural as the stalks themselves. When the wind moves the reeds — and there is sometimes wind, slight, damp, the Sump's slow respiration of air — the climbers sway with the reeds, and the swaying is not different from the swaying of unoccupied stalks, and the camouflage is not deliberate but structural. They are the same thing. They are the reed-stand's fauna the way the reed-stand is its own flora. The distinction between the two has not yet been established here.

They communicate. You are sure of this, though you cannot identify the channel. It is not sound — they make no calls, no clicks, no stridulations. It is not visual — the light is too dim and they are too dark and the distances are too short for visual signaling to carry meaningful information. It may be chemical. The Sump is a world of chemical gradients, of dissolved information, of messages carried in water and air and the film of moisture on every surface, and the climbers, with their smooth wet skin pressed against the smooth wet stalks, are in constant contact with this medium, and the medium may carry their words in concentrations of pheromone or traces of metabolic byproduct, and they may be reading each other the way the amphibian-things read their pools, by taste, by the chemistry of presence.

One of them descends to within a meter of your head and stops. It orients toward you. It has no expression — its face is not built for expression, its features too minimal, its geometry too simple — but it holds its position, and its body is very still, and the stillness is a kind of attention. It is registering you. It is determining whether you are food, threat, feature of the landscape, or something else, and the category "something else" may not exist in its cognitive architecture, and so it may be having a novel experience, which is an experience the Sump does not typically provide, because the Sump does not typically contain novelty.

It stays for a long time. Long enough that the condensation forms on its body and runs down its skin in tiny rivers, and the rivers carry its chemical signature into the air, and the air carries it to the other climbers, and the other climbers shift in the stalks, and the shifting is slight but it is coordinated, and you understand that your presence has been noted and discussed in a language of dissolved molecules, and the conclusion is — what? Tolerance? Curiosity? Indifference? You cannot tell. The climbers resume their movement. The one above you ascends, slowly, loop by loop, back into the dim upper structure of the reeds. You have been catalogued. You have been filed. The Sump has absorbed the information of your existence the way it absorbs everything: slowly, wetly, without urgency.

## The Registrator

And then there is the one that watches you from a meter away.

It is the one you remember. It is the one that stays. The waders pass. The climbers ascend. The insects stridulate around you in columns of warm vapor. The amphibian-things complain in their pools. And this one stays. It is low-slung, the size of a large dog, with a body that is still negotiating between legs and fins — four limbs, yes, but the limbs are broad and flat and seem to have webbing that extends to their tips, and the tips are soft and do not have the jointed articulation of legs but rather the flexible, undulating movement of fins that have not yet committed to being legs or have not yet committed to being fins. The body is elongated, heavy in the middle, tapering at both ends. The skin is remarkable — translucent at the edges, along the flanks, at the margins of the limbs, so that you can see the slow pulse of internal structures, the movement of fluids, the dark mass of organs at the body's core, and at the center the skin becomes opaque, a mottled gray-green that suggests concealment, suggests the same camouflage the waders carry, the same damp-camouflage of a body that does not want to be seen in a place where seeing is rare.

It has oriented toward you. It has not approached. It has not retreated. It is not frozen, not still in the way that an animal freezes when it is afraid — it is moving, slowly, its limbs shifting, its body adjusting its position on the mesh with small, continuous corrections, the way a person standing in water will shift their weight to maintain balance. It is simply *present*, and its presence includes you, the way the presence of a stone includes the air around it.

It is the thing in the Sump that is most like you. Not in form — its form is nothing like yours, its body a draft, a sketch, a thing in progress. But in its *attention*. It is paying attention. Not the reflexive, reactive attention of the amphibian-things, who attend to the presence of others only to complain about them. Not the functional, mechanical attention of the waders, who attend to their environment only to navigate it. The Registrator is attending to you the way you are attending to it — with something that, if you are generous with your definitions, you might call curiosity, or interest, or the precursor to curiosity, the biological substrate from which curiosity will eventually be built, still in beta, still in testing.

It does not have a future. That is the thing to understand about it. It does not anticipate. It does not plan. It does not construct models of what will happen next and test them against reality. It is entirely, completely, radically present, and its presence is so complete that it includes you without separating you from the background, without distinguishing you from the stalks and the pools and the mesh and the vapor. You are all part of the same field of experience, and the Registrator is experiencing the field, and you happen to be in it, and that is all.

It is not afraid because fear requires a model of the future in which something bad might happen, and it does not have that. It is not aggressive because aggression requires a model of the future in which something good might be obtained by force, and it does not have that. It is not indifferent because indifference requires a model of the world in which some things matter and other things do not, and it does not have that either. It is simply *there*, processing, registering, its body a living instrument that takes in the Sump and everything in it and converts it to — what? To experience? To data? To the slow, wet, analog thought of a nervous system that has not yet learned to think in the way you would recognize as thinking?

It breathes. You can see it breathing — the translucent flanks expanding and contracting, the internal fluids shifting with the pressure change, the slow pulse at the core quickening and slowing with a rhythm that is not your rhythm, that belongs to a metabolism you cannot guess at, that runs on fuel you cannot identify. It is warm. You can feel the heat of it from a meter away, or perhaps that is the Sump's heat, distributed evenly through everything, impossible to attribute to any single source. It is alive in a way that is so thorough and so unselfconscious that it makes you feel, by contrast, like a thing that is only partially alive, a thing that is alive in a narrow and specialized way, a way that has traded completeness for efficiency.

It will outlast you. Not in lifespan — you do not know its lifespan, and it does not know its lifespan, because the concept of lifespan requires a concept of ending, and endings are not a feature of the Sump. It will outlast you because it belongs here and you do not. It will continue to register, to process, to exist in the warm wet present tense of this place long after you have left, and it will not remember you, not because it has forgotten but because it never stored, because storage is a technology for beings who need to carry information from one time to another, and the Registrator does not carry information from one time to another because all times are the same time, and the time is now, and the now is the Sump, and the Sump is warm, and the Sump is wet, and the Sump is home.

## The Others

There are others. You do not see them all. You cannot. The Sump is too dense, too saturated, too full of life in forms too various and too small and too provisional for any single observer to catalogue. There are things in the mesh that you will never see — organisms that live in the fibers, that eat the fibers, that are the fibers. There are things in the water that is not quite water that move at scales below your perception, that are the cloudiness in the condensation, that are the prickle on your skin. There are things in the vapor columns with the insects that are not insects but that ride the same currents and feed on the same particles and occupy the same niche at a different scale. There are things in the gelatinous mats between the reed-stands, the not-quite-plant not-quite-animal colonies, and the things inside them, and the things inside those.

The Sump is not a habitat that contains life. The Sump is life that has become habitat. The distinction between the two — between environment and organism, between home and inhabitant — has not yet been established here, and may never be, because the Sump is not moving toward that distinction. It is not evolving toward differentiation. It is evolving toward integration, toward complexity, toward a state in which the mesh and the things in the mesh, the water and the things in the water, the air and the things in the air, are all one thing, one organism, one continuous body of warm wet life that breathes and eats and complains and registers and becomes.

What lives in the Sump is the Sump. That is the final answer, the answer that the slow observation yields, the answer that the heat and the sound and the dim light have been telling you since you arrived. The insects live here. The amphibian-things live here. The waders and the climbers and the Registrators live here. The mesh lives here. The water lives here. The air lives here. And they are all the same living, all parts of a single process that has been going on since before you arrived and will continue after you leave and does not require your observation to validate its existence.

You are the only thing in it that does not belong.

Everything else is home.
