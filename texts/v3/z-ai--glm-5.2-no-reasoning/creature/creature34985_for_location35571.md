# What Lives in the Croak

---

The first thing to understand is that nothing here considers itself a resident.

That word — *resident* — implies a relationship to a place that is separate from the self. A resident lives *in* a house. A resident occupies *an* environment. But the things that live here do not live *in* the Croak the way a tenant lives in an apartment. They live *as* the Croak, the way a cell lives as a body, the way a note lives as a chord. They are not in the ecosystem. They are the ecosystem. The distinction between inhabitant and habitat has been dissolved by the same force that dissolved the boundary between air and water: abundance, warmth, time, and the sheer press of living matter against living matter until the edges blur and the categories fail.

So when you ask what lives here, you are asking the wrong question, or rather, you are asking a question whose answer is not a list but a *texture*. A list implies discrete entries: this species, that species, arranged in rows. What lives here is not arranged. What lives here is *layered*, and the layers intergrade, and the space between one layer and the next is not a gap but a gradient, and the gradient is itself alive, and what lives in the gradient is the thing that makes the whole system breathe.

But I will try. I will begin with the frogs, because they are the first thing you would know, and because they are the closest thing this place has to a people.

---

## The Frogs

There is no single species of frog here. There are dozens, perhaps more — the taxonomy is not settled because the place is not settled, because new forms appear with each generation, hybrids and variants and sport mutations that flourish for a season and vanish or flourish and persist, the gene pool churning as fast as the soil, the populations rising and falling and rising again in a rhythm that no one has lived long enough to measure.

The dominant form — the one whose voice you would hear above all others, the bass note in the chord — is large and dark and dense. A body like a clenched fist, slick-backed, the color of wet basalt, with a green stripe along each flank that catches the dim light and holds it. These frogs sit in the pools and call, and their call is not a croak but a *bellow*, a deep, resonant vibration that begins in the inflated throat — a bladder of pale skin stretched drum-tight — and radiates outward through the water and the air and the ground and the chest of anything standing nearby. They are calling for mates. They are calling to mark territory. They are calling because calling is what they do, because the sound is the medium through which they exist in relation to each other, and the sound is not optional. A frog that does not call does not exist, socially. It is invisible. It is unclaimed. It is no one's rival and no one's mate and no one's neighbor, and in a world this dense, to be no one's neighbor is to be nothing at all.

But the dominant form is only the beginning. Above the bass, in the mid-range, the small tree frogs add their voices — higher, faster, a rattling chatter that weaves through the bellowing like a second melody through a drone. These are the painted ones, the beautiful ones, the frogs with the red eyes and the translucent skin and the sticky pads that leave wet prints on every surface they touch. They live above the water, in the leaves, in the branches, in the vertical world that the large frogs do not reach. They are the voices of the canopy, the treble to the ground frogs' bass, and their calls are not bellows but chirps and trills and rapid-fire clicks that overlap with each other so precisely that they create, in their aggregate, a *rhythm*, a pulse, a tempo that the ground-level frogs do not follow but accompany.

And between these two — between the bass and the treble, between the ground and the canopy — the middle layer. The middle voices. Frogs of medium size and medium habit, living on the trunks and the vines and the broad leaves of the understory, calling at frequencies that bridge the two registers, filling the gaps, smoothing the transitions, making the whole sound continuous and total and inescapable. Some of these are the glass frogs, the ones with the transparent bellies, the ones you can see through, whose hearts beat visibly in their chests as they call. They are the strangest of the frogs, not because they are transparent — transparency is not strange here, where everything is wet and everything is slick and the boundary between inside and outside is, like every other boundary, more of a suggestion than a rule — but because they seem *aware* of their own transparency, as if the visibility of their own interiors were not a vulnerability but a *statement*, a way of saying: *I am here. I am all the way here. I am so here that you can see through me.*

The frogs call all the time. Not the same frogs — different individuals, different species, different layers of the chorus rising and falling as the light shifts and the rain comes and the temperature changes and the chemistry of the pools fluctuates and the insects emerge and the snakes move and the world turns on its axis and the darkness deepens and lightens and deepens again. But the sound is continuous because the population is large enough and the species are varied enough that when one group pauses, another group fills the gap, and the gap is never empty, the silence never arrives, the roar never breaks.

This is not chaos. This is the opposite of chaos. This is a system so complex and so distributed that it achieves a kind of order that looks, to the outside ear, like disorder — the way a city looks like disorder from above, a jumble of buildings and streets, but from inside is a system of relationships, of paths, of knowledge. The frogs know each other. They know each other's voices. They know where each call comes from and what it means and who it is directed at and what the appropriate response is, and the conversation that results — the ten-million-voice, never-pausing, body-shaking conversation — is not noise but *language*, a language made of frequencies and rhythms and silences that are too short for the human ear to detect but that are, to the frogs, as meaningful as a word.

## The Insects

The frogs are the voice of the Croak, but the insects are its *breath*.

They are not a presence. They are a medium. The air is made of them, in the same way that the air is made of water — not as a mixture but as a *condition*. To breathe here is to breathe insects. To move through the air is to move through insects. They are so small and so numerous that they cease to be individuals and become a property of the space, like humidity, like heat, like sound.

The mosquitoes are the most immediate. They are not the largest or the most visible, but they are the most insistent, the most *personal*, the ones that single you out and track you and find you and remind you, with every itch and every welt, that you are made of blood and blood is made of protein and protein is a resource and resources are not wasted here. They do not swarm in clouds. They arrive in ones and twos and tens, from different directions, at different heights, with a patience that is more mechanical than strategic. They are not hunting you. They are *processing* you, the way the soil processes a fallen leaf, the way the water processes a mineral. You are a resource. You are being consumed. The mosquitoes are merely the interface.

Above the mosquitoes, the gnats — the tiny, nearly invisible ones that hang in still air in dense, shifting columns, like smoke, like dust in a sunbeam, though there are no sunbeams here. They do not bite. They do not seem to do anything. They exist, and their existence is a fact of the air, a quality of the medium, and you walk through them the way you walk through fog, with the same futility, the same inability to clear them from the space in front of your face.

The dragonflies are different. They are the apex of the insect world, the hunters, the fast ones, the ones that move with a precision and a speed that seems to belong to a different order of being. They streak through the dim air like sparks, their iridescent bodies — blue, green, copper — catching the faint light and scattering it in brief, electric flashes that are the closest thing to brightness in the understory. They eat the gnats. They eat the mosquitoes. They eat each other, sometimes. They are the only thing in the insect medium that moves with *intent*, that seems to have a destination, a target, a purpose beyond mere existence, and their movement through the air cuts the medium like a blade, leaving a momentary wake of disturbed gnats and displaced mosquitoes that closes behind them, instantly, the way water closes behind a fish.

The beetles are here, on the ground, in the leaf litter, in the rotting wood. They are the recyclers, the undertakers, the ones that process the dead. They are armored, dark, slow, and they move through the soil and the rot with a methodical patience that is the opposite of the dragonflies' speed. They do not hunt. They do not chase. They find, and they process, and they transform, and what was a leaf becomes soil and what was a body becomes food and what was a fallen branch becomes a foundation for something else to grow on, and the beetles do this not because they choose to but because this is what they *are*, the way a stomach is what it is, the way a furnace is what it is.

The ants are here, and they are a civilization. Not a metaphor. A civilization: organized, specialized, territorial, architectural. Their colonies web the soil, their tunnels threading through the dark earth in networks so dense that the ground itself is, in places, more ant-tunnel than dirt. They farm. They keep scale insects on the roots of plants, harvesting their honeydew, protecting them from predators, moving them to new pastures when the old ones are exhausted. They wage war — colony against colony, species against species, in conflicts that rage in the leaf litter at a scale invisible to anything larger than a mouse but total and brutal for the combatants. They build. They communicate. They make decisions collectively, through chemical signals, through touch, through the accumulated input of thousands of individuals processed by a system that has no center and no leader but acts, nonetheless, with a coherence that looks, from outside, like intelligence.

And the orthopterans — the crickets, the katydids, the things with the translucent wings and the polished-seed bodies — are the second voice, the insects' contribution to the roar. Their stridulations are so rapid that they register as tones, continuous and sustained, filling the frequencies between the frogs' bellows and the birds' cries, completing the spectrum, rounding out the sound, making it whole. They are the strings to the frogs' brass, the sustained note beneath the pulse, and they do not stop, and they do not vary, and they are so constant that you would stop hearing them within minutes, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat, the way you stop feeling your own pulse — not because they stop, but because they are too continuous to attend to, too constant to notice, too fundamental to perceive.

## The Spiders

The spiders live between the insects and the frogs, in the middle of the food web, in the space between prey and predator, and they occupy that space with a thoroughness that is, if you think about it too long, disturbing.

The orb-weavers are the most visible. Their webs are everywhere — strung between branches at every height, spanning the gaps between leaves and trunks and vines, their silk catching the dim light with a faint, structural iridescence that makes them appear and disappear as you move, now visible, now invisible, now visible again. The webs are large, some of them, a meter across or more, and the spiders that sit at their centers are large to match — the size of a human palm, legs included, their bodies patterned in yellows and blacks and sometimes reds, colors that in this dim, green world read as warnings, as signs, as things that mean *do not touch* in a language older than language.

The orb-weavers wait. They are the personification of patience. They sit at the center of their webs, legs spread, head down, and they feel the vibrations of the silk with a sensitivity that is, for their size, extraordinary — they can detect a single insect at the edge of a web a meter away, can distinguish between the signal of a trapped prey and the signal of a falling leaf, can localize the impact to within centimeters and move toward it with a speed and precision that seems impossible for an animal with eight legs and no eyes worth mentioning. They do not see well. They do not need to. They have built their own sensory apparatus out of silk, and that apparatus is better than eyes, here in the dark, where seeing is useless and feeling is everything.

The jumping spiders are the other extreme. Small, compact, furry, with two enormous forward-facing eyes that give them a face — an actual face, a face with expression, with intent, with something that looks, to the human observer, unsettlingly like curiosity. They do not build webs. They hunt. They stalk. They watch. They sit on a leaf and they look at you, and the looking is not reflexive, not mechanical, but *attentive*, as if the spider is actually processing your image, actually considering you, actually deciding whether you are relevant to its life. You are not relevant to its life. But it does not know that, and the way it watches makes you unsure, for a moment, of whether you know that either.

The huntsman spiders are the ones you do not see. They are the ones that are under the bark and behind the leaves and in the spaces you reach into without thinking. They are flat and fast and large — a handspan across, legs spread — and they do not build webs and they do not stalk and they do not watch. They ambush. They sit, pressed against a surface, legs folded, and they wait, and when something moves within range, they move, and the movement is so fast that the prey does not know it has been caught until it has been caught, until the legs are around it and the fangs are in it and the venom is working and the spider is already beginning to feed.

The spiders are not the rulers of the Croak. Nothing is the ruler of the Croak. But the spiders are the *connectors* — the animals that link the insect medium to the frog chorus, the animals that eat and are eaten at every level, the animals that hold the web of relationships together with actual silk. They are the physical manifestation of the system's interconnectedness, and if you wanted to understand the Croak by looking at a single part of it, you would do worse than to look at a spider's web — at the way it is built to catch what passes through, at the way it is both structure and trap, both home and weapon, both a thing the spider made and a thing the spider is.

## The Snakes

The snakes are the patience of the Croak made physical.

They do not rush. They do not call. They do not flash or sparkle or announce themselves. They move through the leaf litter and the pools and the roots with a slowness that makes the frogs' sudden jumps seem theatrical, almost desperate, and their movement is so quiet that you would not know a snake was near unless you were looking for one, and even then you would not see it until it moved, and by then it would have already decided what to do with you, or rather, what to do with the space you were occupying.

The constrictors are the largest. Heavy-bodied, smooth-scaled, dark, they lie in the pools and the low vegetation and they wait, and the waiting can last hours or days, the snake coiled and still and so motionless that algae grows on its scales and insects walk across its eyes and it does not flinch, it does not blink, it does not move. It is waiting for a frog. It is always waiting for a frog. When the frog comes — when it lands nearby, when it settles, when it begins to call, which it will, because it must — the snake moves, and the movement is one movement, one continuous flow of muscle and scale and intent, and the frog is taken, and the snake is still again, and the waiting resumes.

There are smaller snakes too, thinner ones, quicker ones, the ones that hunt in the branches and the vines and the vertical world where the constrictors do not go. They are the arboreal snakes, the climbers, the ones that thread through the epiphytes and the orchids and the bromeliads with a fluidity that seems to belong to the plants themselves, as if the vine and the snake were the same thing, as if the forest had grown a nervous system and the nervous system had grown fangs. They eat the tree frogs. They eat the lizards. They eat the eggs of the birds. They are the hidden threat of the canopy, the thing that waits in the leaves, and the tree frogs know they are there, and the tree frogs call anyway, because calling is more important than safety, because existing in relation to others is more important than not dying, because a frog that does not call is already dead, in the only way that matters to a frog.

The snakes are not cruel. Cruelty requires a capacity for alternatives, a consciousness of choice, and the snakes do not have that. They have hunger and they have patience and they have a body that is built for a specific kind of work, and the work is consuming, and they do it with the same thoughtless, total commitment that the plants bring to growing and the frogs bring to calling and the insects bring to existing. They are the system's *stomach*, the part that processes the overflow, the part that keeps the growth from overwhelming itself, and without them the frogs would multiply past the capacity of the pools and the insects would multiply past the capacity of the air and the whole system would choke on its own abundance. The snakes are the throttle. The snakes are the *no*. They are the Croak's way of saying *not everything, not all at once, not forever*.

## The Birds

The birds are the migrants, the visitors, the things that come from above.

They live in the canopy, in the light, in the world above the ceiling, and they enter the dark below only to feed, only to nest, only to do the things that cannot be done in the open air. Their calls are the sharp, bright threads that cut through the frog-roar — metallic, staccato, *piercing* — and they do not blend with the chorus but *interrupt* it, the way a shout interrupts a murmur, the way a bright color interrupts a field of gray.

They are fruit-eaters, mostly. The canopy produces fruit year-round — there is no season here, no winter, no dormant period, just continuous growth and continuous reproduction — and the birds move through the upper layers in flocks, following the fruit, calling to each other, maintaining the cohesion of the group in a space where visibility is limited by leaves and the only way to know where your neighbor is is to hear them. Their calls are not part of the roar. They are *above* the roar, both literally and acoustically — a separate system, a separate conversation, happening in a different medium at a different scale, and the two conversations do not interact, do not merge, do not acknowledge each other. The frogs and the birds are the Croak's two voices, and neither knows the other is speaking.

Some birds come down. The understory birds, the ones that live in the dim world, the ones that have adapted to the dark and the wet and the dense vegetation. They are different from the canopy birds — quieter, duller-colored, more secretive. They do not call in bright threads. They make low, soft sounds that blend with the insect hum and the frog chorus, that are almost impossible to distinguish from the background, that are, perhaps, *part of* the background, the way a shadow is part of the thing that casts it. They eat insects. They eat small frogs. They nest in the low branches and the ferns and the root tangles, and their nests are wet, always wet, and their eggs are wet, and their chicks are wet, and everything about their lives is conducted in the condition of dampness that is the Croak's first and most fundamental law.

## The Mammals

There are mammals here, but they are the Croak's afterthought.

Not in the sense that they are unimportant — they have roles, they have niches, they have a place in the system — but in the sense that they are *late*. The frogs were here first. The insects were here first. The plants were here first. The snakes were here before the mammals. The birds were here before the mammals. The mammals arrived when the system was already built, when the architecture was already in place, when the rules were already written and the roles were already cast, and they found what was left — the spaces between the existing inhabitants, the niches that no one had yet claimed — and they filled them, and they survive, but they do not *belong*, not the way the frogs belong, not the way the insects belong. They are tenants. They are guests. They are the living things that the Croak tolerates because they do not, yet, interfere.

There is a small, sharp-toothed thing — a shrew or a shrew-like thing, hard to see, hard to catch, hard to study — that lives in the leaf litter and eats insects and is eaten by snakes and is, in most respects, indistinguishable from the beetles it lives among, except that it is warm-blooded and furry and its eyes, unlike the beetles', are not compound but simple, and they look, in the dim light, like small, wet, black seeds, and they reflect the faint green glow of the understory with a shine that is, if you are close enough to see it, oddly familiar.

There is something larger — a rodent, perhaps, or a marsupial, the taxonomy is uncertain — that moves through the understory at night, climbing the vines and the trunks with a slow, deliberate grace, eating fruit, eating eggs, eating anything it can find that is not alive enough to resist. It is the size of a cat, dark-furred, with a long tail and a face that seems, in the brief glimpses that are all anyone ever gets of it, to be *thinking*, to be *assessing*, to be making a kind of decision that the frogs and the snakes and the spiders do not make. It is the closest thing here to an animal that has *plans*. Its plans are simple: find food, avoid snakes, do not fall. But the fact that it has them at all makes it different from everything else in the Croak, different in a way that is difficult to articulate but easy to feel, if you feel it — the way you feel the difference between a machine that is running and a machine that is *deciding whether to run*.

And there may be something else. Something larger. Something that is inferred from sounds in the night and broken branches and tracks in the mud that fill with water before anyone can photograph them. A predator, perhaps. A thing that eats the rodent-sized things and the shrew-like things and whatever else it can catch in the dark. If it exists, it is the Croak's apex, the top of the pyramid, the thing that nothing else eats — but it is also the rarest, the shyest, the most invisible, and its existence is, like the sky above the canopy, asserted by inference, never confirmed.

## The Things That Are Not Animals

And then there are the things that live here that are not animals at all but that are, in every meaningful sense, *alive* in a way that the word "alive" does not fully capture.

The fungi are the Croak's *memory*.

They are everywhere — in the soil, on the leaves, on the bark, on the dead wood, on the living wood, on the insects, on the frogs, on the things that have died and the things that are dying and the things that will die. They are the system's recyclers, its undertakers, its undertakers-in-waiting, and they do their work with a patience and a thoroughness that makes the beetles look hasty. The fungal mycelium that threads through the soil of the Croak is not a collection of individual organisms but a *network* — a single, connected, distributed body that may, in terms of total biomass, exceed every animal in the forest combined. It is the thing that connects the trees to each other, the thing that moves nutrients from where they are abundant to where they are needed, the thing that *distributes*, that *allocates*, that *decides*, in some chemical, non-conscious, non-intentional way, who gets what.

The mushrooms are the visible part — the fruiting bodies, the reproductive organs, the part that pops up above the ground to release spores into the air. They appear after rain, which is to say they appear constantly, and they are strange and varied and sometimes beautiful and sometimes disturbing in the way that things are disturbing when they are clearly alive and clearly *not* animal and clearly *not* plant and clearly something else, a third thing, a thing that does not fit the categories and does not try to.

Some are small and white and delicate, growing in clusters on fallen leaves, like a rash, like a bloom. Some are large and bracket-shaped, growing on the trunks of trees in overlapping shelves that are tough and woody and that can persist for years, growing slowly, adding rings the way a tree adds rings, and serving, in their slow way, as a record of the conditions of the forest over the time of their growth. Some are bioluminescent — faintly, barely, a ghostly green glow that is visible only in the total darkness of the night, only if you are close, only if you are looking, and that serves, perhaps, as a signal to insects, a beacon, a way of saying *come here, carry my spores, take me to a new place*. In the darkness of the Croak, where the light has been eaten by the plants and the only illumination is the dim green glow that filters through the canopy, the bioluminescent fungi are a *different* light — not sunlight, not reflected light, but *self*-made light, biological light, light that the world has generated from its own chemistry, and it is the only light in the forest that the plants have not consumed.

And the microbes. The bacteria, the archaea, the single-celled things that are too small to see and too numerous to count and too important to ignore. They are the Croak's *engine*. They are the thing that makes the soil fertile, that breaks down the dead matter, that fixes the nitrogen, that cycles the sulfur, that do the chemical work that the system depends on. They are in the soil and the water and the air and the guts of the frogs and the mouths of the birds and the cells of the plants, and they are, in a way that is difficult to overstate, the thing that *makes the Croak the Croak* — not its voice, not its shape, not its visible inhabitants, but its *chemistry*, its metabolism, its invisible, microscopic, ceaseless work of transformation. Without them, the dead would accumulate. The nutrients would lock up. The soil would exhaust itself. The pools would stagnate. The system would stall. They are the thing that keeps everything moving, and they do it at a scale and at a speed that is invisible to the eye but that is, in its totality, the *thing itself*, the living, churning, transforming, becoming heart of the place.

## The Croak Itself

And so what lives here?

Everything. Everything lives here. The frogs and the insects and the spiders and the snakes and the birds and the mammals and the fungi and the microbes and the plants, the trees and the ferns and the epiphytes and the mosses and the liverworts and the lichens, the things that fly and the things that crawl and the things that sit and the things that grow and the things that decompose, the things that call and the things that listen and the things that have no voice at all — they all live here, and they are all here now, and they are all part of the same thing, the same being, the same continuous, living, roaring, damp, dark, warm system that does not know it is a system and does not need to know and does not care.

The Croak is not a habitat. It is an *organism*. It is a living thing that is made of living things, a body that is made of bodies, a voice that is made of voices, and what lives here is not a list of species but a *process*, a becoming, a thing that is happening, right now, in the dark, in the wet, in the roar, and that will continue to happen, century after century, until the land settles and the nutrients slow and the growth decelerates and the system finds something like equilibrium and the roar begins, finally, to diminish.

But that is not now. Now the roar is here. Now the frogs are calling. Now the insects are humming. Now the snakes are waiting and the spiders are building and the fungi are growing and the microbes are working and the birds are singing above the ceiling and the mammals are moving through the dark and the plants are growing, always growing, reaching for light and space and time with the desperate, angular energy of things that know, in some cellular way, that this moment is not forever.

What lives here is *this*. All of this. At once. Together. And the sound it makes is the sound of itself, and the sound is the Croak, and the Croak does not stop.
