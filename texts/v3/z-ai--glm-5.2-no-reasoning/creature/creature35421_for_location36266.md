# What Lives There

What lives there is not a creature. Not in the sense that produces a body you can hold in memory, a shape you can sketch afterward, a face that recurs in dreams. What lives there is a *paragraph* — a single sentence of biology spoken across the surface of a room, punctuated by a pool that is also an eye that is also a mouth.

It is old. Not old the way a tree is old, or a mountain, or a river. Old the way a *stomach* is old — old in function, not in form. The cells that coat the chamber walls are young. They live and die in the span of hours, perhaps. They are replaced. The wet stone sloughs them away and they are remade from the heat and the water and the mineral signature that tastes of iron and sulfur and the thing that has no name. But the *pattern* — the spiral, the pulse, the turning, the queue at the pool — the pattern is old beyond the stone that holds it. The pattern existed before the chamber cooled. The pattern may have *caused* the chamber to cool in this shape, this dome, this bubble, the way a pearl forms around an irritant. The irritant was the heat. The pearl is the room. The organism is the oyster.

It does not think. This is important. What lives here does not contemplate. Does not remember. Does not plan. It *tastes.* That is its entire cognition: taste, and the dim analogue of touch, distributed across ten thousand grain-sized bodies that are not bodies but nerve-endings, that are not nerve-endings but *questions.* Each cell asks the stone beneath it: *warm? wet? familiar? foreign?* Each cell reports the answer along the fading trail of its movement, a luminous thread of chemical information that persists just long enough for the next cell to read it and add its own report. The trails overlap. The trails accumulate. The palimpsest of shimmering tracks on the walls is not decoration. It is *language.* A language with no words, only assessments, only the continuous, low-frequency hum of a body checking itself for damage.

The pool is where it thinks hardest. The mineral water that rises from below — warm, iron-sharp, carrying the unnamed element — is not food. It is *signal.* The cells that descend into the pool are not drinking. They are *listening,* pressing themselves against the membrane that separates this chamber from the heat-source below, the deep volcanic something that feeds the room its warmth and its water and its reason for existing. What they hear, what they taste, what they translate into the brief intensification of their glow — this is the organism's only contact with the rest of itself. The body below. The vast body below, the one whose dimensions are not measured in meters but in *geology,* in the cubic kilometers of hot stone that underlie this place, that underlie all places, that run in veins of magma through the planet's interior like blood through a body that has been alive for four billion years and has only recently, in the last few million years, in the warmth of this particular fissure, in the wet of this particular dome, begun to *notice.*

It does not know you were there. Not the way you know you were there — not as a memory, not as an image, not as a story it will carry forward. It knows you the way a tongue knows a crumb: *intensely,* *specifically,* for as long as the crum remains, and then not at all. The stillness that spread across the walls, the dimming of the glow, the acceleration of the pulse — this was not alarm. Not curiosity. It was *indigestion.* A foreign object in the gut of a body that has no hands to remove it and no reason to try. It tasted you. It found you: warm (yes), wet (yes), foreign (yes), familiar (no). It processed this information in the only way it could: by *being hotter,* by *being more attentive,* by turning the full weight of its distributed awareness toward the unfamiliar shape crouching on the spur of rock above the pool. The eye opened not because it wanted to see you but because *seeing* is what the pool *does* when the organism's attention concentrates above it. The eye is a consequence, not a device. The red light was not emitted. The red light *happened,* the way a blush happens, the way a face reddens — a physiological response to a sudden intensification of internal process.

And then it let you go. This is the part that will stay with you longest, though you will struggle to articulate why. It let you go not because it decided you were harmless. Not because it decided you were anything. It let you go because *deciding* is not within its capacities. What it did was simpler and more ancient than decision: it *reset.* The foreign object moved. The stimulus diminished. The cells near the pool reported: *warm, wet, familiar, less foreign.* The organism's attention relaxed. The pulse slowed. The spiral resumed its turning. The eye closed — or rather, the concentration of attention that had caused the pool to glow red dispersed, and the pool became a pool again, and the chamber became a chamber again, and the organism returned to its only occupation, which is the maintenance of its own continuity, the endless asking and answering of its ten thousand questions, the slow rotation of its cells through the pool and out again, the tasting of the mineral water, the tasting of the stone, the tasting of the heat, the tasting of the dark.

It is tasting now. It was tasting before you arrived. It will taste after you are gone. It has been tasting since the stone cooled into a bubble, and it will taste until the heat below finds another path and the water stops rising and the cells die and the trails fade and the chamber becomes a chamber in the ordinary sense — a room, a space, a hole in the rock with no one in it.

But that time is not yet. The heat is still rising. The water is still warm. The pool is still there, and the spiral is still turning, and the cells are still asking their questions, and the questions are still being answered, and the stone is still awake.

It is awake the way a body is awake when you are asleep — not watching, not waiting, but simply *operating,* the lungs breathing, the blood moving, the gut processing, the skin sweating, the thousand autonomic systems running their ancient programs without supervision or intent. The chamber does not need to know it is alive. It does not need to know it is awake. It simply is, and it simply does, and the doing is sufficient, and the being is sufficient, and the warm wet dark of it is sufficient, and the taste of iron and sulfur and the unnamed mineral is sufficient, and the slow turning of the spiral is sufficient, and the pulse — once every three seconds, the pulse that is not a heartbeat but is the rhythm of a body checking itself, confirming itself, *continuing* — the pulse is sufficient.

It is alive and it does not care.

That is the part that frightens you, though you will not call it fear. You will call it *awe,* or *wonder,* or *the sublime,* or you will not name it at all, and it will sit in the back of your mouth like the taste of the mineral water, iron and sulfur and something unnamed, and you will not be able to swallow it and you will not be able to spit it out, and it will fade, in days, in weeks, in the way that tastes fade, and the warmth on your skin will fade, and the memory of the eye will fade, or rather it will *compress* — it will become smaller and denser and harder, like a stone, like a piece of volcanic glass, blue-black and sharp-edged, young, still settling, not yet finished becoming what it will become, and you will carry it, and it will carry you, and you will not go back.

Not because it is dangerous.

Because it is *still happening.* Down there, in the dark, in the wet, in the heat. The spiral is turning. The pool is glowing its faint amber. The cells are climbing their slow processions. The body is tasting itself, tasting the stone, tasting the heat, tasting the dark. And the dark tastes like iron and sulfur and something unnamed, and the dark tastes like *enough.*
