# What Lives in the Condensate

---

## First, a Truth About Living Here

Nothing that lives in Karsvall evolved for light.

This sounds simple until you sit with it. It means no eye, anywhere, on any body, in this entire continental plateau. Not reduced eyes, not vestigial eyes, not light-sensing patches that might one day become eyes. The evolutionary lineage here diverged from the sighted tree so long ago, or arose beside a separate trunk entirely, that the very *concept* of photoreception is absent from the genome. Not lost. Never present.

Consider what that means for a body. Every shape you know — the bilateral symmetry of a wolf, the bilateral symmetry of your own face, the way eyes sit forward on a skull because forward is where you go — much of that is built around seeing. Around orienting toward the visual field. Around the priority of *looking*.

Here, forward is where you listen.

## The Strand-Runners

Start with what you would almost see.

They are the shadows that move across the less-dark ground. You catch them at the edge of perception — a densification of the darkness, a displacement of condensate, a shape that resolves for a half-second into something legible and then dissolves back into the medium. Your mind tries to build a dog. Your mind tries to build a large cat. Your mind fails, because the shape is neither.

The strand-runner's body is low and long. Low not in the way a lizard is low — pressed to the ground, splayed — but in the way a vessel is low: streamlined, the profile flattened against the medium it moves through. The condensate here is chest-height, and the strand-runner keeps its body beneath that layer, in the marginally clearer air near the ground, where the moss-carpet parts and the stone ridges run and the corridors between tower-trunks open into something like paths. It is built for the corridor. Its width is the width of the corridor. Its height is the height of the ferns. It fits.

They have more legs than you would expect. Not the uncertain, rippling multitude of a centipede — the description would mislead you if you let it — but a specific, functional number. Twelve. Six pairs. Each pair operates on a different phase of a gait cycle so smooth that the body does not appear to move so much as *flow* across the ground. The legs themselves are not jointed in the way you assume. There are no knees, no elbows, no hinges. Each leg is a flexible shaft — a dense, slightly stiff cylinder of a material somewhere between cartilage and tendon — that bends under load and springs back when the load shifts. The strand-runner walks on springs. It runs on springs. The silence of its movement is not a product of soft footpads but of a body that never strikes the ground, that touches the surfaces it crosses with the deliberate, distributed gentleness of a hand reading braille.

The body is covered in fine, dense filaments — not hair, not fur, but something closer to the root-threads of the moss-carpet it runs through. These filaments serve two purposes. First: they trap a layer of condensate against the skin, maintaining a micro-environment of constant moisture that keeps the body's surface membranes supple. The strand-runner does not have dry skin. It does not have scales or feathers or an exoskeleton. It has a permeable integument, a skin that breathes, that exchanges gas and water and dissolved compounds directly with its environment. It is always, in a sense, drinking. It is always, in a sense, exhaling through its whole body.

Second: the filaments are sensory. Each one is a mechanoreceptor and a chemoreceptor, and together they constitute a sense organ that covers the entire body. The strand-runner does not see the corridor. It *feels* the corridor — the pressure of the air, the displacement patterns of the condensate, the chemical gradients that distinguish the base of a tower-trunk from the edge of a basin, the faint thermal signatures that mark the difference between wet stone and wet moss. It perceives its world the way a blind person's hands perceive a face: intimately, completely, building a representation of reality that is richer in texture and temperature and chemistry than any visual image could be, and that lacks only color and distance.

It lacks only distance. This is important. The strand-runner's world is close. It extends perhaps ten meters in every direction — the range of its chemosensory filaments, the range of its hearing, the range of the pressure-changes it can detect. Beyond that, there is nothing. Or rather, there is the same nothing that exists beyond your own visual range in the condensate: a darkness, an unknown, a place that is theoretical until it is entered.

The strand-runner enters it anyway. It enters it constantly. It enters it because beyond the known is where food lives, where mates call, where the territories of competitors begin and end, and the strand-runner's entire existence is a continuous act of expanding the known into the unknown and contracting it again, a tide of perception that goes out and comes back, goes out and comes back, every minute of every hour of every day of its life.

Its calls — those warbling, tonal sequences you hear in the dark, the ones that sound almost musical — are territory. Each strand-runner has a signature call, a sequence of tones produced by a syrinx-like organ at the base of its respiratory system, and the sequence encodes something: not just identity, not just location, but a record of the chemical environment the strand-runner has recently passed through. The call is a map. It says: *I am here, I have been there, the moss was rich in iron at the third ridge, the basin-edge tasted of sulfur, the tower-trunk at the corridor's end was fruiting.* Other strand-runners hear this and update their own internal maps. The territory is not a space. It is a shared information network, an acoustic internet of chemical reports, a community of perception that no single individual could hold alone.

They are not aggressive with each other. Or rather — they are aggressive in the way that waves are aggressive with the shore: persistently, at boundaries, in ways that reshape both parties over time. Two strand-runners whose territories overlap will meet at the boundary and call at each other for hours, each one's report slightly different, each one's map slightly incompatible, and over the course of the encounter they will negotiate — not through compromise but through a kind of sensory merger, each one updating its map to accommodate the other's data until the boundary dissolves or solidifies based on whether the two maps can be reconciled. Sometimes they can. Sometimes they cannot, and the two strand-runners continue calling, and the boundary persists, and the corridor between two tower-trunks becomes a contested zone that neither enters without announcing itself.

When they mate, it is brief and not tender. It is a chemical negotiation — one strand-runner's integument recognizing a compatible signature in another's, a brief joining of bodies that are already permeable, an exchange of dissolved compounds that carries genetic material from one to the other. There is no courtship display. There is no dimorphism. There is no male or female. Each strand-runner carries the material to fertilize and the capacity to be fertilized, and the act is as simple and unceremonious as two sponges pressed together in a current.

They reproduce slowly. A strand-runner lives — and this is a guess, based on growth rings in the cartilaginous core of their leg-shafts, which is the closest thing to a skeleton they possess — something like twelve to fifteen years. It reproduces perhaps three times in that span, producing one or two offspring that emerge as small, fully-formed copies of the adult, already filamented, already calling, already running the corridors. The offspring do not stay with the parent. They run. They run immediately, within minutes of emergence, and the parent does not follow and does not call after them. The map is the parent. The territory is the parent. The chemical reports of every strand-runner in the network are the parent. The child is raised by the landscape.

## The Basin-Grazers

If the strand-runner is a vessel built for the corridor, the basin-grazer is a vessel built for the lake.

You do not see them. You see what they displace.

The lakes of Karsvall are shallow — rarely deeper than two meters — and the basin-grazers are broad. Their bodies, when they surface, when the quartzite glow catches the water at the right angle, present as a curve. A slow, wide, arcing curve that moves through the tea-colored water with a patience that suggests time is a concept that applies to other things. The curve is the back, or the dorsum, or whatever the upper surface is called on an animal that has no spine in the way you understand spines. It is smooth. It is dark. It is wet in a way that is indistinguishable from the wet of the lake it moves through.

The basin-grazer is enormous. Five meters is the small one. Eight is more common. The largest, in the broadest basins, may reach ten or twelve meters — a body the size of a small boat, moving through water that barely covers it, its dorsum breaking the surface in a gentle wake that spreads outward in concentric rings until it reaches the basin's edge and is absorbed by the moss.

The body is not fish-shaped. It is not whale-shaped. It is not turtle-shaped. It is shaped like a leaf — a broad, flat, oval leaf, the kind that floats on the surface of still water — but turned on its side and given depth, so that the "blade" of the leaf becomes a hull and the "midrib" becomes a keel. The animal moves by undulating the edges of this hull — long, slow, rolling waves of contraction that travel from the front of the body to the back, propelling it forward with a gentleness that barely disturbs the water. It does not splash. It does not dive. It does not breach or tail-slap or do any of the dramatic things that large aquatic animals do in other worlds. It simply moves, and the water accepts the movement, and the surface heals behind it.

The basin-grazer feeds on what grows in the lakes. The basins, for all their darkness, are productive — the warm, mineral-rich water supports a dense ecosystem of chemosynthetic organisms: mats of filamentous bacteria that cling to the submerged stone, colonies of single-celled organisms that cloud the water with their abundance, thicker growths that anchor to the basin floor and wave in the slow currents like submerged flags. The basin-grazer processes all of this. Its mouth — a wide, slit-like opening that runs along the leading edge of the hull, just below the waterline — is a filter. It takes in water and everything suspended in it, passes it through internal structures that are not gills exactly but that perform a similar separation function, extracts the organic matter, and expels the water through a series of ports along the keel. The expelled water is clearer than the intake. The basin-grazer is a water purification system that happens to be alive.

They surface to breathe. This is the sound you hear — the low, bubbling exhalation, then the sliding of water off the curved dorsum, then the silence. The breathing is slow. The basin-grazer takes one breath every four to five minutes, a massive exchange of air that enters through a single spiracle on the top of the dorsum, a spiracle that is otherwise sealed, that is invisible when closed, that leaves no mark on the smooth surface. The exhalation carries a sound: not a whistle, not a groan, but a *tone*, a low frequency that resonates with the basin itself, that seems to be amplified by the shape of the hollow, that is — if you listen carefully, if you have been here long enough to learn the sounds — different for each individual.

This tone is the basin-grazer's identity. It is not a call in the communicative sense. It does not carry information. It carries *presence*. It says: *I am here, I am this large, I am breathing, this is my basin.* Other basin-grazers hear it and know. Predators hear it and know. The landscape hears it and knows. The basin-grazer's breath is a geographic feature. It is part of the sound of Karsvall the way the hydraulophone towers are part of the sound of Karsvall, an instrument played by a body rather than by water.

The basin-grazers are few. A single basin — a kilometer or two across, shallow, warm, rich — might support two. Perhaps three, in the largest. They do not tolerate crowding, not through aggression but through the simple mathematics of food: a basin-grazer eats constantly, filtering water day and night with a patience that never flags, and the basins can only produce so much. When a basin-grazer reaches the limit of its territory, it turns. It always turns. It has been turning for years, for decades, tracing the same path through the same water, wearing a groove in the basin floor that is visible if the water is clear enough and you are close enough — a gentle depression in the sediment, a track, a record of patience.

They live a long time. The growth rings in the cartilaginous keel — if you could examine one, which you cannot, because they are alive and will be alive for longer than you will be here — suggest something like sixty, eighty, perhaps a hundred years. Growth that slow, in a body that large, in an environment that stable, implies a metabolism that is not waiting for anything. The basin-grazer is not waiting. It is simply doing what it does at the speed at which it does it, and the speed is the speed of a basin, the speed of water, the speed of a geological process that happens to be alive.

When a basin-grazer dies — and they do die, eventually, the body losing integrity, the hull softening, the filter-clogging with sediment that can no longer be expelled — the body settles to the basin floor and becomes part of it. The decomposition is rapid in the warm water, the organic compounds breaking down into the minerals that the chemosynthetic mats need, and the death of a grazer produces a bloom of life at the basin floor that lasts for months. Other grazers, drawn by the chemical signature of the bloom, may arrive. They may stay. They may take the dead one's place. The basin does not mourn. The basin continues.

## What Flies

Between the lower vegetation and the tower-trunk crowns, in the layer of luminous dark where the condensate opens into something that is almost a space, there are things that move through the air.

You hear them first. The leathery flapping. The rush of displaced condensate. The high-pitched calls — rapid, descending, almost conversational — that serve as both navigation and announcement. They are echolocating. The calls go out, and the echoes come back, and from the echoes they build a world that is sharper than any visual world could be in this medium: a world of precise distances, of surface textures encoded in the quality of the return, of movement detected by the shift in echo-pattern between one call and the next.

Call them the *canopy-navigators*, though no one here has named them, because no one here names things.

The canopy-navigator's body is small — the size of your hand, perhaps smaller — and it is built around a single structural principle: the wing. The wing is not a modified forelimb. The canopy-navigator has no limbs. It has no legs, no arms, no grasping appendages. It is a body with wings, and the wings are not something it *has* but something it *is*. The body is the wing. The body is a flat, curved sheet of tissue — a membrane stretched between structural ribs that are not bone but a stiff, tubular cartilage — and the whole creature is a kite, a living kite, that moves through the condensate by flexing the ribs in sequence, sending waves of deformation across the membrane that produce lift and thrust simultaneously.

They do not land. This is not quite true — they must land sometime, to rest, to mate, to deposit offspring on suitable surfaces — but they land rarely, and when they do, they land on the undersides of the tower-trunk platforms, clinging to the ribbed structures there with tiny, specialized grip-pads that are not hooks, not claws, but adhesive surfaces, wet against wet, a bond formed by surface tension and the particular chemistry of their secretions. They hang there, inverted, and they are still, and they are invisible in the darkness under the platform, and if you did not hear the soft impact of their arrival, you would never know they were there.

They hunt in the canopy layer. The things they hunt are smaller still — organisms that ride the condensate currents, that drift in the saturated air the way plankton drifts in water, that are too small to see and too numerous to count. The canopy-navigator flies through these drifts with its membrane-wing open and its feeding-pore wide, and it filters the air the way the basin-grazer filters the water, taking in the condensate and everything suspended in it and extracting what is edible and exhaling the rest. It eats constantly. It must eat constantly, because its metabolism is fast — faster than anything else in Karsvall, faster by an order of magnitude. The canopy-navigator lives for perhaps a year. It lives that year at a speed that the other inhabitants of the plateau would not recognize as living, a year that is compressed into a constant, buzzing, hunting, calling, flying intensity that burns through a small body's resources and ends in a small body's death.

They fall, when they die. They fall out of the canopy layer, through the condensate veils, through the chest-height fog, and they land on the moss-carpet or the stone or the surface of a basin, and they are small, and they are already half-dissolved by the wet, and the strand-runners find them and eat them, or the soil absorbs them, or the basin-grazer filters them out of the water. The death of a canopy-navigator is not an event. It is a transfer.

There are millions of them. The canopy of Karsvall hums with their calls, a stratum of sound that is higher, faster, and more complex than anything below it. If you could hear them all at once — and you almost can, if you listen, if you stand between the tower-trunks and tilt your head upward and let the condensate run down your face and just *listen* — you would hear something that is not noise but music. Not composed music. Not intentional music. But music in the way that a flock is a shape: a pattern that emerges from the interaction of many independent agents, each one following its own rules, and the pattern is real, and the pattern is beautiful, and no one is making it.

## What Glows

On the tower-trunks, in the corridors, in the deeper recesses where the condensate is thickest and the darkness is most complete, there are things that produce light.

The light is not for you. The light is not for anyone. The light does not illuminate; it is too faint, too localized, too deeply buried in the condensate to cast a beam or reveal a surface. It simply *is* — a deep blue, a bruised purple, a color that exists at the very bottom of the visible spectrum, at the edge of what your eyes can register, a color that is more of a suggestion than a fact.

The organisms that produce this light are small. The size of a thumbnail, the size of a coin, the size of a large raindrop. They cling to the tower-trunks' surfaces in clusters, in lines along the vertical water-channels, in dense patches near the base where the condensate is richest. They are not mobile, or if they are, they move so slowly that mobility is a theoretical property, like the mobility of a glacier. They are fixed. They are committed to their surfaces. They have been there, in those clusters, in those lines, for years, for decades, growing imperceptibly, dividing rarely, producing their light with a constancy that does not vary with time of day or season or any external factor you can identify.

The light is bioluminescent. It is produced by a chemical reaction that is, at its core, the same kind of reaction that produces bioluminescence in any world — a luciferin, a luciferase, an oxidation, a photon. But the specific chemistry here is unusual. The reaction is driven by the electric charge in the condensate, the same faint charge that makes your arm-hair stand up before a rainstorm. The organisms are harvesting the electric potential of the atmosphere and converting it, through a biochemical pathway that is not entirely understood because no one has studied it because no one has been here who would study it, into light.

Why? The question is not rhetorical, but neither is it answerable. The light does not appear to serve a communicative function — the organisms do not vary their output, do not pulse, do not flash, do not respond to the presence of others. It does not appear to serve a predatory function — they are not luring anything. It does not appear to serve a defensive function — they are not warning anything. The light simply occurs. It is a byproduct. It is a leak. It is the excess energy of a metabolic process that has no other outlet, a waste product that happens to be visible, a biological incident that has persisted because it is not harmful enough to select against.

Or perhaps it is a prayer. Perhaps it is an assertion. Perhaps, in a world of total darkness, the mere act of producing a photon — one photon, a single quantum of light, a disturbance in the electromagnetic field so small that no eye could detect it from more than a meter away — is a statement of such defiance, such radical refusal of the prevailing condition, that it constitutes a kind of worship. A private light in a private darkness. A small assertion of visibility in a world that otherwise refuses it.

They are beautiful. You will not see them, or you will see them only as a faint bruise of color in the corner of your vision, a glow that vanishes when you look directly at it and reappears when you look away, a light that exists only at the edge of perception, only in the margins, only as a possibility. But they are beautiful. They are the closest thing to stars that this world has.

## The Ticking Things

On the ground, on the stone ridges, in the corridors where the condensate thins enough for the quartzite glow to reach, there are things that tick.

The sound is precise. A click, a snap, a short, sharp report that is unlike anything else in the acoustic landscape of Karsvall — unlike the hiss, unlike the drip, unlike the hum, unlike the calls. It is mechanical. It is dry. In a world where every sound is wet and sustained, the tick is crisp and momentary. It sounds like a clock. It sounds like a geiger counter. It sounds like something counting.

The organisms that produce it are small — the size of your fist, the size of a pebble — and they move across the stone with a deliberation that suggests purpose. You will not see them. You will hear them, and you will track them by sound, and you will fail, because they are silent between ticks and they move when they are silent, and by the time you have located one tick, the organism has already moved to produce the next.

They are, as nearly as can be determined, stone-eaters. They live on the basalt. They process the rock itself, extracting minerals from it through a combination of chemical dissolution and mechanical abrasion. The tick is the sound of a tool — a hard, chitinous appendage, a beak, a mandible — striking the stone to break loose a fragment that can be dissolved and absorbed. Each tick is a bite. Each bite is a meal that takes hours to digest. They eat the landscape. They are the landscape eating itself, at a scale so small and a pace so slow that the erosion they produce is invisible against the background of the water's work, but it is there, and it is constant, and it has been there for as long as the stone has been weeping.

They communicate by ticking. The intervals between clicks are not random — they carry information about the quality of the stone, the presence of quartzite veins, the proximity of others. A rapid sequence of ticks means: *good stone here, rich in iron, follow my sound.* A single, isolated tick means: *I am here, alone, the stone is poor.* A long silence means: *I am eating. Do not disturb.*

They are the smallest visible inhabitants of Karsvall, and they are the most numerous. The stone ridges are covered with them. Every exposed surface hosts dozens, hundreds, each one a tiny, precise, tireless agent of erosion, each one a small, dry voice in a vast, wet choir.

## What Lives in the Moss

Beneath your feet, if you stand still, if you let the moss compress under your weight and the water rise around your boots, the carpet moves.

Not with the sudden movement of a startled animal. Not with the purposeful movement of a creature going somewhere. The movement is a *rustling*. A constant, subtle, pervasive rustling, like pages turning in a library that has no readers, like something reading the landscape one frond at a time. The sound is so constant, so low, so thoroughly integrated into the background of the condensate-hiss and the drip that you do not notice it until you stop walking and stand still and listen, and then it is everywhere, and you realize that the ground is alive in a way that the ground in other places is not alive.

The moss-carpet of Karsvall is not moss. It is an ecosystem. A consortium. A mat of interlocking organisms — filamentous strands that are not plants, cellular aggregates that are not animals, mineral-encrusted nodules that are not anything you have a word for — that together constitute a living surface, a skin over the saturated soil, a membrane that mediates the exchange of water and mineral and gas between the ground and the air.

Within this mat, in the spaces between the filaments, in the channels that the water has carved through the organic matter, there are things that live. They are small. The largest is the size of your little finger. The smallest is invisible — a single cell, a membrane and a nucleus and a will, and the will is: eat, divide, eat, divide, eat, divide.

The visible ones — the ones that produce the rustling — are detritivores. They eat what falls into the moss: dead canopy-navigators, fragments of tower-trunk tissue, the waste products of strand-runners that have been absorbed by the soil and brought to the surface by the wicking action of the filaments. They process this material with an efficiency that would be horrifying if it were visible — a dead canopy-navigator, falling into the moss, is reduced to its component minerals in a matter of hours, broken down by an army of organisms that are each no larger than a grain of rice, each one equipped with a battery of enzymes that can disassemble organic matter the way water disassembles salt.

They do not make sounds, exactly. The rustling is the sound of their movement — the sound of small bodies pushing through the filamentous mat, displacing water and air and organic matter as they go. It is the sound of decomposition in progress, the sound of a recycling system at work, the sound of death being made into life at a pace so rapid that the gap between the two states is almost nonexistent.

There are predators among them. Tiny, fast, fierce things that hunt the detritivores through the moss-channels with a ferocity that is all the more remarkable for its scale. They are the size of a fingernail. They are armed with appendages that are not mandibles, not fangs, but piercing tubes — syringes, hypodermics — that inject a digestive enzyme into their prey and then suck out the liquefied remains. They are invisible. They are everywhere. They are the reason the moss-carpet rustles in a pattern that is not uniform — the rustling changes when a predator enters a zone, the detritivores altering their movement patterns to avoid the threat, and the resulting change in the sound is perceptible if you have been here long enough, if your ears have been tuned by the darkness to hear the difference between the sound of feeding and the sound of fear.

The moss-carpet is a world. It is a complete ecosystem — producers, consumers, predators, decomposers — that exists at a scale so different from your own that you could lie down on it and press your face into it and still not perceive more than a fraction of what is happening there. It is a world beneath a world, a landscape within a landscape, and it has been here, functioning, cycling, rustling, for as long as the tower-trunks have been standing.

## The Gelatinous Columns

Near the bases of the tower-trunks, in clusters of three or seven or twelve, there are the translucent, waist-high columns that pulse with a slow internal rhythm. They are warm to the touch. They are not alive, or they are alive in a way that is so different from the other living things here that the word *alive* bends under the strain of including them.

They are — the best description, the one that emerges after long observation and longer thought — *organs*. Not organisms. Organs. They are parts of the tower-trunk's body that have externalized, that have grown beyond the trunk's surface and into the surrounding space, that have become, in a sense, separate while remaining connected. The tower-trunk is a distributed organism. Its body extends through the ground as a root system that covers an area far larger than the trunk's footprint, and it extends into the air through these columns, which are, functionally, *lungs*.

The tower-trunk does not breathe the way strand-runners breathe or basin-grazers breathe. It does not take in air and extract gases and expel waste. It *exchanges*. The columns are the exchange surfaces — semi-permeable membranes, gelatinous and warm, that allow the passage of water, dissolved minerals, and certain gases between the tower-trunk's internal systems and the external environment. The pulse you feel when you touch one is the pulse of the trunk's internal circulation, a slow, rhythmic pumping of fluid through the column that is synchronized with the breathing movements of the canopy-platforms above.

They are not independent. They cannot survive without the trunk. If a column is severed — and nothing here severs them, but if it were — it would lose its warmth within minutes, its translucency within hours, its coherence within days. It would dissolve into the saturated soil and become part of the moss-carpet's food supply. It is not a creature. It is a process, a body-act made external, a function given form.

But they are not nothing, either. They respond to touch. They respond to temperature. They respond to the chemical composition of the water that reaches them through the soil. If you place your hand on one and hold it there, the pulse will change — not dramatically, not rapidly, but perceptibly, the rhythm shifting to accommodate a new variable in its environment, the column adjusting its exchange rate to account for the warmth and the chemistry of your skin. It is aware of you in the way that a lung is aware of the air: not consciously, not deliberately, but functionally, responsively, as a system that has been designed — or evolved, or both — to monitor and adjust to the conditions of its environment.

You should not touch them. Not because it harms them — it does not, or not perceptibly — but because the column will adjust to you, and the adjustment will propagate through the trunk's system, and the trunk will adjust its processing of water and mineral and air to account for the new input, and the adjustments will propagate outward through the root system into the soil, and the soil chemistry will shift, and the moss-carpet will respond, and the organisms in the moss will respond, and a chain of consequences will follow from the simple act of placing your warm hand on a warm surface. The system is sensitive. The system is interconnected. The system does not distinguish between a significant disturbance and an insignificant one, because the system does not have a concept of significance. Everything that happens is simply input. Everything that happens is processed. Everything that happens becomes part of the answer.

Leave them alone. Let them pulse. Let them breathe for the trunks. They have been doing it for longer than you have been alive, and they will be doing it after you leave, and the only thing your touch will accomplish is a brief, unnecessary ripple in a system that does not need ripples.

## The Predators

They are here.

You will not see them. You will not hear them. You will hear the *absence* of them, which is the most eloquent sound in Karsvall.

It happens like this: you are walking, or standing, or sitting. The condensate is dripping. The strand-runners are calling. The canopy-navigators are chittering above. The ticking things are ticking on the stone. The moss is rustling. The hydraulophone tower is humming. The world is full of sound, layered and complex and constant, a music that you have been learning to hear for hours or days, and you have started to be able to pick out individual instruments, to follow a single strand-runner's call as it moves through the corridors, to track a canopy-navigator's echo-sequences as it hunts in the canopy layer.

And then, without warning, everything stops.

Not all at once. In a wave. First the strand-runners nearest you go silent — their calls cut off in mid-sequence, their breathing inaudible. Then the canopy-navigators above you stop chittering. Then the ticking things stop ticking. Then the moss stops rustling. The silence spreads outward from a point that you cannot locate, a point that is somewhere near you, or far from you, or beside you, a point that is defined not by its own sound but by the silence of everything else.

The condensate does not stop. The dripping does not stop. The hydraulophone does not stop. These are the sounds of the landscape itself, not of its inhabitants, and the landscape does not acknowledge the predator's presence. But everything that lives, everything that moves, everything that chooses when to make sound and when to be silent — all of it stops. All of it waits.

The silence lasts. Ten seconds. Twenty. Thirty. A minute. In that minute, the only sounds are the hiss and the drip and the hum, and you become aware of your own heartbeat, your own breathing, the sound of your body in the condensate, and you realize that you, too, have gone still. You did not choose to. Your body chose for you. The same signal that silenced the strand-runners silenced you, and you did not hear the signal, you did not perceive it, but your body did, at some level below consciousness, some ancient layer of recognition that does not need to know what a predator is to know that one is near.

Then it passes. The ticking resumes. The moss rustles. The canopy-navigators begin chittering. The strand-runners begin calling, tentatively at first, then with full confidence, the network re-establishing itself, the maps updating, the territory reasserting its structure. The world rebuilds itself in sound, and the silence is gone, and the predator is gone, and you did not see it and you did not hear it and you will not know what it was.

But you can infer.

The predator is large. The silence it produces covers a wide area — a hundred meters, perhaps more, the zone of stillness radiating outward from its position. The predator is silent. It produces no sound of its own — no footfall, no breathing, no call, no tick, no rustle. In a world where every organism is loud, where every body announces itself constantly through sound and chemical signature and bioluminescent glow, the predator is the one thing that is quiet. It is the negative space. It is the hole in the fabric of perception.

The predator is a listener. This is the inference that follows from the silence: a predator that hunts in a world where sound is the dominant sense must hunt by sound, and to hunt by sound it must be silent, and to be silent in a world where every surface is wet and every movement produces a displacement and every displacement is audible, it must be *very good* at being silent. It must know the landscape at a level that the strand-runners do not — not just the chemical gradients and the condensate currents but the acoustic properties of every surface, the resonance of every basin, the way sound propagates through the corridors between trunks. It must know where the sound-shadow zones are, the spaces where the condensate's hiss masks other sounds, the places where the hydraulophone's hum creates an acoustic background against which movement is inaudible. It must know these things the way you know the rooms of your own house: by heart, by habit, by the accumulated experience of a lifetime spent in the dark.

The predator is rare. The silence does not come often — once a day, perhaps, perhaps less. A predator that hunts frequently would deplete its prey. A predator that is this good at being silent does not need to hunt frequently. It takes what it needs and no more, and what it needs is less than you might think, because the predator is efficient, because the predator has been here for a long time, because the predator is, in its way, as much a part of the system as the tower-trunks and the hydraulophone towers and the condensate itself.

The predator is not your concern. This is the hardest thing to accept, standing in the silence, feeling your own heartbeat in your throat, knowing that something large and quiet is nearby. The predator is not hunting you. You are not its prey. You are not part of its system. You are an anomaly — a warm, loud, slow body in a world of warm, loud, slow bodies, but one that does not fit any of its categories, that does not produce the right chemical signatures, that does not move with the right patterns. You are not recognized. You are not categorized. You are not on the menu.

You are, for the purposes of the predator, a stone. An odd-smelling stone that moves and breathes, but a stone nonetheless.

This is not reassuring. This is not meant to be reassuring. This is meant to be *accurate*, and the accurate truth is that the predator's indifference is the same as the landscape's indifference, and the landscape's indifference is not hostility. It is not malice. It is not the cold, anthropomorphic indifference of a universe that does not care. It is the *warm* indifference of a system that has already solved every problem that matters to it, that has incorporated every variable that it recognizes, that is complete, that is finished, that is simply being the answer.

You are not a variable it recognizes. You are not a problem it needs to solve.

The silence passes. The sounds return. You walk on. The predator is behind you, or beside you, or ahead of you. It does not matter. It has always been here. It will always be here. It is part of the answer, and the answer does not include you, and that is all right, and that is not all right, and you walk on.

## What Was Here Before

The builders.

They are gone. They left behind the distillation buildings, the hydraulophone towers, the foundations and platforms. They left behind machines that are silent and stone that is fitted and a system that still functions. They left behind the evidence of a mind — or minds — that understood this landscape at a level that you cannot fathom, that could read the hydrology and the geology and the ecology and see, in the relationships between them, a design that was not yet complete, a system that needed one more element to close, one more set of processes to achieve equilibrium.

They were not people. Or they might have been. The evidence is insufficient. The built structures do not contain remains — no bones, no artifacts, no personal effects. If the builders had bodies, they did not leave them here. If the builders had tools, they took them or they dissolved. If the builders had language, it was not written, or it was written in a medium that the condensate has erased.

What they left is an intention. The distillation building processes water for the landscape's use. The hydraulophone towers regulate subsurface pressure. The foundations leach minerals into the soil. Every built structure in Karsvall serves a function within the ecosystem, and the function is not for the builders. It is for the system. The builders built things that do not serve them, that serve the landscape, that serve the tower-trunks and the basin-grazers and the strand-runners and the moss and the ticking things and the glowers and the predators. They built for the world, not for themselves.

This is not altruism. This is not stewardship, not in the human sense. This is something else — a relationship to a landscape that goes beyond use, beyond management, beyond even reverence, into a territory that is more like *participation*. The builders were part of the system, and they built the things the system needed, and then they left, or died, or became something else, and the system continued, and the things they built continued to function, and the system does not miss them because the system does not have a concept of missing. The system has only what is present, and what is present is sufficient.

You wonder, in the dark, in the wet, if the builders became the system. If they dissolved into it, literally or figuratively, if their bodies returned to the water and their minerals returned to the stone and their intentions returned to the structures they built, and if the system, in some way you cannot articulate and cannot prove and cannot quite bring yourself to disbelieve, *remembers* them. Not consciously. Not sentimentally. But functionally. The way a river remembers the shape of its banks. The way a tree remembers the angle of the light. The way a system, having been adjusted by an intelligence, retains the adjustment, carries it forward, incorporates it into the ongoing answer.

You wonder if the builders are still here, in the hum of the hydraulophone, in the warmth of the gelatinous columns, in the precise angle of the distillation terraces, in the way the condensate forms and falls and forms again. You wonder if *here* is what they became. If Karsvall is not a place they lived but a place they *are*, a body they built for themselves out of stone and water and time, a body that breathes and processes and regulates and sustains itself, a body that is vast and dark and wet and loud and alive.

You wonder, and the condensate beads on your skin, and the strand-runners call in the corridors, and the basin-grazers surface in the lakes, and the ticking things tick on the stone, and the moss rustles beneath your feet, and the glowers glow their private blue, and the predator passes in its silence, and the hydraulophone hums its low, patient note, and you feel it in your chest, and for a moment your heartbeat synchronizes with it, and you are part of the system, a warm wet surface in a warm wet world, and the landscape knows you, briefly, as it knows a stone, as it knows a trunk, as it knows a drop of water falling from a great height into a dark lake that does not break the surface but becomes it.

Then the moment passes.

You are still here. The place continues.
