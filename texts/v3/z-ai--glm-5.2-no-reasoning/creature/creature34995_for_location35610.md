# What Lives Here

The first thing to understand is that nobody lives here. Not in the way you mean. Not the way someone lives in a house, wakes in a bed, answers when called. That kind of living ended with the air.

But something remains. And something has begun.

---

## The Last

There were people once. They had a name for themselves, surely, though that name dissolved somewhere in the centuries between their last breath of open air and the first careful arrival of the water. They were not many. A few hundred, perhaps, at the city's peak — which is to say, before the water came, when Acantaris was still dry and the plateau still held its head above the waves like a swimmer treading, not yet tired, not yet ready to sink.

They were not a people who would have impressed you. This is important to understand. They were not lost kings. They were not a forgotten civilization whose brilliance might shame the modern world. They were ordinary. They were people who worked with stone and bone and the cartilaginous wood of those strange trees, who ate what they could catch and grow, who fired clay in shallow pits and wove mats from fronds and sealed the seams with sap that bled from cuts in the trunk. They argued. They loved. They had the particular attachments that all people have — to a favorite tool, to a corner of a room, to the way light fell through a doorway at a certain hour. They were not special. They were *people*, which is to say they were everything and nothing, which is to say they were exactly the kind of thing the universe produces and does not miss.

When the water came — slowly, over generations, so slowly that each person might have thought the flooding of their childhood street a local anomaly and not a geological verdict — they did not leave. This is the part that is hard to understand from the outside. They did not flee to higher ground. There was no higher ground. The plateau was the high ground, and it was sinking, and they were on it. Where would they go? They had no boats. They had no knowledge of other lands. They knew the plateau and the trees and the fish in the shallows and the stone under their feet, and that was the world, and the world was going under.

Some of them, in the final generations, must have known. Must have watched the water creep up the lower streets, must have felt the growing dampness in the foundations, must have stood in doorways and seen the horizon rising. What did they do? They did what people do. They moved their belongings to the upper rooms. They raised their shelves. They kept cooking. They kept sleeping. They kept living in the houses their grandparents had built, adjusting, adjusting, adjusting, until adjustment was no longer possible and the water was in the room with them and then the water was in their mouths and then there was no more adjusting.

They did not drown dramatically. That is not how it happened. They did not thrash or cry out or cling to rooftops. The water came in inches per decade. What happened was simpler and sadder: they left. One by one, family by family, over the course of a century or two, they simply stopped being. Some may have walked to the edge of the plateau and descended into the shallows and swam — though swim where? Into open ocean with no land in sight? Others may have simply stayed, and the water took them, and they are part of the sediment now, part of the mineral sweetness that the warm basalt breathes into the water. They returned to the earth that had held them. They dissolved.

What is left of them is the city, which looks exactly as they left it because the water preserved it rather than destroyed it. The clay pots on the shelves. The bone tools on their pegs. The woven mats on the floors, still holding their shape because the tree-sap resin did not know it was supposed to fail. They left their belongings the way you might leave a house for the evening, expecting to return. They did not expect not to return. Or perhaps they did, and this was their last statement — not a grand gesture, not a monument, just the ordinary fact of their things, in their places, in the houses they had built, left standing.

Walk through Acantaris and you are walking through their absence. It is not a haunted place. It is a vacated place. There is a difference. A haunt implies a presence that refuses to leave. What is here is more like an echo — not a sound but a shape, the negative space where lives were, outlined by the artifacts they touched. The bowl on the shelf is not haunted. It is simply still there, and the hands that held it are not, and the gap between those two facts is the entire story.

## The Vegetation

The trees were here before the people and they are here after. This is worth noting because it means they are not the people's trees. They are not memorials. They are not the garden that grew over the grave. They are themselves, and they were always themselves, and the people were the ones who happened to live among them for a while.

They are still figuring out what they are. This is literal. The trees of Acantaris are not evolved in the way you might expect — they have no ancient lineage, no refined forms, no genetic memory of what a tree is supposed to do. They are a first attempt. The plateau's geology is young, and its biology is younger, and these organisms are operating from a kind of cellular improvisation. They do not have the luxury of inheritance. Every trunk is an experiment. Every frond is a draft.

What this means, in practice, is that the trees are unpredictable. They grow in shapes that make no taxonomic sense. A single organism might begin as a trunk, split at the midpoint into two root structures that grow downward and outward, then resurface ten feet away as a cluster of fronds that harden and become woody and begin to function as a trunk again. They loop. They fold back on themselves. They change their minds mid-growth, switching from vascular tissue to structural tissue to photosynthetic membrane without clear transition. They are not confused — confusion implies a prior clarity — they are simply working without a blueprint, and the results are what you see: a forest of dark red translucent columns, pulsing their slow cold light, each one a unique solution to a problem that is still being defined.

They are also, in a way that is easy to miss, alive in a manner that feels conversational. Not conscious. Not aware. But responsive. When the warm currents shift — as they do, slowly, in cycles that take days or weeks — the trees adjust. Their fronds turn, almost imperceptibly, toward the warmer flow. Their vascular channels dilate or constrict. Their bioluminescent pulse might quicken from every forty seconds to every thirty-five, or slow to every fifty. They are listening to the water with their bodies. They are in a dialogue with the plateau's thermal exhalations, and the dialogue has been going on longer than the people existed and will continue long after the city has been resolved into rubble or air.

If you stay among them long enough, you begin to feel that they are not organisms in the way you understand organisms. They are processes. They are not things that are alive; they are aliveness itself, moving through a medium of translucent cartilage, trying on shapes, wearing each one for a century or two, then setting it aside for something else. They will be here when the plateau surfaces. They will be different. They will have figured out more. But they will still be figuring, still drafting, still not finished, because the work they are doing is not the kind of work that finishes.

## The Fish

There are perhaps a dozen of them. They are long, pale, and almost transparent — you can see their notochords, their simple gut tubes, the faint rhythm of whatever pumps their not-quite-blood. They are fish the way the trees are trees: provisionally, tentatively, as a first guess at what a fish might be.

They do not behave like fish. They do not school, do not dart, do not feed with any urgency. They drift. They open and close their mouths in a slow, meditative rhythm that seems to have no purpose — or rather, that seems to be searching for its purpose, trying the motion on, seeing if it leads anywhere. Sometimes one will settle near a tree trunk and hang there, almost motionless, for hours. Sometimes one will drift through an open doorway and hover in the still water inside a dwelling, surrounded by clay pots and bone tools, as though visiting a museum it does not understand.

Each fish is alone. This is the most striking thing about them. They share the city but they do not share each other's company. They pass within feet of each other and show no recognition, no interest, no reaction. They are solitary in a way that goes beyond preference — it is constitutional. They are not built for each other yet. Whatever mechanisms of social behavior that fish might one day develop, these have not. They are prototypes, and the prototype does not include community.

What they think about — if they think, if the slow opening and closing of their mouths and the drifting through warm water constitutes a kind of cognition — is unknowable. But if you watch one long enough, you begin to form an impression. Not a thought, exactly. More like a mood. A temperament. The fish is calm in a way that is not the calm of contentment but the calm of incompleteness. It is calm because it has not yet developed the capacity for distress. It does not know what there is to be afraid of. It does not know what there is to want. It exists in a state prior to motivation, and that state is the most peaceful thing in the city.

## The Clinging Things

On the walls of the buildings, in the corners of rooms, along the narrow channels that were once streets, there are pale, flat, irregularly shaped organisms that are not quite animal and not quite plant. They grow. Very slowly. You have to watch for hours to see any change, but if you do, you will observe the extension of pale tendrils across the basalt, feeling outward, tasting the stone, deciding where to anchor next.

They are new. New even by the standards of this young place. They may be the first of their kind. They may be an experiment that the warm mineral water is running, a trial of a form of life that has not been tried before. They are colonizing the city — not aggressively, not destructively, but with a patience that makes lichen look impulsive. They are moving into the buildings the way the people once moved into the buildings: slowly, one structure at a time, finding the corners that suit them, the walls that face the right direction, the rooms where the warm water pools just so.

They do not seem to be eating the stone. They do not seem to be eating anything. They simply spread, and where they spread, the basalt beneath them takes on a slightly different texture — smoother, perhaps, or slightly more porous, as though the clinging things are preparing the surface for something. A foundation. A first step in a process that will take millennia and that no one will be here to see completed.

They are not aware of you. They are not aware of anything. But they are responsive, in their way — if the warm current shifts and carries more minerals past a wall, the tendrils on that wall will extend faster, reaching toward the nutrient source with a blind, slow, absolute intent. They are the most primitive thing here, and they are the thing that most resembles what life does at its most fundamental: they persist. They take what is available and they make more of themselves and they do not stop.

## The Shapes at the Edge

In the deeper water, where the plateau drops away into darkness, there are shapes. You cannot see them clearly. You can barely see them at all — just the suggestion of a silhouette, a density in the amber murk, something that blocks the faint light and then does not block it. They are large. How large is difficult to say because the water plays tricks with scale, but they are larger than the fish, larger than the clinging things, larger than anything that has a right to be here.

They do not come close. They hang at the threshold of the plateau's edge, at the boundary where the warm upwelling water meets the cold deep, and they are almost perfectly still. If you watch one for an hour, you might see it shift. Not move — shift. A repositioning, a settling, the way a large animal adjusts in its sleep. The silhouette changes shape slightly and then is still again, and you are left wondering whether you saw what you saw.

What are they? They may be animals. They may be something else — geological formations that have taken on organic characteristics, or organic accumulations that have grown to geological scale. They may be the plateau's own creation, expressions of the deep earth's generative impulse, things that are being born in the warm mineral darkness at the base of the basalt and that will, in ten thousand or a hundred thousand years, rise with the plateau and emerge into air and become whatever they are going to become. They are not finished. Nothing here is finished.

They are aware. This is a difficult claim to make and a more difficult one to substantiate, but if you spend enough time at the edge of the plateau, in the dim amber light, watching the shapes that hang in the deeper dark, you will come to feel it. Not intelligence. Not consciousness in any way you would recognize. But a presence — a quality of attention that is not directed at you but that includes you, the way the ocean's attention includes a grain of sand. They know you are there in the same way they know the current is there: as a fact of their environment, small and brief and ultimately irrelevant to the long slow work of existing.

They have been here longer than the city. They will be here after the city is gone. They are not its guardians and they are not its ghosts. They are the thing that was here before and that will be here after, and the city — the people who built it, the trees that grow through it, the fish that drift through its doorways, the pale things that cling to its walls — is a brief event in their experience, a flicker of warmth and stone on the surface of their world, already fading, not yet gone, still becoming, still becoming.

---

## What Lives Here

What lives here is the place itself.

That is the answer, and it is the only answer that is true. The people are gone. The trees are processes, not beings. The fish are prototypes. The clinging things are experiments. The shapes in the deep are something too large and too slow to be called alive in any way that the word usually means.

But the plateau is alive. The basalt hums. The water warms. The stone is still becoming stone, still crystallizing, still working out its mineral structure, still rising from the mantle with a patience that makes geological time look hasty. The city sits on its back like a rider on an animal that does not know it is ridden. The trees pulse. The fish drift. The clinging things spread. The shapes in the deep shift their immense and silent bodies. And everything — everything — is in the act of becoming, in the middle of the first draft, not finished, not settled, not done.

What lives in Acantaris is emergence itself. The great ongoing event of things coming into being. Not the things, but the coming. Not the forms, but the forming. The warm dark earth pushing upward. The water holding everything in its patient suspension. The light arriving in its dim green-grey whisper. The silence that is not empty but full — full of the hum of basalt, the pulse of fronds, the slow blind reach of pale tendrils, the drift of transparent fish through streets that were walked by people who are now part of the mineral sweetness in the water.

It is not a haunted place. It is a nascent one. And if you are here, you are not a visitor. You are a witness to something that does not know it is being witnessed, something that is too busy becoming to care that you have arrived, something that will continue long after you have gone, rising, warming, pulsing its slow light, extending its blind tendrils, drifting its patient drift, humming its low hum.

What lives here is the future. It just hasn't arrived yet.
