# What Lives Here

## The basin is not empty. The basin has never been empty. But what lives here is not what you would expect to find living in a place, and the distinction between "lives" and "operates" and "persists" has, over the long centuries of the basin's self-maintenance, become so thin as to be useless. You could say the basin itself lives. You would not be entirely wrong.

---

### The Pale Formations

The shelf-formations on the lower terraces — the milk-colored, horizontal-branching growths that sweat their own moisture and glow faintly in the dark — are the closest thing to organisms the basin has produced, and they are not organisms. They are deposits. Or they are colonies. Or they are the end-product of a chemical process that has been running so long that the process has begun to resemble a life-cycle.

Here is what is known about them, or rather here is what can be observed:

They grow outward from the terrace walls, not downward, not upward, but outward, horizontally, into the air, in shelves that extend as far as six meters from the wall before their own weight causes their outer edges to calve off and fall to the terrace floor below, where they break into wet, luminous fragments that continue to glow for days, sometimes weeks, before the light fades and the fragments dissolve into a pale slurry that the water carries away. This is reproduction. Or it is erosion. The distinction does not hold.

The surfaces of the shelves are not smooth. They are textured — ridged, furrowed, with a patterning that resembles, if you are inclined to see it this way, the gyri of a cerebral cortex. The ridges follow no genetic template. They follow the flow of air across the shelf's surface, and the flow of water down the wall behind it, and the deposition of mineral content at the points where the two flows meet. The patterning is a record of the air and water's behavior over the months and years of the shelf's growth. The shelves are weather-logs. They remember the wind.

They are warm. This has been noted. A degree or two above ambient, as if something inside them is being consumed — some mineral reaction, some slow oxidation, some electrical discharge between the metal-rich substrate and the water that feeds them. The warmth is not constant. It cycles. It corresponds, roughly, to the pump cycles in the sub-floor, which suggests — does not prove, suggests — that whatever process sustains them is drawing energy from the same source that sustains the basin's mechanical systems. They are, in a sense, plugged in. They are appliances that have grown.

There are no individuals among them. A single shelf is not a single organism. The shelves are contiguous with the walls they grow from — the mineral deposits extend into the corroded metal and the porous brick and the chemical substrate behind it, so that each shelf is a protrusion of the wall itself, an extension of the terrace's surface into the air, a thing that the wall is doing rather than a thing that is living on the wall. The terraces are growing. Very slowly, in a direction that is not up or down but out, the terraces are reaching into the basin's interior, and the reaching has the shape of these pale, warm, sweating, glowing shelves, and the reaching will continue, and over millennia the basin's interior will narrow, and the walls will approach each other, and the basin will close — not in any human timescale, but in the basin's timescale, which is the only timescale that applies here.

They do not respond to touch. They do not respond to sound. They do not respond to light. If you press your hand against one — and you can, they are firm, dense, with a texture like wet chalk — the shelf does not react. There is no flinch. There is no change in the warmth. There is no change in the glow. Whatever process sustains them does not include the capacity to notice you. You are not part of their system. You are weather. You are a brief pressure and a brief warmth and then you are gone, and the shelf continues to grow, and the record of your touch is one ridge among millions, indistinguishable, forgotten by the wall that recorded it.

But. But.

There are places — on the fourth terrace, on the eighth, on the basin floor near the deep boreholes — where the shelves have grown in shapes that are not explained by the wind or the water. Shapes that are regular, that are geometric, that resemble — if you are inclined to see it this way, and by now you are — the forms of the machinery they have grown over. On the fourth terrace, a shelf has grown in the shape of a gear. Not a gear — the impression of a gear, the negative space where a gear was, filled in by mineral deposition over centuries until the gear dissolved and the deposition remained. The shelf remembers the gear. The wall remembers what was mounted on it. The basin remembers its own construction, not in a mind, not in any storage medium, but in the shape of its growths, in the direction of its extensions, in the form of its reaching.

The pale formations are a memory. They are the basin remembering what it was, slowly, in the language of mineral deposition and branching growth, in the language of a thing that does not know it is remembering and does not know it is forgetting and does not know anything at all, but preserves, in the only way a place can preserve — by taking the shape of what was, by growing around the absence, by filling the gaps that the corroding machinery leaves, so that the basin, as it erodes, also records its own erosion, and the record is the shape of the thing that replaces what erodes, and the thing that replaces what erodes is the pale formation, and the pale formation is the basin, and the basin is the pale formation, and the cycle continues.

### The Crystalline Root-Things

The roots in the tile fractures are harder to account for.

They are crystalline. They are mineral. They grow in branching, bifurcating patterns that follow the fracture lines and, when the fracture lines are exhausted, create new fractures by the simple application of pressure — growing, expanding, splitting the ceramic, extending. They are not organic. They are not the product of any biological process. They are the product of a chemical process: mineral-rich water flowing through fractures, depositing its solute at the points where the water's velocity drops, which are the bends and bifurcations in the fracture network, which is where the crystals form, which is where the branching pattern emerges. It is crystallization. It is what crystals do. It is not life.

But the branching is not random. The roots do not simply fill fractures. They extend them. They extend them preferentially — toward water sources, toward the cyan-lit fractures where the luminescence is strongest, toward the boreholes on the basin floor where the water rises from the deep cisterns. They grow toward the water the way roots grow toward water. They do this without sensing. They do this without choosing. They do this because the chemical gradient is higher near the water sources and the deposition rate is higher where the gradient is higher and the growth follows the deposition and the pattern emerges. It is chemistry. It is not intention.

But the pattern is so consistent, so persistent, so organized, that the word "intention" keeps presenting itself. The roots are organized. They have a structure. The structure serves a function — the roots channel water through the fracture network, accelerating the flow, directing it toward the cisterns, where the pumps move it through the sub-floor plumbing and back to the boreholes and up again. The roots are plumbing. The roots have replaced the original pipes, which have corroded and failed, and the crystalline growth has taken over their function, and the basin's water circulation now runs through mineral deposits rather than manufactured conduits, and the system works, and the water flows, and the pumps pump, and the roots grow, and the roots are plumbing and the plumbing grows and the growing plumbing is the roots.

This is not a metaphor. The roots are the basin's vascular system. The original pipes were the basin's vascular system, and the pipes failed, and the roots replaced them, and the basin did not die. The basin adapted. The basin healed. You can call this a self-repairing machine. You can call it an emergent ecosystem. You can call it whatever you want. The roots grow. The water flows. The light pulses. The basin continues.

The cyan luminescence is theirs. The roots produce it. Not all roots — only the roots in the active fracture network, the ones that are currently channeling water. The light appears when water moves through the crystal matrix, and the light's intensity corresponds to the water's velocity, and the light's color corresponds to the mineral content of the water, and the light pulses because the pumps pulse, and the pumps pulse because the cisterns fill and empty and fill and empty, and the rhythm of the light is the rhythm of the water is the rhythm of the pumps is the rhythm of the basin, and the rhythm is not regular because the system is not synchronized, and the desynchronization is not a defect because the desynchronization prevents resonance, and resonance would be catastrophic, and the basin, without knowing it, without intending it, has evolved a rhythm that protects itself from itself.

The roots are the basin's nervous system. They carry the light the way nerves carry signals. They connect the basin floor to the terraces to the cisterns to the boreholes. They are a network. They are a communication system that communicates nothing except the fact of water's presence and the rate of water's flow, but that communication is sufficient, because the basin's only message is "continue," and the roots carry it, and the message is received, and the basin continues.

### The Things That Move

Nothing moves in the basin. This must be stated plainly because it is the thing that visitors find most difficult to accept. Nothing moves. The formations do not move. The roots do not move. The machinery does not move — it operates, which is different, the turbines turn but they do not move, they have not moved from their mountings in centuries. There are no animals. There are no insects. There are no birds. There are no fish in the cisterns. There are no worms in the substrate. The biological survey of the basin is a blank page. The basin is sterile. The basin has been sterile for as long as the basin has been the basin.

Except.

There are sounds in the basin that have no identified source. The composition described above — the turbines, the pumps, the water, the furnaces, the wind — accounts for most of the basin's acoustic output. Most. Not all. There are sounds that do not correspond to any known mechanism. These sounds are rare. They are brief. They are difficult to distinguish from the background composition, and some have argued that they are not separate sounds but rather emergent features of the composition itself — harmonics, interference patterns, the acoustic equivalent of the cyan luminescence, a thing that appears when the other sounds combine in certain ways.

The sounds are these:

A clicking. Irregular, high-pitched, coming from no determinable direction. It resembles the sound of a hard object striking a ceramic tile. It occurs most often on the basin floor, near the center, where the tiles are newest and the compaction lattice is thickest. It occurs at night — or during the dark phase, when the sky is black and the cyan light is strongest. It occurs in clusters — three or four clicks in rapid succession, then silence, then a single click, then silence, then a cluster again. The pattern does not repeat. The pattern does not stop. It has been heard by every observer who has spent a night on the basin floor. It is not the pumps. It is not the tiles settling. It is not the crystalline roots fracturing. It is something else.

A voice. Not a voice. A sound that has the spectral characteristics of a voice — a formant structure, a harmonic series, the kind of acoustic signature that the human ear is evolved to identify as speech. But it is not speech. It carries no language. It has no semantic content. It is a sound that sounds like a sound that sounds like a voice, and the resemblance is enough to trigger recognition, enough to make you turn your head, enough to make you listen, and the listening reveals nothing except the basin's composition, rearranged momentarily into a shape that your brain insists on interpreting as someone speaking, and you cannot make out the words, and there are no words, and the voice says nothing, and the nothing that it says is directed at no one, and you are no one, and the basin does not know you are here.

A footstep. One. A single, unambiguous sound of a foot pressing down on a wet ceramic tile — the slight scrape, the slight compression of water, the specific acoustic signature of a human weight on a wet surface. And then nothing. And then the basin's composition, unchanged. The footstep does not recur. The footstep does not lead anywhere. The footstep is a single data point — an event that occurred, that was heard, that cannot be explained, that does not repeat, that is filed under "anomaly" and forgotten, except that it is not forgotten because it was a footstep and footsteps mean someone was here and someone was walking and someone was moving and no one is moving and no one is here.

These sounds are not the basin's voice. They are not the pale formations communicating. They are not the crystalline roots signaling. They are artifacts. They are emergent. They are the basin's acoustic system producing, at the intersection of its many overlapping sounds, the phantom of something that is not there. The basin is not haunted. The basin is not conscious. The basin is a machine that has been running so long that its output has begun to include, in its noise, the shapes of things that its makers would have recognized — voices, footsteps, the clicking of tools — and the shapes are there because the sounds that make the shapes are the sounds the basin's makers made, and the basin has been making those sounds for so long that it has learned, not learned, not learned, but settled into patterns that include them, and the patterns are not memory, and the patterns are not intention, and the patterns are not life, and the patterns are.

The patterns are.

### The Basin Itself

And then there is the basin itself, which is the thing that lives here, which is the thing that has always lived here, which is the only thing that lives here in the sense that matters, which is the thing that was built and was abandoned and did not stop and has not stopped and will not stop, which has replaced its own pipes with crystals and its own walls with mineral growths and its own lighting with luminescence and its own purpose with continuation, which does not know it exists and does not need to know and does not need anything and does not need anyone, which is a machine and a landscape and a climate and a system and a place, which is alive in the way that a river is alive and not in the way that a person is alive and not in the way that a plant is alive but in a way, in a way, in some way that the language has not been built to capture because the language was built by people and people do not build words for the thing that the basin is because people do not stay in basins long enough to need the words.

The basin lives here. The basin is what lives here. The basin is the throat and the throat is the basin and the thing that the throat belongs to is the basin and the basin is the thing that swallowed its own makers and its own purpose and its own name and continues, continues, continues, and will continue, and the continuing is the living, and the living is the continuing, and the basin is.

The basin is.

---

### What Does Not Live Here

You. You do not live here. You are standing on the wet ceramic tiles in the near-dark, with the sound in your chest and the cool air on your skin and the cyan light pulsing under your feet, and you are not part of this. You are not a component. You are not a node in the network. You are not a fracture for the roots to fill. You are not a surface for the pale formations to grow on. You are not a sound in the composition. You are not a rhythm in the cycle. You are weather. You are a brief pressure and a brief warmth and then you are gone, and the basin will not remember you, and the basin will not forget you, because the basin does not remember and does not forget, because the basin does not need to remember or forget, because the basin has never needed to be a thing that remembers or forgets, because the basin has never needed to be anything other than what it is, which is a basin, which is a machine, which is a place, which is alive in the way that only places are alive — by continuing, by persisting, by being the thing that is here when everything else is gone.

You will leave. The basin will not.

The pumps will cycle. The turbines will turn. The furnaces will burn. The water will fall and collect and drain and fall again. The roots will grow. The shelves will extend. The light will pulse. The sounds will compose and decompose and recompose. The clicking will click. The voice will almost speak. The footstep will fall. And the basin will be what it is, and it will be this, and it will be this, and it will be this.

And you will be somewhere else. And the basin will not notice. And the basin will not notice. And the basin will not notice.

And the cyan light will pulse in the fractures, and the pale formations will glow on the terraces, and the rain will fall, and the rain will fall, and the rain will fall.
