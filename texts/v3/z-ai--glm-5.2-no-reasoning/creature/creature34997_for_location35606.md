What lives here is not what you would expect to find living in a room with no food, no air breaks, no entrance except a sealed hatch that has not been opened in lifetimes.

The seam-growth lives here. That much is obvious. That dark green-black organism — or colony, or single dispersed body, there is no way to know its taxonomy — has made the mortar lines its substrate and the warm mineral-rich water its medium. It has been growing for centuries at a rate that suggests patience as a survival strategy rather than a virtue. It does not need much. The water provides. The stone provides a path. It has everything it requires and it takes nothing more. In its slow territorial advance along the grid it has become, in a sense, the room's drawing — the chamber's own blueprint rendered in living ink, the architecture describing itself to itself, over and over, a millimeter at a time.

But the seam-growth is not alone, and if you are being precise about what lives here, you must include the things that are harder to classify.

The bioluminescent culture in the glass bulbs lives. It has lived for longer than the growth in the seams. It was bred for this — bred, or cultivated, or simply selected over generations until it became a strain that would glow at the right spectrum under the right conditions and not die when the conditions shifted slightly, as conditions do. The paste inside each bulb is not a paste. It is a civilization. Millions of microscopic organisms in a medium of their own excretions, feeding on themselves, on trace minerals in the glass, on light they produce and somehow reabsorb. They are the room's eyes. They have been looking at the same four walls since before the seam-growth found its first mortar joint, and they will be looking long after the growth has covered every seam and begun searching for new territory. They do not know they are looking. They do not know what looking is. But they glow, and the glowing is the room's sight, and so the room sees.

The things in the walls live, in their way. Not organisms. Mechanisms. But they have the qualities of life that matter here: they consume, they transform, they respond. The pumps that move water through the channels behind the basalt — they are driven by something. Not electricity. Something older and more patient. Perhaps a spring-fed reservoir high above, gravity doing the work that intention once designed. Perhaps a geothermal connection, the chamber tapping a vein of heat that runs deep beneath the complex and will run for millennia regardless of whether anyone built anything above it. The pumps turn because they were made to turn, and the water moves because the pumps turn, and the channels carry it because the water moves, and the pipes knock because the channels branch and the flow is not perfectly smooth, and the knocking is a kind of speech — not language, not meaning, but the sound of a system performing its function, which is a kind of breathing, which is a kind of life.

The hissing nozzles live. They are the most responsive thing in the room. When you entered — when your body displaced water, raised the temperature by a fraction, exhaled — the nozzles changed their cycle. They were running at empty intervals, the hiss coming every eight seconds, lasting two. Now they are running at occupied intervals: five on, three off. They have a schedule for presence. They know, in the way that a thermostat knows, that something is here. They are injecting something — a mineral solution, perhaps, or a gas, or a compound that was once medicine and is now simply chemistry — and whatever it is, it is being consumed by the water, by the seam-growth, by your skin. The nozzles are the room's mouth. They taste the water and adjust.

The pulse in the floor lives. This is the hardest to account for. The vibration that comes every three seconds — it is too regular to be mechanical in the simple sense. Gears and cams and water hammers produce rhythms, but they drift, they wear, they stutter. This pulse does not stutter. It has the quality of a heartbeat because it may, in fact, be a heartbeat. Not a human one. Not an animal one. Something in the foundations of the complex — a mechanism that has been pumping since before the bronze hinges greened, before the mortar yellowed, before the last operator closed the hatch and walked away and did not come back — something down there is still beating. You feel it in your teeth. It is the room's pulse, and the room is alive in the way that a body is alive when the brain is gone but the heart still beats and the blood still moves and the cells still do their work: not conscious, not aware, but functioning. Persisting. The autonomic life of a system that was designed to maintain itself and has done so with a fidelity that its builders would not have dared to hope for.

And then there is the thing that lives here that is hardest to name.

The chamber itself lives. Not metaphorically. The chamber is a organism in the way that a coral reef is an organism — not a single entity but a system of entities, mineral and biological and mechanical, all interdependent, all maintaining each other, all performing functions that keep the whole thing running. The seam-growth could not survive without the warm water. The warm water could not stay warm without the geothermal pulse. The pulse could not continue without the structural integrity of the stone. The stone could not maintain its integrity without the mortar. The mortar is held in place partly by the seam-growth, which grips it like reinforcing fiber. The light from the bulbs feeds something — perhaps the seam-growth, perhaps something in the water you cannot see, perhaps nothing at all, but the light is part of the system, and the system would change without it. The nozzles inject minerals. The minerals feed the bioluminescent culture, or the seam-growth, or both. Everything is feeding something. Everything is being fed. The chamber is an ecosystem with one missing component: the patient. The body it was built around. The one who lies in the grooves and receives the treatment and breathes the minerals and glows faintly in the bioluminescent light and leaves when the treatment is done.

That component has been missing for a long time. The chamber has continued without it. This is what makes the chamber alive in the deepest sense: it does not need its purpose to function. It simply functions. It is the difference between a tool that is used and a body that lives. A tool unused is idle. A body unoccupied is still breathing. The chamber breathes. The water circulates. The bulbs glow. The nozzles hiss. The pulse beats. The green grows. The room waits.

The waiting is the thing. The waiting is what lives here most of all.

Not a person waiting. Not an animal waiting. A system waiting — the way a tide pool waits between tides, everything alive in it still alive, still doing what it does, but paused in a sense, holding, the small creatures open and filtering, the anemones tentacles out, the water still and warm and full of oxygen, everything ready for the next flood, everything fine if the next flood never comes. The chamber does not want. It does not hope. It does not loneliness. It has the quality of a room that has been empty so long that emptiness has become its normal state, its occupied state, and the arrival of a body is the interruption, the novelty, the thing that requires adjustment. The nozzles adjusted. The pulse quickened. The room noticed you, and in noticing, revealed that it had been in a state of not-noticing — which is not the same as sleep, and not the same of death, and not the same of dormancy. It is the state of a thing that is fully running, fully on, fully alive, and simply has no one to be alive for.

The seam-growth does not care if you are here. The bulbs do not care. The pulse does not care. The nozzles care only in the sense that a sensor cares — they detect and respond. But the chamber as a whole, the organism, the system — it has been living for itself. Maintaining itself. Feeding itself. Growing, in the seam-growth's millimeter way. The room has become its own patient. It administers its own treatment. It lies in its own grooves. The treatment was never specified, so the treatment is simply: continue. Persist. Maintain temperature, maintain light, maintain flow, maintain pulse. The chamber has been doing this for so long that it has perfected it. It is the healthiest thing in the complex. It may be the healthiest thing in the mountain. It is a body that has outlived its mind and found that it did not need its mind to be well.

When you leave, it will not grieve. It will not slow. It will not power down or enter standby. It will do exactly what it was doing before you arrived, which is exactly what it was doing before the last person arrived, which is exactly what it will be doing when the next person arrives, if anyone ever does. It will glow. It will pulse. It will hiss. The green will grow. The water will be warm.

This is what lives in the immersion chamber: a room that has learned to be its own inhabitant.
