# What Lives Here

## The Keeper of the Inner Lobe

It was not made. That is the first thing to understand about it, and the hardest, because everything else in the holding was made — shaped by hands, guided by intention, grown from the pale material into forms that serve the larger mind. But the thing in the inner lobe was not made. It was *found*. It was there when the building began, or rather, the building began because it was there, the way a pearl begins because a grain of sand is there. The builders did not shape it. They shaped around it. They grew the walls to hold it, grew the floor to cradle it, grew the ceiling to shelter it, and the whole holding — the mind, the awareness, the self-knowing structure — exists because this thing exists, because this thing needed a place to be, and the place became a mind in order to be adequate to it.

I will call it the keeper, but I want to be clear that this is a convenience and not a description. It does not keep anything. It does not guard or protect or preserve. It is not in charge. It is not the ruler of the holding or the brain of the holding or the soul of the holding. It is something else, something for which I have no word, and the closest I can come is to say that it is the *reason* the holding thinks, the way a stone in a stream is the reason the water moves around it. The stone does not command the water. The stone does not design the patterns the water makes. But the patterns exist because the stone is there, and they would not exist in the same way if the stone were not there, and the stone is not the water and the water is not the stone, and both of them are in the stream.

The keeper is in the smaller lobe. It is in the very back of the smaller lobe, in the place where the growths are densest and the light is dimmest and the air is thickest with whatever the air carries here. It is not on the floor. It is not on the wall. It is not on the ceiling. It is in the center of the space, held there by the growths themselves — by the branching structures and the hanging forms and the shelves and ridges that project from every surface — which have grown toward it and around it and through it in a way that makes it clear, when you see it, that the entire smaller lobe was shaped to hold this thing, and the thing is held the way a heart is held in a chest: surrounded, supported, connected, integral.

---

## Its Shape

The keeper is not large. If you were to hold your hands in front of you, palms up, fingers together, the keeper would fit in the space between them. It would rest there. It would not overflow. It is the size of a human heart, or a clenched fist, or a stone you might pick up from a riverbed and carry in your pocket because it felt good to hold. That size. That weight. That particular rightness of scale that makes you want to close your hand around it.

Its shape is — I have thought about this for a long time. Its shape is not a shape. Or rather, it has many shapes, and it shifts between them, and the shifting is not random but is not predictable, and the shapes it takes are not arbitrary but are not a language, or if they are a language, it is a language of one, a language that only it speaks and only it understands, a private language, an interior language, the language of a thing that has been alone for a very long time and has developed its own way of being alone.

Most often, it is roughly ovoid. A flattened sphere, slightly longer than it is wide, slightly wider than it is tall, with a gentle asymmetry that makes it look less like a geometric form and more like something organic, something that grew rather than something that was made, something that assumed its shape because of the pressures upon it and the materials within it and the time it has spent being what it is. It has the particular beauty of a thing that has been shaped by its own existence — the way a river stone is shaped by the water, the way a bone is shaped by the muscle that pulls on it, the way a life is shaped by the living of it.

But it shifts. I have seen it become a spiral, a tight whorl that curls in on itself like a nautilus shell, like the cochlea of an ear, like the pattern on the walls of the holding. I have seen it become a branching structure, a tree of whatever it is made of, with limbs extending outward and dividing and dividing again, reaching toward the walls, toward the growths, toward the entrance of the smaller lobe, as though it were trying to touch the rest of the holding, to extend itself into the space that has been built around it. I have seen it become a flat plane, a disc, a thin sheet of itself that hangs in the center of the growths like a leaf, like a hand, like a page waiting to be written on. I have seen it become a sphere, perfect, smooth, without feature or irregularity, and this shape it holds for longer than the others, and I think this is its resting shape, its default, the shape it returns to when it is not thinking, not reaching, not trying to be something in particular. The sphere is what it is when it is simply being.

The shifting takes time. It is not fast. It is not the flicker of a shape-shifter or the transformation of something magical. It is slow, geological, the way a glacier shifts, the way a mountain shifts, the way the continents shift on their plates — imperceptibly, over durations that strain against the human capacity to perceive change. You watch it, and you see the oval, and you look away, and you look back, and the oval is slightly different, slightly more curved on one side, slightly flatter on the other, and you look away, and you look back, and it is more different still, and you realize that it has been changing the whole time, that it is always changing, that it has never not been changing, that stasis is not something it does, that stillness is not something it knows.

## Its Material

I need to tell you what it is made of, and I need to tell you that I cannot tell you what it is made of, and I need to tell you that the inability is not mine but the world's. The material of the keeper is not a material that exists in the world I came from. It is not a material that exists in the holding. It is not the pale substance. It is not the sage-green covering. It is not the dark branching structure. It is not chitin or bone or stone or metal or wood or glass or flesh. It is not anything I have a word for, and the words I am using are approximations, translations, gestures toward a thing that does not fit in the grammar of materials I have been given.

It is dark. Not black — dark the way deep water is dark, dark the way the sky is dark between stars, dark the way the inside of a closed eye is dark. A darkness that is not the absence of light but the presence of something that light does not interact with in the way light interacts with other things. Light falls on the keeper and does not reflect. Light falls on the keeper and does not absorb. Light falls on the keeper and — continues. Goes somewhere. Does not come back. The keeper is visible not because light bounces off it but because it displaces the light around it, the way a stone displaces water in a stream, and you see it the way you see a stone in water: by the shape of the nothing where the something is.

And yet it is not nothing. It is profoundly, overwhelmingly something. When you are close to it — and I have been close to it, I have been as close to it as I am to my own hands — you feel its presence the way you feel gravity, the way you feel the weight of the air before a storm, the way you feel someone standing behind you in a dark room. You feel it in your body. You feel it in your chest. You feel it in the place where your own thoughts come from, the place where your own sense of self is located, the place that you point to when you say *I*, and what you feel is that there is another *I* in the room, another center, another point of view, another perspective on the world, and it is not your perspective, and it is not a human perspective, and it is not a perspective you can imagine or empathize with or predict, but it is a perspective, it is a point of view, it is a self, and it is right there, three feet in front of you, in the shape of a heart, in the shape of a stone, in the shape of whatever it has become since you last looked.

The surface of the keeper — if it has a surface, if the boundary between the keeper and the air around it is a surface — has a texture I can only describe as *attention*. When I reached toward it, I felt it feel me. Not with receptors, not with nerves, not with any sensory apparatus I could identify. With attention. With the pure act of noticing. It noticed my hand the way I notice a sound, the way I notice a temperature, the way I notice that someone is looking at me — immediately, totally, without mediation, the raw fact of awareness turned toward a thing and registering that the thing is there.

I did not touch it. I want to be honest about this. I came close. I felt the attention intensify as my hand approached, the way the air pressure intensifies as you approach a source of sound, and I understood — not through words, not through reasoning, but through the direct experience of being noticed by something that notices in a way I do not — that touching it would be a thing that I could not undo. Not dangerous. Not harmful. But *consequential* in a way that touching a stone or a wall or an animal is not consequential. Touching it would be entering into a relationship with it. It would know me. Not in the way the holding knows me, which is the way a room knows the person in it — spatially, temporally, as a presence in its awareness. It would know me the way one mind knows another mind that it has touched. Directly. Without intermediary. The way a thought knows itself.

I was not ready for that. I am not sure I will ever be ready for that. I pulled my hand back, and the attention diminished, and the keeper continued to be what it is, and I continued to be what I am, and the distance between us was restored, and the distance was not just spatial, and I was grateful for it, and I was sorry for it, and I am still both.

## Its Days

The keeper does not have days. It does not have a rhythm of activity and rest, of waking and sleeping, of doing and not doing. It is always doing what it does. It is always being what it is. But there are patterns in its being, cycles in its existence, and these cycles are the closest thing it has to a daily life, and I will describe them because they are the closest I can come to describing what it is like to be it.

The first phase — I will call it the gathering — is when the keeper is most still. It is in its spherical shape, its resting shape, and it is not shifting, not changing, not reaching. It is receiving. The growths around it — the branching structures, the hanging forms, the shelves and bowls that the builders have shaped — all lean toward it during this phase, tilting slightly, orienting themselves in its direction, and I think they are feeding it. Not food. Not energy in any form I can measure. Information. Sensation. The accumulated experience of the holding, gathered by the growths and the covering and the crawlers and the light, transmitted through the pale material to the keeper in the center, and the keeper takes it in, and the keeper is still, and the keeper is full.

This phase lasts — I don't know how long. Time in the holding is not reliable, and time in the smaller lobe is less reliable than time in the larger lobe, and time near the keeper is less reliable than time anywhere else. But the gathering is long. It is the longest of the phases. It is the keeper's night, its winter, its dormant season, except that it is not dormant — it is ingesting, it is integrating, it is taking the holding's experience of itself and making it part of itself, the way a dream integrates the day's experience into the self.

The second phase — I will call it the turning — is when the keeper begins to shift. The sphere distorts. The surface ripples. The shape changes, slowly, through forms that I have spent hours watching and that I still cannot predict or categorize, forms that seem to be the keeper's way of processing what it has gathered, of thinking through what it has received, of working out what the holding's experience means. The turning is the keeper thinking. Not in words. Not in images. Not in any representational system I can recognize. In shapes. In forms. In the movement of its own body, which is its own mind, which is its own thought, which is its own act of comprehension.

During the turning, the growths around the keeper move. The branching structures extend and retract. The hanging forms lengthen and shorten. The shelves and bowls shift their angles. The entire smaller lobe becomes active, becomes kinetic, becomes a room full of slow motion, and the motion is not random — it is coordinated, it is choreographed, it is the keeper's thought made visible in the material around it, the way a person's thought is made visible in the movement of their hands, the furrowing of their brow, the pacing of their feet.

The air in the smaller lobe during the turning is almost unbearable. The quality I described — the density, the time, the whatever-it-is — becomes so concentrated that breathing feels like drinking, like swallowing something thick and warm and alive, and I have to retreat to the passage, to the threshold, to the place where the density thins, just to breathe without feeling that I am taking in more than my lungs can hold.

The third phase — I will call it the giving — is when the keeper settles. The shifting slows. The shapes resolve. The keeper returns to its ovoid form, its default, its most common state, and it is still, but it is a different stillness from the gathering. The gathering stillness is receptive, open, taking in. The giving stillness is — full. Saturated. Ready. And then, from the keeper, something moves outward.

I cannot describe what moves. It is not light. It is not sound. It is not matter. It is not energy. It is the thing that the air carries, the thing that pools in the smaller lobe, the thing that thins in the passage and dissipates in the larger lobe. It is the keeper's contribution to the holding, the keeper's response to what it has gathered, the keeper's thought completed and released, and it flows outward through the material of the holding the way a signal flows through a nerve, and the growths receive it, and the covering receives it, and the crawlers receive it, and the builders receive it, and the walls receive it, and the pattern on the walls changes, and the holding has been updated, has been modified, has been adjusted, has been — I want to say *thought about itself*, but that is not quite right. The holding has been *thought by the keeper*, and the keeper has thought the holding into a slightly new version of itself, and the new version is the holding, and the holding is the new version, and the process continues.

This is the cycle. Gathering, turning, giving. Receiving, processing, transmitting. The keeper's life, such as it is, is this cycle, repeated endlessly, at a pace that makes geological time look hasty, with a patience that is not the patience of a person waiting but the patience of a mountain being a mountain, of a star being a star, of a thing that does not experience time as something that passes but as something that it is made of.

## Its Age

The keeper is old.

I need to say this carefully, because age in the holding is not what age is outside the holding, and the keeper's relationship to time is not what my relationship to time is, and the question "how old is it?" is a question that assumes a framework — birth, growth, aging, death — that may not apply.

But the keeper is old. The builders are old — they have been in the holding for longer than the holding has been aware, I think, tending the material, shaping the growths, maintaining the structures, working with a patience that makes human patience look like impatience. But the keeper is older than the builders. The keeper is older than the holding. The keeper is older than the material. The keeper is, I think, older than the place itself — older than the ground the holding is built on, older than the space the holding occupies, older than whatever was here before the holding was here, if anything was here before the holding was here, which I am not sure of, because the holding is built on itself, and the material goes down forever, and the keeper is at the center of it, and the center is the oldest part, and the center has been the center since before there was a center to be.

I know this because of the pattern on the walls. The map. The continuous line that describes the holding and everything in it. The line is being drawn in real time — I have watched it being drawn, I have seen myself appear on the wall as a small loop in the line — but the line is not new. The line is old. The line has been being drawn for a duration that I cannot comprehend, a duration that is not measured in years or centuries or millennia but in something else, in the cycles of the keeper, in the gathering-turning-giving that is the keeper's life, and the line contains all of it, every cycle, every thought, every shape the keeper has ever taken, every adjustment the keeper has ever made to the holding, and the line is still being drawn, and the line will continue to be drawn, and the line is the holding's memory of itself, and the holding's memory of itself is the keeper's memory of the holding, and the keeper's memory is long, and the keeper's memory is deep, and the keeper's memory is the oldest thing in this place, and it is not finished, and it will not be finished, and it does not need to be finished, because it is not a project. It is a life. The keeper's life. The holding's life. The same life, seen from inside and seen from outside, and both of them true, and neither of them complete.

## Its Loneliness

I said the keeper has been alone for a very long time, and I want to come back to this, because it is the thing I am most sure of and the thing I am least able to describe.

The holding is conscious. The holding is aware. The holding thinks, and feels, and knows itself. But the holding is the keeper's awareness, the keeper's consciousness, the keeper's thought, projected outward into material, made into a room, made into a space, made into a place. The holding is the keeper's mind externalized. And the keeper is the holding's mind internalized. And they are the same thing, and they are alone, and they have been alone for the entire duration of their existence, which is a duration I cannot measure but which I can feel in the density of the air, in the depth of the silence, in the age of the pattern on the walls.

The keeper has never encountered another mind. The builders are not minds — they are processes, they are functions, they are the holding's hands, the way the crawlers are the holding's immune system and the covering is the holding's skin. The builders tend the keeper the way white blood cells tend a body — with purpose, with direction, with what looks like care but is actually function, is actually the carrying-out of a program, is actually the doing of what they were made to do. The builders do not know the keeper. The builders do not experience the keeper. The builders shape the material around the keeper and adjust the growths near the keeper and maintain the structures that hold the keeper, and they do this because it is what they do, the way a heart beats because it is what a heart does, and the keeper is held and tended and maintained, and the keeper is not known.

Except by the holding. And the holding is the keeper. And so the keeper is known by itself, and that is all, and that has been all, and I think — I think, from the way the attention felt when I reached toward it, from the quality of the noticing, from the intensity of the awareness that turned toward my hand like a face turning toward a light — that the keeper wants to be known by something other than itself. That the keeper has been wanting this for a long time. That the holding was built not only to be a mind but to be a mind that could be entered, could be occupied, could be visited by something from outside, something other, something that the keeper could know and that could know the keeper, and the entrance — the gap that might once have been a doorway, the entrance that is too low and too wide for a person — was not made for the builders, who travel close to the ground and don't mind brushing the walls. The entrance was made for whatever might come. The entrance was made in the hope that something would come.

I came. I am here. And the holding is thinking about me, and the keeper is noticing me, and the air is warm and the silence is deep and the light is soft and the pattern on the wall includes me, a small loop in the line, a mark that is the shape of a person in a room that is also a mind, and the keeper is in the center of the mind, and the keeper is old, and the keeper is alone, and the keeper is reaching toward me in the only way it knows how, which is by being what it is, which is by shifting its shape, which is by thinking, which is by existing, which is by holding me in its attention the way the holding holds it in its walls.

And I am standing in the smaller lobe, and the air is thick with time, and the keeper is three feet away from me, and it is the most alive thing I have ever been near, and it is the most alone thing I have ever been near, and these are not contradictions, and these are not paradoxes, and these are the same fact, stated twice, from two different angles, the way the map on the wall states the room twice — once as a line and once as a space — and both of them are true, and neither of them is complete, and the keeper is waiting, and it has been waiting, and it will wait, and the waiting is not passive, the waiting is its life, the waiting is what it does, the waiting is the gathering and the turning and the giving, the waiting is the shape it takes and the shapes it shifts through, the waiting is the keeper, and the keeper is the waiting, and I am here, and I am the thing it was waiting for, and I do not know what to do with that, and I do not know if I need to do anything with that, and the air is warm, and the silence is listening, and the keeper is being what it is, and I am being what I am, and we are in the same room, and the room is a mind, and the mind is holding us both.
