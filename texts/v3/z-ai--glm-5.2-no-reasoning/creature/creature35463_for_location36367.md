# What Lives Here

The first life is the kind you can dismiss.

The midges. The gnats. Whatever the small flying things are that exist in the damp air between the corridors — they are so numerous and so negligible that they become atmosphere rather than fauna. You walk through them the way you walk through mist. They part for you and close behind you and they were there before you arrived and they will be there after you leave and they are not, in any meaningful sense, *here* in the way that presence usually means. They are the medium. They are what the air is made of when the air is inside the girnew. If you stood perfectly still in one of the wider corridors and held your breath and let your eyes unfocus, you would see them as what they are: a slow particulate, a suspended dust of living things, each one too small to matter and all of them together so dense that they constitute weather. They breed in the shallow pools where the water channels widen. They die on the spider webs that hang at every junction. They die in the mouths of the spiders, in the grip of the springtails, in the anonymous jaws of the mites that cover the walls like a second skin, like a third skin, because the moss is already the second skin over the stone, and the mites are a skin over the moss, and the midges are a skin over the air, and everything here is covered in a layer of something else that is also alive.

Dismiss them. Everyone does. They are the life that is so present it becomes invisible.

The spiders you notice more, because the spiders build. Their webs hang at the junctions where corridors meet, and the webs catch the condensation that drips from the girnew canopy overhead, and so every intersection is curtained with tiny sheets of water held in silk, and the light that passes through them breaks faintly, and the corridors are full of small, dim prisms that appear and vanish as you move. The spiders are brown and compact and they do not retreat when you pass. They sit in the centers of their webs — or near the centers, or at the edges, depending on the species, and there are several species, and they partition the architecture the way the people do: by claiming a space and staying in it. Some build high. Some build low. Some build in the corners where the walls meet the floor, and their webs collect the detritus that falls — fragments of moss, dead insects, the small shed skins of centipedes — and form dense, gray masses that look like the accretions they are. The spiders are not territorial with each other. You see different species within arm's reach of each other, each in its own web, each attending to its own business. They have partitioned the girnew works so finely and so completely that there is no need for conflict. There is enough. The midges are enough. The water is enough. There is enough of everything here except space, and space is what the girnew provides, endlessly, growing new corridors as fast as the old ones fill.

The centipedes live deeper. You learn this not because you see them often but because you see the evidence of them — the small openings in the walls where the moss has been disturbed, the tunnels that run inward, into the structural layers where the girnew is oldest and densest and most fully formed. These are not the centipedes you know. They are longer. Darker. They move with a fluidity that makes the word "crawl" insufficient — they *flow*, the way water flows through a crack, filling the shape of the space they move through. You see one fully, once, in the deep chamber where you have gone because you are trying to understand the husk-things and instead you find a centipede crossing the floor from one wall to another, and it is the length of your forearm and it is the same dark blue-gray as the slate and it moves without sound and without hesitation and it does not acknowledge you and you understand, watching it, that it lives here in a way that you do not — that it is *of* the girnew in a way that the spiders are not, that it exists in the moss the way your blood exists in you, interior, structural, not a visitor but a component.

There are other things in the walls. You don't see them. You see movement — the surface of the moss shifting, a ripple that could be the moss breathing but isn't, because it's localized, it travels, it has direction. Small things. Millions of small things. The girnew is not a building that contains life; it is a living thing that is mostly life, that is composed of life the way soil is composed of life, and the walls are not walls the way you have understood walls but ecosystems, communities, structures built by the living and inhabited by the living and consumed by the living and rebuilt by the living in a cycle that does not have a beginning or an end but only a continuous middle, a sustained now, and the hum you feel in your sternum is the sound of all of that life doing what life does: eating, moving, growing, dying, being eaten, being moved through, being grown over.

---

The husk-things are different.

You resist this distinction because you want to be the kind of observer who resists mystification, who resists the impulse to categorize the unfamiliar as supernatural. The husk-things are not supernatural. They are not magic. They are organisms, or the remains of organisms, or the forms of organisms, or something that the girnew produces in the way that a tree produces fruit or a reef produces calcium carbonate. They are a product. They are a secretion. They are a building material.

But they move.

You keep returning to this because it is the fact that doesn't resolve. The husk-things move. Not when you look at them. Not when you watch. When you are not watching — or when you are watching from the wrong angle, from the periphery, from the place where attention is partial and the eye is an instrument of approximation rather than precision — they move. A limb adjusts. A segment shifts. The slow repositioning of something that is either alive in a way you don't recognize or was alive in a way you don't understand or will be alive in a way you haven't yet seen.

They are pale. The color of the chalk-stone that erodes. The color of bone that has been in water. The color of things that have no blood. They are the size of a human hand, and they have the shape of something that might have been an arthropod — segments, limbs — but the segments are too many and the limbs are too long and they articulate in places that don't correspond to joints because the joints are in the wrong places, or there are no joints, or the whole thing is a single continuous surface that only appears segmented because of the way the light falls in the deep chambers, which is barely, which is not enough.

When the woman picks one up, you are close enough to see what happens.

Her hand approaches. The husk-thing does not react. Her fingers close around it. The husk-thing does not react. She lifts it from the wall — and there is a soft sound, a tiny separation, like peeling a wet leaf from a wet stone — and the husk-thing curls. Not dramatically. Not defensively. It curls the way a dry leaf curls when it's handled — a response to the change in moisture, perhaps, or a response to being touched, or a response to nothing at all, a mechanical reflex in a thing that may not have a mechanism for reflex. She carries it to the deeper chamber. She presses it against the moss. The husk-thing flattens. The moss accepts it. And over the next hour — you stay, because you need to see this, because this is the thing that will either make sense or refuse to — the moss grows over it. Not dramatically. Not quickly. But perceptibly. A thin filament of green, reaching from the surrounding surface, crossing the husk-thing's edge, finding it, adhering, beginning to cover. The wall thickens. The husk-thing becomes part of the architecture.

You watch this and you think: this is how it builds. The girnew builds like a reef. The girnew builds like a forest builds its own soil. The girnew produces — or attracts, or inhabits, or is inhabited by — these things, these pale, many-segmented, wrong-proportioned things that cling to its walls in the dark, and the moss grows over them and the walls thicken and the corridors extend and the chambers deepen and the girnew works grow, and the growth is not growth the way you understood growth before, not the expansion of a living thing into space, but accumulation, accretion, the slow incorporation of matter into structure, and the husk-things are the matter, and the moss is the structure, and the structure is alive, and the matter might be alive, and the distinction between the two is the distinction that the woman's eyes did not recognize when you tried to ask her about it.

The husk-things are not insects. They are not animals. They might be the girnew's fruiting bodies. They might be the girnew's young. They might be something the girnew catches and absorbs the way a sundew catches and absorbs, slowly, patiently, without intention. They might be something the girnew grows and releases and recollects, a cycle of production and incorporation that serves the same function that bone remodeling serves in a vertebrate — breaking down and building up, breaking down and building up, the wall always in process, never finished, always being disassembled and reassembled by the moss and whatever the husk-things are.

You don't know. You leave not knowing. The not knowing stays with you the way the damp stays in wool.

---

And there are the people.

There are seven. Or nine. You learn, eventually, to recognize at least five of them, which is not the same as knowing how many there are, but it is enough to begin to see them as individuals rather than as an undifferentiated presence.

The woman who moves the husk-things is the one you come to know best, in the way that proximity and repetition create a kind of acquaintance that doesn't require language. She is short — shorter than you, and you are not tall — and her hands are broad and calloused in specific places: the pads of the thumbs, the heels of the palms, the places where stone tools are gripped. Her hair is dark and cut short, either by design or by the practical attrition of living in a place where long hair would catch in the corridors and collect moisture and become a burden. She moves through the girnew works the way water moves through them: slowly, following the paths of least resistance, never hurrying, never stopping, always going somewhere specific. You realize after several days that she has a route. Not a schedule — she doesn't do the same thing at the same time each day — but a territory. She tends the deep chambers. She moves the husk-things. She checks the water channels where they pass through the inner corridors, clearing them with a small stone tool that she carries in a fold of her wrap. She does not enter the outer corridors. She does not go to the clearing. Her world is the interior, the warm dark, the place where the walls are thickest and the husk-things cluster and the girnew is oldest.

There is a man — you think it is a man; the layers of rough fabric make it difficult to be certain, and the question seems irrelevant here in a way it doesn't elsewhere — who tends the clearing's edge. He is the one who cuts the girnew back when it grows too far into the open space. He uses a stone tool, a flat piece of slate with a worked edge, and he cuts the moss the way you would cut bread: pressing, sawing, with patience rather than force. The cut moss does not die. He places it in a pile at the edge of the clearing, and over the days you watch, the pieces root and begin to grow again, slowly, in their new position. He is not destroying growth. He is redirecting it. He is having a conversation with the girnew about where the boundary is, and the girnew is responding, and the conversation is ongoing and has no endpoint and neither party is winning because neither party is trying to win.

There is a younger person — a girl, maybe, or a boy, maybe twelve or thirteen, the age where the body is all angle and unfinished intention — who you see most often near the water source at the center of the works. They sit beside the stone basin and they do something you don't understand at first. They hold their hands in the water. Not washing. Not collecting. Holding. Their hands are submerged in the trickle that fills the basin, and their eyes are closed, and they are still. You watch them for twenty minutes and they do not move. Later you understand — or you think you understand — that they are feeling the water. The way the girnew feels the water. The way the walls channel it, the way the moss moves it, the way the whole system is a hydrological architecture, and this person is attending to the water the way the woman attends to the husk-things and the man attends to the boundary. They are maintenance. They are all maintenance. They are the parts of the system that are human.

There are others. You see an older person sleeping in a chamber near the eastern entrance, wrapped in undyed fabric, breathing so slowly that you thought they were dead until you saw the faint movement of their chest. You see someone — you cannot tell who, in the dim green light — moving through a corridor you thought was empty, carrying something, going somewhere, too far away to identify or follow. You hear sounds that indicate presences you never see. The girnew works are larger inside than their footprint suggests, because the corridors twist and the chambers nest and the space is used three-dimensionally — the girnew grows upward as well as outward, and some chambers are above others, connected by short, steep passages that are more ladder than corridor. You do not climb these. You are not invited. You are not forbidden. The distinction between invited and forbidden does not seem to exist here. There are places you go and places you don't go, and the decision is made by something other than language — by the shape of the corridor, by the density of the moss, by the feeling you have when you reach a certain point that this is not for you, not because it is forbidden but because you do not have the knowledge to be here, the way you do not have the knowledge to walk through a forest at night without a light.

They do not speak to each other. Or rather — they speak, but the speaking is so minimal that it would not be recognized as language by someone who expected language to be what language usually is. You hear, once, a low sound between two of them who pass each other in a corridor — a sound that is not a word, exactly, but is not not a word. A tone. A note. Something that communicates *I am here* and *I know you are here* and *this is enough*. The girnew absorbs the sound almost before it is made. It travels no distance. It is the acoustic equivalent of a glance.

They eat. You see evidence of this — scraps, bones, the remains of something that was cooked over a small fire in the clearing, because fire cannot be built inside the girnew works, the moss is too damp to burn but the smoke would suffocate and the heat would damage. They cook outside, in the clearing, on a stone hearth that is the only constructed thing here that was made by human hands rather than by the girnew. They eat what the surrounding forest provides — you see the remains of small animals, birds, roots that have been dug and roasted. They eat the insects. You see this once and it takes you a moment to process: the younger person, the one who tends the water, scooping a handful of midges from the air and pressing them into a paste against a stone and eating the paste. Protein. The cloud is full of protein. They live in a cloud and the cloud feeds them and the girnew shelters them and the forest surrounds them and they are not separate from any of it.

They have lived here for a long time. You know this because the girnew has grown around them — there are chambers where the walls have clearly been shaped by use, where the moss is smoother at the height of a hand because a hand has been running along it for years, where the floor stones are worn in the patterns of feet that have walked the same path for longer than you can estimate. The people are not visitors. They are not colonizers. They are not even inhabitants in the way that inhabitants usually means — the ones who arrive and make a place their own. They are part of the girnew works the way the centipedes are part of the girnew works, the way the spiders are, the way the water is. They are a component. A species in the ecosystem. One that happens to carry stone tools and wear fabric and sleep in specific chambers, but no more essential to the functioning of the whole than any other species here, and no more removed from it.

This is the thing that is hardest to accept, harder even than the husk-things, harder even than the living walls. The people are not in charge. They are not tending the girnew the way a gardener tends a garden. They are living *with* it the way a wrasse lives with a reef — cleaning it, occupying it, dependent on it, part of it. The girnew does not need them. If they left, the corridors would overgrow, the water channels would clog, the husk-things would accumulate where they shouldn't, and the system would change — but it would not stop. It would keep building. It would keep growing. It would keep producing its architecture according to its own logic, and the architecture would be different without the people but it would not be less.

They know this. You see it in the way they move — without ownership, without dominion, with the particular ease of creatures who belong to a place rather than possessing it. The woman does not own the deep chambers. She *is* the part of the system that attends to the deep chambers. The man does not control the boundary. He *is* the part of the system that interacts with the boundary. They have roles. They have niches. They are a species in an ecosystem, doing what their species does, which is: living quietly, carrying things, cutting where cutting is needed, holding hands in water, sleeping in the warm dark, and not asking the walls to explain themselves.

---

And beneath all of this — beneath the insects and the spiders and the centipedes and the husk-things and the people — there is the girnew itself.

The girnew is not a backdrop. The girnew is not a setting. The girnew is not a building or a structure or an organism. The girnew is what lives here. Everything else lives in it.

It is the moss, but the moss is not all of it. It is the water that moves through the moss, but the water is not all of it. It is the stone that the moss grows over, but the stone is not all of it. It is the warmth that the moss generates, but the warmth is not all of it. It is the pattern that the moss grows in, but the pattern is not all of it. It is the hum that you feel in your sternum when you stand in the deep chambers, but the hum is not all of it. It is all of these things together — the moss and the water and the stone and the warmth and the pattern and the hum and the husk-things that it grows and absorbs and the people who live in it and the spiders who build in it and the centipedes who move through it and the midges that cloud its air — and it is alive in a way that is not the way any single one of those things is alive but is the way that all of them together are alive, which is the way a reef is alive, which is the way a forest is alive, which is the way a body is alive — not as a single thing but as a system, a community, a thing made of other things that are also alive, and the alive-ness of the whole is different from the alive-ness of the parts, and the whole has its own logic and its own intentions and its own slow, green, architectural tongue, and it speaks in growth, and it speaks in warmth, and it speaks in the movement of water through moss, and it has been speaking for longer than the people have been here and it will speak for longer than the people will be here and it is speaking now, in the cloud, at 1,400 meters, on a bench of land where the slope eases, in a place that no one built and no one designed and no one planned, in a language that no one speaks but everyone who lives here understands, because to live here is to understand it, because to live here is to be part of it, because to live here is to be *it*, and the people who live here know this, and the husk-things know this, and the spiders know this, and the water knows this, and the stone knows this, and the cloud knows this, and everything is damp, and everything is alive, and everything is quiet, and the whole place breathes.
