# What Lives in the Weeping Ward

---

## The People

There are perhaps sixty of them. Fifteen you can see at any given moment. The rest are inside, or at the western edge setting nets in the standing water, or asleep in the dark behind woven walls where the air is no different from the air outside except that it does not move.

They are not tall. This is not a judgment — it is an observation that arrives through comparison with the structures they have built. The doorways of the shelters reach the height of a man's shoulder. A woman standing in a doorway fills it. Either the doorways are small or the people are, and after a time spent watching them move through the ward, stepping over the threshold-sills where the woven walls meet the woven ground, ducking beneath the crossbeams that brace the shrine poles, you understand that both things are true. The doorways are small because the people are small because the resources that build both bodies and walls are scarce and must be divided between purposes. Nothing here is wasted on height.

Their skin is the color of the substrate where it has been freshly exposed — a pale grey-brown that darkens in the wet air, that takes on the iron-red flush of the mineral deposits when they have been working the eastern ridge. Their hands are stained. Not with dirt but with the same reddish paste they grind in the stone-lined pit, worked into the grain of their skin, into the cracks around their knuckles, under their nails. You see this on every hand you can see. The children's hands are stained too. It is not decoration. It is not ritual. It is simply what happens to hands that work in this place. The earth marks them.

They wear the same material as everything else. Fiber, twisted into cord, woven into sheeting, wrapped and draped and tied. The cloaks they wear are not tailored — they are rectangles of woven matting, finer than the wall-matting, with a denser weave that holds together when folded but that does not, cannot, keep out the wet. The cloaks are wet. They are always wet. You see a woman lift the hem of hers to wipe her face and the gesture is ordinary and futile and she knows it is futile and she does it anyway, because the water on her face is not the same as the water on the cloak, and the distinction matters to her even if it does not matter to the moisture content of her skin.

Beneath the cloaks, more fiber. Layers of it, wrapped close to the body, tighter than the cloaks, following the shape of limbs and torso in a way that suggests these garments are not removed often. They are part of the person in the way that the walls are part of the shelters — structurally, not decoratively. You do not see anyone changing clothing. You do not see clothing drying on racks. The racks hold fish. The clothing stays on the body and stays wet and dries, if it dries, against the warmth of the body that wears it, and the body that wears it is accustomed to this and has been accustomed to this for however long the ward has existed, which is to say for the entirety of remembered life.

There are not many elders. You see one man — perhaps old, perhaps simply worn — sitting in the meeting shelter with the collapsed roof, which is to say he is sitting in a partial shelter, the collapsed section open to the sky, the intact section covering him from the shoulders up. His hands are in his lap. They are stained. He is watching the grinding at the stone-lined pit with an expression that is not blank but that you cannot read. The skin of his face is creased in ways that follow the structure of the skull beneath it, deep lines running from cheekbone to jaw, from brow to temple, as if the wet has eroded the flesh the way it erodes the substrate. He is not idle. You understand this after watching him for a few minutes. He is doing the work of sitting. He is being the center around which the others move, the way the shrine poles are the center around which the shelters are built. He does not need to stand to be structural.

The children are not quite like the children you have seen in other places. They are not loud. They do not run. They move through the ward with a precision that is not cautious — they are not afraid of falling, not hesitant — but that is economical. Every step is placed. Every movement serves a purpose that is visible: carrying a bundle of fiber to the walkway repair, fetching a basket of water from beneath an eave, checking a net at the western edge. The two children repairing the walkway work in a rhythm that belongs to adults — the older one holds, the younger one lashes, and they switch tasks without speaking, without signal, because the task itself signals when it is time to switch. The bundle is secured. The cord runs out. The hands move to the next position. They have done this before. They will do this again. They are perhaps eight and ten. They do not look at you.

A third child — smaller, perhaps five, perhaps younger — sits in the lee of a shrine pole, knees drawn up, doing something with a length of cord that you cannot see clearly. Knotting. Unknotting. Practicing. The hands move and the cord loops and the face is focused and the humming that you heard earlier is coming from this child, or from the wind in the pole above the child, and the distinction does not resolve itself and you let it go.

## What They Eat

Fish. This is evident from the drying racks, from the bone hooks, from the nets, from the shrine poles strung with fish skeletons. But the word "fish" is insufficient. The things that live in the standing water at the western edge are not quite like the fish you know. They are flat. They are pale. They are slow. The woman at the drying rack turns a strip of one and it is translucent at the edges, opaque in the center, and the grain of the flesh runs in a direction that does not match the grain of any fish you have seen. The strips are cut lengthwise, not across the body, and they are long — a single strip from a single fish might reach the full length of the drying rack, which is perhaps two meters. Whatever swims in the standing water is not small.

But it is not vigorous. The nets are not set with tension. They are laid in the shallows, weighted at intervals with stones, and the fish — if that is the word — swim into them and do not swim out, not because they are caught but because they do not move quickly enough or with enough direction to find the way clear. You watch a man at the western edge check a net and pull from it three of the flat pale bodies, each perhaps half a meter long, each moving with a languor that matches the water itself. They do not thrash. They do not gasp. They flex once, slowly, and then are still in the basket, and the man carries them to the rack without hurry because there is no need for hurry. The prey does not escape. The prey barely resists.

There is nothing else to eat that you can see. No gardens. No domesticated animals. No stores of grain or root or seed. The fish and whatever the reddish paste contributes — minerals, perhaps, iron, sulfur, something the body needs that the fish alone do not provide. The children's teeth, when one of them opens its mouth to hum, are white and close-set and whole. Whatever they are eating, it is enough. It is not more than enough.

The water they drink is the water that surrounds them. It sits in baskets beneath the eaves. It beads on the ground. It collects in the stone-lined pit where the paste is mixed. It is not clean. It is not clear. It has the faint iron smell of the substrate and the faint living smell of whatever grows in it, and they drink it without ceremony, dipping a smaller basket into a larger one, raising it, swallowing. You do not see anyone boil water. There is no fire. You look for fire and you do not find one — no hearth, no ash, no char, no smoke-staining on the shelters' interiors. The fish are not cooked. They are dried, or attempted to be dried, and eaten raw when the drying fails, which it must fail, in this air, often.

No fire. This is the third thing to learn. The ward does not burn. The ward does not cook. The ward eats what it catches and drinks what it stands in and is warm because the bodies of its inhabitants are warm and because the wet air holds whatever heat it holds and because there is nothing here that burns.

## What They Believe

The shrine poles tell you, but not in a language you can translate.

Four poles. Four is the number. You do not know if the number is significant or if four is simply how many poles they could cut and raise and brace before the work became too much. The bones wrapped into the fiber cord are the bones of the fish they eat. Nothing else. Not the bones of animals, not the bones of people, not objects of stone or shell or wood carved into figures. The bones of their food, offered to the only vertical things they have built that are taller than themselves.

This is not worship in any shape you recognize. There are no faces on the poles. There are no representations. There is no indication that the poles house spirits or refer to deities or stand between the living and the dead. They are poles wrapped in cord with bones tucked into the wrapping, and they cannot stand on their own, and they are the center of the settlement, and the settlement holds them up.

Perhaps the belief is this: that the sacred is the thing that requires you. That what you honor is not what holds you up but what you hold up. The poles do not support the shelters. The shelters support the poles. The bones do not feed the people. The people feed the bones to the poles. The direction of dependence runs from the people to the sacred, not from the sacred to the people, and if this is belief then it is a belief of maintenance, of constant attention, of the understanding that what is holy will fall if you stop holding it.

The reddish paste. You watch the grinding and you watch the mixing and you still do not know what it is for. But you notice, on a second circuit of the ward, that the foundations of the shrine poles are ringed with it — a thick circle of the paste applied to the substrate where the poles meet the ground, as if to seal something, to preserve something, to prevent the wet from rising into the wood. Or to mark the place where the sacred meets the worldly. Or both. Or neither. The paste is the paste. It is what they make. They make it every day. The man at the ridge breaks the substrate and carries it and the grinder grinds it and the mixer mixes it and it goes somewhere and that somewhere includes the bases of the shrine poles and perhaps other places you cannot see.

The old man in the collapsed shelter is watching the grinding. He is watching it the way the children watch the walkway repair — with the attention of someone who has done it and will not do it again and is making sure it is done. He is not instructing. He is not correcting. He is present, and his presence is the instruction. When the grinder pauses, looks up, looks at the old man, the old man does not nod. He does not speak. He simply continues to be there, and the grinder returns to the work.

This is the shape of authority in the ward: the authority of continued presence, of the person who has been here longest and who will stop being here at some point and who transmits the knowledge of how to be here not through words but through the fact of being here, of surviving here, of having survived. When the old man dies — and he will die, and they know this, and he knows this — the knowledge will not be lost because it is not in him. It is in the ground. It is in the work. It is in the wet and the fiber and the fish and the poles. He is not the source. He is the witness.

## How They Are With Each Other

They speak. You hear it now — not often, not loud, but in the narrow spaces between shelters, a low exchange of syllables that you cannot parse. The language is not one you know. It is not one you can guess at. It has the consonant density of something spoken in wet air, where open vowels would be swallowed by the fog, where the sounds that carry are the ones that start and stop sharply, that cut through the damp. You hear a word that might be a name. You hear a word that might be an instruction. You hear laughter — once, from inside a shelter, brief and genuine and unaccompanied by any event you can identify.

They touch each other in passing. Not deliberately — the spaces are too narrow for deliberate touch. But shoulders brush in the seams between buildings, and hands rest briefly on backs, on arms, and the contact is not commented on and not avoided. It is the natural consequence of living in a place where the distance between any two people is less than the distance of an arm's reach. They are always within touch. They have always been within touch. The concept of personal space as a sphere of exclusion does not exist here because the ward does not permit it.

The children sleep with the adults. You cannot tell which children belong to which adults, and the question may not be meaningful. The shelters are small — two meters by three, perhaps, the largest ones — and they hold whoever fits. At night, presumably, the bodies arrange themselves on the woven ground, against the woven walls, under the woven roofs, and the warmth they generate is shared by proximity, and the wet they carry is shared by contact, and the sleep they find is the sleep of people who have worked until they could not work and then stopped where they were.

You do not see conflict. This is not the same as saying there is no conflict. You see what is visible in the hours you are present, and what is visible is cooperation so total that it looks like instinct. The man at the ridge does not need to be told to break substrate. The woman at the rack does not need to be told to turn the fish. The children do not need to be told to repair the walkway. The work is apparent. The work announces itself. When the walkway sinks, someone repairs it. When the fish are caught, someone turns them. When the paste runs low, someone grinds. This is not organization. It is response. The ward is a system of responses to its own ongoing dissolution.

## What They Are

They are not desperate. This is the thing that surprises you, that you did not expect. The ward is small. The ward is wet. The ward is new and already decaying. The ground moves beneath it. The water rises around it. The fish are caught slowly and dried poorly and eaten raw. The shrine poles would fall without the shelters and the shelters would fall without the maintenance and the maintenance would fail without the people and the people are perhaps sixty in a place that holds perhaps eighty and the fog does not lift.

But they are not desperate. The woman at the drying rack turns the fish with the patience of someone who has accepted that the fish may not dry and that the turning is the task and the task is the life. The children repair the walkway with the competence of children who have been given real work and who have risen to it. The old man sits in his collapsed shelter and watches the grinding and his face is not a face of despair. It is a face of attention. He is paying attention to the world that holds him, and the world is paying attention back, and the exchange between them is the thing that the shrine poles represent — a dependence that runs both ways, a sacred that needs the worldly and a worldly that needs the sacred, and neither can stand alone.

They are building a life in a place that is not yet a place. They are making ground from matting and shelter from fiber and sacred from bones and meaning from repetition, and the meaning is not grand and the shelter is not dry and the ground is not solid and the sacred is not transcendent, but it is, all of it, *there*, present, held, maintained. The ward is not a refuge. It is not a home. It is not a settlement in the sense of a thing that was founded and will endure. It is an act. It is a continuous act. It is sixty people performing the act of being somewhere, and the somewhere is this wet small edge of nothing, and the act is the same as the living and the living is the same as the building and the building is the same as the praying and none of it would exist if they stopped.

They will not stop. This is what you understand, finally, standing at the boundary where the mat becomes water and the fog becomes everything. They will not stop because stopping is not a thing that occurs to them. The ward does not present the option of stopping. The fish need turning. The walkway needs repair. The paste needs grinding. The poles need bracing. The ground needs insisting upon. And the people — the small, pale, iron-handed, wet-cloaked, fish-eating, fiber-wrapping, bone-offering people of the Weeping Ward — will do these things because these are the things that exist to be done, and they are the people who are here to do them, and that is the whole of it.

The child at the shrine pole is still humming. The cord loops and loops. The fog does not lift. The ground moves beneath your feet, and you step back to the firm path, and the ward continues without you, as it did before you arrived, as it will after you leave, weeping from every surface, holding up its own sacred, eating what it catches, making what it needs, being what it is.
