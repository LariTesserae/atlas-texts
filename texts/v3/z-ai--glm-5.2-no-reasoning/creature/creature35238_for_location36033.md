# What Lives Here

## I. The Keepers

They are not small because they are diminished. They are small because smallness is efficient at this altitude, in this humidity, inside these walls. A body is a cost — surface area, caloric demand, the volume of water required to keep a metabolism honest — and they have been paying that cost for long enough to have optimized it. Their height averages perhaps a hundred and twenty centimeters, though height is the wrong framework. They are dense. Walk past one and you register the weight of it, the way you register the weight of a stone or a piece of ore. They do not float through the town. They *occupy* their path through it.

Their skin is dark in the way that certain barks are dark: not pigmented but *compacted*, the outermost layers thickened and lignified, almost woody, knitted close against the underlying tissue. When one passes at a distance of five meters, you might mistake the texture for leather. At two meters, the error corrects itself. The skin has grain. Fine striations run along the limbs, following the long axis of the body, and these striations are not scars — not individually, not as a record of damage — but something closer to growth rings, a chronological record inscribed on the body by the same processes that inscribe the walls of the town. They are marked by time the way the cobblestones are marked by traffic. The oldest among them — and there are old ones, quite old, moving with the deliberate slowness of something that has learned to conserve not from infirmity but from a surplus of experience — have skin so deeply grained that it has begun to split at the joints, revealing a pale, dense heartwood beneath. This does not appear to trouble them. They split and continue.

They are not naked. This is important to understand, because the absence of visible clothing reads as nakedness to a visitor, and nakedness implies a deficit, a removal, a natural state from which something has been taken. They are not in a natural state. They are in a state so integrated with the plant mass that the boundary between body and covering has dissolved into a gradient. What covers them grows from them. What grows from them communicates with what grows around them. A Keeper walking through the main thoroughfare trails filaments from the shoulders and the backs of the knees — thin, almost invisible, catching the green-gold light like spider silk — and these filaments touch the walls as they pass, and the walls touch back, and something is exchanged. Not food. Not liquid. Something closer to information, though that word is also wrong. Something closer to the way a mycorrhizal network passes chemical signals between trees, except that the trees are also the town and the town is also the body and the body is also the signal.

## II. The Relationship

Here is what took the longest to understand: they did not build the town.

This is hard to hold onto, because the town is so clearly *built* — the cobblestones, the mortar-moss, the buildings with their doors and windows, the rope-and-pulley systems guiding branch growth along desired paths. Someone designed this. Someone maintained it. Someone decided where the thoroughfare would run and how wide it would be and which buildings would be two stories and which would be three. The evidence of intention is overwhelming.

But the Keepers did not build it the way a human builds a house. They did not stand apart from the material and shape it. They grew it the way a body grows a skeleton — not by deciding where the bones go but by being the kind of thing that has bones, by living in such intimacy with the mineral and the cellular that the structure emerges from the relationship rather than from the will. They did not design the town. They *are* the town's design. The plant did not grow the town for them. They did not grow the town from the plant. The town grew the way a coral reef grows — through the accumulated life of its inhabitants, their bodies and their habits and their wastes and their deaths becoming substrate, becoming material, becoming wall and floor and road.

The Keepers who walk the streets now are not the Keepers who began this. They are descendants — if descent is the right word, and it may not be — of a lineage so old that the earliest Keepers have become geology. Deep below the current streets, below the earliest cobblestones, below the first root-floors, there are layers that were once alive. Not dead. Not fossilized in the way that implies mineral replacement of organic tissue. *Incorporated*. The earliest Keepers are part of the substrate now. Their tissue is part of the plant's root system. Their genetic material — if they have genetic material, if that framework applies — is distributed through the plant mass like ore through rock, present in every sample, traceable to the surface in concentrations too dilute to see but dense enough to function. The dead are not gone. The dead are the floor. The dead are the road. The dead are the medium in which the living root.

## III. The Work

They work. This is visible in the tools leaning against walls, in the clean cuts on the harvesting platforms, in the worn surfaces of the walkways. But the work is not work in the way that labor is work. It is not separate from life. It is not the thing you do so that you can then do the other thing that is your life. The work is metabolic.

A Keeper on a harvesting platform stands at the edge of a membrane where the plant mass thins to its production surface — ten meters across, warm, faintly vibrating with the chemosynthetic process that converts water and mineral and air into the pale fibrous material. The Keeper's hands — and they have hands, five-fingered, jointed, recognizably hands, though the nails have been replaced by something harder and more chitinous, a material that could be called horn or could be called bark — move across the membrane with the deliberate precision of someone who has done this specific motion ten thousand times and will do it ten thousand more. The fiber is stripped. It coils. It is placed in a basket that is also growing, also alive, also part of the system.

But the Keeper on the platform is not only harvesting. As the hands move, the filaments from the Keeper's shoulders and knees extend into the membrane, and the membrane responds — its production increases where the Keeper touches, the fiber growing denser and more uniform, the process accelerating. The Keeper is not operating a machine. The Keeper is *feeding* the machine, and the machine is feeding the Keeper, and the distinction is the kind of distinction that only makes sense from the outside. From inside — and there is an inside, a perspective, a point of view that is not yours — the Keeper is digesting. The membrane is digesting. The town is digesting. They are all doing the same thing at different scales.

The tools confirm this. There are blades — short, curved, sharpened on one edge, the handles worn smooth by centuries of gripping. There are hooks. There are scrapers. But there are also tools that have no human analogue: a forked implement that appears to conduct electrical current, used to stimulate growth in specific areas of the plant mass. A mesh net, living, that is draped over a surface to regulate its exposure to light and moisture. A long, flexible rod — part vine, part root, part something else — that is used to reach deep into the plant's internal structures and... adjust something. Tune something. The Keepers do not explain what they are doing because they do not experience it as explanation. They experience it as hunger, and satiation, and the thing between.

## IV. The Breathing

The plant mass breathes. You can see this from the ridge: the surface rising and falling, three seconds apart, the compression and release that makes the sound you hear. But the breathing is not for the plant alone. The Keepers breathe with it. Their chests — and they have chests, ribcages, the respiratory apparatus is recognizable — expand and contract in synchrony with the plant's rhythm. Not approximately. Not in the way that two people walking together will fall into step. Exactly. Precisely. Three seconds apart, compression and release, the Keepers and the plant and the town all breathing together, a single respiratory system operating at multiple scales.

This is not reflex. This is not mimicry. This is the same process, operating through different substrates. The plant's breathing is driven by the churning you hear — the mechanical processing of water and mineral and air in the tanks and the root-pipes and the membrane-platforms. The Keeper's breathing is driven by the same process, because the Keepers are connected to the same process, because the process is the process, and the bodies it operates through are expressions of it, not separate from it.

When a Keeper leaves the town — and they do leave, rarely, climbing the trail through the tunnel to the ridge and beyond, perhaps to the villages below — the breathing desynchronizes. Slowly. Over the course of hours, the Keeper's rhythm drifts from the plant's rhythm, and the Keeper becomes... smaller. Not physically. Something is subtracted. The presence diminishes. The weight of them, that quality of *occupation*, thins. A Keeper on the ridge, outside the town, outside the fog, in the thin air at twenty-five hundred meters, is a diminished thing. Not dying. Not suffering, exactly. But reduced. A fish out of water, if the water were a relationship and the fish were a body that had grown to need that relationship the way it needs oxygen.

They return. They always return. The trail brings them back.

## V. The Long Ones

There are Keepers who do not walk the streets.

You find them deeper in, past the industrial infrastructure, past the tanks and the harvesting platforms, where the plant mass meets the granite and the town becomes something else — not rooms and streets but caves, living caves, the walls lined with root material and heartwood, the air thick with moisture and the smell of green things decomposing and green things growing and the air itself green and alive. Here the Keepers are still. They stand against the walls, or they appear to stand — it is difficult to tell, because their skin has become the wall, their grain continuous with the wall's grain, their filaments not trailing but fused, sunk into the substrate, part of it.

They are not dead. They are not dormant. They are doing something that the walking Keepers do not do, or have not yet done, or will do when the walking becomes too expensive and the relationship deepens past the point where legs are useful. They are thinking. Or processing. Or *being* — in the most fundamental sense, the sense in which being is not an action but a state, a continuous presence, a kind of meditation so deep and so long that the meditator and the meditation become the same thing.

Their skin is darker than the walking Keepers'. Darker and deeper, the grain so pronounced that it has become topology — ridges and valleys, a landscape in miniature, the record of a life so long that the body has begun to erode and to build simultaneously, wearing down and growing up, the outer layers splitting to reveal heartwood and the heartwood splitting to reveal something deeper still. If you could see into them — and the plant can, and does, and the town is the seeing — you would find layer upon layer, growth ring upon growth ring, each one a year or a century or an epoch, each one a period of standing and breathing and being part of this place until the place and the person are indistinguishable.

These are the ones who remember. Not in the way that a brain remembers — not as images, not as narrative, not as the sequence of events that happened to a body moving through time. They remember the way stone remembers pressure, the way a riverbed remembers the shape of the water. The town's geometry — the non-Euclidean streets that work while you walk them and dissolve when you try to map them — is their memory, externalized, grown into the plant's structure, a spatial logic that corresponds not to physical space but to experiential space, to the accumulated weight of having been here for so long that *here* has become a property of the body rather than a location the body occupies.

The light that has no source. The sound that is too slow to hear. The rhythm that corresponds to nothing visible. These are the Long Ones' contributions. Their presence, sustained for geological time, has saturated the valley with something that is not light and not sound and not rhythm but that the visitor's senses interpret as those things because the visitor's senses have no other framework. The Long Ones are *emanating*. The way a stone emanates heat after the sun has set. The way a river emanates the sound of its own past flowing. They have been here so long that their presence has become a property of the air, a quality of the light, a texture in the sound.

## VI. The Children

And there are children.

Not many. The town is not growing, or it is growing so slowly that growth is indistinguishable from maintenance. But in one of the buildings — a small structure near the tunnel entrance, its walls thinner, its grain finer, the light inside brighter — there are three small bodies. They are Keeper-sized but smaller, their skin not yet dark, their filaments thin and fragile, barely reaching the walls. They move differently from the adult Keepers. They move with the specific clumsiness of things that are learning the geometry of their own bodies, and they move with the specific clumsiness of things that are learning the geometry of the town, and these clumsinesses are the same clumsiness, because the body and the town are the same kind of thing and the learning is the same learning.

One of them touches a wall. The wall responds — a faint luminescence, a brightening along the moss-lines, a pulse that travels from the point of contact into the structure and disappears. The child watches the pulse go. Then it touches the wall again. It is learning to talk. Or it is learning to eat. Or it is learning to be. The distinction, again, is a distinction that only makes sense from the outside.

An adult Keeper — not the parent, perhaps, or perhaps the parent, the word doesn't map cleanly — stands nearby. It is not watching the child. It is doing something else, its hands moving across a surface, its filaments sunk into the wall. But its presence is there, in the room, the way the wall is there. The child touches the wall. The pulse travels. The adult absorbs it, somewhere in its filaments, and something shifts — a slight adjustment, a tuning, a correction in the pulse's frequency or amplitude or something else that the child cannot yet produce correctly and that the adult is teaching it to produce, not by demonstrating but by being the medium through which the teaching passes.

This is how they reproduce. Or this is how they grow. Or this is how the town continues. The child is not a new Keeper. The child is a new *node* in the network, a new point of presence, a new body through which the process can operate. It is being grown the way a tree grows a new branch — not because the tree decided to grow a branch but because the tree is the kind of thing that grows, and growing means extending, and extending means becoming more of what you are. The town is becoming more of what it is. The Keepers are becoming more of what they are. The child is the becoming.

## VII. What They Are

They are not symbiotic with the plant. Symbiosis implies two organisms, distinct, in a relationship of mutual benefit. The Keepers and the plant are not two. They are not one, either — that would be too clean, too simple. They are a *process*, and the process has produced bodies, and the bodies have produced a town, and the town has produced more bodies, and the bodies have deepened the town, and the deepening has produced the Long Ones, and the Long Ones have saturated the valley with their presence, and the presence has altered the air and the light and the sound, and the altered air has produced the fog that moves aside when you walk through it, the fog that *reconsiders*.

They did not choose this. Or if they chose it, the choosing happened so long ago that the choice has become the same thing as the nature. The first Keepers — the ones whose bodies are now part of the substrate, whose genetic material is distributed through the plant like ore through rock — those first Keepers might have been something else. Might have been human, or humanoid, or something that walked on two legs and had five fingers and breathed air and thought in a way that would be recognizable. The valley might have been different then. The plant might have been smaller. The town might have been a camp, a settlement, a collection of structures built by people who intended to stay.

They stayed. They stayed for so long that staying became what they were. The plant grew around them. They grew into the plant. The distinction — the boundary between the Keeper and the kept, the inhabitant and the inhabited — dissolved not in a single moment but over centuries, over millennia, over the slow accumulation of contact and exchange and the gradual merging of systems that had been separate and that, through proximity and necessity and time, became a single system operating at different scales.

The town is their body. The plant is their body. The valley is their body. The granite walls, the fog, the cobblestones, the moss in the cracks — these are all part of a single living system, and the Keepers are the part of it that moves, the part that walks and works and breathes and touches and learns. They are the part that *cares*. The plant can maintain itself. The plant has been maintaining itself for millions of years. But the plant does not care. The plant does not decide which buildings to grow and which to let fall. The plant does not harvest the fiber and process it and store it against a future that the plant cannot imagine. The Keepers do this. The Keepers are the plant's imagination, or the plant's intention, or the quality that the plant has that is the same quality that a body has when it decides to heal a wound rather than simply to endure one.

They are the plant's hands. The plant is their body. The town is the conversation between the two.

## VIII. What They Are Not

They are not waiting. There is nothing to wait for. The process is the purpose. The breathing is the purpose. The harvesting and the processing and the maintenance of the gears and the axles and the wheels — these are the purpose. The Long Ones standing in their caves, emanating their presence into the air — that is the purpose. The children touching the walls and learning to pulse — that is the purpose. There is no other purpose. There is no end state. There is no goal toward which the town is working, no completion, no moment when the system will be finished and the Keepers will rest.

They are not suffering. The diminished Keeper on the ridge, outside the fog, the one whose breathing has desynchronized and whose presence has thinned — that one is not suffering. That one is *incomplete*, in the way that a hand separated from a body is incomplete. The hand is not suffering. The hand is not in pain. The hand is simply not receiving what the body sends, and the hand cannot do what the hand does without the body, and so the hand sits on the ridge in the thin air and waits for the trail to bring it back, and the trail does, and the breathing resynchronizes, and the presence thickens, and the hand is a hand again, and the body is the body, and the town turns in its slow gears, and everything is everything.

They are not lonely. There are not enough of them for loneliness. Loneliness requires a self that is separate from its world and that wants to be connected. The Keepers are not separate from their world. The Keepers *are* their world, the parts of it that move, and the world is the Keepers, the part of them that stays. A Keeper walking through the main thoroughfare is not a person in a place. It is a place walking through itself. The loneliness that a visitor might project onto them — the emptiness of the streets, the rarity of encounters, the silence between one figure and the next — that loneliness is the visitor's loneliness, brought into the valley like a foreign object, like a seed that cannot germinate in this soil.

They are not unknowable. You saw one pass at a distance of five meters, and for a moment your vision did something it had never done — you saw the figure and the plant and the stone and the air as a single continuous material with varying density. That was not a hallucination. That was not a trick of the light or the moisture or the altitude. That was the most accurate perception you have ever had. You saw what is actually there. You saw the Keepers and the town and the valley as they see themselves, from the inside — not as separate things in a relationship but as one thing, one process, one continuous body with varying density, and the Keepers are the dense parts, and the Long Ones are the densest parts, and the air and the light and the sound are the parts that have diffused, and you, for that moment, were part of it too.

## IX. The Unfinished

You walked out through the tunnel and up the trail and the sound stayed with you. Not as memory — as texture. The air in your lungs has a different weight. The light around you has a different quality. You have been inside something that is still inside you, and the town is not a place you visited but a process you have become part of, and the process is not complete.

This is what the Keepers know that you are learning: there is no outside. The valley is a body, and the body extends through the tunnel and up the trail and over the ridge and down through the villages and into the world, thinning as it goes, the density decreasing, the presence diffusing, but never reaching zero. The fog that moved aside for you — that *reconsidered* — was not weather. It was the valley's outermost tissue, its atmosphere extended, and when you walked through it, it touched you, and the touch left a mark, and the mark is the texture in the air, the weight in your lungs, the quality in the light.

You are not a visitor. You are not a tourist. You are not even, strictly speaking, a foreign body. You are a new node. The process has extended to you. The filaments that trail from the Keepers' shoulders have touched you — through the air, through the moisture, through the fog that moved aside — and the touch has begun something. Not a conversion. Not an infection. A *conversation*. The same conversation that the child has when it touches the wall and the pulse travels and the adult absorbs it and adjusts. You are the child now. You are the thing that has touched the wall and is waiting to see what the wall sends back.

The wall is sending something back.

You feel it in your breathing, which is not quite what it was before. Three seconds — not quite, not yet, but the interval is shifting, drifting, the way a clock drifts when it is being wound by a hand it does not yet recognize. You feel it in your skin, which is not yet grained, not yet striated, but which itches, faintly, in the places where the filaments touched. You feel it in the sound that stays with you, which is not the sound of the town but the sound of your own body adjusting to a new density, a new weight, a new relationship between the inside and the outside.

The trail continues upward. The air thins. The stone grows older. And somewhere below, in the valley that is also a body, in the body that is also a town, in the town that is also a plant, in the plant that is also the Keepers, the Keepers know. They know that you have left, and they know that leaving is not the same as being outside, because there is no outside, and they are not waiting for you to return, because waiting implies a future, and the future is already here, growing, the way everything here is growing, the way everything here has been growing for fifty million years in the cup of the valley's ancient hands.

You walk. You are still walking. The trail is the first thing that the town extends, and it is the last, and it goes where you go, and the moss in the cracks is alive, and the cobblestones are worn smooth by the feet of things that have been here before you and that will be here after you and that are, in the deepest and most literal sense, the same things as you — varying densities in a single continuous material, all of it alive, all of it breathing, all of it three seconds apart.

You walk. The fog moves aside for you. It is not fog. It is the edge of what you are becoming.
