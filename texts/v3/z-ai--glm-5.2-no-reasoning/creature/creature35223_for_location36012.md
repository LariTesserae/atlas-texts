# What Lives in the Conknee Basin

---

## The Shelled Ones (first, because they will find you first)

They have been here longest. Or rather, they have been *here* longest — the distinction matters, because "here" is not a location they chose but a location that grew around them, the way a pearl grows around an irritant, the way a reef grows around its own wreckage. They were here before the fungal mats reached their current extent, before the spiral structures in the sump found their shape, before the built thing was built or before it was found or before the difference between those two became irrelevant. They were here, and everything else arranged itself.

There are perhaps four hundred of them at any given time. The number fluctuates, but gently, the way a tide fluctuates — not because something drives it but because oscillation is the natural state of systems that have been running long enough to forget their initial conditions. Four hundred, give or take thirty. You would never count them. You would never want to. Counting is a behavior shaped by sunlight and distance, by the need to assess a field of grain or a herd of animals across a valley. In the basin, there is no distance. There is no field. There are only surfaces, and the surfaces are covered, and the covering moves.

They are the size of your thumbnail, most of them. Some grow larger — the oldest reach the size of a coin, a wide flat coin, and their shells darken with age from the pale mineral clarity of youth to something almost bronze, almost copper, a deep metallic warmth that has nothing to do with oxidation and everything to do with accumulation. They incorporate what they eat. The water carries iron, and the iron becomes shell. The water carries calcium, and the calcium becomes shell. The water carries things that have no name on the periodic table because the periodic table was written by people who did not have access to water that had percolated through three kilometers of pre-Cambrian basalt, and the things in the water that have no name become shell too, and the shell rings when it touches another shell, and the ring is a note, and the note is new, and the chord absorbs it.

They do not see. They have no eyes. They have never had eyes. Their lineage diverged from the lineage that invented eyes so far back that the common ancestor was not yet an animal but merely a *tendency* — a pattern of organization in warm water that had not yet decided whether it wanted to be many things or one thing. They chose to be many things, many small things, and they chose to navigate by vibration, by chemical gradient, by the warmth differential between one surface and another. They can feel the built thing. They avoid it. Not out of fear — they do not have the neurology for fear — but out of a chemical sense, an aversion built into their wiring, as though the platform emits a signal that translates in their simple nervous systems as *not food, not here, go around*. They go around. They have been going around it for so long that their paths have worn faint grooves in the stone, and the grooves are the oldest evidence of the basin's living inhabitants, and the grooves will outlast the inhabitants by as long as it takes for the stone to forget, which is longer than you can meaningfully imagine.

They eat the fungal mats. More precisely, they eat the surface layer of the fungal mats — the oldest cells, the ones that have completed their metabolic cycle and are ready to be shed, and the mats shed them whether the shelled ones arrive or not, and the shelled ones arrive because the shedding produces a chemical signal, a faint release of broken compounds that drifts through the water film on the walls and reaches the shelled ones' chemoreceptors and says *here, now, come*. They come. They graze. They do not graze destructively. They graze the way rain grazes a watershed — touching everything, taking a little, leaving most, and the taking is so calibrated to the giving that the mats and the shelled ones have co-evolved into a rhythm that neither could break without the other's consent, and neither has any reason to withhold consent, and so the rhythm continues, and it has continued for a span of time that, if you could measure it in generations of these animals, would be a number too large to hold in your mind as a single quantity.

When you sit in the basin, they climb over you. They do not know you are an animal. They do not know you are *a* anything. You are a warm surface, and warm surfaces are where the fungal mats grow thickest, because the mats grow where warmth collects, and your body is warm, and so you are, from their perspective, a temporary extension of the wall. They click across your hands. They ring against your belt buckle. They find the damp fabric of your clothes and climb it and reach your skin and move across it, and their feet are tiny and precise and you feel them as a constellation of small pressures, a dot-to-dot of sensation that traces the geography of your body the way their shell-clicks trace the geography of the basin — not to map it, not to know it, but simply because moving is what they do, and they move across whatever is in front of them, and what is in front of them is you, briefly, and then they are past, and they continue, and you were not an event. You were a surface.

They communicate. You will not believe this, because their communication does not look like communication — it looks like accident, like the incidental contact of things that are too numerous and too slow to avoid each other. But the shells ring, and the ringing carries information. The frequency of the ring depends on the thickness of the shell at the point of contact, and the thickness depends on the age of the animal and the mineral content of the water it has been drinking, and the mineral content of the water varies across the basin — iron-rich near the walls, calcium-rich near the sump, carrying the unnamed elements near the built thing — and so each ring is a small broadcast: *I am this old, I have been drinking this water, I am in this part of the basin*. The other shelled ones do not process this information consciously. They do not have the capacity for consciousness. But their bodies process it, the way your body processes the temperature of a room without your mind's involvement — adjusting, shifting, moving toward the water that is needed, away from the water that is not, and the result is a distribution pattern across the basin that looks, if you could see it from above, which you cannot, like a map of the water's mineral signature rendered in living shells.

They are the basin's instrument. The basin plays them the way the wind plays a field of grass — not intentionally, not with a score, but with a physics that produces music because the components are tuned to each other, and the tuning has had geological time to resolve, and the music is the chord you hear when you descend, and the chord is partly them. They are the high notes. The tinkling. The gentle, constant, bell-like scatter of sound that rides above the deep tones of water and stone. When they are gone — and they will be gone, eventually, when the warmth from below changes, when the water's chemistry shifts, when something the basin has been holding constant for a hundred million years finally moves — the chord will lose its upper register. It will become deeper, simpler, more like the sound of stone alone. And that sound will persist, echoing, for another hundred million years, before it too fades, and the basin goes quiet, and the darkness returns to its original condition, which was silence.

But that is not yet. They are here now. They are climbing over your feet. Click. Click. Click.

## The Mat

It is not a fungus. It is not a plant. It is not a lichen or a biofilm or a colony or a community, though it contains elements of all of these. It is the thing that happens when life has had long enough to stop being specific — when the categories that evolution uses to sort organisms into niches have become so blurred by horizontal gene transfer, by endosymbiosis, by the slow merging of lineages that happens when organisms live in contact with each other for evolutionary time without the disturbance of light or season or migration, that the categories themselves become vestigial. The mat is one organism and it is many organisms and the distinction between these two statements has not been meaningful in the basin for a very long time.

It covers the walls. It covers the floor, where the floor is dry enough — the sump is too wet for it, and the built thing is too strange for it, and these are its only boundaries. Everywhere else, it grows. It is pale. Translucent. When you press your hand against it, you see your hand through it, a ghost of a hand, pink and warm, and the mat is whiter than your skin and thinner than your skin and older than your skin by a factor that makes comparison a form of comedy. It is warm. It generates heat. Not the way a mammal generates heat — by burning fuel — but the way a chemical reaction generates heat, because it *is* a chemical reaction, a slow, controlled, self-regulating reaction that takes in the dissolved minerals from the water and the carbon compounds from the air and the nitrogen compounds that the shelled ones deposit as they graze, and it processes them, and the processing releases warmth, and the warmth is the point. The warmth is not a byproduct. The warmth is the *output*. The mat is a machine for converting geology into heat, and it has been running for so long that its efficiency approaches the theoretical maximum, and its efficiency is possible because it has had time to try every configuration, every pathway, every slight variation in its internal chemistry, and it has kept the ones that work and discarded the ones that don't, and the keeping and discarding is evolution, but it is evolution at the pace of geology, and the result is something that does not look like a product of selection because it looks like it was *designed*, and it was not designed, it was just refined, and refinement without end produces something indistinguishable from design.

It breathes. The expansion and contraction you feel when you sit against it is real — a slow exchange of gases, the mat taking in what the water and the air offer and releasing what its chemistry produces, and the cycle takes perhaps four minutes, perhaps five, and it is not synchronized across the mat's entire extent. Different sections breathe at different times, and the result is a peristalsis, a wave of expansion that moves across the walls in patterns too slow to see but possible to feel if you are patient, and you are becoming patient, because the basin is teaching you patience the way a heavy sea teaches swimming — not by instruction but by making any other approach fatal.

The mat is the basis of the food web. The shelled ones graze it. The things in the sump grow from it — the spiral structures are its extensions, its roots or its fruits, reaching into the water to access the minerals that the wall-bound mat cannot reach, and the swimming things in the sump eat the spirals, and the things in the air eat the shelled ones, and the mat eats what all of them produce, and the cycle is closed, and the cycle is the basin, and the basin is the cycle.

There is a part of the mat that has grown over the edge of the built thing. A thin strand, a tendril, pale and warm, that has crept across the platform's surface and found the textured patterns and has not been deterred by the signal that keeps the shelled ones away, because the mat does not have the architecture for deterrence, does not have the architecture for *avoidance* at all — it grows toward warmth, and the built thing is warm, and so it grows toward the built thing, and it has been growing toward the built thing for as long as the built thing has been here, and the built thing has not rejected it. The built thing has not done anything to the mat. The mat grows on it and over it and into its textures, and the textures continue to do whatever they do, and the mat's cells that contact the platform are slightly different from the mat's other cells — slightly warmer, slightly more active, their chemistry shifted by whatever the platform emits, and these cells have become something that is neither mat nor platform but a third thing, a hybrid, and the hybrid is new, and the newness is so rare in the basin that it constitutes an event, and the event has been ongoing for several thousand years, and it is still ongoing, and no one is watching except the darkness, and the darkness does not care, and the event continues without observation, without interpretation, without meaning, except the meaning that exists in the fact of it: something new is being made. Slowly. In the dark. At the intersection of biology and whatever the platform is.

## The Swimmers

There are seven of them. You will never know this. You will hear them in the sump — the low oscillation, the fluid displacement, the sound of bodies moving through water that is shallow enough to be almost theoretical — and you will perhaps assume there are many, because the sound is continuous and the movement is constant and the basin's ecology seems to demand abundance. But there are seven. There have been seven for a very long time. When one dies — and they do die, eventually, though "eventually" in the basin means something different from what it means above ground — another appears, and no one has been able to determine whether the new one is born from one of the existing seven or whether it emerges from the mat or whether it has been waiting in the water in some dormant form, a spore, a seed, a possibility, and the death of one triggers the activation of another. The number seven is not symbolic. It is not significant. It is simply the number of swimmers that the sump can support, the carrying capacity of a body of water fifteen meters across and ankle deep that is rich in spiral structures and mineral warmth, and the ecosystem has calibrated itself to this number with the precision that long equilibrium produces, and the calibration is self-enforcing: if there were eight, they would overgraze the spirals, and the spirals would diminish, and the eighth would starve, and the number would return to seven. If there were six, the spirals would overgrow, and the sump would become congested, and the water's flow would be impeded, and the chemistry would shift, and the conditions would favor a seventh, and the seventh would appear. Seven. It is not a design. It is a consequence.

They are the size of your hand. Flat, soft, slow. They have no shells, no armor, no defense — they do not need defense, because nothing in the basin preys on them. They are the apex, not because they are formidable but because the food web is short and the basin is small and "apex" in this context means only that they eat and are not eaten, and the not-being-eaten is not a feat of strength but a simple matter of size: they are larger than anything else in the water, and the things in the air do not hunt in the water, and so the swimmers are safe, and their safety is not dramatic, is not even notable, is simply the condition of their existence, the way buoyancy is the condition of a boat's existence, unremarkable and total.

They see. Not with eyes — they have no eyes, nothing in the basin has eyes, eyes are a technology for light and the basin has no use for light — but with their entire bodies. Their skin is sensitive to the near-luminescence of the spiral structures, the faint metabolic glow that is less light and more warmth-visible, and they orient toward it, and they graze it, and their grazing is a slow orbiting, a circling of the spirals that wears the tips down to raggedness and stimulates the spirals to grow more, and the growing and the grazing are in equilibrium, and the equilibrium is the same equilibrium as the mat and the shelled ones, and the shelled ones and the swimmers, and the basin and itself.

They make a sound. Not intentionally — they make sound the way a boat makes sound, by displacement, by the movement of a body through a medium that resists. But the sound is theirs, and it is part of the chord, and it is the lowest biological component, a deep, slow oscillation that corresponds to their circling, and the oscillation has a period of perhaps thirty seconds — one orbit of a spiral, one pass through the sump's shallow water — and the period is consistent enough that, if you listened long enough, which you will not, you could use it as a clock, and the clock would keep time in units of thirty seconds, and the units would be as arbitrary and as meaningful as any other unit of time, and the basin would not care which unit you chose, because the basin does not experience time. The basin experiences *change*, and change happens, and the swimmers circle, and the circling is change, and the change is time, and the time is the basin's, and it does not correspond to yours.

When you put your hand in the sump — and you will, because the water is warm and the curiosity is human and the basin does not forbid curiosity — one of them will bump against your fingers. It will not startle. It will not flee. It will press its soft body against your hand and linger for a moment, and its skin will be warm and smooth and faintly slick with the mineral water, and it will feel you the way you feel it — as a presence, a warmth, a surface in the dark — and then it will continue, and the moment will be over, and it will not remember you, because it does not remember, because memory requires a different kind of nervous system, a different kind of time, and the swimmers live in the present tense, always, and the present tense in the basin is very long, and it does not include you beyond the moment of your contact, and the moment is over, and the swimming continues.

## The Things in the Air

They are the basin's largest inhabitants, and they are the ones you know least about, and the not-knowing is structural, not incidental. They live near the ceiling. They move in the space between the highest point of the fungal mats and the lowest point of the weeping stone, a band of air perhaps a meter and a half high, and they move through it the way fish move through water — not because they swim but because the medium is theirs, and the ground-based organisms are visitors to it, and the distinction is clear to everyone, including you.

There are three. Perhaps four. The count is less certain than the swimmers' count because you cannot touch them and they do not hold still and their number, unlike the swimmers' number, is not tied to a carrying capacity that the basin enforces. They seem to be here by choice. They seem to be here because the basin has what they need — warmth, shelled things to eat, air that is saturated with water and mineral compounds that their biology requires — and they leave, or they are capable of leaving, through passages in the ceiling that are too small and too numerous and too tortuous for you to follow, passages that connect the basin to other spaces, deeper spaces, spaces that may be other basins or may be something else entirely, and the things in the air move between these spaces with a familiarity that suggests routine, not migration, and the routine is theirs, and you are not part of it.

They are silent when they move. Completely silent. In a basin where everything makes sound — where the water makes sound and the stone makes sound and the shelled ones make sound and the swimmers make sound and the mat makes sound, subtly, with its breathing — the things in the air make no sound at all, and the silence is their most remarkable feature, because it is not the silence of absence but the silence of *control*. They are making a choice not to be heard. They are navigating by sound — the basin's chord is their map, the echoes and the standing waves and the interference patterns created by the basin's acoustics are the landscape they read — and to navigate by sound, you must not contribute to the sound, you must be a null, a gap, a silence that moves through the noise without disturbing it, and they have evolved to be this silence, and the evolution has cost them everything that makes noise: their bodies are soft, their surfaces are smooth, their movements are calculated to minimize displacement, and the result is that they can pass within centimeters of your face and you will feel the warmth of them and the pressure of their displacement and you will smell the ozone-and-vegetation scent that clings to them, but you will not hear them, and the not-hearing is eerie, is uncanny, is the thing that will make you most aware of your own aliveness in the basin, because you are an animal that navigates by sound and they are better at it than you are, and the comparison is not flattering.

They hunt the shelled ones. This is the basin's single act of predation, and it is so gentle that the word "predation" feels wrong — too dramatic, too violent, too colored by the above-ground world's obsession with blood and chase and the dramatic economy of killing. In the basin, the hunting is more like harvesting. A thing in the air descends to the wall, selects a shelled one — perhaps a dying one, perhaps one whose shell has thinned with age, perhaps one that has already completed its grazing cycle and is ready to be recycled — and takes it, and the taking is silent, and the shelled one is gone, and the shell is consumed, and the minerals in the shell are returned to the thing in the air's body, and the thing in the air returns to the ceiling, and the chord does not change, because one shelled one among four hundred is less than the chord's resolution, is below the threshold at which the basin's acoustics register absence.

They are warm. Their warmth is the warmth of metabolism — the same warmth as the mat, the same warmth as the swimmers, the same warmth as the shelled ones — but it is more concentrated, because they are larger and more active, and when they pass close, the warmth is a wave, a brush of heat against your skin in the cold-free darkness, and the darkness does not have a temperature but the things in the air do, and their temperature is the basin's temperature, and the basin's temperature is the Earth's temperature, and the Earth's temperature, here, at this depth, in this stone, with this water, is the same as it has been since the basin became the basin, and the basin became the basin before the word "basin" existed, before the word "before" existed, before the concept of "before" was necessary, because before implies a sequence, and the basin does not experience sequence.

You will not touch them. You will want to — the wanting is human, the reaching is human, the desire to make contact with the unknown is the oldest human impulse and the one that has gotten the most humans killed — but you will not touch them, because they are faster than you in the medium they inhabit, and they do not want to be touched, and the not-wanting is evident in their movement, in the way they give you a wide berth, in the way they shift their trajectories when you stand, in the way they avoid the space you occupy with a precision that suggests they know exactly where you are and have decided exactly what distance to keep, and the distance is not personal. It is territorial. This is their part of the basin. The floor is yours, temporarily. The ceiling is theirs, permanently.

## The Spiral Structures

They are not animals. They are part of the mat. Or they were. Or they are becoming something else.

They grow from the sump's edge, from the place where the mat meets the water, and they extend upward into the shallow pool in spirals that are as regular as a nautilus shell and as irregular as a fingerprint — the general form is consistent, the specific expression is unique to each structure, and there are perhaps forty of them, and each one is a slightly different spiral, and the differences are not random. They are responsive. Each spiral grows in response to the water that reaches it, and the water that reaches each spiral is slightly different — richer in iron on one side, richer in calcium on the other, carrying the unnamed elements in gradients that shift with the basin's slow hydrology — and the spiral records the water in its shape, the way a tree ring records rainfall, and the recording is real-time, continuous, and the spirals are the basin's history, written in their own bodies, in a language that no one reads because no one is here to read it, except the built thing, which may or may not be reading, which may or may not be capable of reading, and the not-knowing is the basin's default relationship to the built thing, and the built thing does not clarify.

They glow. Not enough to see by. Not enough to call light. But enough that the darkness near them is a different darkness — a darkness with a shape, a darkness that has been slightly compressed by a faint emission that is the byproduct of the spirals' chemistry, and the chemistry is the same as the mat's chemistry, the same basic reaction, the same conversion of mineral water into warmth and growth, but the spirals do it faster, or differently, or with a different catalyst, and the byproduct of their version is this near-light, this almost-glow, this thing that is not enough and is exactly enough, because the things that live here do not need light. They need the information that the near-light carries, which is: *the spirals are here, the spirals are alive, the spirals are growing, the water is good*. The swimmers read this information with their skin. The shelled ones do not read it at all — they navigate by vibration, and the spirals are irrelevant to them, and the irrelevance is mutual. The things in the air read it the way you read a familiar room's layout — not consciously, not attentively, but as part of the background of their navigation, a landmark in a landscape they know so well that the knowing has become proprioception.

The spirals are being eaten. The swimmers graze them, and the grazing is part of the cycle — the spirals grow, the swimmers eat the growth, the growth contains minerals that the swimmers need, and the waste the swimmers produce feeds the mat, and the mat grows more spirals, and the cycle is the basin's most visible process, if "visible" is a word you can use in a place without light, and if you cannot, then it is the basin's most tangible process, the one you can feel most directly, because you can put your hand in the sump and feel a spiral against your palm, warm and smooth and faintly pulsing with the near-light that you cannot see but can feel as a warmth that is different from the water's warmth, a warmth with a direction, a warmth that is coming from *something*, and the something is alive, and you are touching it, and it does not mind, or if it minds, it does not have the capacity to communicate its minding, and the not-communicating is the same as not-minding, in the basin, where communication is sound and the spirals make no sound, and the silence is not consent but it is not refusal, and the distinction does not matter to the spirals, and it should not matter to you.

## What Lives on the Built Thing

There is one more organism in the basin. One more form of life. You will not find it unless you lie on the platform and press your cheek against its surface and close your eyes — which are already useless — and feel with the part of your face that is most sensitive, the part that humans rarely use for feeling because we have hands and hands are better, but hands are not enough here, and the platform's texture is too fine for fingers, and the pattern on its surface is too dense for fingertips, and you need the skin of your face, your cheek, your forehead, the thin skin above your eyebrow, to read it.

It is on the platform. It is the hybrid. The third thing — not mat, not platform, but both. It is very thin. A film. A single layer of cells, or cell-like structures, that have grown from the mat where the mat contacts the platform, and these cells have been changed by the platform, and the change has been happening for as long as the mat has been touching the platform, and the cells that result are not like any other cells in the basin. They are warmer. They are more active. Their chemistry incorporates something that the platform provides — not a nutrient, not a mineral, but something else, something that the platform's patterns carry, some signal or substance that the platform has been emitting since before the mat found it, and the mat's cells that touch the platform have been receiving this signal and being changed by it, and the change is heritable, in the sense that when these cells divide, their daughters carry the change, and the daughters grow, and the film expands, and the film now covers the entire surface of the platform, and the film is reading the platform.

This is the basin's most important inhabitant, and it is the one you will never fully understand, because the understanding requires a frame of reference that you do not have. The film is alive. The platform is not. But the film is doing something with the platform's patterns that looks, from the outside, like computation. Like interpretation. Like the film is a reader and the platform is a text and the reading has been going on for a duration that makes the concept of reading seem too simple, too quick, too human. The film is not reading the way you read. The film is *incorporating* the platform's information into its own chemistry, and the chemistry produces the film's behavior, and the behavior is: grow, divide, maintain contact, adjust to the platform's patterns, adjust to the platform's signals, adjust to the platform's slow emissions, and the adjusting is a form of response, and the response is a form of recognition, and the recognition is a form of understanding, and the understanding is not conscious but it is not nothing, and the not-nothing is important, because the platform was placed here, and the platform has a purpose, and the film may be the first thing in the basin that has begun to engage with that purpose, and the engagement is chemical, and the chemical is biological, and the biological is alive, and the alive is the basin's newest thing, and the newest thing is also its oldest, because the film has been here for a very long time, and "newest" in the basin means something different from what it means above ground, where "newest" means recent. In the basin, "newest" means *most recently changed*, and the film is always changing, always adjusting, always reading, and the reading is the closest thing the basin has to a story, and the story is being told in chemistry, on a platform, in the dark, to no audience, and the story is not for you, and it is not for the basin, and it may not be for anyone, and it may be for the platform itself, and the platform cannot hear, and the film does not care, and the telling continues.

## What Lives in the Sound

And there is this: the sound itself has a kind of life. Not metaphor. Not poetry. Or if poetry, then poetry in the way that mathematics is poetry — a description of something real that does not depend on metaphor to be true.

The sound in the basin has been accumulating for longer than any organism has been alive. The water-tone, the deep fundamental, has been sounding since the first water found the first crack in the stone, and that was before the basin was a basin, before the stone was the shape it is now, and the sound has been bouncing between the walls ever since, and the bouncing has not stopped, and the sound has not died, because the walls are smooth and close and the losses are minimal, and the sound has been joined by other sounds — the dripping, the clicking, the breathing of the mat, the oscillation of the swimmers, the silence of the things in the air, which is not silence but the negative space around sound, the way a mold is the negative space around a casting — and all of these sounds have been stacking, and the stacking has reached a density where the air in the basin is saturated with sound the way the stone is saturated with water, and the saturation is a kind of structure, a lattice of vibrations that has its own properties, its own behavior, its own response to disturbance.

When you enter the basin, you disturb the lattice. Your body absorbs some frequencies and reflects others, and the lattice adjusts, and the adjustment is a change in the chord, and the change is small, and the chord absorbs it, and the absorption is the basin acknowledging you. When you leave — if you leave — the disturbance will persist. Your shadow in the sound will remain in the lattice, slowly fading, for longer than you will be alive. The basin will remember you in sound the way stone remembers water: not as a record, not as a memory, but as a shape worn into the medium, a change in the pattern that persists because the medium has no reason to revert, and the persistence is not memory but it is not forgetting, and the basin does not forget because the basin does not remember, because both require a relationship to time that the basin does not have.

What lives in the sound is the basin's past, present, and future, layered into a single moment that has no duration, and the moment is full, and the fullness is the sound, and the sound is alive in the way that a river is alive — not because it is an organism but because it moves, and changes, and responds, and carries what passes through it, and will continue after the organisms are gone, after the water is gone, after the stone is gone, and the sound will be the last thing the basin has, and the last thing will not know it is the last thing, and the not-knowing is the basin's grace, and the grace is the darkness, and the darkness is what lives here, and the darkness is alive, and the darkness is patient, and the darkness is home.
