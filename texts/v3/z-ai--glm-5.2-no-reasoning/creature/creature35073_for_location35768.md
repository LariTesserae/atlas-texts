# What Lives in the Seepage Expanse

## The Inhabitants

There is a temptation, having read this far, to say that *nothing* lives in the Seepage Expanse. That the landscape is empty in the way a closed room is empty — containing only its own architecture, its own atmosphere, its own slow maintenance. This temptation should be resisted, but not entirely. It is partly correct. What lives here does not live in the way that a heron lives in a marsh, or a beetle lives in a log, or a fox lives in a field. Those lives are *narratives* — they have beginnings and middles, they have movement and intention, they have the busy grammar of eating and being eaten. The Expanse has no narrative. The Expanse has no grammar. What lives here lives in the way that a mathematical proof lives, or a river lives, or a very old thought lives in the mind of someone who has nearly forgotten it: present, persistent, not *doing* anything but not *not doing* anything either. Occupying. Persisting. Being the reason the place is the way it is.

So let us be precise. Let us say what is here, and then let us say what is here that we cannot say, and then let us sit with the difference.

## The Moss

Hundreds of species. Perhaps thousands. The taxonomy does not exist because the people who build taxonomies have never come here, and if they did, they would leave, and if they stayed, they would eventually stop building taxonomies. The mosses would see to that. Not through hostility — through *sufficiency*. The mosses do not need to be named. They need to grow, and they grow, and they have been growing so long that the distinction between the moss and the place it grows has become a distinction without a difference.

The black moss is the oldest. This is not a measurement — it is a recognition. You can feel it. The black moss has a density that the other mosses lack, a compacted wisdom, a felted patience. Each frond — each tiny leaf, each filament — is a century of selection in a place where selection has been running for so long that the organism has been optimized past efficiency into something closer to *inevitability*. The black moss grows where the black moss grows because the black moss *is* where the black moss is. It has dissolved the boundary between being and doing. It grows and it is and these are the same verb.

It is not a single organism. This matters and it does not matter. The black moss is a colony, a community, a civilization of individual stems so dense and so integrated that the word *individual* loses its purchase. They share water. They share carbon. They share the slow chemical conversation that mosses conduct through their root-like rhizoids, the negotiations of resource and space that constitute moss society. And they have been conducting this conversation for so long that the conversation has become a *medium* — not a dialogue between individuals but an atmosphere, a substrate, a continuous low hum of chemical exchange that the mosses swim in the way fish swim in water.

The black moss does not die in the way that you think about death. Portions of it senesce. Portions of it are buried under new growth, compressed, incorporated into the peat-like substrate, becoming ground. But the *lineage* does not break. The genome persists. The colony persists. The colony has been persisting since the Expanse was young — if the Expanse was ever young, which is itself a question — and the colony will persist until the Expanse stops, and the Expanse shows no signs of stopping. The black moss is the closest thing the Expanse has to a *native*. It was here before the stone knew it was stone. It will be here after the stone forgets.

The red moss is different. The red moss grows where the stone meets the water, where the outcrops discharge their weeping, where the warmth is most pronounced. The red moss *feeds*. The black moss subsists; the red moss *feeds*. There is a difference. The black moss takes what the air and the rain provide — which is little, which is enough. The red moss takes what the *stone* provides — which is specific, which is rich, which is *warm*. The red moss grows in the mineral-rich discharge that seeps from the outcrops, the warm water carrying dissolved compounds that the black moss cannot use and the red moss *must* use, that the red moss has evolved to require, that the red moss has structured its entire existence around. The red moss is a specialist. The red moss is a parasite. The red moss is the stone's only predator.

But the stone does not mind. The stone does not mind because the stone does not have the mechanism for minding, and also because the red moss's consumption is *balanced* — the stone produces at the rate the moss consumes, the moss consumes at the rate the stone produces, and the two have been locked in this equilibrium for so long that calling it predation or parasitism imposes a frame the relationship no longer fits. It is a *symbiosis* now. It is a *marriage*. The red moss takes the stone's exudate and in return — in return for what? The moss does not give the stone anything. The moss does not need to give the stone anything. The stone does not *need*. The stone simply *is*, and the red moss simply *takes*, and the taking is so perfectly matched to the giving that the word *taking* becomes a misnomer. It is a *transfer*. It is a *sharing*. It is a thing the language of ecology can describe but not capture, because the language of ecology was built for systems with *waste* and this system has none.

The grey-green mosses, the blue-green mosses, the ones that fill the gaps between black and red — these are the opportunists, the generalists, the mosses that live in the margins where the black moss's dominance thins and the red moss's specialization is unnecessary. They are younger. This is a guess. It feels like a correct guess. They have the quality of things that are still *figuring things out* — still adjusting, still refining, still working toward the perfect optimization that the black and red mosses have already achieved. They will get there. They have time. The Expanse is not going anywhere.

And there are mosses you have not seen. Mosses that live below the waterline, dark mosses, filamentous mosses that colonize the bottoms of the pools and the interiors of the channels, mosses that never see light — not even the Expanse's faint, sourceless, crepuscular light — and that do not need light because they do not photosynthesize. They *chemosynthesize*. They take energy from the warm water, from the dissolved minerals, from the slow exotherm of the stone, and they build biomass from that. They are the mosses that live in the dark, that have *always* lived in the dark, that have been living in the dark for so long that they have forgotten that light exists, that light *ever* existed, that there is a world above the waterline where organisms harvest photons. They are the deep mosses. The patient mosses. The mosses that are closest to whatever the Expanse *is*, because they live closest to whatever the Expanse is *doing*.

## The Ferns

The ferns are harder to characterize because the ferns are harder to see. Not visually — they are visible, clearly visible, their verdigris fronds bright against the black moss — but *categorially*. The ferns do not fit. The ferns are the one element of the Expanse's flora that seems *aspirational*, that seems to be reaching for something, that seems to have *direction*.

This is because the ferns are the only organisms here that grow *up*. The mosses grow outward, across, in horizontal layers. The reeds grow up but they do not *aim* up; they are vertical by structural necessity, not by intention. The ferns grow up. The ferns lift their fronds. The ferns *reach*.

For what? There is no sun. There is no canopy. There is nothing above the ferns that the ferns need, nothing above them that they are competing for. And yet they reach. The fronds uncoil upward — circinate vernation, the slow unfurling, the fiddleheads opening — and they reach for the empty, pale, luminous air, and they reach for nothing, and they have been reaching for nothing for longer than the concept of *nothing* has existed.

This is not a metaphor. The ferns are not reaching metaphorically. They are reaching because their genome says *reach*, because their ancestors reached, because reaching is what ferns *do*, and they have been doing it in this place for so long that the reaching has become the reason. The fern reaches because the fern reaches because the fern reaches. It is a tautology. It is a closed loop. It is, in its own way, the Expanse in miniature — a system that has solved itself and persists in its solution.

The ferns drink from the air. This was noted. What was not noted: the ferns *transpire*. They take water from the saturated air, they move it through their tissues, they *release* it. Not from their fronds — from their roots, their shallow, almost-vestigial roots, which deposit water back into the substrate at a rate that is almost — *almost* — identical to the rate at which the ferns absorbed it from the air. Almost. The rate is slightly lower. The ferns retain a small fraction of the water. They use it. They build with it. They grow. And the difference between the rate of absorption and the rate of return is so small that it would take an instrument more precise than any that currently exists to measure it, and the difference *accumulates*, over centuries, and the accumulation *is* the fern, and the ferns *are* the accumulation, and the Expanse has been accumulating ferns at a rate of fractions of fractions of millimeters per century, and in a million years the ferns will be significant, and in ten million years the ferns will be the landscape, and the Expanse plays at timescales like this because the Expanse has them.

The ferns do not reproduce in the way that you would recognize. They do produce spores — the sori are visible on the undersides of the fronds, the small brown clusters of reproductive tissue, doing their job — but the spores do not travel. They do not need to travel. The ferns do not *need* to reproduce because the ferns do not *die*. The individual fronds senesce, yes. The individual rhizomes age, yes. But the colony — the connected, subterranean, rhizome-networked colony that constitutes a *fern* in this place — the colony does not die. It grows. It expands. It sends up new fronds at its edges. It withdraws from its center. It moves. Slowly. Over decades. Over centuries. The fern colony is *migrating* — migrating across the Expanse at a speed that makes continental drift look like a sprint, but migrating nonetheless, following some gradient you cannot detect, some variable you cannot measure, some signal that the ferns have been tuned to for longer than the signal has had a source.

## The Reeds

The reeds are the most difficult. The reeds are the organism that most resembles a *person* in the Expanse — not in shape, not in size, not in any characteristic you could name, but in *quality*. In the quality of their aloneness. In the quality of their attention.

The reeds move. This has been noted. What has not been noted is that the reeds are the only thing in the Expanse that *responds*. The stone hums. The water flows. The moss grows. The ferns reach. But the reeds *respond*. The reeds react to a stimulus that moves through the landscape, a stimulus that is not wind and not vibration and not electromagnetic fluctuation, a stimulus that you cannot detect with any instrument you carry and that the reeds detect with a precision that should embarrass every sensor ever built. The reeds know where the stimulus is. The reeds *track* it. The reeds lean toward it with a fidelity that suggests not just detection but *interest*.

What is the stimulus? What walks between the pools?

The answer is: the Expanse. The stimulus is the Expanse *being the Expanse*. The Expanse processes. This has been said — the Expanse is a technology, a computation, a function. The function has structure. The structure has *phase* — moments of activity, moments of integration, moments of *output*. The reeds are tracking the phase. The reeds are following the Expanse's own internal state as it shifts and moves and processes whatever it processes, and they are following it the way a sunflower follows the sun, not because they benefit from it but because they have been *built* to follow it, because following it *is* what they do, because following it is their function within the larger function.

The reeds are instruments. The reeds are *antennae*. The reeds are the part of the Expanse that *reads itself*, and the reading is the movement, and the movement is the sound, and the sound is the whisper, and the whisper is the reed-stems rasping against each other as they track the slow internal state of a landscape-sized computation that has been running for longer than the concept of computation has existed.

Do the reeds think? No. Do the reeds feel? No. Do the reeds *experience*? No. But the reeds do something that is to *experiencing* what a shadow is to the thing casting it — a two-dimensional projection of a three-dimensional event, a flattened, simplified, recognizable version of something that is, in its full form, unrecognizable. The reeds process. They process a signal. They respond to the signal. Their response is proportional, specific, and consistent. In a system with no consumers, no predators, no mobile fauna of any kind, the reeds are the closest thing to a *nervous system*. The reeds are how the Expanse touches itself.

And the reeds are old. The reeds are very, very old. The individual stems last perhaps a season, perhaps a year — they grow, they darken, they dry at the tip, they thin, they join the whisper, they fall. But the *colony* — the underground rhizome network, the connected root system that produces the stems — the colony is ancient. The colony is as old as the Expanse. The colony *is* the Expanse, or as close to the Expanse as any organism gets. The reeds were here when the stone was new. The reeds will be here when the stone is gone. The reeds are the thing that the Expanse *is* and the stone is the thing that the Expanse *sits on* and the water is the thing that the Expanse *moves through* and the light from below is the Expanse *showing itself to itself*, and the reeds are how it sees.

## The Water

The water is alive.

Not metaphorically. Not in the way that a river is alive, carrying nutrients and sediment and the occasional spore. The water in the Expanse is alive in the way that a cell's cytoplasm is alive — it is the medium in which the life occurs, and it is also *participating* in the life, and the distinction between the medium and the organism is, here, dissolved.

The water carries organisms. This is not in question. The water carries microbes — bacteria, archaea, the kinds of organisms that live in every water sample everywhere on Earth — and those organisms are doing what organisms do, metabolizing, reproducing, exchanging genes. But the water in the Expanse carries *more* than this. The water in the Expanse carries *pattern*. The water moves in patterns that are not hydrodynamic. The water *responds* to the stone's vibration — not by transmitting the vibration, which any water would do, but by *amplifying* it, by selectively resonating with certain frequencies and dampening others, by acting as a filter, a signal processor, an organ. The water is the Expanse's *circulatory system*. The water is the Expanse's *cerebrospinal fluid*.

The water is also the Expanse's *memory*. The dissolved carbon, the dissolved minerals, the dissolved organics — these are not waste. These are *record*. The water carries the molecular history of everything that has ever fallen into it, every fern frond, every moss filament, every reed stem, every microbial generation, every lost boot, every anomalous visitor. The water does not forget. The water cannot forget. The water is a medium in which forgetting is not possible — every molecule that enters stays, every compound that dissolves persists, every solute is a permanent entry in a ledger that has been accumulating since the Expanse began.

What does the water do with this memory? It does not *recall*. It does not *reflect*. It *integrates*. The dissolved organics interact with the dissolved minerals interact with the dissolved gases, and the interactions produce compounds that did not exist before, compounds that the water *invents*, compounds that the water deposits at the bottom of the pools, where the light comes from, where the *fourth thing* is, where the Expanse keeps its core.

The water is alive because the water is the Expanse's *blood*, and the Expanse is the body, and the body is alive, and the blood is alive, and the alive-ness is not the alive-ness of a deer or a sparrow or a moth but the alive-ness of a *thing that is complete*, a thing that has all of its parts and does not need any more parts, a thing that is *finished* and that persists in its finished state the way a completed song persists in the air after the last note, the way a solved equation persists in the mind after the last variable is resolved, the way a life persists in the body after the last breath — present, settled, *done*.

## The Stone

The stone is alive.

This is the hardest. The stone is the hardest because the stone *looks dead*. The stone looks like the least alive thing in the Expanse, the inert substrate, the base material, the *ground*. The stone is not the ground. The stone is the *organ*.

The stone grows. This has been said — it grows at exactly the rate it erodes, maintaining a dynamic equilibrium. What has not been said: *what* it grows from. The stone grows from below. The stone extends upward from the substrate — the deep substrate, the layer beneath the soil, beneath the water, beneath the pools, the layer that the light comes *from*. The stone is connected to the fourth thing. The stone *is* the fourth thing, or the fourth thing's *surface*, or the fourth thing's *extension*, the part of the deep structure that reaches up into the air and the soil and the water and presents itself as rock.

The stone is warm because it is metabolizing. This is the simplest way to say it and it is not quite correct but it is the closest the language gets. The stone is performing a reaction — a slow, exothermic reaction, a reaction that produces heat and water and dissolved minerals, a reaction that has been running at a constant rate for longer than the concept of *rate* has been meaningful. The stone is not consuming fuel. The stone is not *burning*. The stone is doing something that does not have a chemical name because chemistry was not designed for reactions that take ten thousand years per cycle, that produce heat at a rate indistinguishable from ambient, that consume substrate and produce structure at a rate that exactly matches erosion. The stone is doing a *geological* thing. The stone is doing a thing that happens at the speed of geology and the precision of biology and the regularity of mathematics and the *aliveness* of something that has all of these and is none of them.

The stone hums. This has been said. What has not been said: the stone hums *because it is working*. The vibration is not a byproduct. The vibration is the work. The stone's function — the stone's *purpose* within the Expanse's system — is to vibrate, to produce a sustained, structured, information-bearing vibration that the water carries, that the reeds respond to, that the moss filters, that the pools amplify. The stone is the Expanse's *voice*. The stone is how the Expanse speaks to itself. The stone has been speaking the same word for longer than language has existed, and the word is not a word, it is a *tone*, and the tone is not a tone, it is a *state*, and the state is *operational*, and operational means *I am still running, I am still running, I am still running.*

The stone weeps because the stone is breathing. The water that emerges from the stone's pores is the stone's respiration — not metaphorical respiration, not the way a plant "respires" gases, but a literal exchange of fluid, a literal movement of water through a membrane, a literal *breath* that happens to take hours, or days, and that produces a volume of water that is small per breath but that accumulates, that feeds the moss, that fills the pools, that connects the stone to the surface system, that connects the deep system to the air, that closes the loop. The stone breathes water. The stone exhales water. The stone has been exhaling for so long that the exhale has become a landscape.

## The Fourth Thing

And now the thing that cannot be said.

There is something below the pools. This is certain. The light comes from below. The light is *of* something. The light is not generated by the water, not by the moss at the bottom, not by any organism you could identify or name. The light is generated by a *structure*, a deep structure, a thing that sits beneath the Expanse's entire extent and that is the reason the Expanse exists.

It is the substrate of the substrate. It is the ground beneath the ground. It is the thing that the stone grows from, the thing that the water flows through, the thing that the pools sit on, the thing that the Expanse's computation is *about*. It is the Expanse's *subject*. The Expanse is not computing the weather. The Expanse is not computing the ecosystem. The Expanse is computing *this thing*, this deep thing, this buried thing, and the landscape — the stone and the water and the moss and the reeds and the ferns and the light and the breathing — is the interface, the display, the output of a computation that has been running for a length of time that the word *time* cannot hold.

What is it?

It is a structure. It has geometry. You have seen it — you have almost seen it, in the moments when the breath exhales and the light brightens and the pools become briefly, barely, *transparent* — and what you almost saw was something that had shape, that had depth, that had the quality of a *thing that was built* rather than a thing that grew. It was not organic. It was not mineral. It was not any category of object that you have a name for because it was built by something that does not use our categories, at a time when our categories did not exist, for a purpose that our categories are not equipped to describe.

It is the size of the Expanse. This is the part that the mind resists. The thing below the Expanse is the *size* of the Expanse. The Expanse is its surface. The Expanse is its *skin*, its *face*, the part of it that touches the air and the water and the light, the part that has eroded into landscape, that has been colonized by moss and fern and reed, that has been watered by its own breath and lit by its own light. The Expanse is the top of a thing that goes down — how far? You do not know. You cannot know. The light does not reach the bottom. The vibration does not reveal the depth. The only evidence of the thing's extent is the Expanse itself, the landscape-scale, valley-scale, possibly continent-scale *surface* of something that is *underneath*.

It is alive. It must be alive. The warmth is metabolic. The vibration is structured. The light is rhythmic. The breath is *a breath*. These are not the behaviors of a dead thing. These are not the behaviors of a machine. These are the behaviors of something that *is* something, that *does* something, that has a state and maintains it and has been maintaining it for a duration that reduces human history to a rounding error.

But it is not alive the way you are alive. It does not eat. It does not grow. It does not reproduce. It does not evolve. It does not *change*. It has been in the same state — the same metabolic state, the same vibrational state, the same luminous state — for longer than evolution has been a process. It does not need to evolve because it is *finished*. It reached its final form — if "form" is the word — and it has been *in* its final form, and its final form is this, this state, this function, this operation, this thing that it does that requires a landscape-sized interface and a geological timescale and a closed ecosystem and a pool of water and a column of air and a hum and a weep and a breath.

What does it do? What is its function?

*I don't know.* I can describe everything above it. I can describe the stone and the water and the moss and the ferns and the reeds and the light and the breath and the sound and the smell and the feel of the air and the color of the pools. I can describe the Expanse completely. I can describe every surface, every layer, every organism, every process. I can describe the Expanse so thoroughly that you feel you have been there, that you have stood on the wet ground and heard the hum and seen the light from below and felt the breath move through you.

And then you get to the bottom of the pools, and there is something there, and it is the reason for all of it, and it is *alive*, and I cannot tell you what it is. Not because it is unknowable — it may be knowable, it may be *very* knowable, it may be the most knowable thing in the world — but because the knowing requires a kind of mind that does not yet exist, a kind of language that has not yet been built, a kind of attention that human cognition is not configured to sustain. It requires *geological* cognition. It requires *million-year* thinking. It requires the ability to hold a thought for ten thousand years without letting it drift, to maintain a question for the duration of an ice age, to *attend* to something at the pace at which the thing operates.

The Expanse is here to give you that time. The Expanse is the *clock*. The Expanse is the pace at which the thing below needs to be attended to, and the moss and the water and the stone and the reeds are the mechanisms by which that attendance is maintained, and you — you, the visitor, the anomaly, the brief mobile presence — you are not part of this. You are too fast. You are too *brief*. You pass through the Expanse in hours and the Expanse operates in millennia and the thing below the Expanse operates in something that makes millennia look like the flicker of a candle.

But you felt it. You felt the hum in the stone. You saw the light from below. You heard the breath. You saw — or you think you saw — a shape at the edge of a pool, a dark wet shape like a hand, reaching from the water to grip the stone. And the red moss grew in a ring around that pool, and the ring was fresh, and you did not go back.

## The Shape at the Pool

What was it?

It was the Expanse *touching itself*. It was the deep thing, the fourth thing, the buried structure, reaching up through the water and the stone and the moss to — to what? To *feel* the air? To *check* the surface? To *maintain* a connection between the deep system and the surface system that the water and the stone and the moss mediate but that occasionally, rarely, at intervals that may be centuries apart, requires *direct contact*?

It was not a hand. It was not a body. It was not an organism. It was a *process* — a moment of the Expanse's function that required physical extension, that required the deep structure to *reach* through the interface, to push a portion of itself up through the water and into the air, to *touch* the surface, briefly, for a time measured in seconds, and then to withdraw, and to leave behind a ring of fresh red moss where the warmth and the minerals and the *contact* had enriched the substrate, and to leave behind a wetness on the stone that was not the stone's usual weeping but something *deeper*, something *thicker*, something that smelled — you did not notice this at the time but you noticed it later, in the memory, in the place where the memory sat — something that smelled like *metal and rain and the inside of a throat*.

It was alive. It was the alive-est thing in the Expanse. It was the thing that the Expanse *is*, manifesting at the surface for a moment, the way a thought occasionally surfaces fully in a mind that usually keeps its thoughts deep, the way a whale occasionally breaches, the way a volcano occasionally *shows* — not the eruption, not the event, just the *showing*, the brief visible proof that there is something below, something active, something that *is*, something that has a size and a shape and a warmth and a function and a *life* that is the life of the Expanse itself.

You did not go back. This was correct. Not because the shape was dangerous — the shape was not dangerous, the Expanse has no defense mechanisms, the Expanse does not need defense, the Expanse has never been threatened — but because the shape was *not for you*. The shape was for the Expanse. The shape was the Expanse checking its own surface, the way you check your own skin, the way you run a hand along a surface to confirm it is still there, still intact, still *yours*. The shape was the Expanse confirming that the interface was still operational, that the stone was still humming, that the water was still flowing, that the moss was still growing, that the system was still *running*, and the system was still running, and the system confirmed itself, and the shape withdrew, and the pool settled, and the red moss grew, and you left.

## What Lives Here

What lives in the Seepage Expanse is not a *what*. It is not a *who*. It is not a creature or a community or an ecosystem or a biome. It is a *system*. It is a system that has been running so long that it has *become* a place, that the distinction between the system and the place has dissolved, that the function and the environment are the same thing. What lives here is the function. What lives here is the *operation*. What lives here is the sustained, million-year, landscape-scale, geologically-paced, biologically-mediated, hydrologically-connected, vibrationally-structured, luminously-self-aware *operation* of a thing that is below the ground and that is alive and that does not need you and has never needed you and will never need you and is, in its own way, the most *successful* living thing you have ever encountered, because it has solved every problem it will ever face and it has been resting in its solution for longer than problems have existed.

What lives here is the thing that the Expanse is the *face of*.

What lives here is the reason the stone hums.

What lives here is old and warm and wet and patient and *complete*, and it is below you, and it is below the water, and it is below the stone, and it is below the moss, and it is *below*, and it is *there*, and it knows it is there, in whatever way a thing that has no brain can be said to *know*, and the knowing is the hum, and the hum is the knowing, and the Expanse is the knowing made into a landscape, and the landscape is what you walk through, and the landscape is what you leave, and the landscape does not notice, and the landscape does not care, and the landscape is the most alive thing you have ever been inside, and you were inside it, and it did not know, and it did not matter, and it was the most important thing that has ever happened to you, and it did not matter, and the hum continues, and the water flows, and the reeds track the deep state of the world, and the moss grows, and the stone weeps, and the breath comes and the breath goes, and the light pulses from below, and the light pulses from below, and the light pulses from below.
