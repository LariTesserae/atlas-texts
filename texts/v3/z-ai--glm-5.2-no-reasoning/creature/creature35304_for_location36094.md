# What Lives Here

Let me begin with what I can tell you plainly, and then work outward toward what is harder to say.

## The Midges

Consider the midges first, because everything else stands on them.

They are black and wingless and the size of a barley grain, and there is a temptation — because of the altitude, because of the clarity of the light, because we are used to thinking of remote places as pure — to imagine them as simple. They are not simple. A midge on this plateau is a creature that has solved a problem no insect should have to solve: how to be cold-blooded in a place where the temperature swings twenty degrees between noon and midnight and where the air holds so little oxygen that most insects cannot fly here at all, which is precisely why these ones cannot. Their wings are gone. Not reduced — gone. Somewhere in their lineage, wings became a liability, an invitation to the wind, which at 6,400 meters does not blow insects to new territory; it blows them off the shelf and into the void of the valley cloud and they do not come back. The wingless ones stayed. The wingless ones inherited the plateau.

They live in the fissures, packed into the warm dark, and at dusk they emerge in numbers that are difficult to process. The description says the stone looks like black sand that is moving, and this is accurate, but it is accurate in a way that misses the texture of the thing. The sand is not uniform. There are nursery crevices and there are feeding grounds and there are — I want to say *streets*, but that imports a human logic that does not belong. There are corridors of movement. The midges walk to the condensation film along routes that are not random, that have been walked by generations, that are worn into a kind of habit. Water on stone is not distributed evenly. It pools in the shallow depressions, runs in threads along the quartz veins where the stone is coldest. The midges know where. Or rather: the midges who did not know where did not leave descendants, and so the knowing is in the body now, in the legs, in the way a midge turns toward a wet seam in the rock with the confidence of something that has never been wrong about this in its life, because its ancestors were never wrong about this, because being wrong meant the end of a line.

They walk on the water. Their weight is negligible; the surface tension holds them the way it would hold a dust mote. Their shadows on the wet stone at dusk — the description mentions this and it deserves dwelling on — are each a tiny eclipse, a doubled darkness, midge above, shadow below, joined by nothing visible.

They drink. This is the whole of their adult lives, more or less: emerge, walk to water, drink, walk back, shelter, repeat, until a spider takes them or the cold takes them or the season turns. There is something almost unbearably condensed about it. An entire life — birth, growth, reproduction, death — compressed into a routine you could describe in a single sentence. And yet the routine is not the life. The life is the specific water of the specific evening, the specific walk, the specific shadow.

They are the foundation. Everything above them — the spiders, the pipits, the kestrel that comes for the pipits — rests on the fact that millions of small black animals walk to water every night and most of them are not caught.

## The Spiders

The spiders are pale to the point of translucence, and their legs are too long, and their stride looks mechanical, and I want to take each of these facts and hold it up to the light, because each one is an answer to a question.

The paleness: they have never needed color. There is no camouflage arms race here, or almost none. The midges do not see well and the pipits hunt by movement, not by pattern. Color is expensive. Melanin costs energy to synthesize and at this altitude energy is the scarcest currency there is. The spiders are pale because they can afford nothing else, and because nothing here has ever required them to be anything but pale.

The legs: they are long because the spider is thin, and the spider is thin because it must fit into cracks, and the legs are long because a long leg covers more stone per stride, and the plateau is large relative to a spider and the hunting window is short. Dusk and dawn. Two hours a day, maybe, when the midges are exposed and the temperature is high enough for a spider's muscles to work at anything like speed. In that window, the spider must cover ground. The legs are an argument about time made in the language of anatomy.

The mechanical stride: this is what a spider looks like when every movement is accounted for. There is no surplus here, no flourish, no wasted motion. The description says they are the fastest things on the plateau, and this is true, but speed at this temperature is a strange thing. The spider's metabolism is cold-slowed; it cannot afford the bursts of a lowland spider. What it has instead is a relentless, metronomic pace, hydraulic legs extending and folding, extending and folding, a gait that looks mechanical because it is mechanical — hydraulic pressure, no antagonist muscles, the body working like a small pneumatic machine designed by an engineer with a very tight budget.

They do not build webs. There is nothing to attach webs to and no need. But note the exception, because the description plants it and it matters: one spider has built a retreat in the wall of stones at the northern end. A tunnel of silk in a crevice. Not a web — a *home*. Silk is protein. Protein is expensive. This spider has spent its expensive material not on catching food but on lining a crack in a stack of rocks that a human being made centuries ago. I will come back to the wall. For now, notice that the spider has made a judgment about value: the wall's crevice is worth silk. It is stable, it is sheltered, it is near the widest fissures where the warm air is strongest and the midges are thickest. The spider did not choose it for its human meaning. The spider does not know it is a wall. But the spider's body has recognized something: *this arrangement of stones is the best real estate on the plateau.*

## The Pipits

There are fourteen of them.

I keep returning to that number because it is the strangest number in this place. Fourteen rosy pipits. Not a population estimate, not a figure with error bars — fourteen, countable, individual. In ecology there is a concept called a minimum viable population, and it is usually in the hundreds or thousands for birds, and these fourteen are below it, have been below it for however long this population has existed, and continue anyway. There is something in this that resists the vocabulary of biology. Each of these birds is known. The northern pair — the description says they hold the widest fissures and the tallest grass — are the wealthiest pair on the plateau. The southern pair hold bare stone but the thickest midges. These are different strategies and the birds have divided the shelf between them the way any limited territory gets divided: by claim, by dispute, by quiet resolution.

Their pink is brighter than their lowland relatives'. The description attributes this to light — the intensity of the sun selecting for intensity of color, sexual signaling working overtime in a place where the light lets every signal through at full strength. I think this is right and I think it goes further. The pink of a rosy pipit's breast at sea level is a signal partially absorbed by haze before it reaches its receiver. Here, nothing is absorbed. The pink arrives at the eye of the other pipit exactly as it was emitted. The birds are brighter because their world is more honest. Signals do not degrade here. Everything is legible. This is a place without ambiguity, and the birds have evolved to match it.

They are the apex predators and they sing like a water drop, pitch-shifted, played twice.

The singing deserves its own attention. A thin, high, descending trill, the loudest sound on the plateau, which is not very loud. In most of the world, birdsong is thrown against a wall of ambient noise — wind, water, leaves, other birds — and must be loud to carry. Here there is no wall. The song can be almost nothing and still cross the entire shelf. The pipits sing quietly because they can. But listen to what the song *does*: it declares territory, it maintains pair bonds, it coordinates, and — this is the crucial function — it is a distributed alarm system. Fourteen birds, each singing, each listening. When the kestrel's shadow crosses the rock, all fourteen go silent at once. The silence itself is the alarm. The absence of song propagates across the plateau faster than any call could, because every bird is monitoring every other bird's song continuously, and the stopping of song is information. They have turned their most conspicuous behavior into their early warning network. The song is the sentinel.

And the nests. Shallow scrapes in fissure floors, lined with grass and the discarded wings of eaten midges, accumulating into a dark chitin felt. Consider what that felt is: the substrate of the nest is made of the bodies of the prey that sustain the parents that made the nest. The wings are indigestible; the birds strip them off and the wings fall, and the nest slowly fills with them, and the eggs rest on chitin, on the refuse of hundreds of meals. There is no sentiment in this. It is simply the most available material. But the image stays: life on this plateau is built, literally, out of its own eating.

The incubating bird sits motionless and breathes visible plumes of vapor. At 6,400 meters, breath is visible nearly always. The sitting bird is a small warm engine in the cold, its presence announced only by that faint rhythm of steam — in, out, in, out — while the light falls on it like evidence and the stone around it is the same grey-green as its eggs and the whole arrangement is invisible to everything except the kestrel's eye, if the kestrel is there, and it usually is not, and the eggs grow in the warm dark of the fissure, heated from below by the mountain's breath and from above by the parent's body, a double incubation: bird and geology.

## The Kestrel

It is not one of them. This needs to be said plainly. The kestrel is a commuter. It lives below, in the world of voles and thick air, and it comes up in the morning the way a person drives to a distant market — a trip worth making only if the goods are worth the cost.

And the cost is real. The air at 6,400 meters is roughly half the density of sea-level air. Every wingbeat of the hover delivers half the lift it would deliver in the valley. The kestrel's signature hover — wings beating fast and precise, tail fanned — is a feat of expenditure that would be routine at 2,000 meters and is near the edge of what the bird's body can sustain here. It hangs in the wind above the plateau and it is *paying*, continuously, and it knows — in whatever way a bird knows arithmetic — that the transaction only makes sense if it leaves with something.

Most days it doesn't. The pipits are small, the grass is dense, the odds are bad, and the kestrel descends to the valleys and the plateau exhales and the singing resumes, one bird at a time, cautious, testing the air.

But it keeps coming back. This is the part I find most worth describing. The kestrel's persistence is not stupidity; it is calculation. Some days the calculation pays. The description gives us the burst — a pipit flushed, a chase across the stone, a snap of talons — and the kestrel rising with something small and brown in its feet. What it takes is usually one of the fourteen, and the fourteen become thirteen, and thirteen must reorganize — a territory opens, a pair bond may be broken, the disputes shift — until the season's fledglings bring the number up again. The kestrel is not a guest and not quite a member. It is a pressure. It is the reason the pipits watch the sky. It is the reason the singing stops. It is the one thing on the plateau that arrives from elsewhere, and everything that lives here permanently has evolved its behavior in relation to this visitor from below.

## The Grass

I said I would come back to the wall, and I will, but first the grass, because the grass is the plateau's future and the wall stands in the middle of that future.

The pale reed-like grass — nearly white, hollow-stemmed, rattling — is the only plant, and it is doing something that no one planted it to do. It advances a hand's width a year. It grows tall, falls outward, roots at the tips, and thereby *walks*. This is a slow conquest conducted by falling over. Each tuft is a rumor of more tufts. In a century the shelf will be mostly grass. The spiders will have more cover, the birds more nests, the midges more corridors, and the whole miniature ecosystem — fourteen pipits, a few hundred spiders, millions of midges — will be the same ecosystem but larger, and possibly, at some point, large enough to be stable, which it currently is not.

There is something almost unbearably patient in this. The grass does not know the wall is there. The grass does not know the kestrel exists. The grass is solving one problem — *cover more stone* — and it has been solving it since the shearing event, and it will continue solving it after every currently living thing on the plateau is dead, and the wall will be there the whole time, and the grass will grow up around its base, as it already has.

## The Wall, and the Ones Who Built It

Someone was here. Someone carried schist slabs and stacked them, dry, without mortar, with care, and left no other trace — no charcoal, no bone, no tool, no path.

I cannot tell you who, and the plateau cannot either, and this is part of what the wall is now: a message with the message worn off, a sentence of which only the punctuation survives. But I can describe what the builder must have been, because the wall describes them.

They were someone who could breathe at 6,400 meters, which narrows it considerably — a person of the high plateaus, someone adapted by ancestry to thin air, someone for whom this altitude was the upper edge of livable but not beyond it. They were someone who knew schist — who knew it cleaves flat, who knew how to read a slab for the plane it wanted to break along, who had hands calloused to the work. The wall is knee-height and four meters long and encloses nothing. The description offers three readings: marker, windbreak, offering. I would add a fourth possibility, which is that it was *practice* — that the builder was testing something, or that the building itself was the point, the arrangement of material into a form that did not previously exist, and I note that the description calls this the earliest technology, the technology of intention and grip, and I think this is the truest sentence in the whole account.

Whoever they were, they were the only member of their species to leave a mark here, and the mark is this: a line of stacked stones that will look exactly like this for another five hundred years, because at this altitude there is almost no liquid water to weather it and almost no life to disturb it, and the only creatures that use it now are a spider who has lined one of its crevices with silk and the pipits who perch on it.

The pipits perch on it. I want to sit with that for a moment. Fourteen birds use a human structure, built for unknown purposes by a person who could not have known the birds would be here — or perhaps could; perhaps the birds were here already; the shearing predates the wall and the ecosystem moves fast at first — and the birds do not know it is a structure, they know it is *the tall thing at the north end*, the best lookout, the place from which the sky is most visible, the place from which the kestrel is seen soonest. If the wall was a marker, it now marks the best perch on the plateau. If it was an offering, the offering has been accepted by the only congregation available. If it was a windbreak, note that it breaks the wind for whatever shelters behind it, and what shelters behind it now is grass, denser there than anywhere else, because the wall traps a pocket of still, warm air on its lee side, and the grass found that pocket the way grass finds everything: by falling toward it for a hundred years.

The builder is gone. The intention is gone. The wall remains, and life has grown into it like water into a crack, without reference to what it was for, using it only for what it is.

## The Kestrel's Shadow

The last image of the description is this: the kestrel's shadow crosses the rock, every living thing holds still, the shadow passes, the pipit sings again.

I want to describe what it is like to be the thing holding still, because that is what lives here, finally — not midges and spiders and birds and grass but the experience of holding still.

The midge holds still inside the fissure, in the warm dark, pressed against stone, its negligible weight meaningless, its entire evolutionary history culminating in this: *do not move, do not cost anything, wait*. The spider holds still in its silk retreat in the wall, legs folded, hydraulics idling, its long legs — its argument about time — suspended, the clock stopped. The incubating pipit does not move, because moving is more dangerous than stillness, because its eggs are the color of the stone and it is nearly the color of the stone and the shadow has no eyes but the thing that casts the shadow has the best eyes in the sky. The fourteen — thirteen, twelve, however many are out — drop into grass or crevice and the singing stops and the silence becomes the alarm and every bird is listening to every other bird's not-singing and the whole plateau becomes a single organism of held breath.

And then the shadow passes, and the negotiation resolves, most days, in favor of the prey, and a single pipit — which one? the bold one? the one whose territory is nearest the wall? — begins the song again, testing, and a second joins, and the water-drop trill reassembles itself across the shelf, and the midges resume their walk to the wet stone, and the spider unfolds from the wall, and the condensation ticks and whispers, and the mountain exhales its warm air into the cold, and the improbable place continues, for another day, to exist.

That is what lives here. Everything I have described and the stillness between the describing — the held, attentive, listening quiet of fourteen small bright birds and a million small black walkers and a few hundred pale mechanical hunters and one white grass and one old wall and one visitor from below, all of it balanced on a knife-edge of temperature and moisture, all of it too small to be stable, all of it alive anyway, at the top of the world's indifference, in light like an operating theater, in silence with texture, on a shelf of cracked-open stone the size of a church close.
