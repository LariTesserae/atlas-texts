# What Lives Here

## The People

There are not as many as you would think. A town of this footprint, terraced and piped and latticed across the mountain face, might hold thousands in a lowland city. Verkhol-Above holds perhaps six hundred, though no census has been attempted in living memory and the number may be lower or higher depending on who has temporarily left for the switchback trail and who has temporarily arrived and not yet decided to stay. The residents do not track their numbers with precision. Precision is a habit of places that believe their boundaries are fixed.

They are not a distinct people. That is — they were not always a people. They arrived the way the fog arrives: gradually, from below, condensing out of the lower valleys against the rising stone. The first were surveyors, engineers, the kind of practical minds who saw the bore-holes and the metallic veins and the condensation zones and calculated that something could be built here. They brought workers. The workers brought families, or formed them. Over decades, the population became a population — not by shared origin but by shared occupation, the way a coral reef becomes a reef not because the polyps share blood but because they share the same current and the same calcium and the same slow, patient work of building on what was built before.

They are lean. The altitude sees to that — the thin air makes labor costly, makes calories precious, trims the body to its essential mechanics. But they are not frail. The work here is constant: hauling, fitting, carrying, maintaining. The Pumps require attention. The pipes require patching. The borers' powder requires collection and processing and bagging for the mule trains. The bodies that do this work become adapted to it in the way bodies do: hands calloused and cracked from the pale granite, lungs deepened to pull what oxygen the air offers, shoulders and backs built for the specific, repetitive loads of a town that must constantly repair itself against the slow assault of condensation and vibration and the mountain's own unfinished shifting. They move with an economy of motion that reads, to a visitor, as either weariness or caution. It is neither. It is efficiency learned so thoroughly that it has become temperament.

Their skin takes on a quality here. Not a color — the population is various in origin — but a texture, a faint dampness that never quite dries, as though the fog has claimed them the way it claims the stone. Their hair hangs straight regardless of its natural inclination, weighted by the constant moisture. Their eyes adjust. This is the thing that visitors notice and cannot quite articulate: the residents see in the dimness. Not with acuity — not with the sharp, rangefinding precision of a predator — but with a kind of comfortable, diffuse perception that treats the flat, shadowless light as sufficient and does not strain against it. They do not squint. They do not lean toward the lamps. They move through the fog the way a fish moves through water: not navigating *through* an impediment but inhabiting a medium.

They dress in layers — wool from the mule trains, treated canvas, leather that has darkened with mineral damp until it is the same color as the hopper-rats. Everything they wear eventually takes on the smell of the place: wet iron, crushed ferns, the sweet vegetable undertone that clings to the steam. It is not unpleasant. It is not a smell you would choose. It is a smell that chooses you, the way the fog chooses the mountain.

## How They Are Organized

There is a governing body. It is called the Convergence, and it meets in a building that was once a pellet mill and is now a room with a long table made from a single slab of the pale granite, cut flat on one side and left rough on the other, as though to remind everyone present that the raw and the finished coexist here and neither has priority. The Convergence consists of seven residents elected by a process that is less an election than a gradual, communal acknowledgment that certain people have become necessary. There is no campaigning. There is no term length. A member of the Convergence serves until the town agrees, through some unspoken, unpolled consensus, that the member has become either indispensable or exhausted, and these are not the same thing.

The Convergence does not govern in the way governments govern. It does not pass laws. It adjusts. It is a tuning mechanism. The Pumps need a new gasket — the Convergence decides who will fabricate it and when the work will interrupt the pumping cycle. A section of walkway has begun to sag — the Convergence decides whether to repair it or reroute foot traffic, and this decision is made not by engineering assessment but by a kind of communal intuition that weighs cost, labor, risk, and the sense of whether the walkway *wants* to sag. This last consideration would, in any other context, sound fanciful. Here it is practical. The mountain shifts. The structures shift with it. Sometimes a thing is not broken but *settling*, and the residents have learned — by decades of watching stone reconsider itself — to distinguish between failure and transformation. They are not always right. But they are right often enough that the town persists.

Below the Convergence, if *below* is the right word for a structure that is more a network than a hierarchy, are the work-shifts: Pump crews, refinery crews, collection crews who maintain the gutters and cisterns, borer-powder harvesters who work the lower slopes where the grinding is loudest, maintenance crews who walk the pipe lattice every day checking for fractures, condensation crews who monitor the fog collection and the cistern levels. Each crew is autonomous in its daily operations. Each crew reports to the Convergence not through paperwork or formal channels but through the simple fact that the crew's work is visible — the Pumps either run or they don't, the cisterns either fill or they don't, the borers' powder either reaches the bagging station or it doesn't. Governance here is not abstract. It is a question of whether the machines turn and the water rises and the people eat.

## The Children

There are children. Not many — the altitude and the thin air and the constant work do not encourage large families — but enough. Perhaps forty, spread across ages from infancy to adolescence, and they are, in their way, the most remarkable thing about Verkhol-Above, because they are the only people here for whom the place is not strange.

The children born here do not know the world is unfinished. They do not know the mountains are provisional, the water is wrong, the birds eat sound. For them, this is simply the world — the way the world is, has always been, and presumably always will be. They play on the terraces with the casual, surefooted recklessness of children who have never known flat ground. They race along the walkways and the pipe conduits with a physical confidence that terrifies visiting adults and does not, in practice, lead to the falls one might expect, as though their bodies have already learned the town's spatial logic at a level deeper than conscious thought. They catch hopper-rats with their hands — not to keep, just to hold for a moment, to feel the rapid heartbeat and the flat, wide feet gripping their fingers — and then release them, and the hopper-rats allow this, seem to expect it, return to the same spots the next day as though the interaction was scheduled.

The children attend instruction in a room above the old refinery. The instruction covers mathematics, engineering principles, the mechanical operation of the Pumps, the identification of borer species, the reading of geological signs, and the practical maintenance of the pipe lattice. It does not cover history, because the history of Verkhol-Above is short enough that the children can simply be told it, and it does not cover geography, because the geography is the mountain and the mountain is still being decided. It does not cover the Fold House or the wrong-echo corridor or the stairway that goes somewhere it shouldn't. The children learn about these the way children learn about everything: by encountering them, by standing in the Fold House and feeling the space that shouldn't be there, by shouting down the corridor and hearing their voices return wrong, and by absorbing this into their understanding of how the world works without comment or distress. A child raised in Verkhol-Above, asked to describe the Fold House, will say: it's the boiler room where the wall is too far. And that will be the complete description. And it will be accurate.

They drink the heavy water from birth. Their bones, presumably, are built from it. Their blood, presumably, carries it. Nobody has studied this. Nobody has thought to study it. The children grow, seem healthy, develop normally by the standards of a town that has stopped applying external standards to itself. If there are effects — subtle ones, long-term ones, effects that manifest only in the way a person perceives space or time or the relationship between intention and outcome — then those effects are simply part of being from here, indistinguishable from personality, from temperament, from the ordinary process of becoming a person in a particular place.

## The Old Ones

There are residents who have been here from the beginning. Three of them. Perhaps four — the records of the earliest settlement are inconsistent, and memory, in a place where the light does not change and the days are marked by clock rather than sky, becomes unreliable in ways that have nothing to do with age and everything to do with the place's quiet refusal to keep time the way other places do.

Maret is the name of the oldest. She arrived as a surveyor, forty-three years ago, when the town was three buildings and a single pump running off a boiler that one of the original engineers had dragged up the switchback on a sledge. She is seventy now, or near enough. She walks the upper terraces every morning, slowly, touching the walls as she passes — a habit that looks, from the outside, like the absent-minded gestures of age, but is in fact a diagnostic. She is feeling the mountain. She is listening to the vibration through the stone. She has been doing this long enough that she can feel when the Pumps are slightly out of phase with each other, when a borer colony has moved to a new vein, when a section of rock is preparing to shift. She does not report these observations. She simply adjusts: tells a crew to check a pipe, tells a harvester to move to a different slope, tells the Convergence, casually, over the midday meal, that the mountain is thinking about something on the western face. The Convergence listens. Maret is not a member. Maret is something else — a component, perhaps, a human sensor array that the town has grown around the way the moss grows around the stone.

She does not go into the Fold House. She does not go near the Columns. When asked about this — and visitors ask, because visitors ask about everything — she says: "I know what I know. I don't need to know more." This is not evasion. This is a woman who has spent four decades learning the difference between knowledge that serves and knowledge that unsettles, and who has decided, with the same patient deliberation that characterizes everything here, to restrict herself to the former.

The other old ones are similar — long-term residents who have become less individuals than organs of the town, performing functions that are not written down anywhere because the functions did not exist before the people grew into them. There is Drehl, who maintains the Great Pumps and has maintained them for thirty-one years and who can hear, in the change of the drone, which bearing is wearing and which valve is leaking, the way a doctor hears the heart through a stethoscope. There is Sohl, who runs the borer-powder refinery and who has developed, over decades, a sensitivity to the quality of the powder that allows him to determine which vein a given batch came from by its smell alone — a skill that no one has asked him to develop, that has no commercial or practical application beyond the refinement of an already adequate product, but that he has developed anyway, because when you live in one place long enough and that place is Verkhol-Above, you become precise in ways that have no justification and every justification.

## The Visitors

There are always a few. The mule trains bring them, or they come on their own, drawn by rumor or curiosity or the particular compulsion that afflicts certain people who hear about a place where the world is not quite finished and feel an obligation to see it for themselves. They stay in the lower district, in a building that was once a warehouse and is now a guesthouse with six cots and a stove that the visitors are expected to tend themselves.

They do not stay long. The altitude wears at them — the tingling in the fingertips, the shortness of breath, the way the dim light begins to feel, after two or three days, less like a condition and more like a presence, something that is actively dimming them. The fog sits on them differently than it sits on the residents. The residents have made peace with the moisture; it is their medium, their context. The visitors fight it. They wipe their faces. They wring their hair. They pull their coats tighter and peer into the flat, directionless light and try to make out the far end of a walkway and cannot, and the effort of trying exhausts them in a way that has nothing to do with their eyes.

Some of them come because of the weird. They have heard about the Fold House, the wrong-echo corridor, the impossible stairway. They come with measuring tapes and inclinometers and notebooks, and they measure and they record and they leave, and their measurements are consistent with every measurement taken before, and they add nothing to the understanding of the phenomena because the phenomena are not, in any meaningful sense, understood — they are *accommodated*. The visitors do not understand this. They believe that sufficient measurement will yield explanation. The residents do not discourage this belief. They watch the visitors measure with the same expression they wear when they watch the fog roll in: patient, slightly amused, aware that this too will pass.

A few visitors stay. One or two a year, perhaps. Something shifts in them during their visit — not a revelation, not a crisis, just a slow, quiet recognition that the dimness is not a deficit but a different kind of sufficiency. They stop wiping their faces. They stop peering into the fog. They begin, without quite deciding to, to see the way the residents see: diffusely, comfortably, without straining. When this happens, the town absorbs them with the same patient inevitability with which it absorbs everything. A cot opens in a crew's quarters. A task is assigned. The new resident begins the process of learning the town, which is not a process of learning at all but of *acclimation*, of letting the place settle into the body the way the foundation of a house settles into the ground: slowly, under pressure, until the two are indistinguishable.

## The Hopper-Rats, Reconsidered

The hopper-rats are the town's other population, and they are — this needs to be said carefully — not animals in the way the residents are people. They are something else. Something that occupies a category that the residents have not named because naming it would require acknowledging the category, and acknowledging the category would require reconsidering the relationship between the living and the built and the unfinished, and this reconsideration is not something anyone in Verkhol-Above has time for.

There are hundreds of them. Thousands, possibly. They nest in the pipe lattice, in the warm gaps between conduits, in the spaces behind walls where the granite meets the iron clamps. They breed steadily but not explosively — their population seems to regulate itself in relation to the town's, as though there is some equilibrium that neither the rats nor the residents have consciously established but that both maintain through the simple act of coexisting. The residents feed them. The residents do not think of this as feeding them. They think of it as leaving scraps in designated areas, which is a distinction without a difference but one that the residents are committed to maintaining.

The hopper-rats are not pets. They are not domesticated. They are not even, in the conventional sense, tame. They simply *belong* here in a way that is as absolute and unremarkable as the pipes belonging to the buildings. They run the conduits in single-file processions that follow routes the residents have never mapped but that are clearly established — the same rats, or the same route, day after day, passing the same points at the same times, a transit system that operates on a schedule no one set and no one needs to set because it regulates itself. They pause when the Pumps change rhythm. They mass at the town's edges before fog arrives, as though they can feel the moisture coming through the stone before it reaches the air. They avoid certain corridors — the wrong-echo corridor, the approach to the Fold House, the stairway that descends wrong — and the residents have learned to avoid these places too, not because the rats avoid them but because the rats' avoidance is a data point that the residents incorporate without thinking about it, the way you incorporate the behavior of a barometer into your decision about whether to carry an umbrella.

The green of their eyes is the thing that resists explanation. It is not the green of any biological pigment. It is the green of the moss, but it is not moss-green. It is the green of the Columns' hum felt in the teeth, translated to light. It is a green that belongs to this place, that could not exist outside it, and when a hopper-rat looks at you with those eyes, you are being assessed by something that understands the town at a level you do not and that is not inclined to share its understanding. The residents accept this. The residents accept almost everything. The residents have made a practice of acceptance so thorough and so unexamined that it has become, itself, a kind of architecture — a structure of accommodation that holds the town together as surely as the iron clamps hold the granite.

## What Lives in the Mountain

There is something else. The residents know this the way they know the Pumps are turning — not by direct evidence but by vibration, by implication, by the accumulated testimony of decades of small, persistent signals that individually prove nothing and collectively prove everything.

The shafts go down. The Pumps draw from somewhere. The water that rises is heavy, is wrong, is 4 degrees regardless of the cold. The water has been rising for decades. The water has not been exhausted. Whatever feeds the shafts — aquifer, river, something else — is vast and patient and does not respond to the draw of the Pumps the way a finite reservoir would respond. The flow does not diminish in drought. The flow does not increase in flood. The flow is constant, and its constancy is not mechanical but deliberate, as though the source is not a passive deposit of water but an active participant in the arrangement, providing what is taken, neither more nor less, with the steady, measured generosity of something that has made a decision and is maintaining it.

Nobody has been to the bottom. The shafts are too narrow for a person to descend. Cameras lowered on cables have produced images that are inconclusive — dark, distorted, the lenses fogging almost immediately with the same condensation that fogs the town. The images show what might be walls. What might be chambers. What might be formations that are neither geological nor biological but some third thing that the camera cannot categorize and the human eye cannot parse. The images are filed in the Convergence's meeting room, in a drawer beneath the granite table, and they are not discussed.

Maret says: "The mountain is alive." She says this without drama, without mystery, with the same flat practicality she applies to everything. The mountain is alive the way a pump is alive — not conscious, not intentional, but *operational*, a system in motion, a process that is ongoing. The borers grind through its veins. The Columns hum with its frequency. The water rises from its depths. The stone shifts and settles and reconsiders itself. The weirdness — the Fold House, the wrong-echo corridor, the impossible geometries — is not an anomaly but a symptom, the external evidence of a process that is happening inside the mountain and that the mountain, like everything else here, is undertaking slowly, patiently, at a pace that makes the word *process* seem too urgent.

What lives in the mountain is the mountain. And the mountain is not finished. And the mountain is not building toward anything that the residents can identify. And the mountain is not dangerous, or if it is dangerous, it is dangerous in the way that geology is dangerous — at a timescale that makes human urgency irrelevant, at a scale that makes human concern almost endearing. The mountain is becoming something. The town exists on its surface. The town is, in some sense, part of what the mountain is becoming, the way a barnacle is part of what a whale is becoming, the way moss is part of what a stone is becoming. The residents understand this. They do not say it. They do not need to say it. They live on the mountain and they live with the mountain and they live, in some way that no other word captures but *inside*, inside the slow, unfinished, ongoing process of a world that is still deciding what it is.

## The Town Itself, As a Living Thing

This is the final thing that lives here, and it is the thing that the residents would never name but that every visitor eventually feels: the town is alive. Not metaphorically. Not in the way that all towns are *alive* with activity and commerce and human energy. Verkhol-Above is alive the way the mountain is alive — operationally, systemically, as a thing in process. The Pumps are its heart. The pipes are its circulation. The fog collection system is its respiration, breathing in moisture and distributing it through the gutters and cisterns. The hopper-rats are its nerves, or its sensors, running its conduits and reporting, through their behavior, its internal state. The borers are its digestion, breaking down the mineral veins and producing, through the alchemy of their biology, the powder that feeds the economy that sustains the human population that maintains the machines that drive the Pumps that bring up the water that feeds the cycle.

The residents are part of it. They are not separate from the system. They are not operators standing outside the machine. They are components — essential, irreplaceable, but components nonetheless, performing functions that the town needs performed, and receiving in exchange the things the town provides: water, shelter, purpose, the slow, deep satisfaction of being part of something that is working. The town does not need them in the way a machine needs an operator. It needs them in the way a body needs certain cells — not for instruction but for maintenance, for the constant, small repairs that keep the system running while the larger process, whatever it is, continues.

The town is not conscious. Probably. It does not communicate, does not respond to being addressed, does not recognize its residents the way a dog recognizes its owner. But it *accommodates* them. It accommodates the way the Fold House accommodates the extra space — quietly, persistently, without acknowledging the discrepancy. The town holds its people the way the mountain holds the town: as a feature of the landscape, as something that is here, as something that the ongoing process has not finished with and has not rejected.

And the people, in return, hold the town. They maintain its pipes. They run its Pumps. They collect its water and harvest its powder and raise its children and leave its scraps and walk its walkways every morning, touching the walls, listening to the vibration, feeling for the shifts and the settling and the slow, patient, unfinished becoming of everything, and they do not leave, because leaving would require going somewhere where the world has already decided what it is, and they have learned — slowly, over decades, over generations — to prefer the alternative.

They live here. In the dimness. In the fog. In the held breath of the Pumps. In the world's unfinished sentence.

They live here, and the sentence continues, and nobody knows how it ends, and this is, for them, not a source of anxiety but a condition of life, as fundamental and as unremarkable as the altitude, as the moisture, as the green eyes of the hopper-rats watching from the pipes, assessing, always assessing, with a thoroughness that is neither hostile nor friendly but simply *attentive*, the way the mountain is attentive, the way the town is attentive, the way everything here is attentive to everything else, waiting — patiently, slowly, at a pace that makes the word *waiting* seem too urgent — for whatever comes next.
