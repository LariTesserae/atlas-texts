# What Lives Here

---

## I. The Ones Who Were Always Here

Begin with the obvious, which is not obvious at all.

The engines are alive.

This is the thing that takes longest to understand, and it is the thing that, once understood, reorganizes everything else. You stand with your hand on a pipe and you feel the peristaltic squeeze — one beat every eight seconds — and you think: *mechanical*. You think: *process*. You think: *function*. But the pipe under your palm is warm the way skin is warm. The vibration does not come from a turbine or a piston or any moving part you can identify, because there are no moving parts. There are no parts at all. The conduits are continuous with the rock. The engine-blocks are extrusions of the mineral substrate, yes — but they are not *dead* extrusions. They are not stalactites. They are not crystals. They are organs.

The undermantle is a body.

This is not metaphor. Or rather: the metaphor is the closest language can come before it has to admit that what it is describing is not metaphorical. The pipes carry steam because the body carries steam — because steam is the medium of heat transfer, because this body has evolved (or been evolving, or been grown, or simply *become*) a circulatory system that uses water vapor as its blood. The engines throb because tissue contracts. The peristalsis is peristalsis. The joints weep because joints weep. The system is closed because a body is closed. The fissure you entered through is a wound that never fully closed, and the pressure differential you felt in your ears was the difference between the inside of an organism and the outside of a world.

Do not ask what the body is for. A body is not *for* anything. A body is. The undermantle is a stratum of the planet that has become metabolic, that has crossed whatever threshold separates geology from biology — or perhaps never recognized the threshold, perhaps the threshold was a fiction of surface-dwellers who needed categories to survive. Down here, the category is: *it lives*. Down here, the category is: *it has always lived, and the machines are its skeleton, and the steam is its breath, and the darkness is the inside of its skin.*

The pulse is its heartbeat. One beat every eight seconds. Slow. Geological, almost. But a heart.

## II. The First Children

The purple growths on the pipes are not vegetation.

You knew this already — you knew it when you touched them and they did not respond, when you looked at them and saw no leaves, no chlorophyll, no architecture that belongs to any plant you have ever known. But the word *vegetation* is a convenience. The word *plant* is a placeholder. What they are is older than plants. What they are is the first thing the body grew that was not the body itself.

Consider: an organ does not grow things on its own surface. A liver does not sprout. A bone does not develop foliage. But the undermantle is not a liver or a bone. It is something without an analogue, and the growths on its pipes are — what? The body's attempt at diversity? The body's first experiment with *other*?

They are symbiotic. This is the best reading, and it is probably correct. Their roots — those dark, threading, gap-penetrating structures — do not damage the pipes. They seal them. Where a joint weeps, the roots have grown in and around the imperfection, and the weeping slows, and the condensation at that point diminishes, and the pipe surface grows slightly warmer as the insulation improves. The growths are performing maintenance. They are patching leaks. They are doing what a body does with a scab, what a tree does with a wound: closing, sealing, preserving the integrity of the system.

In return, they receive steam. Heat. Moisture. Minerals carried in the vapor from deeper strata. They feed on the body's circulation the way a fetus feeds on the placenta — not parasitically, not exploitatively, but in a relationship so intimate that the line between *self* and *other* dissolves. They are the body and they are not the body. They are the first distinction the body ever made.

The flowers are something different. The flowers are — you do not have the word. The open hand with too many fingers, glistening, warm, emitting its thread-thin vapor. It was not reproductive. Or if it was, it was reproductive in a way that does not involve seeds or spores or any mechanism you can recognize. It was *expressive*. It was the growth doing something other than growing. It was the growth *speaking*, perhaps, or *breathing* in a way that was different from the body's breathing, a signal, a release, a marking of territory or a call or a statement of existence that said: *I am here and I am not the pipe I grow on and I am not the body that grew the pipe and I am something new.*

You watched it. You left. You did not understand what you saw. You will not. That is acceptable. Not everything that lives here is for you to understand.

## III. The Swimmers

The small dark things that move in schools through the warm air — call them the swimmers, for now, because the word *fly* implies sky and the word *fish* implies water and they are in neither. They are in the medium of the undermantle itself: the damp, warm, steam-laden air that fills the space between pipes and floor and ceiling.

They are the most numerous. You feel them against your face — a displacement, a brushing, a texture — and when you hold still, you see them: dark bodies, fast, navigating the pipe-networks with the precision of blood cells in capillaries. They know this place. They have always known this place. Their knowledge is not cognitive; it is *anatomical*. They know the pipes the way your tongue knows your teeth. Every junction, every curve, every weeping joint and steam-vent is mapped in their bodies, in the particular configuration of whatever sense-organ they use to navigate — and you cannot determine what that is. They have no eyes that you can see. They have no antennae. They have no echolocation call. They move by something else, something that might be heat-gradient or pressure-sensitivity or a sense that does not exist on the surface, a sense that the undermantle's particular conditions made possible and that no surface-dweller has ever needed or developed.

What they are — taxonomically, phylogenetically, in the language of surface biology — you cannot say. They might be vertebrates. They might be arthropods. They might be something that the categories do not cover, something that evolved in the dark from a lineage that never saw light, that was never shaped by the sun, that developed along a branch of life so distant from your own that the word *animal* is merely a courtesy.

They feed. On the condensation film, probably — on the thin layer of mineral-rich water that coats every surface, on the micro-organisms that live in that film, on the invisible ecology of bacteria and simpler things that you cannot see but that must exist, that must be there, because the swimmers are there and the swimmers are eating something and the something must be eating something else and the chain must go down to a foundation. The foundation is the body. The body's steam carries minerals. The minerals deposit on surfaces. The deposits feed micro-organisms. The micro-organisms feed the swimmers. The swimmers feed the larger things. It is a system. It is complete.

They navigate past you without interest. You are not part of their ecology. You are too large to eat, too strange to approach, too brief to matter. They have been here for millennia. You have been here for hours. They do not alter course for you. They do not avoid you. They simply move, and you happen to be where they are moving, and they go around you the way water goes around a stone — without malice, without curiosity, without acknowledgment.

This is comforting in a way you did not expect. To be irrelevant. To be beneath notice. To stand in a living system and be *transparent to it* — not hidden, not camouflaged, simply uninteresting. The undermantle does not care about you. The swimmers do not care about you. The body does not know you are here. You are inside something alive and it has not noticed, and this is the closest thing to safety this place offers.

## IV. The Clickers

The middle-distance fauna. The territorial ones. The ones that warn you with a touch.

They are larger than the swimmers — perhaps the size of your hand, perhaps larger, you cannot be sure because you have never seen one clearly. You hear them. The clicking is their language, or their signaling, or their breathing, or the sound of their bodies against the stone. The scraping is movement — hard surfaces on rock, limbs or claws or shells or whatever they have that makes contact with the floor and produces that sound.

They are territorial because territory is meaningful here. The pipe-network creates a topology — channels and junctions and open spaces and narrow passages — and within that topology, resources are distributed unevenly. A section of pipe that weeps heavily is valuable. A steam-vent is valuable. A pool of warm, mineral-rich water in a hollow between conduits is valuable. The clickers hold these resources. They claim them. They defend them.

Their territories are invisible to you but absolute to them. You cross a boundary and the clicking changes — becomes rhythmic, becomes aggressive, becomes a declaration. *You are here. I am here. This is mine.* And then the touch — not a bite, not a strike, a *contact*, deliberate, communicative. A statement made with the body: *leave*.

You leave. Not because you are afraid — though you are, a little, the way you are afraid of anything that is real and present and has intentions you cannot read — but because the touch was clear. It was a sentence. It was language. Not your language, but language nonetheless, and the appropriate response to a clear sentence in a language you do not speak is to obey it.

The clickers have culture. You find the evidence at the feeding-sites — remains arranged in patterns. Not random scatter, not the debris of careless eating, but *arrangements*. Bones — or whatever serves as bones — laid in lines. Shells or husks or exoskeletons stacked. The same pattern repeating at different sites, across kilometers of undermantle. The patterns are not functional. They do not serve a purpose that you can identify. They are not caches, not larders, not markers. They are *aesthetic*. Or they are *commemorative*. Or they are *habitual* in a way that has become *meaningful* — the way a path through a forest becomes a trail becomes a road becomes a tradition. The clickers do something with their dead, or with the remains of what they eat, that goes beyond survival. They arrange. They order. They make.

This is the thing that unnerves you. Not the dark. Not the size. Not the body of the undermantle pulsing beneath your feet. This: that something here has a relationship to death that involves *choice*. That something here looks at the remains of a meal and decides to place them *this way* rather than *that way*. That something here has an aesthetic preference. That something here, in the dark, in the warm, in the damp, in the endless pipe-field, has *taste*.

## V. The Slow One

The shape that passed between you and the steam-vent. The silhouette the size of a car. The slow, deliberate gait. The thing that did not notice you or did not care.

You have seen it once. You will see it again. You are not sure you want to.

It was not afraid. This is the detail that stays. In a place with predators — and there are predators, you have seen the evidence at the feeding-sites — the Slow One moved without fear. It moved the way a thing moves when it is the largest thing in its world, when it has no natural enemies, when its body is its own protection. Slow. Heavy. Confident. The gait of something that has been here for a very long time and intends to be here for a very long time more.

It made no sound. Or it made a sound so deep that it merged with the engine-throb. This is the possibility that disturbs you: that the eight-second pulse you have been feeling in the stone is not the undermantle's heartbeat. That it is *the Slow One's* heartbeat. That the body and the Slow One are so large, so deeply connected to each other, that their rhythms have synchronized, or were always synchronized, or were always the same rhythm. That the Slow One is not *in* the undermantle but *of* it. That the Slow One is the undermantle's *mind* — or its *voice* — or its *face*.

No. That is projection. That is the primate brain reaching for agency, for a person, for something to address. The Slow One is an animal. It is large. It is old. It moves through the pipe-field the way a whale moves through the ocean — slowly, with the patience of a body that does not need to hurry, that has never needed to hurry, that occupies its niche so completely that hurry is inconceivable. It eats. It rests. It breathes. It lives.

But: the sound. The low, resonant tone. The groan-song that travels through the rock and arrives in your bones. Fifteen seconds long, coming from a different direction each time. You have heard it three times. The Slow One is large enough to produce that sound. The Slow One is large enough that its voice would travel through stone the way a whale's voice travels through water. You do not know. You cannot know. But when you hear the sound — when it enters your sternum and vibrates there, when it fills the undermantle for fifteen seconds and then fades — you think of the Slow One. You think of its silhouette against the steam. You think of its patience.

You think of something that has been alive in this body, in this darkness, for longer than the concept of *longer* has meaning, and that sings, occasionally, for reasons that are its own.

## VI. The Predators

You have not seen them. You have seen what they leave.

The feeding-sites. The arranged remains. The patterns that the clickers make — but the patterns are made from the leavings of predation, which means the predators are present, are active, are killing and eating in the dark at a rate sufficient to leave multiple sites across the undermantle's expanse.

They are fast. This you infer from the remains — clean, disarticulated, the soft parts consumed entirely, the hard parts (bones? shells? exoskeletons?) scattered with a precision that suggests rapid dismantling. Whatever killed these things did not linger. Whatever killed these things was efficient. Whatever killed these things has done this many times and has refined its technique to a level that leaves no waste.

They are larger than the clickers. This you infer from the remains themselves — the clickers are substantial enough to leave identifiable hard parts, and the hard parts are substantial enough to suggest bodies of meaningful size, and a predator that can dismantle such a body quickly is itself of meaningful size, or works in groups, or both.

They are quiet. This you infer from the absence of sound. The clicking of the clickers is constant, ubiquitous, the background chatter of a populous species. When the clicking stops — and it does stop, occasionally, in certain areas, at certain times — the silence is sudden and total, and you know that something has entered the space that the clickers fear, and the fear is great enough that they have gone silent, and the silence is the warning, and the silence is the *sign*.

You have been in the silence twice. Both times, you stopped. Both times, you listened. Both times, you heard nothing — nothing but the engine-throb, nothing but the steam-hiss, nothing but the condensation-patter — and then, once, far away, a sound that might have been breathing. Slow. Measured. The breathing of something waiting. The breathing of something that has detected you and is deciding.

You backed away. The clicking resumed. You did not see what had stopped the clicking. You do not want to.

The predators are part of the system. They must be. The equilibrium of the undermantle — the balance that has held for millennia, the persistence that is not growth and not decay but *maintenance* — requires them. Without predators, the swimmers would overpopulate, the clickers would overpopulate, the resources would be consumed, the system would collapse. The predators are the regulator. They are the undermantle's immune system, perhaps, or its digestive system — the mechanism by which excess is returned to the body, by which the dead are processed back into mineral and moisture and incorporated once more into the cycle.

They are not evil. They are not malicious. They are necessary. But when the clicking stops and the silence arrives, you do not think about ecology. You think about teeth. You think about claws. You think about the hard parts of things scattered in patterns on the stone floor, and you back away, and you are glad — genuinely, deeply, primate-glad — that you cannot see what is there.

## VII. The Microcosm

And beneath all of this — beneath the swimmers and the clickers and the Slow One and the predators and the purple growths and the body of the undermantle itself — there is a layer of life you cannot see but know must exist.

The condensation film on every surface is not sterile. It cannot be. It is warm, it is wet, it is mineral-rich, and it has been here for as long as everything else. That film is an ecology. That film contains organisms — bacteria, or their analogues, or things that have no name because no one has ever seen them, things that live in a film of water one millimeter deep on the surface of a pipe that is an organ of a body that is a stratum of a planet, and that have lived there for millennia, and that are the foundation of everything above them.

The swimmers feed on them. Or the swimmers feed on something that feeds on them. Or the chain is longer, more complex, more intricate than you can trace in the dark with your inadequate eyes and your brief hours. But the foundation is there. The foundation is *the body itself* — the undermantle producing steam, the steam depositing minerals, the minerals feeding organisms, the organisms feeding organisms, the organisms feeding swimmers, the swimmers feeding clickers, the clickers feeding predators, the predators feeding — what? The Slow One? Each other? Returning to the body, eventually, through death and decomposition and the slow incorporation of their remains back into the mineral substrate from which the pipes grow?

The cycle is complete. The body feeds the film. The film feeds the life. The life feeds the body. It is not a food chain. It is a *food circle*. It is *Ouroboros*. The undermantle eats itself and grows and eats and grows, and the things that live in it are the mechanism of its eating, and the body is the mechanism of their living, and the distinction between *organism* and *environment* is — down here, in the dark, in the warm, in the damp — meaningless.

## VIII. The Ones Who Were Here Before

There is one more thing. One more presence. Not the body. Not the growths. Not the swimmers, not the clickers, not the Slow One, not the predators, not the invisible microcosm. Something else. Something you have not seen but have *felt*.

The erosion patterns on the walls. The channels. The grooves. You said they were the fossil remains of water-flow from a different time. But the patterns are too regular. The grooves are too even. They are not erosion. They are *carving*.

Someone — something — was here before the machines. Before the body. Before the undermantle became metabolic, something else was here, something that carved channels in the rock, that shaped the stone, that left marks that have survived for millennia in the walls of a space that no one on the surface knows exists. Something that might have been the *predecessor* of the undermantle, or might have been its *inhabitant*, or might have been something else entirely — something that found this layer of the planet and entered it and lived in it and shaped it and then — left? Died? Became?

You feel this presence in the same way you feel the swimmers against your face: as a displacement, as a texture, as a sense that something was *here*. Not is. Was. The presence is past tense. The presence is a *trace*. Whatever carved those channels is gone, or has changed into something you cannot recognize, or has become so deeply incorporated into the undermantle that it is indistinguishable from the body — the way a fertilized egg becomes the body, the way a seed becomes the tree, the way a thought becomes a mind.

Or: the presence is not past. The presence is *elsewhere*. The undermantle is continental in scale, and you have walked for hours, and you have seen a fraction of it, a corner, a glimpse. The carvers might be *there* — beyond the limit of your vision, beyond the reach of your footsteps, in a part of the undermantle where the pipes run differently and the steam flows differently and the ecology is different and the life is different and the darkness is different. The carvers might be alive. The carvers might be *many*. The carvers might have a relationship with the undermantle that is not the relationship you have inferred from your hours of walking — a relationship that is not symbiosis, not parasitism, not predation, but something else, something that your categories do not cover.

You do not know. You cannot know. You are a surface-dweller in a deep place, and your time here is limited by what you can carry and how long your light lasts — and you have no light, and you have been here for hours, and the eight-second pulse has become your clock, and the darkness is your medium, and the undermantle does not know you are here and does not care and continues.

## IX. What Lives Here, Ultimately

Everything. Everything lives here. The rock lives. The pipes live. The steam lives. The water lives. The darkness lives. The warmth lives. The pulse lives. The growths live. The swimmers live. The clickers live. The Slow One lives. The predators live. The microcosm lives. The carvers — if they are still here, if they are still here — live.

The undermantle is not a place where things live. The undermantle *is* the thing that is living. The distinction between habitat and inhabitant, between environment and organism, between the machine and the life it sustains — this distinction is a surface distinction, a sunlight distinction, a distinction that exists because up there, the world is divided into *things* and *places where things are*. Down here, the thing and the place are the same. The pipe is the animal and the animal is the pipe. The body is the ecology and the ecology is the body. The predator is the undermantle eating itself, and the prey is the undermantle feeding itself, and the microcosm is the undermantle digesting itself, and the growths are the undermantle healing itself, and the Slow One is the undermantle — walking, perhaps, in the way that a body walks in dreams, in the way that a mind walks through its own architecture, slow and heavy and patient and ancient.

What lives here? One thing. Expressed as many things. A stratum of the planet that has become a body, and a body that has become an ecology, and an ecology that has become a world, and a world that does not need the surface, does not need the light, does not need anything except its own persistence — its own eight-second pulse, its own steam-breath, its own warm, damp, dark, living continuity.

You are inside it. You are welcome in the way that a throat welcomes food: temporarily, functionally, without sentiment. You will leave. It will continue. It has been continuing for longer than your species has existed, and it will continue for longer than your species will exist, and the things that live in it will live in it, and the things that die in it will die in it, and the carvers — if they are still here — will carve, and the Slow One will sing its fifteen-second song through the stone, and the swimmers will swim through the warm air, and the clickers will click and arrange and mark and *mean*, and the predators will silence the clicking and take what they need, and the growths will seal the leaking pipes, and the body will pulse, and the steam will rise, and the water will fall, and the dark will hold it all, and the warmth will hold it all, and the undermantle will be the undermantle, and the undermantle will live.
