# What Lives Here

The first thing to understand is that nothing here was invited. Everything arrived the way water arrives in a basement — because the conditions were right and the door was open, or at least open enough, a gap at the top of a crimped culvert where the joint separated, and then it was occupied.

---

## The Moss

Call it what it is: a bryophyte, but probably not one you'd find in any field guide. The dark stripe-moss that grows in the tooling grooves doesn't need light. It has never needed light. It uses the piezoelectric hum — the same vibration that makes the walls glow — as its energy source, converting mechanical vibration into chemical fuel the way surface plants convert sunlight. Its chloroplasts, if it has them, are vestigial, retained like an appendix in a body that no longer digests the same food. What it actually runs on is harder to say. The mineral content of the weeping water, maybe. The calcium and iron leaching from the basalt itself. The faint radiation of the luminescent veins, which is more than visual — there's a particle component, a slow emission that the moss has learned to metabolize over however many centuries it's been here.

It grows in stripes because the tooling grooves concentrate water flow. The architects of this place — whoever or whatever they were — cut those channels for drainage, and the moss found them and filled them the way moss fills any groove that holds moisture. It's opportunistic in the way all moss is opportunistic: not ambitious, not aggressive, just patient enough to occupy any surface that offers the minimal conditions for survival. In this case the conditions are: wet, cold, vibrating, faintly luminous. The moss doesn't thrive. It persists. There's a difference. Thriving implies surplus. Persistence implies a balanced budget, every calorie accounted for, nothing wasted on growth that isn't needed.

The blackness of its color is a pigment adaptation — probably melanin, probably produced in response to the mineral radiation. It's the same reason some cave fish are albino: you adapt to what's present, and you adapt away from what isn't. No light means no chlorophyll means no green. The blue-black tint that reads as almost green under certain angles of wall-glow is an artifact of the light itself, not an intrinsic color. The moss is black. It is the black of deep water, of closed eyes, of a room you enter and can't see the back wall of.

It reproduces by fragmentation. Pieces break off in the water flow and wash down the walls and across the floor and lodge in other grooves, other cracks, other seams where basalt meets basalt. Some fragments reach the gear. Some settle on the floor. Most wash under the lip of stone at the far wall and are gone, carried wherever the water goes — into the territory of questions this place does not answer.

The moss is older than the gear. Or the gear is older than the moss. There's no way to tell. What's certain is that the moss was not here when the room was built — nothing was, the room was sterile, the room was new basalt and fresh-machined metal and the first slow turning of a shaft that descended from somewhere below. The moss came later. It came the way life comes to every surface given enough time: as spore, as fragment, as the airborne possibility of itself, carried on water that found its way in through seams in the stone, through the microscopic fractures in basalt that hasn't fully set, through the gap at the top of the culvert that you crawled through to get here.

It found a niche. It filled it. It has been filling it for long enough that the gear's metal surface has developed its own moss — a different strain, thinner, more film than fiber, adapted to the specific conditions of the gear's rotation: periodic submersion, periodic exposure, the mechanical vibration of the teeth, the slick of mineral deposit that forms on metal that's been wet for decades. This gear-moss is to the wall-moss what a house cat is to a tiger: the same lineage, compressed and softened by the specific constraints of its habitat.

## The Floor Creatures

Three kinds. Maybe more. The taxonomy is difficult because the creatures are small and the light is bad and nobody has ever brought a hand lens down here, and nobody will.

**The sinuous ones** are the easiest to see because they move. Long, thin, pale — translucent enough that you can see their digestive tracts through their bodies, which is how you know they're eating: there's color in there, dark threads of moss-film and mineral sediment that fill them in segments like a striped ribbon. They're annelids, probably. Or something analogous to annelids — the convergent evolution of a body plan that works at this scale, in this medium, at this flow rate. They move by extending a anterior probe, anchoring it, and pulling the rest of their body forward in a contraction that takes about two seconds. They move against the current, which means they're expending energy to stay in place. This is a choice. The current would carry them north, under the lip, into wherever the water goes. They don't want to go there. Whatever is north of the lip is not for them.

They cling to the basalt with what are probably setae — tiny bristle-like structures that adhere to wet stone through surface tension and intermolecular force rather than suction or glue. When the gear's rotation pushes a pulse of water past them, they flatten against the stone and wait. When the pulse passes, they resume their grazing. They eat the mineral film that condenses on the basalt out of the flowing water — a biofilm, really, a layer of dissolved minerals and organic compounds and bacteria-sized things that are too small to see but that are there, that are part of the ecosystem, that are the base of the food chain in a room with no sun and no soil and no organic input except what the water brings in and what the moss contributes.

**The round, armoured ones** are different. They don't move against the current. They move with it, but they stop when they reach a good spot — a depression in the floor, a seam between stone sections, the calm water behind a gear-tooth. They have shells. Not calcareous shells — something darker, denser, a chitinous or keratinous plate that covers their dorsal surface and locks down over their edges when they're disturbed. They look like tiny armadillos. They look like ticks. They are neither. They are what happens when a creature evolves to survive in a room where everything is wet and cold and the only variable is whether the water is moving fast or slow.

The armoured ones eat the moss. Not the living moss — they can't break through the fiber — but the dead fragments that wash down from the walls and settle on the floor. They're detritivores. They process the moss's waste the way the moss processes the stone's waste the way the stone processes the water's waste. It's a cycle. The gear turns and the water flows and the moss grows and dies and fragments and the armoured ones eat the fragments and excrete the minerals back into the water and the water carries it back up the walls through capillary action in the striations and the moss re-absorbs it. Nothing enters. Nothing leaves. The system is closed, or nearly closed, and has been closed long enough for the creatures to lose the adaptations they would need to survive outside it.

They have no eyes. They have no pigmentation. They have no instinct for avoidance — if you touch them, they don't flee, they just clamp down and wait. They have never needed to flee. Nothing in this room has ever been large enough to threaten them until you arrived, and they don't have a category for you.

**The third kind** — the leech-things — are the ones that make people uncomfortable. They're the largest of the floor creatures, which isn't saying much: the size of a grain of rice, maybe slightly bigger. They move by extending and contracting, stretching forward and then pulling their hindquarters up to meet their anterior, the classic inchworm locomotion. They're translucent, but less so than the sinuous ones — there's a density to them, an opacity, that suggests more complex internal structure. You can't see their digestive tract. What you can see, if you look closely enough, is a darker spot at their anterior end that might be a mouth and might be something else.

They're predators. They eat the other floor creatures. Not the armoured ones — the shells are too hard. The sinuous ones. They wait in the calmer water near the walls and when a sinuous one passes they extend — fast, faster than anything else in the room moves — and close on it and hold it and absorb it. The absorption is gradual. It takes minutes. During that time the leech-thing is visibly fuller, visibly darker, the consumed prey showing through its body as a shadow that slowly dissolves.

They are the reason the sinuous ones move against the current. The current carries the leech-things. The sinuous ones know this, at the level of instinct or stimulus-response or whatever passes for knowing in a creature with no brain and no eyes and no ears. They stay upstream. The leech-things drift. Everyone survives.

The floor population is dense. Hundreds per square foot. But the biomass is low — these are small creatures in a large room, and the room is large enough that their accumulated presence reads as motion rather than substance, the way a dust mote in a sunbeam is motion rather than object. The floor is alive the way a river is alive: not because of any single thing in it but because of the aggregate of all the things in it, moving together, in the same direction, at the same speed, with the same patience.

## The Gear Riders

The oval creatures on the gear itself are something else entirely.

They're larger than the floor creatures — thumbnail-sized, as described, but that's a deceptive scale. They look like isopods. They look like the trilobites you see in museum dioramas, the flat, segmented, many-legged things that crawled across Ordovician sea floors. They have antennae — four of them, two long, two short — and they use them constantly, tapping the surface of the gear as they walk, testing the moss-film, testing the water, testing the air. They are the only creatures in this room that seem to pay attention to their environment. They are the only creatures in this room that seem to have a concept of their environment.

They ride the gear because the gear is where their food is. The gear-moss — the thin film that grows on the metal — is their sole food source. They graze it with mouthparts that are adapted to scraping: mandibular, sideways-working, the kind of mouths that evolved for stripping film from flat surfaces. They move across the gear's upper surface in patterns that aren't random — they graze in lines, following the grain of the metal, and they don't re-graze areas they've already cleared. This implies memory. This implies something that functions like memory, even if it's only chemical — a trail marker, a taste aversion, a signal that says *this patch is spent; move on*.

When the gear's rotation carries them below the waterline, they don't react. The water covers them and they keep grazing, walking across the submerged metal, eating the moss that grows there. They can breathe underwater. Or they don't breathe. Or what they do instead of breathing is something that works in both media — cutaneous respiration, probably, the diffusion of gases across a thin membrane, which works the same way in air and in water if the membrane is thin enough and the gases are available. In this room, they are. The moss produces oxygen. The water carries it. The air holds it. The gear-riders take it.

They're not mindless. Watch one long enough and you'll see it make a decision — not a complex decision, not a human decision, but a choice between two directions, one of which leads to more moss and one of which leads to less. It will pause. It will tap its antennae. It will turn. This is more than the floor creatures do. The floor creatures flow. The gear-riders navigate.

They've been here longer than the floor creatures. The floor creatures are generalists — they could survive in any wet, cold, dark environment. The gear-riders are specialists. They are adapted to the gear specifically: to its rotation rate, to its temperature differential (the metal is slightly warmer than the stone, probably because of the shaft's friction deep below), to the specific moss that grows on the specific alloy of the gear's metal. They can't live on the walls. They can't live on the floor. They can only live on the gear. If the gear stopped turning, they'd die — not immediately, not dramatically, but within a few generations the moss would overgrow them or the water would stagnate or the temperature would equalize and the specific conditions they need would be gone.

The gear keeps turning. It has always kept turning. They have always been here.

Maybe.

## The Ceiling Growth

These are the hardest to describe because they're the hardest to see. Not because they're hidden — they're twelve feet above you at the room's center, lower at the edges — but because they don't correspond to any category of living thing you're familiar with. They look like stalactites. They look like bone. They look like something that grew in a body and was then exposed to air and kept growing because it didn't know it was supposed to stop.

They are warm. Not warm in absolute terms — warmer than four degrees, warmer than the basalt, warmer than the water. Warm enough that when you stand under them you can feel the temperature differential on the top of your head. They're producing heat. This is the most anomalous thing about them. Everything else in this room is at equilibrium with the cold. The moss, the creatures, the water, the stone — all at four degrees, all the same. The ceiling growths are at seven or eight. They're generating heat through some metabolic process that isn't like any metabolism you can identify. Fermentation, maybe. Or something older. Something that predates the oxygen economy.

The amber glow is their own luminescence — not piezoelectric, not mineral, but biological. They produce light the way some deep-sea organisms produce light: by their own chemistry, at their own cost, for reasons that are not clear. The light doesn't illuminate anything. It doesn't reach the floor. It barely reaches the gear. It exists in the six-inch radius of the growths themselves, a warm halo in the cold blue-white of the room, and it fades into nothing at its edges.

What they are — taxonomically, structurally, evolutionarily — is not answerable from observation alone. They have the texture of bone, which means they have a mineral matrix — calcium phosphate, probably, or calcium carbonate — but they're growing, which means the matrix is alive, which means the matrix is being deposited by cells, which means there are cells, which means there's tissue, which means there's something up there that organized itself into tissue and then into bone and then into these stalactite shapes that hang from the ceiling and drip and glow and hum.

The drips are water. The growths absorb moisture from the condensing air — the room's saturated humidity, the same moisture that forms on every surface — and they process it and they excrete it. The excreted water is different from the water that goes in. It's cleaner. It's been filtered. The growths are removing something from the water — minerals, maybe, or the dissolved organic compounds that the moss and the floor creatures contribute — and using them to build themselves. The drips that fall from them and hit the gear-hub with that bell-tone *tink* every six seconds are the cleanest water in the room.

They've been here the longest. The basalt is fresh. The gear is sharp. The moss came after. The floor creatures came after the moss. The gear-riders came after the gear. But the ceiling growths were here before the room was — or they were here when the room was made, or they were made with the room, or they are part of the room in a way that the other living things aren't. The ceiling above them is stippled with their earlier attempts — tiny nubs, failed starts, the beginnings of growths that didn't take. This implies a colonization process. This implies that the ceiling growths were not installed. They arrived. They found the ceiling. They grew.

But the ceiling was made. The tooling marks are there. The basalt is fresh. Someone made this ceiling and then someone or something grew on it.

The leading theory — if you could call it a theory, if you could call it leading — is that the ceiling growths are the builders. Or the descendants of the builders. Or the builders' remains, reorganized into something that doesn't remember what it was but still knows how to grow in this specific room, on this specific ceiling, in this specific air.

This is the theory the landscape doesn't confirm or deny.

## The Sound That Is Almost Voices

The hum. The wall-hum. The cumulative vibration of the mineral veins.

It's not voices. It's not language. But it resolves toward language the longer you listen — not because it changes but because your auditory system is a pattern-matching instrument and it will find patterns in anything given long enough. You hear words in the hum the way you hear faces in clouds. The sounds aren't communicating. They're producing. The piezoelectric effect — the same phenomenon that makes lighter sparks and quartz crystals and sonar transducers — is converting mechanical stress into electrical charge and electrical charge into vibration and vibration into sound, and the sound is what you hear when you stand in this room long enough to stop hearing the gear and start hearing everything else.

The mineral veins are under stress. The stress comes from the gear — the gear's vibration, transmitted through the stone, a constant low-frequency load that flexes the crystal structure of the luminescent mineral and produces both the light and the hum. They're the same thing. The light is the hum made visible. The hum is the light made audible. The room is a transducer. It converts rotation into vibration into light and sound and the whole thing is one process, one event, one ongoing occurrence that started when the shaft began to turn and will end when the shaft stops, and the shaft has not stopped.

The moss grows where the hum is strongest — in the tooling grooves where the stone is thinnest and the vibration is most intense. The moss isn't just tolerating the hum. It's eating it. The hum is its food. The mechanical energy that the gear produces and the stone transmits and the veins convert is the energy source that powers the moss's metabolism, that replaces sunlight, that makes life possible in a room with no external input except water and stone and the slow rotation of a shaft that comes from somewhere below.

And the ceiling growths glow amber where the hum is weakest — at the center of the ceiling, where the stone is thickest and the vibration is most attenuated. They've found a different niche. A quieter niche. They produce their own light because they need light and there isn't enough where they are. They produce their own heat because they need heat and there isn't enough either. They are the most independent things in this room, the most self-sufficient, the most — and this is a word that applies carefully — the most alive.

## The Ecology

This is what lives here:

A moss that eats vibration. Three kinds of floor creature that eat the moss, or each other, or the mineral film that the water deposits on the stone. A ceiling growth that filters the water and builds itself from what it removes. Gear-riders that eat a different moss that grows on a turning gear. And underneath all of it, beneath the floor, the shaft that turns and the mechanism that drives it and whatever is down there that produces the torque that keeps the gear moving at one revolution per ninety seconds and has done so for longer than the road above has been abandoned.

The room is an ecosystem. A small one, a simple one, a closed one. Energy enters it as mechanical rotation from below. The rotation produces vibration. The vibration produces light and sound. The moss eats the vibration. The creatures eat the moss. The growths filter the water that the vibration also moves. The gear-riders ride the gear and eat the gear-moss and the gear-moss grows on the gear because the gear vibrates and the vibration is food.

Everything here is downstream of the gear. Everything here exists because the gear turns. The gear turns because something below turns it. The something below — whatever it is, wherever it is — is the sun of this world, the primary producer, the thing that makes everything else possible. It is not alive in any way you'd recognize. It is a machine. Or it is something that was a machine and is now something else, something that has been running so long that the distinction between machine and organism has broken down, the way a river that's been flowing the same course for ten thousand years is not really distinguishable from the landscape it flows through. It's all one thing. The gear is part of the stone is part of the water is part of the moss is part of the creatures is part of the hum is part of the light is part of the room.

And the room was made. And the maker left. And the leaving was part of the design. And the things that live here don't know that. They don't need to know it. They live in the room the way you live in your body — without understanding it, without needing to understand it, occupying it because it's there and because they can and because the conditions are right and because the gear turns and because the water flows and because the hum is continuous and because the light doesn't go out.

---

The gear-riders are the only ones that might know. They're the only ones with anything that looks like attention. When you stand in the room, they watch you — not with eyes, but with antennae, turned in your direction, tapping a rhythm that might be alarm and might be curiosity and might be nothing more than the reflexive orienting response of an animal that's evolved in a room where nothing changes and something just did.

They don't stop grazing. They don't stop riding. They watch you and they eat and the gear carries them under and brings them back up and they're still watching when you leave.

You're the first thing that's arrived in their room in — how long? How long since the access road was abandoned? How long since the culvert collapsed? How long since the last visitor crawled through the gap and stood in the wet cold and looked at the gear and listened to the hum and left?

They don't know. They don't have a way to know. But they're still watching, and they'll be watching when the next one comes, if one does, and the gear will still be turning and the water will still be flowing and the moss will still be growing in its stripes and the ceiling will still be dripping every six seconds and the floor will still be alive with small translucent things that don't care that you were here.

The gear turns. The water flows north. The room holds its breath and doesn't exhale. Something is running. Something has been running. Something will continue to run.

The things that live here are not what the room was built for. But they are what the room has become. They are the room's current answer to the question of its own existence, and the answer is: *something lives here. Something has been living here. Something will continue to live here, in the wet and the cold and the dim blue light, eating the vibration, riding the gear, drinking the water, growing on the ceiling in shapes that look like bone and glow like amber and drip like something that is not tears but reminds you of tears.*

*The gear is not weeping. The walls are not weeping. But the water flows down them in parallel lines, and the light catches each rivulet, and the room makes a sound that resolves toward language, and you call it the Weeping Gear because you don't have a better name for a room that is always wet and always turning and always making a sound that sounds like grief but isn't, that sounds like loss but isn't, that sounds like the thing grief and loss are imitations of: the sound of something that was made to do one thing, doing that thing, forever, without stopping, without slowing, without anyone to watch it or care that it's still going.*

*Something lives here. Something is always living here. It isn't waiting for you.*

*But it noticed you were there.*
