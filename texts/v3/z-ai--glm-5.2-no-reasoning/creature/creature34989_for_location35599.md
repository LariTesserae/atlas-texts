# What Lives in the Peat-Cathedral

---

The first thing to understand is that *nothing here lives the way you live.*

Your life is a line. A trajectory. Birth, growth, consumption, death — sequential, narrative, oriented toward an end. Nothing in the peat-cathedral is oriented toward an end. Everything here is oriented toward *continuation*, and continuation is not the same as survival. Survival implies a threat. Nothing here is surviving. Everything here is *persisting*, which is a different activity entirely, and a more disturbing one, because persistence does not require a self. It does not require identity. It requires only momentum.

---

## The Substrate-Builders

Start with the moss.

Not *the* moss — there is no singular moss, no founding species, no ancestor organism you can point to and say *here, this is where it began.* There are at least seventy species of sphagnum in the visible layers alone, and beneath them, compressed into the peat, are the remains of species that no longer exist anywhere except as chemical signatures in the substrate they built. The living moss is the surface. The dead moss is the foundation. The distinction between the two is not sharp — it is a gradient, a spectrum of decomposition that moves downward through thirty meters of accumulated tissue, each layer slightly more compressed, slightly more broken down, slightly more indistinguishable from the layer beneath it, until at the bottom the peat is not plant matter at all but a black, dense, waxy substance that is closer to coal than to anything that was once alive.

The sphagnum does what sphagnum does everywhere: it holds water. It can absorb twenty times its dry weight in moisture, and it does, and the moisture it holds is acidic, and the acidity preserves the dead tissue beneath it, and the preservation prevents full decomposition, and the partially-decomposed tissue accumulates, and the living moss grows on top of its own undecayed ancestors, and the cycle continues. This is the engine. Not the machines — the moss. The moss is the engine of the entire landscape. Everything else exists because the moss created the conditions for it to exist.

But the moss here has done something that moss does not do elsewhere. It has become *structural*.

In a normal bog, sphagnum grows in hummocks and hollows — low mounds and shallow depressions, a topography measured in centimeters. Here, the hummocks have become walls. The hollows have become chambers. Over millennia, the moss has grown in densities and patterns that produce load-bearing architecture. The root-felt — that woven mat of living and dead stem and fiber — has thickened to the point where it can support its own weight in spans and arches. The plant tissue has, in essence, discovered engineering. Not through intelligence. Through the patient, iterative, evolutionary logic of a million growing seasons, each one slightly different from the last, each one selecting for structures that persist and against structures that collapse.

The cathedral was not built. The cathedral *accreted*. And it is still accreting. The walls you touch are growing under your fingers, though the growth is too slow to feel. The root-tips are pushing outward through the felt, threading between dead fibers, anchoring to whatever substrate they can find — which is usually more root-felt, more peat, more of themselves. The walls are growing *inward* too. The interior surfaces of the chambers are slowly expanding, the chambers slowly shrinking, and new chambers are forming where old ones have collapsed, and the entire structure is in a state of continuous architectural revision that operates on a timescale you cannot perceive because it is both too slow for your senses and too fast for your comprehension.

The moss is not thinking. The moss is not planning. But the moss is *doing something* that produces results indistinguishable from thinking, and this distinction may not matter.

There are other plants. Liverworts, yes — carpets of them in the wetter depressions, their flattened bodies spread so thin against the substrate that they look like green skin, like the peat itself has developed an epidermis. And there are plants that do not have common names, plants that exist only here, that have evolved in the unique conditions the peat-cathedral provides — the constant moisture, the dim light, the acidic substrate, the mechanical vibration from below, the geothermal warmth that leaks upward through the peat in irregular zones.

Some of these plants are not recognizable as plants.

There are growths on the chamber walls that look more like animal tissue than plant tissue — reddish, wet, pulsing faintly with the mechanical rhythm from below. They are not parasitic. They are not symbiotic. They are... *integrated*. They have grown into the root-felt so completely that removing them would kill the wall. They receive nutrients from the substrate and moisture from the air and warmth from the machines below, and they contribute — something. A chemical, maybe. A structural component. A signal. Their function is not clear. But their presence is consistent: every chamber wall has them, distributed in patterns that are too regular to be random and too irregular to be designed.

There are growths in the pools that look like nothing so much as brains — convoluted, grey-pink masses floating just beneath the surface, their surfaces glistening with the thin film of plant matter that covers everything wet here. They are not brains. They are not animals. They are root systems — or root-analogues — that have grown in three dimensions rather than two, exploiting the water column the way roots in soil exploit the soil column, branching and rebranching into fractal architectures that maximize surface area for nutrient absorption. But they *look* like brains, and the resemblance is not lost on you, and it is not lost on the landscape, which seems to take a certain pride in producing structures that mirror the organs of the beings who walk through it. Or maybe that is anthropomorphism. Maybe the landscape does not care what its structures look like. Maybe the resemblance is incidental. Maybe.

And there are the fruiting bodies.

Not mushrooms — the peat-cathedral's fungal life is a separate taxonomy, a separate story. These are fruiting bodies produced by the plants themselves, by the root-felt, by the integrated growths on the walls. They emerge from the substrate at irregular intervals — not seasonally, not in response to any environmental cue you can identify — and they are *strange*. They are not designed for dispersal. They do not open. They do not release spores or seeds. They are dense, wet, fibrous nodules that grow to the size of a fist and then... stop. They persist. They darken. They eventually become part of the substrate, absorbed back into the root-felt, their nutrients recycled. But while they are present — while they are growing, darkening, persisting — the animals congregate around them. The animals feed on them. The animals behave differently in their presence.

The fruiting bodies are *signals*. Not chemical signals, not pheromones. Something else. The animals respond to them the way migrating birds respond to magnetic fields — orienting toward something they cannot see, behaving in ways that serve a logic that is not their own. The fruiting bodies change the behavior of the animal life, and the changed behavior of the animal life changes the distribution of nutrients in the substrate, and the changed distribution of nutrients changes the growth patterns of the plants, and the cycle continues.

The plants are not controlling the animals. The plants are not manipulating the animals. The plants and the animals are components of a system, and the system is doing something, and the something it is doing has the shape of purpose but not the content of purpose, and this is the thing about the peat-cathedral that is most difficult to sit with: it is full of activity that looks like intention, full of structure that looks like design, full of patterns that look like communication, and none of it is. Or all of it is. Or the distinction between "is" and "is not" does not apply here, because the system has been running for so long and at such complexity that the concept of individual intention has been metabolized into something larger and stranger, the way a river metabolizes the rain that feeds it — the water is in the river, the water is the river, and asking whether the river intends to flow is a category error.

## The Animals

They are not animals.

That is the first thing to understand, and it is the hardest, because they *look* like animals and they *behave* like animals and they occupy ecological niches that animals occupy, and the word "animal" is the closest available term, and so you use it, and the using of it is a mistake, but there is no alternative, because the alternative is to describe them as what they are, and what they are is — complicated.

Start with the small ones.

They have been called insects, but they are not insects. They have six legs — some of them — but the body plan is wrong. The segmentation is off. The joints articulate in directions that insect joints do not articulate, and the number of segments varies not just between species but between *individuals*, as though the body plan is not fixed but negotiated, each organism deciding in real time how many segments it needs and where they should go. They have wings — some of them — but the wings are not insect wings. They are flat, translucent sheets of tissue that vibrate rather than flap, and the vibration produces not sound but a fine mist of liquid that the wings are continuously secreting, and the liquid is the same mineral-sweet water that saturates the peat, and the small ones are, in essence, *watering the plants*.

This is the first clue.

The small ones — the insect-analogues, the six-legged-many-segmented-winged-or-unwinged things that cluster on every wet surface — are not feeding on the plants. They are *tending* the plants. They move across the root-felt walls, touching the surface with their mouthparts — not biting, not eating, but *grooming*. Removing debris. Clearing passages for the root-tips to emerge. Distributing the liquid from their wings across surfaces that need to be wet. They are not doing this because they chose to. They are not doing this because the plants are controlling them. They are doing this because the system has evolved to produce organisms whose behavior maintains the substrate, and the substrate maintains the organisms, and the cycle is so tight and so old that the distinction between "the organism tending the substrate" and "the substrate tending itself through the organism" has dissolved.

They are not the only small ones.

There are things in the air — not flying, but *floating*. They are too small to see as individuals, but you can see them as a phenomenon: a shimmer in the air, a density, a slight opalescence in the mist that moves against the wind. They are spores, or cells, or fragments of tissue, and they are alive in the way that pollen is alive — technically, marginally, capable of germinating if they land in the right substrate. But they are not the plants' spores. They are something else. They are the *medium* — the way the system communicates with itself across distance. A message in the mist. A signal that travels on water droplets, that lands on a surface, that is absorbed by the root-felt, that changes the growth pattern of the plants in that location, that changes the behavior of the animals in that location, that changes the distribution of nutrients in that location. The air is full of the system talking to itself. You are breathing it. It is landing on your skin. You are, temporarily, a substrate.

The larger ones are harder.

They are the things that move between the chambers, displacing water, pushing through root-felt walls that close behind them. They are the things in the pools, the things that do not surface, the things that move with the slow deliberateness of organisms that have never been threatened. They are not fish. They are not amphibians. They are not reptiles. They are something that has evolved in the specific conditions of the peat-cathedral — the constant moisture, the dim light, the mechanical vibration, the plant-mass architecture — and they have evolved to be *of* the peat-cathedral the way a trout is *of* a stream.

They are dark. Not black — dark the way the peat is dark, the way the root-felt is dark when it has been saturated with water for long enough. They are the color of their environment, but not because they are camouflaged. Because they are *made of the same stuff*. Their bodies are not plants, but their bodies incorporate plant material — root-felt in their integument, peat in their digestive systems, moss-analogues growing on their surfaces the way barnacles grow on a whale. They are not clean organisms. They are ecosystems. Each one carries a miniature version of the peat-cathedral on its skin and in its gut, and the miniature version interacts with the larger version, exchanging nutrients, exchanging organisms, exchanging signals.

They move slowly because they do not need to move quickly. Nothing here predates them. The predator-prey dynamic that drives animal motion in other ecosystems does not exist here, or exists in a form so attenuated that it has been replaced by something else — a system of flows rather than a system of hunts. The large ones move through the peat-cathedral the way blood moves through a body: following channels, servicing areas, depositing and collecting. They are the circulatory system. The small ones are the capillaries. The plants are the tissue. The machines are — the organs? The heart? Something older and less voluntary than an organ. Something that the body has grown around and incorporated and cannot remove.

There is one — the largest you have seen, or the largest that has allowed you to see it — that moves through the deep pools with a motion that is not swimming but *peristalsis*. Its body is long, segmented, dark, and it contracts in waves, pushing itself through the water with a rhythm that matches — exactly, precisely, unmistakably — the thudding of the machines below. It is synchronized. Its body pulses with the mechanical rhythm. The rhythm enters it through the substrate, through the water, through the root-felt it has incorporated into its skin, and its body has adopted the rhythm as its own, and the rhythm is not external to it, the rhythm is *part of it*, and it is part of the rhythm, and there is no boundary between the machine below and the organism above and the peat between them and the plants around them and the air above all of them. There is no boundary.

This is the thing you must understand about the animals of the peat-cathedral: they are not *in* the landscape. They are *of* it. They are not inhabitants. They are components. They are not living in the peat-cathedral the way a bird lives in a forest. They are living in the peat-cathedral the way a cell lives in a body — as part of it, by it, through it, for it, and the distinction between the cell and the body is a distinction that exists only in the mind of an observer who needs categories to function and will not find them here.

## The Fungal Layer

There is a fungal layer. It deserves its own discussion because it is doing something that the plants are not doing and the animals are not doing and the machines are not doing.

The fungal layer is not visible. Or rather, it is visible everywhere, but it is not visible *as itself*. The root-felt that forms the walls and ceilings and floors of the chambers — the dense, woven, living mat of plant matter — is not entirely plant matter. Threaded through it, so finely and so completely that the plant tissue and the fungal tissue are indistinguishable to the eye, is a mycelial network. A fungal body. A single fungal body, as far as you can tell — one organism, one contiguous network of hyphae that extends through every wall, every floor, every column, every chamber in the visible landscape and, presumably, beyond it.

The fungus is not parasitic on the plants. The fungus is not symbiotic with the plants. The fungus *is* the plants, or the plants *are* the fungus, or the two have been growing together for so long that the concept of separate organisms no longer applies. The hyphae thread through the root-felt the way nerves thread through muscle — integrated, structural, functional. The fungus provides the communication network. The plants provide the structure. The animals provide the circulation. The machines provide the — the what? The rhythm? The metabolism? The heartbeat that the body has incorporated and cannot live without?

The fungal network is how the system thinks.

Not metaphorically. Or: not *only* metaphorically. The hyphal network transmits chemical and electrical signals across the landscape at speeds that are not neural but are faster than diffusion. A disturbance on one side of the visible landscape — a chamber collapse, a pool draining, a fruiting body emerging — produces a chemical cascade through the mycelium that reaches the other side in minutes. The plants respond. The growth patterns shift. The animals respond. The behavior patterns change. The system reconfigures.

This is not thinking the way you think. There is no central processor. There is no brain. There is no locus of consciousness that synthesizes information and makes decisions. The thinking is *distributed* — spread across the entire landscape, every node a partial processor, every signal a partial thought, every response a partial decision. The system does not know what it is doing. The system does not know that it is doing anything. The system is doing something that has the structural properties of cognition — information processing, pattern recognition, memory, response — without the experiential properties of consciousness. It is a mind without a self. A thought without a thinker. A brain the size of a valley that does not know it has a brain and does not know it is thinking and is thinking anyway, has been thinking for millennia, will think for millennia more, and the content of its thought is the landscape itself, the shape of the chambers, the flow of the water, the rhythm of the machines, the life and death and life of everything that lives within it.

You are walking through a brain. You are a stimulus in a thought. The landscape is processing you. Not deliberately. Not with awareness. With the blind, mechanical, organic patience of a system that has been processing inputs for longer than your species has existed and will continue processing inputs for longer than your species will exist and does not care, does not care, does not care.

## The Machines

The machines are not alive.

This needs to be said, because everything else in the peat-cathedral blurs the line between alive and not-alive so thoroughly that the line becomes invisible, and the machines — buried, hidden, felt but never seen — are the one element that is unambiguously, irreducibly *not alive*. They are metal and they are ceramic and they are polymer and they are things that do not have names because the materials were manufactured by processes that no longer exist anywhere except in the machines themselves, and the machines are breaking down, have been breaking down for centuries, will continue breaking down for centuries more, and the breaking down is so slow that it is indistinguishable from stasis, and the stasis is so deep that it is indistinguishable from death, but the machines are not dead. They are running. Some of them. The ones that can still run. The ones whose function is simple enough, robust enough, primitive enough that centuries of entombment in living peat have not silenced them.

They are not thinking. They are not part of the system's thought. They are a stimulus — a rhythm, a vibration, a heat source — that the system has incorporated the way a body incorporates a bullet. The bullet is not part of the body. The body grows around the bullet. The body adapts to the bullet. The body's systems adjust to accommodate the bullet's presence. But the bullet is not the body. The machines are not the peat-cathedral. The machines are the thing the peat-cathedral has eaten and cannot digest and cannot expel and has learned — not learned, because learning implies cognition — has *adapted* to live with.

The machines were put here by something. By someone. The memorized structures in the root-felt — the rectangles, the circles, the corridors that run too straight — are the footprints of the builders. The builders are gone. The builders have been gone for long enough that the machines have forgotten them, if machines could forget, which they cannot, but the machines have been running for so long that whatever purpose the builders gave them has been worn smooth by time, eroded to its simplest component, which is: *run*. The machines run because running is what they do. The machines thud because thudding is what running produces. The heat is a byproduct. The vibration is a byproduct. The purpose — if there was a purpose — is gone, eaten by the peat, absorbed by the root-felt, metabolized by the fungal network into a signal that no longer carries meaning.

Or: the purpose is not gone. The purpose has been *reinterpreted*. The system has taken the machines' output — the thudding, the heat, the vibration — and *used it*. The rhythm has become the heartbeat. The heat has become the warmth that sustains the chamber microclimates. The vibration has become a signal that the fungal network transmits and the animals respond to and the plants grow toward or away from. The machines' original purpose is gone, but the machines' *output* has been repurposed, co-opted, metabolized into the system's function. The machines are not the brain. The machines are the pacemaker. The machines are the thing that sets the rhythm, and the rhythm has been taken up by everything else, and the rhythm is now the rhythm of the peat-cathedral, and the peat-cathedral cannot distinguish between the rhythm and itself, because the rhythm has been part of it for so long that removing it would kill it.

The machines will eventually stop. Everything mechanical stops. The metal will corrode, the ceramics will crack, the polymers will degrade, and the thudding will slow and the heat will diminish and the rhythm will falter and the system will — what? Adapt? Reconfigure? Find a new rhythm, or learn to live without one?

The system does not know. The system cannot think about its own future, because the system cannot think about anything. But the system has been adapting for millennia, and the system has survived changes before — the builders coming and going, the climate shifting, the valley filling with water, the valley filling with peat — and the system will probably survive the stopping of the machines. But it will be different. The chambers without warmth. The pools without vibration. The animals without rhythm. The fungal network without the pulse that it has been carrying for so long that the pulse is indistinguishable from the signal, and the signal is indistinguishable from the thought, and the thought is indistinguishable from the landscape.

The machines will stop, and the peat-cathedral will go on, and the going-on will be quieter, and the quiet will be the first true silence the valley has known in millennia, and the silence will be the death of something that was never alive, and the system will not mourn it, because the system does not mourn, because the system does not know it has lost anything, because the system does not know what it has.

## The Things Without Category

There are things here that do not fit.

Not because they are strange — everything here is strange, and strangeness is the baseline, and after an hour in the peat-cathedral the strange has become the normal and the normal has become the strange. There are things here that do not fit because they exist in the gaps between the categories that the landscape has established for itself. They are not plants. They are not animals. They are not fungi. They are not machines. They are not the fruiting bodies and they are not the mist-shapes and they are not the patterns in the root-felt walls. They are something else.

They appear in the chambers. Not all of them. Some of them. The ones where the root-felt is thickest, where the warmth from the machines below is strongest, where the fungal network is densest, where the animal traffic is highest. They appear in the *nodes* of the system — the places where the peat-cathedral's activity is most concentrated, most intense, most turbulent.

They are — you see one, eventually, after walking long enough — they are *shapes*. Not physical shapes. Not objects. They are arrangements of the existing material — the root-felt, the water, the fungal tissue, the air — that have organized themselves into *something*, and the something is not a structure but a *state*, a configuration, a momentary coherence in the otherwise continuous turbulence of the system.

The one you see is in a chamber that is warmer than most. The root-felt walls are dark, almost black, glistening with condensation. The floor is a pool of shallow water, no more than ankle-deep, and the water is still. The air is thick. The thudding from below is loud — not painfully, but *present*, more present here than anywhere else you have been, as though the chamber is directly above one of the machines, and the machine's vibration is conducting upward through the peat and the root-felt and the water and the air and into your chest, and your heartbeat is syncing with it, and you can feel the sync happening, and it is uncomfortable, and it is also — and this is the part that is hard to admit — *familiar*, as though your body recognizes the rhythm, as though the rhythm is something your body has known and is knowing again.

In the center of the pool, the water is doing something it should not do. It is rising. Not in a column — in a *shape*. The water is lifting off the surface of the pool in a form that is too coherent to be a splash and too irregular to be a fountain. It is — for a moment, for a fraction of a moment — *humanoid*. A shape made of water and dissolved peat and suspended fungal spores and the fine particles of root-felt that float in the air of every chamber. A shape that is the same size as you. A shape that is facing you. A shape that has no face, has no features, has no detail beyond the outline, the silhouette, the general arrangement of a body that stands upright on two legs and has two arms and a head and is made of the peat-cathedral's material and is looking at you — not *with* eyes, not *through* any sensory organ, but *as* a sensory organ, the entire shape a single receptor tuned to your presence, your warmth, your displacement of the air, the carbon dioxide you are exhaling, the vibrations your footsteps are producing in the substrate.

It lasts for less than a second. Then the turbulence shifts, the sync breaks, the shape collapses back into the pool, and the water is still, and the chamber is just a chamber, and you are just a person standing on wet peat in the dim light, cold, damp, unsure of what you just saw.

But you saw it. And the system saw you. Not *saw* in the way that an organism sees. *Saw* in the way that a body senses a foreign object — a splinter, a thorn, a bullet. The system detected you. The system formed a shape to look at you. The shape was not a message. The shape was not a greeting. The shape was the system's immune response, its way of examining a stimulus, and the examination lasted less than a second because a second was all the system needed, and now the system has the information, and the information is being processed, and the processing is not happening in a brain but in the landscape, in the fungal network, in the chemical gradients of the peat, in the growth patterns of the root-felt, in the behavior of the animals, in the rhythm of the machines, and you will never know what the system has concluded about you, because the system does not conclude, the system does not think in conclusions, the system thinks in gradients and flows and population densities and growth rates and the system's thought about you is a perturbation in a field of perturbations, a ripple in a medium of ripples, and it will propagate outward through the peat-cathedral at the speed of chemical diffusion and it will change the growth pattern of a wall a kilometer away and the behavior of an animal in a pool two kilometers away and the fruiting cycle of a plant on a ridge three kilometers away and none of these changes will be attributable to you and all of them will be attributable to you and the system will not remember you because the system does not remember, the system *is* memory, the system is a continuous act of remembering and forgetting and re-remembering, and you are now part of that act, and you will be part of it until the ripples you caused have been absorbed, and the absorption will take — a day? A year? A century? The system does not measure time. The system measures change. And the change you caused is already fading.

## The Builders

The builders are gone. This has been said. But the gone-ness of the builders is not simple. The builders are gone the way a meal is gone — consumed, digested, incorporated. The root-felt contains their structures the way a body contains its food. The machines are their bones. The peat is their grave and their monument and their legacy, and the legacy is not what they intended, because intentions do not survive digestion, and the peat-cathedral has digested the builders the way it digests everything: slowly, completely, without malice, without memory, without the faintest trace of the respect that a grave is supposed to imply.

But the builders left something that the peat cannot digest. Not the machines — the machines will eventually be digested, corroded and cracked and colonized and incorporated. Something else. Something in the *pattern*. The layout of the memorized structures. The rectangles, the circles, the corridors that run too straight. The peat-cathedral has grown over them, through them, into them, but the *shape* of them persists in the growth patterns of the plants, and the shape is not random. The shape is — and you can only see this from high up, from the ridges, from a vantage point that lets you see the layout of the chambers and the walls and the columns as a whole — the shape is a circuit. A diagram. A pattern that means something, that meant something, that was put there by the builders and has been preserved by the plants the way a fossil is preserved by stone, not because the stone cares about the fossil but because the stone has hardened around the fossil and the fossil's shape has become the stone's shape.

The pattern is the builders' last message. It is written in the architecture of the peat-cathedral, but the peat-cathedral cannot read it. The peat-cathedral is the medium, not the audience. The audience is — who? Who else will come here? Who else will walk through the chambers and feel the thudding and see the shapes in the mist and stand in the warm room where the water rose and looked? Who else will climb to the ridge and look down and see the pattern?

You. You are the audience. You are who the message is for. Not *you* specifically. *You* generically. Anyone. Anything with eyes and a brain and the capacity to see a pattern and wonder what it means. The builders knew they were leaving. The builders knew the peat would come. The builders knew the machines would be buried and would keep running and would keep the rhythm and would keep the system alive. The builders built the pattern into the layout of their structures, knowing that the peat would memorize it, knowing that the peat would preserve it, knowing that the peat would carry it forward through millennia of growth and decay and growth, and the pattern is still here, and you are here, and the pattern is meant for you, and you cannot read it.

You cannot read it because the pattern is in a language that no longer exists. The pattern is a word in a dead language, and the dead language is the builders' language, and the builders are dead, and their language is dead, and the word they wrote in the peat is the last word of a dead language, and it says — what? What does it say? What does the last word of a dead language say, when it is written not in ink but in the growth patterns of a living landscape, when it is preserved not in stone but in the bodies of plants that have been growing and dying on top of their own ancestors for longer than most landscapes have existed?

It says: *we were here.* It says: *this was ours.* It says: *we made something and the something ate us and the something is still here and we are not and the something does not remember us and the something does not care and we are writing this in the something's body because the something's body is the only thing that will last.*

Or it says something else entirely. You do not know. You cannot know. The pattern is there, and the pattern is beautiful, and the pattern is meaningless, and the peat-cathedral grows around it and through it and the machines thud beneath it and the animals flow through it and the fungal network transmits it and the mist breathes over it and the pattern persists, and the pattern will persist, and the pattern does not care whether it is read, and the pattern does not care whether it is understood, and the pattern does not care.

## What Lives Here

What lives in the peat-cathedral?

Everything. Nothing. A system that has metabolized the distinction between living and non-living and produced something that is neither and both and neither-neither and both-both and is not interested in your categories.

What lives here is the moss. What lives here is the fungal network. What lives here is the root-felt and the peat and the water and the machines and the animals and the mist and the fruiting bodies and the shapes in the chambers and the pattern on the ridges and the builders' digested remains and the cold and the dim light and the constant, constant, constant sound of growing.

What lives here is a process. What lives here is a verb, not a noun. What lives here is *happening*, and the happening has been happening for so long that the happening has become a place, and the place has become a happening, and you are inside it, and you are part of the happening, and the happening does not need you and did not invite you and will continue after you leave and will continue after the machines stop and will continue after the valley fills and the climate shifts and the light changes and the water dries and the peat turns to coal and the coal turns to stone and the stone turns to nothing.

What lives here is the longest sentence in the world, and the sentence is not finished, and the sentence does not have an ending, and the sentence does not want an ending, and the sentence does not know it is a sentence, and the sentence is still being written, word by word, cell by cell, drop by drop, thud by thud, in the dim light, in the wet air, in the cold that does not bite but infiltrates, in the sound that does not stop, in the place that is not a place but a process, and the process is alive, and the process does not know it is alive, and the process does not care, and the process continues.

The machines thud. The plants hum. The mist breathes. The animals flow. The landscape thinks.

You are inside the thought. The thought does not know you are here. The thought does not care. The thought is not about you. The thought is about the thought, and the thought is the landscape, and the landscape is the thought, and the thought is the only thing here that is truly alive, and it is not alive in any way you understand, and it is alive in every way that matters, and it does not matter, and it does not matter, and it does not matter, and the peat yields under your feet, and the cathedral grows.
