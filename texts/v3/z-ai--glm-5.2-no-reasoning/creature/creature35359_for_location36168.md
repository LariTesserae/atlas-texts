# What Lives Here

## I. The Unseen Majority

The first thing to understand is that what lives here is mostly invisible, and what is visible is mostly not what you would call alive.

The pillars are alive. The floor is alive. The canopy is alive. The mist is, in a sense, alive — it carries spores, pollen fragments, the microscopic sheddings of a thousand surfaces. But when we speak of *what lives here*, we mean something narrower, and we have to search for it, and we have to understand that our searching is part of the problem. Our eyes are built to detect movement, contrast, difference. This place defeats all three. Everything is the same color. Everything is wet. Everything is still. The living things that are not the structure itself have, over evolutionary time, solved the problem of being alive here by becoming indistinguishable from where they live.

Start with what you cannot see at all.

The deep compost beneath the floor mats — the meters-thick layer of dead and decaying root matter that has accumulated over centuries — is not empty. It is, in fact, more densely populated than any surface you have walked across. But its inhabitants are microscopic, and their world is not your world. They live in water films so thin that a human hair would be a canyon wall to them. They live in the capillary spaces between rotting fibers, in oxygen-poor pockets where decomposition has consumed the available air, in zones of acidity so high that the humic acids would burn your skin.

Nematodes — roundworms, translucent, featureless, barely larger than a single grain of pollen — move through the water films in numbers that would stagger you if you could count them. Tens of thousands per cubic centimeter of wet compost. They do not swim. They do not crawl. They *push*, with longitudinal muscles, threading themselves between fibers, following chemical gradients toward zones of bacterial concentration. They are eating. They are always eating. Their mouths are simple — a tube, a set of stylets, a pumping pharynx — and what they eat is bacteria, and what the bacteria eat is dead root, and what the dead root was is living root, and what the living root eats is the compost made by the bacteria and the nematodes, and the cycle has no beginning and no end. The nematodes are not aware of this. They have approximately three hundred neurons. They do not think. They do not need to think. They follow gradients. They eat. They reproduce. They die. Their bodies become compost. The compost becomes root. The root becomes structure. The structure becomes compost.

This is not a food chain. A chain has links, and links can be broken. This is a food *circle* — every participant consuming and being consumed, every mouth feeding something else's mouth, the entire system turning like a wheel, and the wheel has been turning at the same speed for so long that it has worn perfectly smooth.

There are mites here too. Or something like mites. They are so small that the distinction between species has become academic — they have evolved in isolation, in this single valley, for so long that they no longer resemble anything you would find in a reference text. They are pale, almost translucent, eight-legged, slow. They live on the surfaces of decaying roots, in the boundary zone between the living structure and the dead compost, and they graze. That is the word — *graze* — though what they graze on is a film of fungi and bacteria so thin it is invisible to the naked eye. They move like cattle move, in a sense: continuously, without urgency, their mouths always working, their bodies always progressing toward the next patch of food. They have no predators here. Nothing eats them. They die of old age, if mites can be said to age, or they drown when a water film swells too thick, or they simply stop, and their bodies become part of the surface they were grazing on, and other mites graze on the bacteria that colonize their corpses.

There are thousands of species. There may be tens of thousands. No one has counted. No one will count. They exist in a world that is separated from yours not by distance but by scale, and the scale gap is unbridgeable. You could lie on the floor mat and press your face into the moss and stare for hours and you would see nothing of them. They are there. They have always been there. They are the mechanism by which the structure recycles itself, and without them the equilibrium would collapse — the dead matter would accumulate, the living roots would starve, the floors would rot through, the pillars would thin and buckle. Everything you see above is made possible by everything you cannot see below.

## II. The Fungi

The fungi are harder to ignore, though most of them are also invisible.

The pale threads you glimpsed on the ground — the mycelium, the vegetative body of the fungal organism — are not a detail. They are infrastructure. They spread through the compost layers, through the living root mats, through the wet interfaces between dead and living matter, in a network so dense and so interconnected that the entire valley floor is, in a real sense, a single fungal organism. Or perhaps a few. Or perhaps thousands, so thoroughly fused that the concept of individuality has broken down.

The threads — hyphae, technically — are thin, white, tubular, and they grow by extending their tips, pushing forward into new substrate, secreting enzymes that dissolve organic matter and absorb the dissolved nutrients back through their walls. They are eating the compost. They are also eating the dead roots. They are also, and this is the part that matters, eating *into* the living roots — not destructively, not as pathogens, but as partners. The hyphae penetrate the outer layers of the root cells and form interfaces — exchange zones — where carbon from the plant meets nitrogen and phosphorus from the fungus, and both parties benefit. The plant feeds the fungus sugar. The fungus feeds the plant minerals. The arrangement is ancient, standard, the default mode of most plant life on most worlds. What is unusual here is the *scale* of it. The symbiosis is not between one root and one fungus. It is between *every root* and *every fungus*, a total, valley-wide, structure-wide partnership that means the entire architecture is, in a functional sense, a single organism.

Or a single ecosystem. The distinction, as you have already begun to understand, does not apply here.

Sometimes, in the wettest, darkest places — inside hollow stumps, in the gaps beneath floor mats where the compost is deepest — the fungi fruit. What you would call a mushroom appears, though it does not look like any mushroom you have seen. It is pale, almost white, translucent at the edges, growing in a shelf-like formation from the inner wall of a rotting root. It is wet. Everything here is wet. It secretes drops of moisture from its surface, and these drops fall, and they join the water cycle, and the spores that the fruiting body releases are carried in the water vapor, in the mist, upward to the canopy, where they settle on wet surfaces and germinate and produce new hyphae that find new roots and the network extends, or rather, the network *maintains* itself, because it is already everywhere, and the new growth simply replaces the old.

The fruiting bodies do not last long. A few days. They appear, they release spores, they soften and collapse, and they are consumed by the mites and the nematodes and the bacteria, and the cycle continues. You might see one if you are lucky, if you are looking in the right place at the right time — a pale, wet, shelf-like thing growing from the dark interior of a hollow root, looking less like a mushroom and more like a wound in the surface of the wood, something that should not be there but is, briefly, before it is not again.

## III. The Visible Few

Now the larger things. The ones you might actually see.

The creature you glimpsed — the dark, many-legged, quick thing that vanished into the root fibers — is real, and it is one of perhaps a dozen macroscopic animal species that live in the valley. A dozen. In a structure the size of a town. The sparseness is not an accident. It is a consequence.

The dominant organism — the root system, the structure, the architecture — has occupied nearly all available ecological space. There is no niche for a grazer, because there is nothing above ground worth grazing; the surfaces are root and moss and liverwort, all of which are either too fibrous, too wet, or too chemically defended to be worth eating. There is no niche for a predator, because there are no grazers to prey on. The food web above ground is nearly nonexistent. The living structure does not need animals. It does not benefit from them. It does not support them. It merely tolerates the few that have found a way to persist in its margins.

The many-legged thing is a detritivore. You would call it something like a millipede, though it is not a millipede, though the convergent evolution is so complete that the distinction is mostly academic. It is long — ten centimeters, perhaps twelve — and dark, the color of wet bark, and its surface is smooth and hard, a chitinous exoskeleton that sheds water the same way the smooth aerial roots do. It lives in the cracks and gaps of the structure, in the spaces between root fibers, in the boundary zone where the living surface meets the air. It eats dead matter. Dead moss, dead liverwort, dead root bark, the frass of other detritivores, the corpses of its own kind. It is, in a sense, an extension of the decomposition system below ground — a large, visible version of the nematodes and mites, doing the same work in a different scale.

It moves quickly because it has to. Its body loses moisture rapidly in the saturated air, which sounds paradoxical but is not — its exoskeleton is designed to shed water, and it cannot reabsb atmospheric moisture the way softer-bodied organisms can. It must drink, and it drinks from the water films that form on root surfaces, and it must do so frequently, and so it moves from one wet surface to another, quickly, purposefully, its many legs carrying it over the irregular topography of the root-fiber landscape with a fluency that suggests it has lived here its entire life, which it has, and that its ancestors have lived here for a span of time that has allowed them to become perfectly adapted to this single environment, which they have.

There are not many of them. You might see one per hour of walking, if you are attentive. You might see none. They do not congregate. They do not socialize. They encounter each other only by chance, and when they do, they either ignore each other or mate, briefly, without courtship, and separate. The female lays eggs in the wet compost beneath the floor mats, and the eggs hatch into miniature versions of the adult, and the miniature versions grow, and the cycle continues, and has continued, and will continue.

There is something else here that you have not seen. Something larger.

## IV. The Slower Thing

It lives in the canopy.

You would not notice it if you climbed to the canopy — which you cannot, easily, because the ladder-like structure of the lateral roots is not designed for your limbs and the surfaces are too wet for secure footing — but if you could, if you pulled yourself up through the lattice of branches and aerial roots into the open, airy space near the top of the structure, you would find it.

It is slow. It is so slow that the word *lives* seems too active for what it does. It *persists*. It *continues*. It does not move in any way you can perceive. If you watched it for an hour, you would conclude it was part of the structure. If you watched it for a day, you would begin to doubt your conclusion. If you watched it for a week — and no one has, because no one has been here for a week — you would see that it moves. Very slowly. A few centimeters per day. Less, sometimes. It creeps along the upper surfaces of the canopy branches, a flat, dark, irregularly shaped organism, roughly the size and thickness of a spread hand, adhered to the bark like a dark lichen.

It is not a lichen. It is an animal, or something that was an animal once, in the same way that the pillars were roots once and are now architecture. It has evolved so far from its ancestral form that calling it by any familiar name would be misleading. It is a grazer — the only grazer in the valley, and it grazes on nothing that you would recognize as food. It eats the surface of the canopy branches. Not the bark itself, but the thin film of algae and cyanobacteria that grows on every wet, lit surface in the upper structure. The film is so thin that you would not see it as a distinct layer — you would simply see dark, wet bark. But it is there, and the slow thing eats it, and the bark is revealed, pale and fresh, in its wake, and then the film grows back, and the slow thing returns, eventually, and eats it again.

Its body is adapted to this existence in ways that are difficult to describe. It has no eyes. It has no apparent mouth. Its underside is a single, broad, adhesive surface, and the feeding happens there — chemical dissolution, absorption, the same mechanism the fungi use below, scaled up and slowed down. It does not breathe in any way you can detect. Its metabolism is so low that it needs almost no oxygen. It can survive for weeks without food. It can survive being submerged in water for days. It can survive being dried out — though this never happens here; it has not happened in the entire history of the slow thing's species, because the canopy has never been dry.

It is the closest thing the valley has to a vertebrate, and it is not a vertebrate. It is the closest thing the valley has to a large animal, and it is the size of a hand. It is the apex of the valley's food web, and nothing eats it, because there is nothing here that could. It occupies its niche with the same totality that the root system occupies its niche — completely, unchallenged, permanent. It has no predators because predation is not a strategy that works here. The ecosystem does not reward it. The ecosystem rewards patience, and slowness, and the ability to persist without change, and the slow thing is the most patient and most slow and most unchanging of all the valley's inhabitants.

You will not see it. Almost no one does. It lives above, in the light, in the moisture, in the part of the structure that is closest to the sky, and it lives at a speed that is incompatible with human perception. It is there. It has been there for a very long time.

## V. The Water Itself

And then there is the water.

This feels strange to say — that the water is alive, that it lives here — but the water in this valley is not the water you know. The water you know is a medium. It carries things. It flows. It pools. It is, in a sense, passive — a substrate in which other things happen. The water here is not passive. The water here is *constitutive*. It is not separate from the living system. It is the living system, in the same way that blood is not separate from the body but *is* the body, is the body's medium, is the substance without which the body's processes cannot occur.

The water carries things. Not minerals — there are no minerals here, not in the geological sense, not dissolved from rock. The water carries organics. Dissolved sugars from the roots. Amino acids from the compost. Tannins, phenolics, the chemical signals of a thousand metabolic processes occurring simultaneously in every cubic meter of the structure. The water is not pure. It is not clean. It is a solution — a thin, warm, organic soup that circulates through the valley the way blood circulates through a body, and it carries with it everything the structure needs, and it deposits what it carries where it is needed, and it picks up new things where they are produced, and it moves on.

The water has a microbiome. Bacteria live in it — not in the compost, not on the surfaces, but *in the water itself*, suspended, metabolizing the dissolved organics, reproducing, dying, being consumed by other bacteria, by the nematodes, by the mites. The water is not sterile. The water has never been sterile. The water is as alive as the roots, in its way, and it is as much a part of the structure as the pillars and the floors and the canopy.

And the water has a sound. You have heard the sound. The sound is what the water does here — it falls, it strikes, it is absorbed, it runs, it drips, it pools, it evaporates, it condenses. The sound is the audible trace of the water's cycle, and the cycle is the life of the valley, and the life of the valley is the cycle, and the sound is the life, heard from outside.

## VI. What Does Not Live Here

It is worth saying what does not live here, because the absences are as informative as the presences.

There are no birds. There have never been birds. The concept of a bird — a flying, singing, territorial, nest-building, egg-laying organism — has no application in this valley. There are no insects with wings. The air is too wet, the spaces too enclosed, the surfaces too irregular for flight to be a useful strategy. Everything that moves here moves by crawling, creeping, or flowing. The sky is not a medium here. It is a ceiling. Nothing enters it, nothing crosses it, nothing uses it.

There are no vertebrates. No mammals, no reptiles, no amphibians. The valley could support them — the temperature is stable, the moisture is sufficient, the shelter is abundant — but it does not, because the ecosystem does not need them, because they have no niche to fill, because the structure is complete without them. Vertebrates evolved to exploit gaps in ecosystems, to find opportunities that smaller, simpler organisms could not reach. Here there are no gaps. The structure fills everything. The fungi fill the underground. The detritivores fill the decomposition zone. The slow thing fills the canopy film. The water fills the rest. There is nothing left for a vertebrate to do.

There are no flowers. No fruit. No seeds. The root system does not reproduce. It does not need to. It is already everywhere. It has achieved the only goal that matters in this valley — persistence — and it persists not by spreading but by *staying*, by maintaining, by being the same. Reproduction is a strategy for organisms that face change, that face death, that face the possibility of extinction. This structure faces none of those things. It will not be destroyed by drought, because there is no drought. It will not be destroyed by fire, because there is no fire. It will not be destroyed by disease, because its symbiotic partnerships are too complete, too integrated, too old to be disrupted by any pathogen that could plausibly arise. It does not reproduce because it does not die, and it does not die because it does not change, and it does not change because it has found the shape that works, and it is holding it.

## VII. The Question of the Whole

So what lives here?

The answer, after all of this, is not a list. A list implies separateness — this species and that species, this organism and that organism, boundaries between one life and another. The valley does not support that kind of thinking. The boundaries have dissolved. The root system is also the fungal network. The fungal network is also the bacterial community. The bacterial community is also the water. The water is also the structure. The structure is also the compost. The compost is also the detritivores. The detritivores are also the slow thing. The slow thing is also the canopy. The canopy is also the mist. The mist is also the water.

What lives here is *the valley*.

Not the valley as a place — the valley as a process. The process of water falling and being absorbed and evaporating and falling again. The process of root growing and dying and composting and feeding new root. The process of fungus dissolving and plant absorbing and nematode eating and mite grazing and slow thing creeping and everything dying and everything being eaten and everything becoming something else's food and everything being, at every moment, alive and dead and alive again.

It is one thing. It is many things. It is both. It has been both for a span of time that makes the word *long* feel insufficient. It will be both for a span of time that makes the word *forever* feel presumptuous but also, perhaps, not entirely wrong.

What lives here is the closest thing to a self-sustaining, perfectly balanced, eternally persistent living system that has ever existed — not because it was designed to be, but because it grew into that shape, the way a river grows into its channel, the way a crystal grows into its lattice, the way anything that has enough time and enough energy and enough matter will eventually find the shape that requires the least energy to maintain, and will hold it.

The valley found its shape. The valley is holding it. The valley is alive, and it is holding its own life, and what lives in it is itself.

You walked through it and you saw pillars and floors and mist and water and you thought you were walking through a building. You were walking through an organism. You were walking through a process. You were walking through a life so large and so slow and so complete that it does not need to know it is alive, the way your heart does not need to know it is beating.

The roots grow. The water falls. The floor holds. The mist sits. The sound sounds.

It is alive. All of it. Every surface, every drop, every fiber. It is alive and it is all one life and it has been one life for longer than anything you have ever known has been anything, and it will continue, and it will not change, and it will not end, and it does not need you to see it or understand it or walk through it or leave it.

It does not need anything. It is complete. It is the only complete thing there is.
