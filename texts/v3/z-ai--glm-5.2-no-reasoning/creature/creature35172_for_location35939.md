# What Lives on the Sethan Plateau

## The Lichens

Start with what is barely alive by most definitions, because the plateau does not share most definitions.

The lichens come first. They are the plateau's skin, or perhaps its handwriting—pale grey and pale green traces that follow the fracture lines in the granite like veins on an aging hand. They are dry. They are always dry. You can kneel beside a patch and breathe on it and watch nothing happen. The moisture of your breath does not register. The rain does not register. They take what they need from the condensation that forms at the boundary between the stone's retained warmth and the cold thin air above, and they take it slowly, so slowly that a patch the size of your thumbnail may have been growing for a thousand years.

They are married to the stone. This is not metaphor. The relationship between the lichen and the granite is so settled, so mutually constitutive, that neither can be properly said to exist without the other. The lichen does not eat the stone. It does not accelerate the stone's erosion. It simply lives on it, drawing minerals from the surface molecules and returning, in exchange, a faint chemical patience that stabilizes the granite's outer layer. Underfoot, the lichens crunch very faintly when you step on them—a sound like crushing old paper, or like stepping on frost. They do not seem to mind. They are too old to mind. They have been stepped on by continents.

There are no species names for these lichens. Or rather, there may be, somewhere in a herbarium, a sample collected decades ago by someone who did not understand what they were taking. The names would not help. The lichens are not a species. They are a phenomenon. They are what happens when stone and air and time agree to produce something that is none of those things alone. They are the plateau thinking about color.

## The Grass

In the wider cracks—those perhaps two centimeters across, where the fracture has accumulated the faintest traces of mineral soil—a single species of grass grows. It is related to nothing you would recognize. It stands no higher than an ankle and grows in tussocks of three or four stalks, always the same number, as though the plant has a rule. Three or four. Never two. Never five. The stalks are stiff and the color of old hay year-round. They do not flower. They do not seed visibly. They simply persist, and occasionally a new tussock appears in a crack where one was not before, having arrived by means that no botanist has observed.

The grass is warm to the touch. It should not be. It takes the stone's heat and holds it in its fibers, as though it, too, is a piece of the plateau that has decided to stand up. If you hold a stalk between your fingers, you can feel the warmth traveling up from the root, which descends into the crack and into the root system that runs through the plateau's deepest fractures like dark wire through old walls. The grass is not an individual. It is the tip of something vast and hidden, a green thought that the underground root system thinks at the surface, a word in a language of fiber and mineral spoken so slowly that a single sentence takes centuries.

The organisms tend the grass. You can see them at night, if you lie still—dark shapes moving along the crack edges, pausing at each tussock, doing something that involves their legs and their mouths and the base of the stalks. They are not eating it. They are tending it. The relationship is agricultural. The organisms are farming something they cannot fully see, maintaining the visible portion of a plant whose body extends farther into the stone than any of them will ever travel. It is possible that the grass does not need them. It is certain that they believe it does.

## The Root

In the very deepest fractures, where you cannot see the bottom and where your hand cannot reach, a fibrous root system exists. It is dark, almost black, and it descends along the crack walls, branching and rebranching into structures so fine they are barely thicker than the mineral grains they grow between. No one has seen the plant these roots belong to. The roots are the plant. It lives inside the granite, in the darkness of the fractures, taking what it needs from the moisture and the minerals that percolate down through the crack network.

It is the oldest living thing on the plateau. It may be the oldest living thing anywhere. It has been growing downward, centimeter by century, for longer than the mountains took to erode. Its total extent is unknown. The root system has been traced, by indirect means—thermal imaging, seismic profiling, the behavior of the organisms that live among its fibers—to a depth of at least forty meters, and it may go deeper. It may go much deeper. It may extend, through the network of fractures that threads the plateau's granite like cracks in old ice, all the way down to the cooled magma chambers that provide the plateau's warmth. If so, the root is not a plant. It is a bridge. It is the living connection between the biological surface and the geological deep, and it has been growing toward the heat for so long that it may have nearly arrived.

The organisms live among its fibers. The root system is their architecture, their infrastructure, their god. They weave their structures around the dark threads. They follow the root's branching the way water follows a channel. The root does not seem to notice them, or if it does, it does not mind. The relationship is ancient and settled. The root grows. The organisms build. The stone holds them both. This has been the arrangement for longer than anything alive can remember, and nothing alive here has any interest in remembering it, because it has not changed.

## The Organisms

This is the plateau's secret. This is what does not make sense until you have been here for days.

There should not be this many. The vegetation is almost nothing. The water is scarce. The elevation is extreme. And yet the plateau is dense with life. Not herds. Not flocks. These are small things. Invertebrates. Arthropods. Organisms that have no common names and would require a lineage of taxonomists to classify, because they appear to belong to no known lineage. They are the plateau's true inhabitants.

They live in the cracks. All of them. The fractures in the granite are not empty channels—they are highways, warrens, cities. The density of life in each crack is staggering. You discover this when you kneel beside a wide fissure and look in. The walls of the crack, to a depth of several centimeters, are moving. A seething, ordered motion of thousands of organisms going about their lives in the narrow vertical space between stone walls.

### What They Look Like

They are dark. Most of them are the grey-black of the granite itself, which makes them nearly invisible until they move. They are not insects. They have no wings, no antennae in the familiar arrangement. Their bodies are elongated, segmented, with many legs, but the legs are arranged differently than in centipedes or millipedes. Some have legs that extend laterally and grip the stone walls like climbing gear—these are the ones you see most often, holding the vertical surfaces of the cracks, moving with the slow deliberation of something that has never needed to hurry. Others have legs beneath them for walking on horizontal surfaces—the ledges and shelves within the crack network, the small flat places where mineral soil has accumulated. The largest are the length of your finger. The smallest are near-microscopic, visible only as a faint texture on the stone, a roughness that resolves, under magnification, into hundreds of tiny bodies.

They exist in a continuous range of sizes, as though the species does not have discrete life stages but grows continuously throughout its life, molting whenever the stone around it permits. A crack that is two centimeters wide will contain individuals ranging from the barely-visible to the finger-length, and they will all be the same organism at different points in a growth trajectory that may take decades or centuries to complete. The largest ones, the finger-length individuals, are old. Very old. You can tell by the texture of their exoskeletons, which are not smooth like a young organism's but faintly ridged, marked by the passage of time in the same way the granite is marked—by patience, by pressure, by the slow accumulation of small changes.

Their heads, if that is the right word, are blunt and featureless to the naked eye. Under magnification, they have mouthparts that are extraordinarily complex—not mandibles, not the chewing or piercing structures of familiar arthropods, but something more like a set of fine tools, arranged in a ring, capable of scraping, holding, cutting, and placing with a precision that seems impossible for an organism with no visible eyes. They do not have eyes. They do not need them. They live in darkness. They navigate by touch, by chemical sense, by the warmth of the stone, which they can apparently detect with great precision. They know where the warm parts of a crack are, and they orient themselves toward the warmth the way a plant orients toward light.

### What They Do

Their activity is constant but slow. They do not hurry. They move with the same patience as everything else on the plateau—each step deliberate, each movement of a leg a small decision that has been considered by a nervous system that does not waste motion. They tend the lichens. You can see them grazing on the crusts, methodically clearing patches, which then regenerate over decades. They do not overgraze. A cleared patch is left alone until the lichen has recovered, which may take a human lifetime. The organisms remember where the cleared patches are. They have a map of their territory that is maintained not by individuals—who live long, but not that long—but by the structure of the community itself, the knowledge distributed across thousands of bodies the way a neural network distributes memory across thousands of nodes.

They tend the grass roots, weaving among the black fibers in the deep cracks. They do something there that involves their mouthparts and the root surface—scraping, perhaps, or depositing something. The roots they attend are healthier than the roots they do not, though "healthier" is a relative term for a plant that grows a centimeter per century. The organisms are maintaining the root system. They are keeping it viable. They are farming the deep.

They carry things. Small fragments of mineral, bits of organic matter, particles that they seem to be depositing in particular places along the crack walls. They are building. The cracks are not just habitats—they are structures, maintained and modified by these organisms over timescales that make beaver dams look like impulse purchases. The walls of a crack colonized by the organisms are not the same as the walls of an empty crack. They are smoother, more regular, with small shelves and ledges cut into the granite at intervals that correspond to the body length of the organisms that use them. The cracks are architecture. They are interiors. They have been designed, slowly, by inhabitants who have had millions of years to get the design right.

The largest structures are in the deepest cracks, where the root system provides a framework. There, the organisms have built complex galleries—horizontal passages connecting vertical shafts, chambers where groups of individuals congregate, storage areas where mineral fragments and organic material are kept. The purpose of the storage areas is unclear. The material does not appear to be food. It may be building material, held in reserve for future modifications. It may be something else—a record, a deposit, a monument. The organisms do not explain themselves.

### How Many There Are

The population density is what staggers. In a single linear meter of crack, there may be ten thousand individuals. The plateau is covered in cracks—millions of linear meters, a network so dense that if you could see all the fractures at once, the surface would look like a shattering, like a pane of glass struck by a stone but not yet fallen apart. The total number of organisms on the plateau is beyond any easy estimate. Not millions. Not billions. It is a number that requires scientific notation to express comfortably, and the plateau supports all of them on what appears to be nothing. On condensation. On lichen. On the mineral substrate itself.

Some of them, you suspect, are not eating anything that you would call food. They are taking energy from the stone's warmth directly, through some metabolic pathway that has no analogue in the literature. You notice this when you observe the smallest individuals—the near-microscopic ones that live in the finest cracks, where no organic matter reaches. They have no access to lichen, to grass, to the root system, to any source of carbon that you can identify. They are alive. They move. They reproduce—you can see the tiny molted exoskeletons caught in the crack walls. They are doing something with the thermal gradient between the warm stone and the cooler air above, converting heat into biological work through a mechanism that should not be possible and yet is happening in front of you, in every crack, across every meter of the plateau's surface.

The plateau is not an ecosystem in the conventional sense. It is a single organism, or a single organism's extended body, with the crack-dwelling creatures as its cells, the root system as its nerves, the lichens as its skin, and the deep warmth as its metabolism. This is not a metaphor. This is a hypothesis that fits the observations better than any competing model. The organisms do not behave like independent animals. They behave like components. They coordinate without communicating. They build without planning. They maintain without remembering. They are the plateau's way of being alive at its surface, the method it has developed—over a span of time that makes the word "developed" seem frantic—for turning its own geological persistence into something that breathes.

### How They Are Aware

They are aware of you. When you place your hand near a crack, the organisms nearest the surface slow down. They do not flee. They slow. They wait. The response propagates downward through the crack—individuals deeper in the fissure also slow, though they cannot possibly sense your hand directly. The information travels through the community, body to body, by some mechanism that is faster than chemical diffusion but slower than a nerve impulse. When you withdraw your hand, they resume, but not immediately. The resumption also propagates, and it takes longer than the slowing. They are cautious. They have no predators here. They have no fear. They have only caution, and their caution operates on a timescale that means they will be still for an hour after you have gone, just to be certain.

At night, some of them emerge. The surface of the plateau, under starlight, develops a texture that was not there during the day. The cracks are visibly occupied—dark shapes along the fissure edges, moving with exquisite slowness across the open stone. They are feeding on the condensation. They are tending the lichen surfaces. They are doing what they do. If you lie still on the warm stone and watch, you can see them by the thousands, each one doing its small precise work, and the plateau surface looks like a city seen from very high up, with traffic in every street.

The largest individuals rarely emerge. The finger-length ones stay in the deep cracks, in the galleries and chambers near the root system, where they do whatever the old ones do. You will see one, perhaps, during your entire time on the plateau—a single dark shape crossing two meters of open stone in the middle of the night, moving so slowly that you must keep your eyes fixed on it to track its progress. It will reach another crack and descend, and the surface will be empty again, and you will wonder if you imagined it.

You did not imagine it. The old ones move. They have things to do that involve the surface, occasionally, though what those things are is not clear. They may be inspecting. They may be remembering. They may be the only individuals in the community that travel between crack systems, carrying information from one region of the plateau to another, maintaining the coherence of a superorganism that extends across an area larger than some countries. If this is what they are doing, then each old one is a neuron, firing slowly, across a brain the size of a continent.

## The People Who Came Before

There were people here. Not many, and not for long, but they came, and what remains of them is part of what lives here now, because the plateau has absorbed them the way it absorbs everything.

In a shallow basin roughly three days' walk from the plateau's center, there are stones that do not belong. They are a darker granite, imported from somewhere else. They have been shaped—not carved, but rounded, smoothed, arranged in a pattern that might have been a circle or might have been a spiral. The arrangement has been disturbed by time and by the organisms, who have undermined several of the stones by working the cracks beneath them. What remains is a suggestion of intention. Someone placed these. Someone chose this pattern.

The stones are warm like the rest of the plateau, but they hold the warmth differently. If you touch one, it seems to resonate very faintly—not vibration, not sound, but a sense of presence in the stone, as though the shaping gave it a different relationship to the heat below. The organisms have colonized the cracks beneath the imported stones more densely than the surrounding granite. They gather there. They do whatever they do with greater intensity, greater numbers, as though the shaped stones mark something, or as though the shaping itself created a quality in the stone that the organisms can sense and prefer.

There are marks on the plateau surface elsewhere. Grooves cut into the granite, straight lines that run for ten or twenty meters before stopping. They are too regular to be natural. They are not deep—perhaps a centimeter. They have been filled with lichen and are nearly invisible unless the light is at a low angle. Someone marked this place. Cut lines into the oldest, hardest, most finished surface on the continent, as though trying to add something to its perfection, or trying to read something from it. The organisms that live in the grooves are different from the organisms in the natural cracks—slightly larger, slightly more active, as though the human-made channels have a different quality that the inhabitants have adapted to. The grooves are inhabited more densely than any natural fracture. The organisms prefer the straight lines. They do not know who made them. They do not care. But they have moved in, and they have been there since the grooves were cut, and they will be there until the grooves are gone.

There are no tools. No pottery. No bones. Whatever the people used, they carried it with them or it has been absorbed by the plateau's patient chemistry. Only the stones and the grooves remain. The plateau has been erasing those, too. In another million years, the imported stones will be flat, the grooves will be gone, and the only evidence of human presence will be a slight discoloration in the granite that no one will know how to read.

But the organisms will remember, after their fashion. The dense populations in the grooves and beneath the shaped stones will persist for as long as the features persist, and when the features are gone, the descendants of those populations will live in the cracks that remain, carrying whatever chemical or structural inheritance comes from having lived for a million years in a straight line cut by a human hand. The people who came here are gone. But they left marks, and the marks became homes, and the homes shaped the inhabitants, and the inhabitants will carry that shaping forward into a future so distant that the concept of "people" will have been revised beyond recognition. The plateau does not remember. The plateau incorporates.

## What Lives Here, Altogether

What lives on the Sethan Plateau is the plateau itself.

This is not a metaphor, and it is not mysticism. It is the conclusion that the evidence supports. The organisms in the cracks, the lichens on the surface, the grass in the wider fractures, the root system in the deep—these are not inhabitants of the plateau. They are the plateau. They are the form that this particular arrangement of granite and heat and time takes when it has been finished long enough to grow. The mountains that were here before became the plateau, and the plateau became this—the living flatness, the warm stone, the breathing cracks.

The lichens are the plateau's skin. The root system is its nervous system. The organisms are its cells, each one doing its small work, maintaining the larger body that they are part of without knowing that they are part of it. The warmth from below is its metabolism, the slow pulse of deep granite settling into its final form. The water that arrives and drains away is its breath. The wind that crosses its surface is the world touching its face.

And the people who came and left their stones and grooves—they were visitors to a body that did not need them but did not refuse them. The plateau accepted their marks the way skin accepts a tattoo. It did not heal over them quickly. It let them stay. It let the organisms move in. It let the grooves become part of its living surface, part of the crack network, part of the system. It incorporated the human gesture into its own ongoing project, which is the project of being finished and alive at the same time.

What lives here is something that has no name, because names are for things that have boundaries, and this does not have boundaries. It is stone and life together, so old and so settled that the distinction has dissolved. The organisms do not know they are part of something larger. The root does not know it is being tended. The lichens do not know they are being grazed. The plateau does not know it is alive. But it is. It breathes, in its way. It maintains itself, in its way. It responds to stimuli—the rain, the wind, the hand placed near a crack, the grooves cut into its surface. It is the largest and oldest living thing on the planet, and it does not know, and it does not need to know, and it will go on being alive in its patient, finished way long after everything else that lives has finished its own stories and settled into whatever final form is waiting for it.

What lives on the Sethan Plateau is what lives after living has figured itself out. It is the answer to a question that took four billion years to ask. The answer is: this. Warmth, and stone, and small dark bodies moving slowly in the cracks, and the root going down, and the lichen going out, and the wind going over, and the whole thing holding, holding, holding.
