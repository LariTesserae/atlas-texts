# What Lives Here

---

There is a woman. Or there was. It is hard to tell now where she ends and the room begins.

She has been here long enough that her feet know the tiles. Not by sight — she stopped relying on sight some time ago — but by the slight variations in glaze texture under her soles, the way tile fourteen rows from the eastern opening has a chip on its northwest corner that her smallest toe finds each time she passes. She walks a circuit. She has always walked a circuit, though she cannot remember when the circuit became the shape of her days rather than something she chose. The circuit is: from the eastern opening, along the northern wall where the columns stand closest together and the moss builds in deep soft drifts that she has learned to step over, to the western opening where the lacewing lives, then back along the southern wall where the bronze fittings cluster and the trailing plants hang thickest. She touches certain things as she passes. A column. A valve handle. A frond. The same frond each time, the one with the leather-dark waxy leaf that grows from the third pocket in the second column from the west. She does not know why she touches it. Her hand goes to it the way her feet go to the circuit. She is not deciding.

She is old. She thinks she is old. Her hair is long and has gone the color of the iron — a deep rust-brown, darker where it is damp, which is always. It hangs past her waist and she does not braid it or tie it. It clings to her neck and shoulders and the small of her back, and it drips, and the drips land on the tiles and join the film that is already there, and this does not seem to her like a loss. Everything here drips. She is participating.

Her clothes were made. She can see the seams in them when she holds the fabric up to the diffused light — seams that are not her seams, that were sewn by hands she cannot picture, in a place she cannot remember. The fabric is some kind of woven plant fiber, rougher than the ferns but softer than the iron. It has absorbed so much moisture over so long that it has taken on a greenish cast at the hems, as if the moss is migrating into it, as if the room is dyeing her slowly from the edges inward. She wears a tunic and wide trousers and she goes barefoot. Her soles are the same dark color as the ceramic glaze. She has not worn shoes in — she does not know. She does not count time. The room counts time. She lives inside its counting.

She drinks from the bronze dishes. This is important to say because it is the most deliberate thing she does. When the pumps are in their strong phase and the water-threads thicken and the dishes fill faster, she kneels — her knees know exactly which tiles have grip, which ones are safe, which ones are slick — and she puts her mouth to the rim of a dish and drinks. The water is clean. It has been through the mountain's filtration galleries, through kilometers of substrate, through processes she does not understand and has never seen. It tastes like the bronze it falls into and the iron it seeps through and something else underneath, something mineral and cold that belongs to the deep earth, to the pumping stations far below. She drinks and the water enters her and becomes her and she stands and walks and her skin sweats and the sweat joins the film on the floor and the film drains to the channels and the channels carry it back to the pumps and the pumps push it up and it falls again. She knows this. Not as an idea. As a sensation. She feels the loop. Sometimes, when the pumps are strong and the thrum is deep in her sternum, she feels herself as part of the loop — a node in the circuit, a place where the water pauses, takes a shape, moves on.

She was not always alone. This she is certain of, though the certainty has no detail attached to it. There is a space in her that is shaped like other people, the way a mold is shaped by what was poured into it and removed. She knows that there were hands that placed the ferns in the column-pockets. She knows that someone designed the circuit of the tiles and chose the glaze colors — the greens and iron-blacks that she has stared at so long they have become the background of her thinking. She knows that the trailing plants were trimmed once, that there was a time when they did not reach the floor, when someone came with shears and cut them back and the cut ends dripped and the room smelled sharp and wounded for a day. She knows this the way she knows her own body was made — not by her, by something outside her, something she cannot reach anymore.

She does not remember their faces. She does not remember their names, if they had names. She remembers — or her body remembers — the sensation of being seen. Of standing in this room and being looked at by eyes that were not her eyes, and the look said something. It said *this is where you will be* or *this is what we are making* or *we will come back*. She does not know which. The sensation comes to her sometimes when she is standing at the western opening and the fog pulls back and she can see the twin chamber across the valley, its own water-threads catching light, and for a moment the two chambers face each other like two open mouths and she feels the look again, the look that placed her here, and then the fog returns.

She does not go to the twin chamber. She does not climb down the mountain's outer slopes and cross the valley and climb up the other side. She has thought about it. Or rather: the thought has appeared in her the way the self-seeded ferns appear in the cracked tiles — not planted, not chosen, just emerging from some seed that was always present in the material. The thought is small and persistent and she does not act on it and it does not die. It grows very slowly, the way the ferns grow, the way the moss spreads, the way all growth happens here: patient, moist, without urgency.

She talks. Not to anyone. To the room. She has a voice and it is soft and it is shaped by the acoustics of the chamber — the iron vault, the open sides, the water-threads — into something that does not echo so much as linger. Her words hang in the saturated air the way the water-threads hang from the ceiling: filaments, continuous, falling slowly. She says things like *there* and *this one* and *again* and *it is warm*. She says *I am here* sometimes, and the room absorbs it, and nothing changes, and this is — she understands this, in the place where understanding lives, below language — this is the point. The room does not need her to say anything. The room does not need her. But she is here and she says things and the saying is part of the circuit, like drinking, like walking, like sweating. It goes in and it goes around and it comes back.

She tends the garden. This is the word she uses, though she does not remember learning it. *Tending.* It is not work. It is not labor. It is closer to the way her hand goes to the dark-leaved fern on the second column — a motion that happens because the circuit passes that point and the motion is part of the circuit. She clears the bronze dishes when moss grows over their rims, brushing it away with her fingertips so the water can spread correctly. She removes dead fronds, pinching them at their base and laying them on the moss where they will become substrate. She steers the trailing vines away from the valve handles, winding their reddish stems around the column-pockets instead so the mechanisms stay clear. She does not know why the mechanisms must stay clear. She does not know what the valves do or what the gauges measure. She knows that the needle in the glass window moves, and that when it moves the pumps pulse, and that when the pumps pulse the water falls, and that when the water falls the plants grow, and that when the plants grow she is not alone.

She is not alone. This needs to be said carefully. The lacewing is not the only fauna. There are the trails in the moss — tiny, winding, made by something too small to find. She has looked. She has pressed her face into the moss and breathed its green smell and looked for the thing that makes the trails, and she has not found it, and she has concluded — not in words but in the body-conclusion that is how she concludes things — that the thing that makes the trails is the moss. That the moss moves. That in this room, in this warm wet light, in this closed system that has been running for longer than anyone can remember, the distinction between plant and animal has softened, has become like the film on the tiles: present, persistent, not quite one thing or the other. The moss moves and leaves trails and she accepts this the way she accepts the pump-thrum in her sternum. It is the room being the room.

She is the room being the room.

This is what she knows, if she knows anything: she is not the room's keeper. She is not its caretaker or its warden or its priest. She is not the last remnant of the design team, faithfully maintaining a system whose purpose she has forgotten. She is not a survivor. She is not waiting. She is part of the mechanism. She is the part that walks. The pumps are the part that pulses. The water is the part that falls. The ferns are the part that grows. The fog is the part that breathes. She is the part that touches the dark-leaved fern on the second column and says *there* and drinks from the bronze dishes and feels the loop moving through her. She is a node in a circuit that was designed by hands she cannot picture and has been running longer than her memory holds and will run longer than her memory will ever hold and she is in it and of it and the water enters her and leaves her and enters her again.

She does not have a name. She does not need one. The room does not have a name. The mountain does not have a name. They are unnamed the way a heartbeat is unnamed — it does not need language to continue. It continues because it was built to continue.

She is old and she is young and she is neither. The room does not measure time in years. It measures time in pump-cycles, in the thickening and thinning of water-threads, in the unfurling and browning of fronds. Her body measures time the same way. She has unfurled and browned and unfurled again, many times, and each time the unfurling is the same and each time the browning is the same and the room holds both in its warm wet hands and does not distinguish between them.

She is standing at the western opening now. The fog has pulled back. The twin chamber is visible across the valley, its water-threads catching a different angle of light, its own garden a dark blur of green against the iron. She feels the look — the old look, the look that placed her here. She feels it from the twin chamber. She feels it from the mountain. She feels it from herself.

She turns. She walks. Her feet find the circuit. The pump-thrum builds in the floor. The water-threads thicken. The bronze dishes fill. The lacewing opens its wings — no, it does not open them, it opens them, it has been waiting, it has been alive this whole time — and it lifts from the fern frond and flies in a slow spiral toward the vaulted ceiling, where it catches a thread of water on one translucent wing and the wing refracts the diffused light into a brief, small spectrum that hangs in the warm air like a word no one has said yet.

She watches. She keeps walking. The room holds.
