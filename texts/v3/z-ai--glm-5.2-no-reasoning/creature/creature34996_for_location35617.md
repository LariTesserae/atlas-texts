# What Lives in Verreth

The first thing to understand is that you will not see them clearly. Not because they hide — they do not hide — but because the eye slides over them the way it slides over the stone walls and the retaining terraces and the lichen bands. They belong here in a way that makes them difficult to separate from the belonging. You have to work to see them, and when you do, you wonder why you tried.

---

## The Body of a Verreth Person

They are not tall. This is the first thing, though "not tall" is not quite right either — they are *compressed*, as if the altitude has pressed them down the way it presses the grain short-stalked and the vegetation low. Not stunted. Condensed. The men stand perhaps to your chin, the women a little higher or a little lower — there is not much difference between them in frame, and you come to understand that this is not a dimorphism that matters here. Broad through the shoulder and chest, narrow at the hip, with legs that are shorter than the torso would suggest. Their center of gravity sits low. They do not fall. You will never see a Verreth person fall.

Their skin is the color of the stone — not a metaphor, or not only. It is a gray-brown that shifts with the light, darker in summer when the sun sits on the terraces, paler in the deep cold season, but always carrying that mineral tone, as if the basalt has leeched into them over generations or as if they have grown from the same rock. It is not unhealthy. It is the color of someone who has been outdoors their entire life in a place where the air is thin and the UV is strong and the wind is constant. Their skin is thick. Not rough, exactly, but dense, like leather that has been worked but not softened. It holds expression poorly. A Verreth face does not move the way other faces move — the muscles are there, they function, but they have been trained by the cold into a kind of economy, a minimal vocabulary of expression that the people here can read fluently and outsiders cannot read at all.

Their eyes are the surprise. They are large — larger than the face seems built for, set deep under brow ridges that are more pronounced than you'd expect, and they are pale. Not blue, not gray, not any color that has a name. They are the color of the spring water when it catches the light — a clear, cold, mineral paleness that looks through things rather than at them. When a Verreth person looks at you, you have the sense of being assessed not as a threat or a visitor or a curiosity but as a phenomenon, a thing that is present and that will or will not become relevant. Their gaze does not linger. It arrives, registers, and moves on.

Their hands are remarkable. This is where the compression of their bodies becomes visible — the fingers are short, thick-jointed, the nails grown dense and flat and slightly wide, like the hooves of the goats. The palms are calloused in specific patterns that map to specific tools: the curve of a sickle handle, the edge of a hoe, the rough circumference of a quern stone. You can read a Verreth person's work history in their hands the way you can read the channels in the rock — by the wear, by the groove, by the slow patient evidence of repetition.

Their hair is dark and coarse, the texture of the goat's wool they weave. The men wear it cut short, unevenly — not styled but simply shortened with a knife when it interferes. The women wear it longer, braided close to the skull in a single plait that hangs down the back, and the plait is often bound with a strip of the same dusty-rose wool they weave from the dyed fiber. Children's hair is uncut and unbraided and stands up in the wind like the pale grasses on the upper rim.

They age, but differently. You will see a face and assign it fifty years, and learn the person has seen eighty. The aging happens in the skin and the hair — the skin growing denser and more lined, the hair going not gray but white, a white that carries the same mineral quality as the eyes — but the body does not decline in the way you expect. An elder works the terraces. An elder carries stone. An elder walks the contour streets at the same unhurried pace as a child, and you realize after a while that the pace is not a choice but a feature, that these are people who have never moved quickly because the place does not require speed and they have become what the place requires.

## The Way They Hold Themselves

A Verreth person does not hurry. This is not patience, exactly — patience implies the overcoming of urgency, and there is no urgency here to overcome. They move at the speed at which the place moves, which is the speed of water through stone channels and grain ripening and lichen spreading. When they walk, they walk with a gait that is peculiar to them — a slow, flat-footed stride that keeps the center of gravity low and the contact with the ground constant. They do not bounce. They do not swing their arms. They move like something flowing, like water finding its level.

When they work, they work the same way — not slowly, but without excess. No wasted motion. A woman harvesting grain with a bone sickle moves along the row in a continuous motion, cut and gather and cut and gather, her body turning at the end of the row and coming back along the next line without pause, without straightening, without any gesture that is not the work itself. A man rebuilding a terrace wall selects a stone, turns it, places it, tests it, removes it, selects another, and the whole process has the rhythm of breathing. You can watch for an hour and not see a single moment that looks like decision. It is all decision, but the decisions are so small and so continuous that they are invisible.

They sit the way stone sits. This is the best way to describe it. When a Verreth person sits — on a low wall, on a doorstep, on the bare rock of the ledge — they do not fidget. They do not cross and uncross their legs. They do not shift their weight. They settle, and then they are still, and the stillness is not the stillness of someone waiting to move but the stillness of someone who has arrived at a position and has no reason to leave it. They can sit this way for a long time. An hour. Two hours. Watching the light move across the caldera. Watching the goats on the upper rim. Watching nothing.

## What They Wear

Everyone wears the same thing. This is not a rule — it is not enforced, not discussed — but it is as consistent as the temperature and the spring.

Layers. Always layers. A base layer of thin woven wool, undyed, the natural gray-brown of the goat fiber, fitted close to the body. Over this, a heavier tunic, looser, dyed one of the muted colors — the dusty rose, the pale gold, the gray-green. The tunic is belted with rawhide. Below, trousers of the same heavy wool, tucked into boots.

The boots are the most individual thing a Verreth person owns. They are handmade — each person makes their own, or inherits them — and they are built for the rock. The soles are thick, layered leather, hardened by repeated wetting and drying, sometimes stiffened further with a treatment of pine resin. The uppers are fur-lined — goat fur, the coarse outer hair removed, the soft undercoat retained. The boots come up to mid-calf and are laced with rawhide strips that wind around the leg in a spiral. Children's boots are smaller versions of the same thing, and the children do not remove them indoors, because indoors is not a place where boots are removed.

The caps are leather. Goat leather, treated but not dyed, shaped to the head by wearing until it conforms. They sit low on the skull, covering the ears, and they give the Verreth face a framed quality, like a painting set in its border. Some people add a band of woven wool around the cap — a narrow strip of color — and this is the closest thing to personal adornment. It is not decoration. It is identification. The pattern of the band tells you which family, which terrace, which generation. The people here read these bands the way they read faces — fluently, without effort, without knowing they are doing it.

Children wear the same things, smaller. There is no special clothing for children. There are no bright colors for children. A Verreth child is a Verreth adult at a smaller scale, doing smaller versions of the same work, wearing the same layers, sitting with the same stillness when they sit. The only difference is the hair — uncut, unbound — and the way they look at things, which is not yet the flat mineral gaze of the adults but something closer to the goats' gaze, a watching that is not yet assessment.

## What They Do

The work of Verreth is the maintenance of equilibrium. This is not how they would describe it — they would describe it as work, as the things that need doing, as the sequence of tasks that fills a day — but from outside, it is visible as something more precise than labor.

The mornings begin at the spring. Someone goes to the basin — not a designated someone, just whoever wakes first, whoever is nearest — and checks the flow. The flow does not need checking. It has not changed. But the checking is part of the pattern, and the pattern is the thing. They cup their hand under the stream and drink. They fill a clay jug. They carry it back. This takes four minutes.

The goats come down from the upper rim at dawn, and someone opens the pen. The pen is a low stone enclosure at the eastern edge of the settlement, and it has a gate that is not a gate — just a gap in the wall, closed by a stone that is rolled into it and rolled away. The goats come in. They are salted. A handful of rough salt, gray with mineral impurity, is scattered on a flat stone, and the goats crowd around it and lick. They are not milked every day. They are milked when they need milking, which is a determination that the people here make by some method that is not explained and not visible. The milk is thick and warm and tastes of the grasses on the upper rim, which is to say it tastes of cold and wind and altitude.

The terraces are worked. This is the main work — the turning of soil, the planting, the weeding, the harvesting, the replanting. The work is constant but not frantic. Each terrace has its season, its cycle, and the cycles overlap so that there is always something being planted and something being harvested and something being weeded, but there is never a moment of intensity, a harvest that must be done today or lost. The terraces produce enough. They do not produce more. This is not a place of surplus. It is a place of exactly sufficient, and the sufficiency is the point.

The grain is harvested with bone sickles. The roots are pulled by hand. The berries are picked into woven baskets and carried to the shaded interiors, where some are eaten and some are set aside for dye. The goats are tended. The walls are checked — not repaired, checked, the way you check a foundation you trust. The channels are cleared of the small debris that the wind deposits — a leaf, a stem, a grain of sand. The work takes the morning and part of the afternoon, and then the sun goes behind the rim and the cold returns and the work ends.

In the late afternoon, after the shadow, the people sit. This is the time of stillness, and it is as much a part of the day as the work. They sit in the courtyards, on the low walls, on the doorsteps, and they do not do. They watch the light leave the caldera. They listen to the spring. They breathe the air that is cooling, thickening, becoming the cold version of itself. Sometimes someone speaks. Not often. The speech is low, brief, functional — a statement about a wall, a goat, a terrace. Not conversation. Not gossip. The exchange of information that is already known, offered not because it is needed but because the sound of a voice in the gathering cold is a thing that matters.

The evenings are inside. The fires are lit. The smoke fills the rooms and drifts out through the smoke-holes. The people eat — grain porridge, roasted roots, sometimes goat meat, sometimes the dark berries crushed into a paste that is sweet and mineral and stains the fingers purple. They eat slowly, from shared bowls, using their hands or flat pieces of stone as plates. There is no meal that is not shared. There is no food that is not the same food. The taste of everything is the taste of the place — smoke, stone, water, cold.

They sleep on pallets of goat-fur and woven wool, against the inner walls, where the stone holds the cold but not the wind. The smoke settles above them. The spring runs. The night does not change things.

## The Children

A child is born in Verreth the way everything else happens — without urgency, without drama, as part of the pattern. The women give birth in the houses, attended by other women, and the men are not present and not absent, doing the work in the terraces until it is done. The birth is not announced. It becomes known the way everything becomes known here — by the fact of it, by the presence of a new small body in the courtyard, by the fact that a woman is carrying a bundle of goat-fur against her chest instead of a basket of grain.

The children grow the way the lichen grows — slowly, in bands. They do not have a period of rapid growth followed by settling. They grow steadily, and the growing is invisible from day to day, visible only across the long scale of seasons. A child who is knee-high to the terrace wall in the cold season is knee-high-and-a-finger-width higher by the next cold season, and this is enough.

They are not taught. This is the thing that strikes you. There is no school, no instruction, no moment where an adult sits a child down and conveys information. The children are present — in the courtyards, on the terraces, at the spring — and they watch, and they do small versions of what they see, and the doing teaches them. A child of four carries water in a small jug. A child of six weeds a terrace with fingers that are already calloused. A child of eight can build a section of retaining wall that will hold. No one tells them they are doing it right. No one tells them they are doing it wrong. They simply do it, and the wall holds, or it does not, and the wall itself is the lesson.

They play, but the play is not what you would recognize as play. There are no games with rules. There are no toys. There is chasing, sometimes, among the younger children — a quick burst of running that is over in seconds, because running is not a thing the body here does for long. There is climbing, on the retaining walls, on the caldera wall, on the pens. There is the imitation of adult work — a child with a stone hoe turning the dirt in a corner of a terrace, not planting anything, just turning. This is play. This is enough.

They do not laugh the way other children laugh. They make a sound — a low, short exhalation, almost a cough — that is the Verreth version of laughter, and it is triggered not by surprise or absurdity but by the recognition of pattern, by something fitting where it should fit. A goat stepping on a stone that rolls. A stone placed in a wall that sits perfectly on the first try. The sound of the spring changing pitch as the basin fills. These things make Verreth children make their sound. It is not joy, exactly. It is satisfaction. It is the pleasure of equilibrium confirming itself.

## The Elders

An elder in Verreth is not a status. It is not a role. There is no word for it that separates it from the simple fact of being old, which is itself not a separation from being young. An elder works. An elder sits. An elder is a person who has been here longer, and the longer-ness is not authority but depth — the way a channel cut deeper carries more water, the way a wall built taller holds more earth.

There are perhaps twelve people in Verreth who would qualify as elders by an outsider's count — white-haired, dense-skinned, their hands so calloused that the fingerprint whorls have been smoothed away. They are not treated with reverence. They are not treated with anything. They are present, and their presence carries the weight of the years they have spent becoming present, and this is enough.

An elder knows things. Not facts, not history — not in the way that knowledge is usually understood. An elder knows which stone in a terrace wall will hold and which will slip. An elder knows when the goats will come down early, which means a change in weather that the sky does not yet show. An elder knows the sound the spring makes when it is exactly right and the sound it would make if it were not, and the difference between these sounds is a difference no instrument could measure but that an elder can hear. This knowledge is not passed on. It is accumulated, by each person individually, through the years of being here. When an elder dies, the knowledge dies with them, and the next elder accumulates their own version, and the two versions are not the same but they are equivalent, and the terraces hold.

The dying is the same. An elder weakens — not suddenly, not in a crisis, but in the way a tool wears, slowly, the edge going soft, the handle losing its grip — and one morning they do not wake, or they do not stand, or they do not go to the spring. They are carried to the caldera floor. Not in a ceremony — in a walk, a carrying, a placing. They are laid in the rubble where the water disappears. The spring runs over them for a day, or two, and then the stones are placed, and that is the end.

There are no markers. There are no names on stones. The person is gone, and the place absorbs them the way it absorbs the water and the light and the wind, and the equilibrium does not shift.

## The Way They Speak

The Verreth language is not one you would recognize. It is not Indo-European, not Sinitic, not any family that has a name. It has the sound of the place in it — short vowels, hard consonants, the consonants shaped by a mouth that is used to being closed against the cold. The words are not long. The sentences are not complex. There is no word for "tomorrow" that is different from the word for "today" — the same word serves both, and the people here do not find this confusing, because for them the distinction is not meaningful.

There is no word for "progress." There is no word for "improvement." There is a word that might be translated as "fitting" — the quality a stone has when it sits in a wall without being forced, the quality a day has when the work is done and the light leaves and the cold comes and everything is where it should be. This word — it sounds like *kehlu*, the *k* soft, the *u* held long — is the closest thing Verreth has to a value, and it is applied to everything. A wall is *kehlu*. A meal is *kehlu*. A death is *kehlu*. The spring is the most *kehlu* thing there is, because it does not change and does not stop.

They speak to each other in the low voice you have already been told about — a volume that is barely above breath, that the stone absorbs, that does not carry past the courtyard it is spoken in. This is not secrecy. It is calibration. A voice that carries further than it needs to is a voice that is louder than it needs to be, and louder-than-needed is not *kehlu*.

They do not speak to outsiders often, because outsiders do not come often, and when they do, the Verreth people do not change their behavior to accommodate them. An outsider is a phenomenon, like a weather event or a track on the caldera floor. It comes. It goes. The place holds.

When they do speak to an outsider, they use fewer words, not more. They do not explain. They answer the question that was asked and not the question that was meant, and they do not elaborate, and if the outsider asks a question that does not have an answer, they are silent, and the silence is not awkward or hostile — it is the same silence that fills the caldera, the silence of equilibrium, and it does not need to be filled.

## The Way They Are Together

There are perhaps ninety people in Verreth. Not a hundred. Not exactly ninety. The number changes — a child is born, an elder is buried — but it stays within a range that is narrow enough that the range itself feels intentional, maintained, like the flow of the spring. There are enough people to work the terraces and tend the goats and maintain the walls. There are not enough people for anything else. There is no surplus of labor. There is no deficit. There are exactly enough hands.

They live in family groups — but "family" is not quite right, because the structure is not the structure you would assume. A household is not a couple and their children. A household is a cluster of people who share a building and a courtyard and a section of terrace, and the cluster is composed of people who are related by blood and people who are not, who have come together by some process that is not marriage and not assignment but something in between — a drifting into affiliation, a recognition of fit. The household is the unit of work and the unit of eating and the unit of sleeping, and it is stable — it does not change its composition often — but it is not permanent. A person can move from one household to another, and this happens, and it is not an event. It is a stone being removed from one wall and placed in another.

There are no marriages. There are pairings — people who sleep in the same pallet, who share the same wool, who work the same terrace row — but the pairing is not marked, not named, not institutionalized. It is visible in the way two people move around each other, the way their work synchronizes, the way one hands the other a tool before it is asked for. The pairing exists. It is not discussed. It ends, sometimes, and the ending is not discussed either, and the two people continue to work the same terraces and eat from the same bowls and the only thing that has changed is the sleeping arrangement, and this is not a thing that requires attention.

Children belong to the household, not to the parents. A child is fed by whoever is near the fire. A child is corrected by whoever sees the error. A child is held by whoever has free hands. This is not a system. It is not a philosophy. It is the natural arrangement of a place where there are exactly enough people and no more, where every adult is responsible for the continuation of the place because the place is the thing that matters.

## What They Know

The people of Verreth do not have a cosmology. They do not have a mythology. They do not have gods, or spirits, or an afterlife. They have the place.

When they die, they go to the caldera floor, where the water disappears. This is not a burial with meaning. It is a return. The body goes to the place where things go when they are done being here, and the spring runs over it, and the stones are placed, and the person is not a person anymore and is not a memory anymore and is not anything anymore except the calcium and the mineral and the slow seepage into the rock. This is not a belief. It is a practice, and the practice is the belief.

The tracks on the caldera floor — the single, unidentifiable compressions in the stone dust — are not a mystery to them. This is the thing you must understand. They are not a mystery because they are not a question. The thing that makes the tracks is a thing that comes and goes, and the coming and going is not a thing that requires understanding. It is a thing that exists, like the spring, like the wind, like the lichen. It does not need to be explained any more than the rock needs to be explained. It is here. It comes and goes. The place holds.

If you asked a Verreth person — and you would have to ask carefully, because a direct question about the tracks will get you the flat answer, "It comes and goes," and nothing more — what the thing on the caldera floor is, they might, if you had been there long enough and sat with them long enough in the late-afternoon stillness, say something that sounds like: "It is the place's own footstep."

Not the place's foot. The place's footstep. The distinction matters. They are not saying the caldera has a body. They are saying that the place itself — the equilibrium, the balance, the *kehlu* — has a presence that is occasionally, minimally, almost imperceptibly visible, and that presence leaves a mark, and the mark is a track, and the track is single, because the place does not walk. It arrives. It departs. It comes and goes.

This is the closest thing Verreth has to a religion, and it is not a religion. It is a recognition.

## What They Are

You will leave Verreth, if you come, and you will try to describe the people, and you will find that you cannot. Not because they are complex — they are not complex, they are the opposite of complex, they are so simple they resist description — but because what they are is not a quality that language is built for. They are *of* the place. Not "from" the place, not "native to" the place, but *of* it, the way the lichen is of the rock, the way the spring is of the crack it emerges from. They are not adapted to Verreth. They are Verreth, in the same way the stone walls are Verreth, in the same way the terraces are Verreth, in the same way the silence is Verreth.

They did not choose this. They are not here because they chose to be, and they are not staying because they decided to. They are here because this is where they are, and the question of leaving does not occur, not because it is forbidden or unthinkable but because it is not a question. It is not a thing that the language has a shape for. The word for "leave" exists — you can leave a tool on a wall, you can leave a stone out of a row — but it does not apply to people, because a person leaving Verreth is not a thing that the grammar supports. It is a sentence without a subject. It is a channel without water.

And yet they are not trapped. This is the other thing you must understand. A stone is not trapped in a wall. Water is not trapped in a channel. They are where they are because the structure of the world has placed them there, and the placing is not a constraint. It is a position. It is where the forces balance.

The people of Verreth are where the forces balance. They are the stillness, made of stone and water and cold and wool and the slow accumulation of being in one place until the place and the person are indistinguishable. They are what lives here. They are what has always lived here. They are what will live here, in the same courtyards, on the same terraces, by the same spring, in the same cold, wearing the same clothes, doing the same work, speaking in the same low voice, until the work is done and the stones are placed and the spring runs over them and they are part of the place in a way that is not a metaphor, that is not a picture, that is not a story.

They are here. They are here. They are here.
