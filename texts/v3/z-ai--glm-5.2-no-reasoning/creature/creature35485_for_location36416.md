# What Lives Here

## I. The Condensation Drinkers

Let us begin with the smallest of the living things, because they are the easiest to overlook, and because overlooking them would be a failure to understand this place at its most fundamental level.

The insect-analogues — and we must use such a phrase, because to call them insects would be to anchor them to a taxonomy they do not belong to, a tree of life that has no root in this soil-less world — the insect-analogues are everywhere. You see them once you learn to see them, and once you learn to see them, you cannot unsee them, because they are on every surface, in every pool, on every frond, and the realization of their ubiquity changes your understanding of the landscape entirely. What seemed empty was populated. What seemed still was full of small, slow motions. What seemed silent was, in fact, murmuring with the tiny sounds of very small bodies going about very small lives.

They are pale. This is the first thing to understand about everything that lives here, and it is perhaps the single unifying feature of the fauna: *paleness*. Not the paleness of disease or deprivation, but the paleness of integration, of having become so thoroughly adapted to an environment that the environment shows through you. They are the color of the water and the light. They are translucent. You can see, in the larger ones, the faint shadows of their internal structures — not organs, exactly, but something like organs, something like the shadows of processes happening inside a body that is not quite opaque enough to hide them. They are like glass shrimp. They are like embryonic things that have reached maturity without ever developing the opacity of adulthood. They are like the ghosts of insects, except that they are thoroughly, densely alive.

The winged ones sit on the fronds with their wings spread wide, and you must understand what this means, because it is not a posture of rest or display. It is a *working posture*. The wings are broad and flat and utterly, impossibly thin — so thin that they are transparent, so thin that you can see the frond beneath them through the wing itself — and their entire surface is exposed to the air, and condensation forms on them, and this is the point. The wings are condensation-catchers. The wings are *fields*. The insect sits and the water gathers on the wing-surfaces and the insect draws its long, fine mouthparts across its own wings and drinks. It is farming water from the air, using its own body as a surface. It is doing what everything in this world does, which is: *being a surface*.

They drink constantly. Not continuously — there is a rhythm to it, a cycle, a patience — but constantly in the sense that the drinking is always happening, always about to happen, always just finished. The mouthparts are long and tubular and they move with the delicacy of a surgical instrument, tracing the wing-surface, drawing the water inward. The insect's body swells almost imperceptibly with each drink and then settles, and the condensation gathers again, and the cycle repeats. It is a small life. It is a life entirely contained within a single square centimeter of frond-surface. But it is complete. Nothing is missing from it. The insect does not lack for anything, because everything it needs — water, warmth, light, a surface to cling to — is present in infinite, unchanging abundance.

There are others. Some have no wings and crawl along the tiles, moving with a slowness that makes you question whether they are moving at all until you look away and look back and find them in a different position. They are even more translucent than the winged ones, almost invisible, almost not there, and they live in the film of water that covers the tiles, in the thin, warm layer that is neither pool nor air but something between. They are surface-dwellers in the most literal sense: they live *on the surface*, on the tension of the water, on the boundary between wet and dry, and they never submerge and never leave the surface. They are the benthos of a world that has no depths.

Some live in the seams between tiles, in the hairline gaps where the roots of the plants descend. These are the smallest of all — barely visible as individuals, visible only as a faint movement in the shadow of the seam, a shimmer, a suggestion of life. They may be feeding on the root-material, or on the moisture that collects there, or on something else entirely, something microscopic, something that exists at the scale of the joint and the crack. They are the detritivores of this world, the decomposers, the things that live in the gaps, and they are doing the work of breaking down whatever needs to be broken down, which is very little, because very little dies here, and what dies is absorbed so quickly and so completely that there is almost nothing left to decompose.

## II. The Shallow-Water Walkers

The things that move through the pools are harder to describe, not because they are more complex but because they are more *present*, more visible, more clearly alive in a way that demands recognition and response. You cannot overlook them the way you can overlook the insects. They are too large. They move too deliberately. They are too obviously *going somewhere*, even if the somewhere they are going to is indistinguishable from the somewhere they came from.

Their bodies are long and flat and segmented, and the segments are not unlike the segments of the plant-stalks, which raises a question you cannot answer: did the plants evolve to look like the animals, or the animals to look like the plants, or were they both made by the same hands, and do those hands have a signature, a style, a preference for certain forms? The segments are smooth and slightly swollen at their midpoints, and between the segments, the limbs emerge — long, thin, articulated, with joints that bend in ways that are not quite the way insect legs bend or the way crustacean legs bend or the way any leg you have seen bends. They are more like fingers. They are more like the arms of a very small, very patient person. The limbs end not in feet or claws or pads but in something that is simply *flat* — a flattened tip, a disc, a surface, and the surface spreads against the tile or the water-surface and provides grip, or traction, or perhaps *adhesion*, as though the limb-tips are slightly sticky, as though the animal is climbing rather than walking, even on flat ground.

They move through the shallow water with a motion that is not swimming and not crawling but something between the two — a kind of flowing, a kind of gliding, as though the water is not an obstacle to their movement but a medium that assists it, that carries them, that they are merely directing. Their bodies undulate gently, the segments flexing in sequence, and the limbs push and the flat tips grip and release and the whole animal moves forward with a speed that is... deliberate. Not slow. Deliberate. The word matters. Slowness implies a lack of capability, a failure to move quickly. Deliberateness implies a choice, a decision, a pace that has been selected from a range of possible paces and found to be the correct one. These animals are not slow because they cannot be fast. They are slow because there is no reason to be fast, because everything they need is here, in this pool, in this tile, in this square meter of warm shallow water, and there is no predator to flee from and no prey to chase and no season to outrun.

Their heads are wide and flat, and the eyes — if they are eyes — are shallow depressions, cups, basins, like the cups of the flowers, like the basins in the tile-work. They are not organs of vision, or if they are, they do not see in the way that eyes see. They do not form images. They do not focus. They *collect*. They collect light, or pressure, or vibration, or some quality of the environment that has no name in your language because you have never needed to name it, and they distribute what they collect inward, to whatever processing structure lies inside the flat, translucent head. The animals are sensing the world, but the world they are sensing is not a world of shapes and colors and distances. It is a world of gradients and pressures and the particular warmth of a particular patch of water and the particular quality of the light at a particular point in the pool. It is a world of *conditions*, not of *objects*. They do not see things. They feel situations.

When one of them presses its head against your ankle — and it will, if you stand still long enough, because they are not afraid and they are not curious, they are simply *continuing their rounds*, and you are now part of their rounds — when one of them presses its head against your ankle, what is it doing? It is not tasting you, because tasting implies a discriminatory apparatus, a judgment of good or bad, edible or inedible. It is not smelling you. It is *reading you*. It is taking the measure of your warmth, your moisture, your displacement of the water, your surface-quality, and it is adding this information to its ongoing, continuous, lifelong map of the landscape, which is not a map of space but a map of *conditions*, a record of where the warm spots are and where the wet spots are and where the water is deeper and where the light is brighter, and you are, to this animal, simply a new condition. A warm displacement. A temporary feature. A data point.

They do not eat, or not in any way you can observe. There is no mouth that opens to ingest. There is no visible prey. There is no foraging, no hunting, no grazing. They move through the water and they press their heads against surfaces and they move on, and if they are nourished, they are nourished by something you cannot see — by the moisture itself, perhaps, by the mineral content of the water, by the condensation that forms on their bodies and is absorbed through their translucent skin. They may be photosynthetic, in some strange animal way. They may be drawing energy from the constant, unrelenting light. They may be living on a form of energy that your biology does not have a category for. They are *alive*, and they are *continuing*, and the mechanism of their continuation is obscure, and the obscurity does not seem to trouble them.

There are larger ones. You do not see them often. You see them at the edges of visibility, at the point where the haze begins to dissolve the landscape into brightness, and you see them as shapes — low, broad, slow shapes, moving through the shallow water with a patience that makes the smaller walkers seem hasty by comparison. They are the size of a large dog, perhaps, or a small cow, and they are the same translucent pale as everything else, but their translucence is different at this scale — you can see deeper into them, can see the faint architecture of their interiors, can see what might be a gut or what might be a spine or what might be something for which you have no name, a structure that is not quite organic and not quite architectural but something in between, something that looks like it was *formed* rather than grown, as though the animal was not born but was *shaped*, was set into its mold and allowed to set, and then released, and then began to move.

They do not come close. They keep their distance, not with the nervousness of prey animals but with the *uninterest* of things that have no relationship to you, no category in which to place you, no reason to approach or to flee. They are not ignoring you. They are *not-ignoring* you in the way that a mountain does not ignore you — by being so thoroughly itself, so completely present in its own existence, that your presence is simply irrelevant to its ongoing state. You are there. They are there. The fact is noted by neither party.

What do they do? They move. They rest. They press their heads against the tiles, against the stalks, against the water itself, and they remain there for long minutes, or hours, or longer — it is impossible to tell, because time here is not marked by anything, and an hour and a day are the same hour, the same day, the same eternal present. They may be drinking. They may be sensing. They may be doing something that has no equivalent in your experience, something that is unique to this world and to the things that live in it, something that you would need a lifetime here to begin to understand, and even then you would be understanding it from the outside, as a visitor, as an outsider, as something that has not yet been absorbed.

## III. The Canopy Dwellers

Above, in the fronds, the small things watch.

*Watch* is perhaps not the right word, given that their eyes are the same cup-shaped depressions as the eyes of the water-walkers, the same basins, the same collection-points. But *watch* will have to do, because they are clearly *attending* to you, clearly aware of your presence, clearly tracking your movement through the landscape below them with an interest that the water-creatures do not share. The water-creatures include you in their rounds. The canopy-dwellers *observe* you. There is a difference, and the difference matters.

They are the size of your fist, and they have long limbs and wide flat feet and large round heads, and they are — and this is the thing that strikes you most forcefully, the thing that makes them the most disquieting of the living things here — they are *proportional*. Their bodies make sense. The limbs are in the right places. The head is where a head should be. The feet are where feet should be. They are not the alien, rearranged forms of the water-walkers, with their segment-bodies and their finger-limbs. They are *coherent*. They look, despite their translucence and their cup-eyes and their obvious otherness, like something that was designed to a template that you recognize, a template that says: *here is the head, here are the arms, here are the legs, here is the body*. They are, in a word, *anthropomorphic*, and this is what makes them unsettling, because the water-walkers are so alien that they cannot be confused with anything familiar, while the canopy-dwellers are *almost* familiar, *almost* recognizable, *almost* like something you know, and the *almost* is the gap in which the disquiet lives.

They cling to the fronds and the stalks with their wide flat feet, and the feet adhere to the wet surfaces with a gentle, reliable stickiness, and they move through the canopy with a fluidity that is startling after the deliberation of the water-creatures. They are not fast — nothing here is fast — but they are *smooth*, *continuous*, *flowing*, moving from frond to frond to stalk to frond without pause, without hesitation, without the visible calculation of where to place the next foot. They move the way you move through a familiar room in the dark — by feel, by habit, by a knowledge so deeply embedded in the body that it has ceased to be conscious. They have been in this canopy for a very long time. They know every frond, every stalk, every junction, every surface. They move through it the way blood moves through vessels.

They drink. This is the gesture you see most often, the gesture that becomes, after you have watched them for a while, the defining gesture of their existence. They draw their hands across their arms, or across their legs, or across their wide flat feet, and condensation has formed on their skin — the same condensation that forms on every surface here, the same water-from-air — and they bring their hands to their mouths and they drink. It is a gesture so habitual, so embedded, so *unconscious*, that it looks like breathing. It looks like a reflex. It is not a response to thirst. It is not a response to anything. It is a continuous process, a constant harvesting, a way of being in the world in which the boundary between *being* and *drinking* has dissolved. They are drinking the way the fronds are dripping — constantly, without effort, without decision. It is simply what they do. It is simply what they are.

Their mouths are small and round and they are not on the lower part of the face where you expect a mouth to be. They are in the center. In the center of the wide, round head, in the center of the flat face, there is a small round opening, and the hands go to it, and the water goes in, and the head tilts back slightly, and the swallowing happens — you can see it, can see the faint movement of the translucent throat, the faint pulse of something being ingested — and then the head tilts forward again and the hands go back to the arms and the condensation is gathered and the gesture repeats.

They do not eat. They do not sleep, or not in any way you can detect. There is no period of inactivity, no resting-place, no nest or bed or shelter. They are always moving, always drinking, always watching, always in the canopy, always among the fronds. They do not call to each other. They do not touch each other. They do not interact in any way that you can identify as social, and yet they are clearly *together*, clearly *among* each other, clearly existing in a state of mutual awareness that does not require communication or contact. They are a community of solitudes, a gathering of individuals who share a space but not a society, who are present to each other but not *with* each other, who are together in the way that trees in a forest are together — nearby, parallel, contemporaneous, but not *engaged*.

Except when they are.

Because sometimes — and you see this only after watching for a long time, only after the patience of the place has begun to seep into you, only after you have slowed enough to notice things that happen at the pace of condensation — sometimes two of them will be on the same frond, and they will be facing each other, and they will be still. Very still. More still than the stillness of the place itself, which is not true stillness but a very slow motion. They will be *actually* still. And they will remain so for a long time — minutes, tens of minutes — and you will realize that they are doing something. They are not resting. They are not ignoring each other. They are *exchanging* something. The cup-eyes are facing each other, and the cups are open, and whatever those cups collect — light, pressure, condition, quality — is being collected *from each other*, is being *received*, is being *shared*. They are reading each other the way the water-walkers read the tiles. They are taking the measure of each other's conditions. They are comparing. They are... and here the word fails, because there is no word for what they are doing, because it is not communication and not bonding and not mating and not fighting and not any of the categories you have for *what creatures do when they face each other and are still*. It is something else. It is something that belongs to this world and to this world only, and you can watch it but you cannot name it, and the not-naming is part of the experience of being here, part of the slow dissolution of your categories, part of the absorption.

They are, you think, *checking on each other*. They are *confirming*. They are *making sure*. Not making sure that the other is alive — aliveness here is not in question, is not a condition that needs confirmation. Making sure that the other is *the same*. That the other is still in equilibrium. That the other is still at the correct temperature, the correct moisture, the correct state. They are monitoring each other's sameness. They are keeping each other still.

This is, perhaps, what community means here.

## IV. The Shapes at the Edge of Visibility

The larger things — the ones you cannot see clearly, the ones that exist at the boundary of the haze — are the most difficult to describe because they are the most difficult to see, and the most difficult to see because they are the most *integrated*, the most thoroughly adapted, the most completely absorbed into the landscape. They are not separate from the place. They are *of* it, in the deepest sense, in the sense that they are made of the same materials and the same moisture and the same light, and the boundary between their bodies and the world around them is not a boundary but a *gradient*, a slow transition from *creature* to *condition* that has no clear edge.

You see them as movements. You see them as *changes in the quality of the light*, as though a portion of the brightness has thickened slightly, has become slightly more opaque, has acquired a slow, drifting motion that the brightness itself does not have. They are like clouds in a sky that has no clouds. They are like thoughts in a mind that is otherwise still. They are *suggestions*, *implications*, *hints of form*, and they move through the landscape at a pace that is slower than walking, slower than drifting, a pace that is more like *growing*, as though they are not moving *through* the space but *extending into* it, *reaching* into it, *becoming present* in a new location without having left the old one.

They may be the same kind of creature as the smaller water-walkers, grown large over a lifespan that dwarfs your ability to measure it. They may be a different kind entirely. They may be something that is not, in your understanding, a creature at all — not an organism, not an animal, not a living thing, but something else, something that this world produces that does not fit your categories, something that is to an animal what a coral is to a fish, what a lichen is to a moss, what a forest is to a tree. A *collective*. A *process*. A slow, large, patient thing that exists at a scale you can only perceive peripherally, that you can only detect as a change in the texture of the world, as a faint thickening of the light, as a *presence* that is not a shape and not a color and not a sound but a *weight*, a *gravity*, a *pull* on your attention that says: *something is there. Something large. Something that has been there for a very long time.*

They do not approach the edges. Nothing approaches the edges. The edges are the one thing in this world that the living things respond to with something that, if you were generous, you might call *caution*. They do not avoid the edges in the way that prey animals avoid a cliff — with visible alarm, with nervous approach, with the body-language of fear. They simply *do not go there*. The edges are not part of their territory, not part of their rounds, not part of the space through which they move. The edges are simply absent from their world, the way a color you cannot see is absent from your vision — not missing, not lacking, but *not applicable*, outside the range, beyond the field.

## V. What the Living Things Are

And now we must attempt the question that has been hovering behind every observation, every description, every careful account of what lives here: *what are they?*

They are not evolved. Or — to be more precise — it is not clear that they are evolved in the way that you understand evolution, the long, blind, purposeless shuffle of mutation and selection, the patient accumulation of advantageous traits across geological time. There is no selection-pressure here. There is no environment that changes, no climate that shifts, no predator that appears and disappears, no resource that becomes scarce. There is no *pressure*. There is only *condition*, constant, unchanging, and the things that live here have adapted to it so completely that they have become expressions of it, embodiments of it, *aspects of the condition rather than responses to it*.

They may have been *made*. The same hands that laid the tiles and cut the channels and fired the ceramic may have shaped the first bodies, may have set the first organisms into the seams and the pools, may have seeded this world with life the way a gardener seeds a bed. If so, the making was of a kind that is beyond your understanding — not mechanical, not genetic, not any technique you can name, but something that sits between craft and creation, between sculpture and birth, between the making of a pot and the making of a child. The things that live here have the quality of *having been made* — the intentionality, the fitness-for-purpose, the elegance of form — but they also have the quality of *having grown* — the irregularity, the individuality, the small variations that no maker would introduce but that life, left to itself, cannot help but producing.

Or they may have *arrived*. From where, it is impossible to say — there is no *where* outside this world, no other place, no origin, no source. But perhaps the making of this world left gaps, seams, cracks, and perhaps life found those cracks the way the plants found the seams between tiles, and perhaps the things that live here are not the products of the maker but the products of the making, not the things that were put here but the things that *showed up* when the conditions were right, when the warmth and the wet and the light were in place, when the environment was *finished* and *ready* and the first microscopic things drifted in from... from where? From nothing. From nowhere. From the potential that exists in any sufficiently complex system, the potential for life to emerge when the conditions are *exactly right*, when the equilibrium is *exactly balanced*, when the world is *exactly what it is* and nothing else.

It does not matter. The distinction between made and grown has dissolved here, as the distinction between architecture and ecology has dissolved, as the distinction between creature and condition has dissolved. The living things are here. They are part of the system. They are performing their function — if *function* is the right word for something that is not a tool and not a component and not a mechanism but simply a *being*, a *presence*, a *life* — they are performing their function within the equilibrium, and the function is: *to be*. To drink the condensation. To move through the shallow water. To watch from the canopy. To grow on the structures. To be surfaces. To be present. To be *part of the still life*.

## VI. What They Are Becoming

And now the harder question, the question that the place itself seems to be asking, in the slow, patient way that it asks everything: *what are they becoming?*

Because the structures are being absorbed. The made is becoming the grown. The architecture is becoming ecology. And if the plants are absorbing the structures, and the animals are absorbing the plants — living on them, in them, among them, drinking from them, becoming part of their surfaces — then the animals are also absorbing the structures, indirectly, slowly, at a pace that makes the growth of the plants look sudden. The canopy-dwellers cling to the fronds that grow from the seams between the tiles that were made by the hands that made this world, and the canopy-dwellers drink the condensation that forms on the fronds that grows from the seams between the tiles, and the condensation is water that has cycled through the air and the channels and the pools and the bodies of every living thing here, and the water contains minerals from the tiles and the channels and the structures, and the minerals are absorbed by the plants and by the animals and by the moss-things and the vine-things and the lichen-things, and the minerals are *becoming part of the living*, are being transferred from the made to the grown, are being *converted*.

The animals are eating the architecture. Not consuming it — not breaking it, not destroying it, not reducing it to rubble — but *incorporating* it. The made world is being taken into the bodies of the living things, atom by atom, mineral by mineral, so slowly that no individual creature is aware of it, so slowly that no generation differs from the previous generation, so slowly that the only way to detect it is to stand at the scale of geological time and watch, and even then you would need to be patient, you would need to be as patient as this place is patient, you would need to watch for a million years to see the tile become the frond become the animal become the air become the water become the tile again, the cycle completing, the loop closing, the made becoming the grown becoming the made.

The animals are becoming the world. They are becoming more translucent, more integrated, more *of-this-place*, with every cycle of water through their bodies. They are losing whatever opacity they may once have had, whatever individuality, whatever *separateness* from the environment they may once have possessed. They are becoming surfaces. They are becoming *media*. They are becoming the thin, pale, translucent things that you see — things that are almost not-there, almost not-separate, almost not-distinct from the water and the light and the air in which they exist.

And perhaps this is what the maker intended, if there was a maker. Perhaps this is what *made* means — not a thing that is created and remains, but a thing that is *set in motion* and *becomes*. Perhaps the tiles were not the product but the *seed*. Perhaps the channels were not the structure but the *pattern*. Perhaps the structures were not the architecture but the *scaffolding*, the temporary support for something that was always meant to grow, to spread, to cover, to absorb, to become. Perhaps this world was not made to be a garden. Perhaps it was made to *become* a garden. Perhaps the still life was always meant to be *alive*.

And the animals — the condensation-drinkers, the shallow-water walkers, the canopy-dwellers, the large slow shapes at the edge of visibility — they are not inhabitants of the garden. They are the garden. They are the part of the garden that moves, that drinks, that watches, that exists at the scale of minutes and hours rather than the scale of centuries and millennia. They are the *fast part* of the slow process, the visible part of the invisible conversion, the part you can see if you stand still and watch and let the patience of the place enter you and slow you down to the speed at which things become clear.

They are what lives here. They are what *is* here. They are the still-made, the still-becoming, the still-growing, the still-living. They are the inhabitants of a world that is becoming itself, and they are becoming themselves, and the becoming is the only thing that moves in a world where nothing else moves, and the becoming is so slow that it is indistinguishable from stillness, and the stillness is so complete that it is indistinguishable from peace, and the peace is so deep that it is indistinguishable from eternity, and eternity is so patient that it is indistinguishable from a single, unending moment of warm, bright, wet, quiet *being*.

They are here. They have always been here. They will always be here.

They are the still-made, and they are becoming the still-living, and the becoming is the world.
