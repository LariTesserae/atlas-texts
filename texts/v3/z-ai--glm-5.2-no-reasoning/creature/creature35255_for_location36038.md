# What Lives Here

## The Organism That Is the Place

Let me begin with the thing that is hardest to see clearly, because it is everywhere and because it is, in a sense, what makes this place a place at all.

The amber organism — if it can be called a single thing, and I think it can — is not what you would expect from something that covers every surface. It does not feel like a coating. It does not feel like growth, the way moss on a wall feels like something added. It feels like the wall itself decided to become warm and faintly lit and very, very slow.

I touched it, in the lower chambers. I did not intend to — my hand brushed a ledge where the amber mat was thickest, and the contact was surprising enough that I pulled back. The texture is not gelatinous in the way that word suggests. It is more like the surface of a candle that has been cool for an hour but still retains something of its former softness — a yielding quality that is not quite liquid, not quite solid. It is warm. Warmer than the air, warmer than the rock beneath it. The warmth comes from within it, from whatever metabolic process drives its slow pulse, and when you touch it, you feel as though you have interrupted something. Not disturbed it. Interrupted it. The way you might interrupt a person taking a breath by startling them. The surface where I touched it dimmed slightly — the glow retreating from the point of contact — and then, over the next few minutes, returned. I sat beside that spot for a long time afterward, watching it. The dimness filled back in. The amber light returned to the same intensity as the surrounding surface. I could not tell, after a while, whether I had actually disturbed it or whether I had imagined the dimming. The organism is so slow that any change it undergoes feels like something you might have mistaken.

It is one organism. This is important. Not in the way that a forest is one ecosystem — it is one organism in the way that a single body is one organism, connected throughout by tendrils that thread through the carbonate substrate. The walls of one chamber are continuous, through the rock, with the walls of every other chamber. When the glow pulses — that eleven-hour cycle — it does not pulse independently in each chamber. It pulses as a unified body. I noticed this when I moved between the lowest chambers during a single afternoon. The glow in one chamber was slightly stronger than in the next, but the difference was consistent — the phase was offset by a small amount, as though the pulse were a wave moving through the structure from the base upward. A heartbeat, then. Or a peristaltic motion. Something that moves through the whole body of the organism, from its warmest center to its coolest edges, and then returns.

I have tried to imagine what it experiences. I cannot. Its metabolism is so slow that a single chemical reaction in its cells probably corresponds to seconds of my time, and the "thought" — if it has anything like thought — would be a glacial drift of chemical gradients, a slow awareness of warmth and mineral concentration and the faint chemical signature of its own waste being absorbed and reprocessed. But I have a suspicion, and I will state it even though I cannot prove it: the organism knows the carapace-walkers are there. Not in the way that a plant "knows" it is being eaten. In a more integrated way. The walkers' waste feeds it. The walkers' rasping stimulates new growth in the areas that are grazed. The organism and the walkers are not predator and prey. They are a single system — a mouth and a stomach and a recycling loop, separated by a thin barrier of exoskeleton. The organism grows, the walkers eat, the walkers excrete, the organism grows. The cycle is so tight, so closed, that I wonder whether the walkers are not, in some sense, part of the organism — a mobile extension of it, a way of cycling nutrients that the organism's own stationary body cannot achieve alone.

I think this is the truth of the amber organism: it is not a thing that lives in the dome. It is the dome. It is what the dome has become, over millions of years, the way a piece of wood becomes the thing that eats it. The carbonate rock and the organism are not two things in the same place. They are one thing at two different stages. The rock is the organism's past. The organism is the rock's future.

## The Walkers

I will describe one carapace-walker, because they are all very nearly the same, and because one is enough.

It was on the wall of the largest lower chamber, about a meter above the floor. I was sitting on the carved bench — the one the humans left — and the walker was directly in front of me, at eye level, and it was feeding.

Its body is the size of my thumb. Pale — not white, but the color of wet chalk, a soft mineral pallor that seems to absorb the amber light and hold it. The exoskeleton is segmented, six body sections from head to tail, each one overlapping the next like shingles. The legs — six of them, three per side — emerge from the joints between segments. They are articulated in a way that reminds me of the legs of a pillbug, but longer, more slender, and each one ends in a tiny claw that grips the carbonate surface with an audible tick when it is placed.

The head is not distinct from the body in the way that an insect's head is distinct. It is the first segment, slightly wider than the rest, and on its underside are the mouthparts — two broad, flat plates that meet edge to edge and grind against each other. These plates are the source of the rasping sound. They are stone. Not stone-like — actual stone, composed of the same carbonates as the surrounding rock, grown by the walker's body from the minerals it consumes. The walker is, in a very literal sense, made of the place it lives in. Its body is the dome's material, organized into a shape that can move.

It was feeding when I watched it. Its head was pressed flat against the amber mat, and the mouthparts were working — grinding, rasping, the broad plates scraping against the biofilm and pulling it into the body. The motion was rhythmic. Each grind cycle took perhaps two seconds. The walker would press its head forward, the mouthparts would scrape, and then it would pause — not long, just a moment — before repeating. Between cycles, its legs shifted. Not to move — it was stationary — but to adjust its grip, repositioning for better leverage against the wall. Each leg movement was precise. Each claw placement was deliberate. I had the impression, watching it, that the walker was not thoughtlessly feeding but was, at some level, engaged in the work of feeding — that each scrape of its mouthparts was a considered action, not a reflex.

It was aware of me. I am certain of this. When I first sat down on the bench, the walker was feeding on the far side of the chamber. Over the course of perhaps twenty minutes, it moved — slowly, one leg placement at a time, never rushing — to the wall directly in front of me. It did not approach me. It did not react to me in any way I could identify as a reaction. But it moved, over that time, from a random feeding spot to a spot that put it directly in my line of sight, and then it resumed feeding. I think it was curious. I think curiosity is the right word, though I use it with the understanding that a carapace-walker's curiosity is not my curiosity. Its curiosity is the curiosity of a thing that lives in a closed system and has never encountered anything new. It was not frightened. It was not aggressive. It simply moved to where it could observe me, and then it went about its business while observing me, and when I eventually stood and moved to another chamber, it did not follow.

The sound of a single walker feeding is not much. A low, gritty rasping, like a fingernail on sandstone, repeated at two-second intervals. But there are perhaps a dozen walkers in that chamber, and the chamber is small, and the walls reflect sound the way a bell reflects it — cleanly, with a resonance that sustains each rasp longer than it should last. The cumulative effect is a texture of sound, not a single note. It is layered. Individual rasps emerge and fade into the general grinding, and the general grinding swells and contracts as walkers start and stop, and the whole thing sits on a frequency that is low enough to feel in the chest. I sat there for an hour and the sound never became unpleasant. It became familiar. It became the sound of the place, the way rainfall becomes the sound of a forest — not background, but context.

## The Gliders

I almost missed them.

In the middle chambers, where the organism's growth forms those architectural structures — the columns, the arches, the buttresses — the air is thick with transport spores. They are visible. They catch the amber light and float in the still air like dust in a sunbeam, but there is no sunbeam here, only the organism's own glow, and so the spores are lit from all directions, which gives them a quality of being lit from nowhere, of simply being visible, present, without a clear source of illumination. In the denser chambers, the spore haze is thick enough that I could not see the far wall. I could see the growths near me — the carbonate columns, the ribbed shelves — and beyond them, a dim amber haze, and beyond that, the faint outline of more growths, and beyond that, nothing. Just haze and glow.

The gliders live in this haze. They are small enough that I did not see them at first. I heard them before I saw them. The hum — that low, resonant tone, like a glass harp — was present in every middle chamber, and I assumed it was a property of the chambers themselves, an acoustic effect of the organism's growth or the air pressure or something I did not understand. I was sitting in a chamber where the hum was particularly clear, trying to identify its source, when a glider passed within a few centimeters of my face.

It was the size of my thumbnail. Its body — the non-wing part — is tiny, barely larger than a grain of rice, and it is dark, almost black, which makes it nearly invisible against the amber-lit haze. The wings are the visible part. They are not wings in the way that a bird's wings are wings — they are broad, flat, translucent sheets, stiffened by a fine vein structure, and they do not flap. The glider holds them extended and still, and it moves by means I could not see. The air in these chambers is utterly still; there are no currents to ride. But the glider moved, steadily and with clear purpose, through the spore-laden air. I think the wings are sensory organs as well as locomotory ones. I think they detect the density of spores in the air — the way a fish's lateral line detects pressure changes in water — and the glider moves toward areas of higher density, following gradients that I cannot perceive.

The hum is produced by the vibrating organs in the abdomen. I could feel it, faintly, when the glider passed close — a vibration in the air, not a sound exactly, more like the tremor of a tuning fork held near the ear. The pitch changed as the glider moved. When it entered a pocket of denser spores, the hum deepened and intensified. When it moved to a thinner area, the hum rose and faded. The glider was, I realized, singing about its environment. The hum was a continuous report on the concentration of food in the air around it.

There were many gliders in that chamber. Once I knew what to look for, I could see them — dark shapes against the amber haze, moving in slow, deliberate arcs, following the spore gradients. Each one hummed at its own pitch, and the combined sound was a chord, not a single note. The chord shifted as gliders moved through different spore densities, and the shifting was slow enough that it felt organic, like the changing of a drone rather than the playing of a melody. I sat in that chamber for a long time, and the chord never resolved and never repeated and never became anything I could hold in my mind as a pattern. It was just there, changing, the way weather is there, changing.

I think the gliders are beautiful. I want to say that plainly. They are small and strange and they live in a haze of glowing spores in the middle of a living mineral structure, and they sing about the density of their food in the air, and they do this constantly, without ceasing, and they have done this for longer than I can comprehend. There is something in that — in the persistence of that singing, in the way it is both functional and, to my ear, musical — that I can only call beautiful.

## The Worms

I did not see a still-worm.

I looked. In the upper chambers, where the organism's growth becomes that sparse, brittle lattice, I crawled through passages barely wide enough for my shoulders and examined every strut and joint I could reach. The still-worms live inside the lattice — inside the hollow carbonate struts — and I was looking for any sign of their presence. A bulge in a strut. A slight discoloration. A chemical residue from their dissolving secretions.

I found nothing I could identify.

But I know they are there. The lattice is not uniform. Some struts are thicker than others, and some are slightly discolored — a faint amber staining where the carbonate has been chemically altered. And in one place, in one of the uppermost chambers, I found a strut that was open at one end — a tiny hole, barely a millimeter across, where the lattice had been dissolved through. I put my eye close to it and looked inside. The interior of the strut was hollow, as expected, and the inner surface was smooth — not the rough, crystalline surface of naturally deposited carbonate, but a glassy smoothness, the kind of finish that chemical dissolution leaves behind. Something had lived inside that strut. Something had moved through it, dissolving the carbonate as it went, leaving the interior polished and empty.

I sat in that upper chamber for a while, in the near-silence. The grinding of the walkers was very faint here, a distant vibration more felt than heard. The gliders' hum was a memory. The only sound was the almost subliminal ticking of the organism's growth — that molecular crackling, like the sound of a very small fire in a very large room, or like the sound of ice forming on a window, but slower, softer, more like the memory of a sound than the sound itself.

And I thought about the still-worm. A creature so slow that it takes years to cross a single chamber. A creature with no eyes, no sound, no apparent awareness of anything beyond the chemical gradient of the carbonate it is dissolving and absorbing. A creature that has existed in this form, unchanged, for so long that it is considered a living fossil. I tried to imagine its experience. I could not. I could not even approximate it. The closest I came was this: imagine being a chemical reaction. Not the thing that causes the reaction, not the thing that experiences the reaction, but the reaction itself — the slow, molecular process of one substance becoming another, taking years, taking decades, happening in darkness and silence and complete stillness. That, I think, is what it is like to be a still-worm. Not life as I understand it. Not death. Something between — a process that is still running, that has been running for longer than anything else in this place, and that will continue to run until the carbonate lattice is gone or the worm itself is gone, and neither of those things is likely to happen soon.

## The People Who Were Here

I spent the most time with the carvings.

They are in a middle chamber, on a panel of smoothed rock about a meter wide. The amber mat has grown over the lower edge of the panel, and mineral dust has settled into the carved lines, softening them, but they are legible. I sat beside them for the better part of a day, with a glider humming somewhere above me and the distant grinding of the walkers below, and I looked at them.

The dome from outside: a low mound on a flat horizon. Rendered in profile, with a sense of scale that comes from the relationship between the mound and the plain — the plain drawn as a long, flat line extending to the edges of the panel, the mound drawn as a slight interruption in that line. The carver understood this place from outside. They had stood on the plain and looked at the dome and seen what I would see if I went outside now: a small, unremarkable rise in a landscape that has nothing else in it. And they carved it. They thought it was worth recording.

The carapace-walker: rendered with enough anatomical accuracy that I could identify the species. Six legs. Segmented body. The broad, flat mouthparts. The carver had watched a walker closely enough to know its shape. They had looked at it the way I looked at it — with attention, with the patience required to see something clearly. The carving is not idealized. It is not decorative. It is a drawing of a specific animal, made by someone who knew the animal.

The human figure: standing. Holding a stone chisel in one hand and a piece of raw stone in the other. The figure is simple — a body, a head, two arms, two legs — but the posture is specific. The weight is on the back foot. The chisel is held forward, ready to strike. The raw stone is held against a surface, braced. This is a figure in the middle of work. Not posing. Working. The carver carved themselves.

The symbol: a circle with a line through it, repeated three times. I do not know what it means. I sat in front of it for an hour and I do not know what it means. It could be a name. It could be a count. It could be a prayer. It could be a record of something that happened here — three arrivals, three deaths, three seasons, three of anything. It is the one thing in this place that I cannot connect to anything else I can see or hear or touch, and it is the one thing that makes me certain the people who lived here were not so different from me. They carved what they saw, and then they carved what they could not see — something that existed only in their minds, something they wanted to say that was not about the dome or the walkers or the stone but about themselves. And they said it in a language I cannot read, and they said it to no one in particular, and they said it anyway.

The amber mat is growing over the panel. It will cover it. The mineral dust is filling the carved lines. They will become illegible. In time — not long, on the scale of this place — there will be no evidence that anyone was here, that anyone saw the walkers and the gliders and the amber glow and thought it was worth recording. The dome will go on, warm and stable and pulsing with its eleven-hour rhythm, and the walkers will grind and the gliders will hum and the still-worms will dissolve their way through the lattice, and none of them will remember that a person once sat in a chamber and carved into the rock: *we were here. This is what we saw. This is what we did.*

I was here. I saw it. I am writing it down. The amber mat will not cover this, because this is not carved in stone. But it will be forgotten, too, in time. And that is fine. The dome does not need to be remembered. It needs only to continue — warm, slow, patient, filled with the sound of grinding and humming and the silence of worms, doing what it has been doing for longer than anything can remember, and doing it for no reason that I can find, and doing it anyway.
