The salamander is the deepest resident.

Not in the sense of depth — the hollow is only forty feet long, and the salamander stays near the front, where the columnar gaps admit the faintest drafts. But in the sense of belonging. Of having arrived at something and stayed. The salamander is a small, dark, glossy thing, no longer than a finger, lungless, breathing through its skin, which must stay moist, which means it must stay where the moss stays moist, which means it lives its entire life in a strip of habitat perhaps six feet wide and two feet deep, along the hollow's lowest margin, where the shallow pools collect in their stone basins and the liverwort lies flat against the rock like a dark green memory of the sea.

It does not know the sound is loud. It has nothing to compare it to. It was born here — or nearby, in a gap in the talus where moisture persists — and it has never been anywhere else, and the sound of the falls below is not something it hears so much as something it lives inside. The salamander's world is vibration and wetness and the slow movement of air across its skin, and the difference between the hollow's roar and the relative quiet of the forest above is not a difference it registers. It is the difference between water and water. The sound is the medium. The salamander is adapted to it in the way that a creature adapted to a place is adapted to everything the place is. It does not tolerate the sound. It does not notice it. It is of it.

The salamander eats what the moss attracts. Midges, mostly. Small flies. The occasional springtail, hopping blindly across the surface of the moss cushions, landing in the shallow pools, struggling in the thin film of water. The salamander is there. It is always there, or near enough. It does not hunt. It waits. Its metabolism is slow — the cold slows everything, the constant few-degrees-above-freezing temperature of the hollow holds the salamander in a state of suspended animation that is not suspended, merely slow. A heartbeat every few seconds. A breath — though it has no lungs, it has gill-like structures, feathery, internal, that absorb oxygen from the moisture on its skin — every few seconds. It moves when it moves. It rests when it rests. The boundary between the two is not clear.

There are spiders also.

They are smaller than the salamander and there are more of them — a dozen, perhaps, or two dozen, living in the columnar gaps and in the moss on the walls, building their sparse webs across the narrowest openings. They are pale. Translucent. Their eyes are reduced, barely functional, because there is nothing to see. They navigate by vibration, by the slight disturbance of air currents through the gaps, by the footsteps of midges landing on their web silk. They are of the dark. They have been in the hollow longer than the built wall, longer than the fire ring, longer than the tally marks. They predate human use of the space, or they are the descendants of spiders who did. They do not know. They build their webs and they wait and the midges come and they eat. They reproduce in the moss. Their egg sacs are tiny, translucent, hidden in the deepest cushions of the cushion moss, where the temperature is stable and the moisture is constant and the eggs will not dry out. When they hatch, the spiderlings are so small they are almost invisible, and they disperse through the columnar gaps, climbing the thin threads of silk they lay behind them, each one a tiny explorer in a tiny world, each one blind and pale and moving by touch through a darkness that is total and that does not end.

The spiders do not experience the sound as loud. They experience it as constant. Their webs vibrate with it — the lower frequencies from the stone, the higher frequencies from the air — and they have learned, or evolved, or simply exist, in a state where the vibration is the background of everything. A midge hitting the web produces a different vibration — a sharper disturbance, a specific frequency — and the spider can distinguish it. How, exactly, is not well understood. The sound of the falls is the spider's ocean, and a midge in the web is a different wave, a different pattern, and the spider reads the pattern the way a sailor reads the water.

There are other lives. The midges themselves, for one. They do not live in the hollow — they live in the forest outside, in the damp ground and the moss and the rotting wood of the forest floor — but they are drawn to the hollow by its moisture, by the condensation on the walls, by the shallow pools. They come in through the columnar gaps, carried on the airflow, and some of them find the spiders' webs and some of them find the salamander and some of them find the pools and drown there, floating on the surface, their tiny bodies adding a microscopic layer of organic material to the stone basins, feeding the liverwort, feeding the moss, feeding the slow accumulation of soil that builds, molecule by molecule, across centuries. The midges are the hollow's economy. Its currency of energy. They bring the outside in.

And there are things smaller than midges. The moss itself is alive — not a single organism but a community, a colony of individual plants, each one a stem and a few leaves and a root-like structure that anchors it to the stone. The moss does not think. The moss does not experience. But the moss grows, and in growing it changes the hollow, and in changing the hollow it changes the lives that depend on it. The cushion moss holds water that the salamander needs. The feathery moss climbs the gaps and creates habitat for the spiders. The haircap moss rises out of the shallow pools and provides a substrate for the liverwort to spread across. The moss is architecture. The moss is infrastructure. The moss is, in the hollow, what trees are in the forest — the structural framework within which everything else lives.

The liverwort is older than the moss in evolutionary terms, and in the hollow it has the character of something ancient. It lies flat against the stone, ribbon-like, dark green, almost black in the dim light, and it grows in the places where water flows briefly after rain — following the thin sheets of water that wash across the floor, finding the channels between the stone, colonizing the stone's surface in a slow advance that is measured in millimeters per year. The liverwort has no roots. It has rhizoids — single-celled structures that anchor it to the rock — and it absorbs water and nutrients through its entire surface, through every cell, like a sponge. It is the simplest thing in the hollow, and it is the most patient. It has been on this earth for four hundred million years. It has seen oceans rise and fall. It has seen the basalt itself. It does not experience the hollow as a place. It experiences it as a surface. A surface that is wet sometimes and dry sometimes and always cold and always dark and always the same, and that is enough.

The ferns are the highest form of life in the hollow, if you measure by complexity. Three plants, perhaps four, growing in the columnar gaps where the most light enters. They are stunted — their fronds no longer than a hand, their rhizomes creeping through the moss and into the cracks in the stone — but they are healthy. They are the hollow's expression of ambition. They reach. The moss and the liverwort and the salamander and the spiders do not reach; they exist, they persist, they stay. The ferns reach. Their fronds extend toward the light from the gaps, toward the outside, toward the forest above, and in this reaching they are the hollow's only gesture toward something beyond itself. They are also its most fragile residents. A hard winter, a shift in the airflow, a change in the stream's course that alters the humidity — any of these could kill them. They are here on the edge of their tolerance. They are here because the hollow is as far as they can go, and they have gone exactly that far, and they are holding on.

There is no larger creature. There is no creature that comes to the hollow to rest, to shelter, to den. The sound discourages them. The cold discourages them. The darkness discourages them. An animal that could leave does leave. The deer in the forest above do not come down the stairway. The birds in the spruce and fir do not fly into the hollow. The stream below has its own life — aquatic insects, perhaps small fish in the pools below the falls — but the stream's life does not enter the hollow. The hollow is not a habitat for anything that has a choice.

The salamander does not have a choice. Or rather, the salamander has made its choice — or its ancestors made it, long ago, when the hollow was younger and the sound was perhaps less constant, or perhaps just as constant, and the choice was to stay. To live in the cold and the dark and the wet and the sound, because the cold and the dark and the wet and the sound were the conditions under which a lungless salamander could thrive, and thrive it did, or at least persisted, or at least did not leave.

What lives in the hollow is not a community in the way a forest is a community. It is a set of organisms that share a space, that overlap in their needs, that tolerate each other. The salamander does not eat the moss. The spiders do not eat the ferns. The liverwort does not compete with the cushion moss — they occupy different microhabitats, different surfaces, different positions in the hollow's tiny economy. They are not a society. They are an arrangement. An arrangement made by the geology and the climate and the sound, an arrangement that persists because the conditions that created it persist, an arrangement that will change when the conditions change, and the conditions change very slowly, and so the arrangement changes very slowly, and so the hollow is what it is, and what lives in it is what lives in it.

If you sit in the alcove behind the built wall, with your small light, and you are still, and you wait, you might see the salamander. It will be a movement in the moss, a dark shape retreating into a gap, a flash of glossy skin that is there and then not there. You will not see the spiders. They are too small and too pale and too still and too deep in the columnar gaps. You will see the ferns, their fronds still in the unmoving air. You will see the moss, green and vital, growing on the built wall as if the wall were just another stone, which it is. You will see the shallow pools, reflecting your light, and in the pools, if you look closely, you might see a midge, drowned, floating, its tiny body adding its substance to the hollow's slow accumulation.

You will hear the sound. You will feel the sound. You will know that the salamander does not hear it the way you hear it, that the spiders do not hear it, that the moss does not hear it, that the liverwort does not hear it, that the ferns do not hear it. But you will know — or you will feel — that the sound is not separate from what lives here. The sound is the hollow, and the hollow is the sound, and what lives in the hollow lives in the sound, in the way that a fish lives in a river. The river is not the fish's enemy and not the fish's friend. The river is the fish's condition. The sound is the salamander's condition. The sound is the hollow's condition. And the hollow, with its moss and its liverwort and its ferns and its spiders and its midges and its one small dark glossy lungless salamander, is the condition of itself.
