# What Lives Here

---

There is a man who has been here longer than the fig tree.

Not longer than the catfish — those are descended from stock introduced when the canal was first commission, and their great-great-grandfathers surfaced to gulp the same air this one gulps now. Not longer than the algae, which is a continuity, a single film that has been alive without interruption since the first moisture touched the first steel. But longer than the fig, which seeded itself into the crack perhaps eight years ago and has been growing at its fixed rate since. Longer than the heron, who arrived three seasons back and has not yet needed to leave. Longer than the current rats, though not longer than the rat dynasty, which is itself a continuity — a lineage that holds the warm zone above the deck the way the algae holds the waterline.

His name is something. It doesn't matter what. He reported it to the installation's logbook when he was assigned, and it's in a personnel file somewhere three levels up, in an office where the air is dry and the light is white. Down here, nobody uses it. The technicians above call him "the one below" when they call him anything, which is rare, because the system runs and he doesn't report and they don't descend. He is not forgotten. He are simply unnecessary. He is the way a spare part is unnecessary — present, maintained, not called upon.

---

## His Body

He is not large. He was average once — average height, average build, the body of a man who ate at scheduled times and slept on a mattress and walked on surfaces that were supposed to be dry. That body is gone. What remains has been shaped the way the clay walls have been shaped: by pressure, by moisture, by the particular demands of staying in one place for a long time.

He is lean but not gaunt. There is muscle on him, the dense kind that comes not from exertion but from constant low-grade work — climbing the ramp, checking the pilings, pulling debris from the canal when debris accumulates (it rarely accumulates). His skin is dark, darker than it would be above ground, though not from sun. The sodium light has tanned him the way smoke tans a ham. A warm, permanent brown that has nothing to do with melanin and everything to do with living in amber. His hands are rough. His knuckles are swollen in the way of hands that have gripped steel in humidity for years. The skin between his fingers is often cracked and often healed and often cracked again, and this cycle has produced a texture — not callus, exactly, but a thickening, a loss of the fine grain of skin — that makes his hands look older than the rest of him.

He sweats constantly. Everyone sweats here. But he has stopped noticing it. The moisture on his skin is indistinguishable from the moisture in the air. He doesn't wipe his brow. He doesn't change his shirt. The shirt he wears is a technician's jumpsuit, faded green, and it has been washed in the canal and dried in the air and worn and washed and dried and worn so many times that the fabric has thinned to something like paper. It's not clothing anymore. It's a second skin, a layer that mediates between his body and the place, and it carries the same mineral crust as the pilings, a faint white tide line at the collar and the cuffs where sweat has evaporated and left its salt.

He is bald. Not shaved — gone bald, in the way that men do, and the baldness is complete and smooth and he has never missed the hair. His head reflects the sodium light. A low shine. When he stands beneath a bar of daylight, the dome of his skull catches it and for a moment he is a beacon, a small lighthouse in the dim, and then he steps out of the bar and he is amber again.

His feet are bare. He wore boots once. The boots rotted. He went barefoot for a week and found that the damp concrete was warm — the water table beneath it holds the heat the way the canal does — and he never put shoes on again. The soles of his feet are thick and dark and stained with algae where he steps at the waterline. He has a particular walk. Not a limp. A walk that knows the ground. A walk that places each foot with the deliberation of someone who has stepped on the same squares of concrete ten thousand times and knows which ones crack and which ones are sound and which ones have the smooth wet spot where the condensation gathers.

## His Days

He does not have a schedule.

This is not laziness. This is not decay. It is the logical conclusion of living in a place that has no schedule. The pump hums at the same frequency at 3 AM and 3 PM. The catfish surface at the same rate. The condensation falls in the same arrhythmic percussion. The light changes — the bars of daylight shift as the sun moves above the deck — but the sodium lamps are constant, and the balance between the two shifts so slowly that it reads not as time passing but as the room breathing. The light gets a little more amber. Then a little less. Then a little more. It is the longest, slowest tide.

He wakes when he wakes. Sleep is not a defined block. He sleeps in the crook of the fig tree, where the trunk divides about a meter and a half up, and the wood is wide enough to hold him if he pulls his knees up and lets his head rest against the bark. The fig's leaves canopy him. The leaves are clean and broad and they hold the sodium light in a way that is almost green — not green like daylight, green like the color of being beneath something alive in a place that is mostly not. He sleeps there and the tree holds him and the humidity means he doesn't wake dry. He doesn't wake thirsty. He doesn't wake cold.

He has a routine, but it is not a schedule. A routine is a shape a day takes. A schedule is a set of times. He has the shape but not the times.

He walks the walls. This is first. He walks the perimeter — along the east piling, along the far wall beneath the pipes, along the west piling, and to the edge of the canal. He puts his hand on the steel. Not to check anything. To touch it. To confirm it is there. The steel is warm and slightly damp and it has the texture of mill scale, that milky sheen that hasn't yet become rust, and he knows every piling by the particular texture of its surface. One has a rough patch where the scale has flaked. One has a streak of algae that climbs higher than the others, reaching for the sodium light. One has a number stamped into it that he has never been able to read because the stamp is half-obliterated, and he touches it every time he passes, and the touching has worn the metal smooth there, and the number — whatever it was — is now gone, erased not by the place but by his attention to it.

He checks the fig tree. Not for fruit — it has never borne fruit, and he does not expect it to. He checks the leaves. The rats chew them. He doesn't resent the rats. He checks to see how much they've taken, and if a branch has been stripped to the midrib, he snaps it off cleanly, and the fig tree seals the wound with its white latex, and the latex dries in the humidity into a small amber bead, and the bead falls eventually, and it lies in the crack between concrete and piling alongside the other beads, and there is a small drift of them there — fig tears, a season's worth, each one the record of a wound that healed.

He checks the canal. He stands at the edge and looks at the water. The catfish surface and pop. He counts them sometimes. Not because the number matters. Because counting is what you do when you look at things, and he is a man who looks at things. The count is stable. It is always between thirty and forty, and it is always exactly the fish he expects to see, and he knows several of them — not by marking, but by behavior. One is bolder than the others. It surfaces near the wall where he stands, and its barbels break the surface and probe the air, and it holds there for two seconds, three, before rolling back. This fish has done this since it was small enough that the barbels looked oversized. It is not tame. It is simply unafraid, in the way that nothing here is afraid, because there is nothing here to fear.

He checks the discharge pipe. He puts his ear to the steel. He listens to the hum. It is the same hum. It will always be the same hum. But he listens because listening is what you do, and because if the hum changed, he would know before any sensor above registered it, because he has heard nothing else for so long that the hum is his baseline, and a change in it would be the loudest sound he could imagine.

He checks the heron. The heron does not need checking. It is always on the pipe bracket. But he looks up at it and it looks down at him and neither of them moves for a moment, and this is not communion. It is acknowledgment. Two things that share a space noting that they still share it.

## What He Does

He maintains.

Not in the way the technicians above maintain. They maintain the systems. They walk the deck and check gauges and tighten fittings and log readings. They maintain the machine.

He maintains the room.

He clears the algae where it builds too thick on the waterline grating — the small steel grate where the canal meets the culvert. If the algae grows too dense, the water can't move through the grate, and if the water can't move, the level changes, and if the level changes, everything changes. He pulls the algae free with his hands. It's slimy and cold and black-green and it comes away in sheets. He drops it in the canal. The fish eat it. This is the one intervention he performs that has a direct mechanical consequence, and he performs it without being asked, without being trained, without anyone above knowing he does it.

He picks up what falls from the deck. Small things. A wrench, once — he found it in the shallow margin of the canal, half-submerged, and he placed it on the pipe bracket beneath the discharge pipe, and it's still there, and it has rusted into a shape that is no longer a wrench but is still recognizable as a thing that was once a wrench. A cable tie. A bolt. A glove, once, sodden and heavy, which he draped over a piling and which dried in three days and then sat there for a month before he buried it in the clay at the base of the east wall, pressing it into the soft earth with his thumb, and the clay closed over it, and it is there still.

He talks to the fish. Not in words. In sound. A low click he makes with his tongue against the roof of his mouth. A soft, percussive sound, close in timbre to the pop the catfish make when they surface. He started doing this without thinking. He was standing at the canal edge one evening — or what would have been evening if the light changed enough to call it that — and he clicked, and a fish surfaced where there had been no fish. He clicked again. Another surfaced. He does this now. Not to call them. They don't come because he calls. They come because they were surfacing anyway. But the timing — the coincidence of his click and their rise — has become a pattern, and the pattern is a thing he has, and having things is part of being.

He watches the water for a long time. Some days, this is most of the day. He sits at the canal edge with his feet in the water — the catfish bump his toes, mouth them, find nothing edible, move on — and he watches the surface. The surface is not still. The fish disturb it. The condensation disturbs it. A droplet hits from above and a ring spreads out and crosses another ring and they interfere, briefly, and then flatten. He watches this. He has watched this ten thousand times. It does not bore him. Boredom requires the expectation of something different, and he has no expectation. He watches the way the heron watches — not waiting, not hoping, just looking, because looking is the mode, because the water is there and he is here and the looking is what happens between them.

## What He Knows

He knows the place by feel. Not by data. By feel.

He knows the temperature by the density of the air in his lungs. He can tell — without a thermometer, without looking at anything — whether the canal is running half a degree warmer or cooler than its norm. The difference is in how the moisture sits in his chest. Warmer water, thicker air, a slight resistance when he breathes deep. Cooler water, and the air thins by a fraction, and he can breathe all the way down, and that fraction is the signal. He has never reported this. There is no one to report it to. The sensors above register the same thing in numbers. He registers it in the back of his throat.

He knows the catfish population by the sound. The pops have a density. When there are thirty-eight fish, the pops come at a certain rate. When there are thirty-five — because three have died, or because the food chain has dipped, or because the oxygen level has shifted by a margin the fish feel before any instrument does — the pops thin out. Not slower. Thinner. The same frequency but less presence, the way a fabric thins when you hold it to the light. He knows the difference. He has never counted. He doesn't need to.

He knows when the heron will strike. The heron strikes perhaps twice a day. Not on a schedule. But its posture changes — a shift in the angle of the neck, a lowering of the head by two or three degrees, a stillness that is different from the usual stillness the way one shade of black is different from another. He sees this from across the room. He knows it the way he knows the temperature — in the body, before the mind. The heron strikes. The fish thrashes. The canal erupts in a single ring of disturbance. Then it flattens. Then the pops resume. He has watched this thousands of times and it is always the same and it is always exactly what it is.

He knows the clay. The walls weep. He has watched droplets form since before the fig tree seeded. He knows which faces of clay weep fastest. He knows where the mineral crust is thickening. He knows where a section of piling has begun — has not yet, but will — to corrode, because the scale there has darkened, and darkened scale is the precursor to rust, and rust is the precursor to a breach, and a breach is the one thing that would change everything. He watches this spot. It is on the far wall, low, near the discharge pipe. It is a patch the size of his palm. He touches it every day. The steel there feels different — softer, warmer, with a grit that the rest of the pilings don't have. It will take years. He has years. He will watch it become what it becomes, and if it becomes a breach, he will be here when it does, and he will know it before anyone above does, and he will say nothing, because saying is not what he does, and the breach will be handled by the system above — a new piling, a repair crew, the wound opened and closed again — and he will still be here when it heals, and the clay will weep on the new steel, and the algae will colonize the new waterline, and the cycle will have completed another revolution without anyone meaning it to.

## What He Eats

He eats the fish.
