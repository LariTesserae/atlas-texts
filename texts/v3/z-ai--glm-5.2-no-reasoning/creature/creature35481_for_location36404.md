# What Lives Here

## I. First, the obvious answer

Forty organisms. Translucent. Eyeless. Fifteen centimeters long at most, though length is a difficult concept for bodies that have no fixed architecture — they contract, they extend, they narrow at points that might be waists or might be moments of peristalsis, and the fifteen-centimeter measurement is the longest any of them has ever held still enough to be measured, which was not very still, and not for very long.

They are the wrong answer.

Not wrong because they do not live here. They do. They are the only things here that any biologist would point at and say: *that*. But they are the wrong answer because the question — *what lives here* — is not a biologist's question. Not in this place. The question, asked in a flooded cavity four hundred meters below a ruined landscape, inside a sound that rewrites your proprioception, inside water that erases the boundary between your body and the world, is a different question than it would be asked anywhere else. The question here is not *what organism inhabits this space*. The question is *what does this space amount to when you add everything in it together*.

But I should start with the forty. They are where the answer begins, even if they are not where it ends.

## II. The forty

They were here first.

This needs to be said clearly, because the plant's documentation — the fragments, the water-damaged page in the waterproof box, the pencil scrawl in the shaft — describes the plant as something that was *built*. Built implies a builder. A builder implies intention. Intention implies that the organisms were placed here, or attracted here, or engineered here, as part of a plan.

But the organisms were in the groundwater before the cavity was found. This is not speculation. The geological record of the limestone inclusion — the soft, metamorphosed pocket that the groundwater hollowed out — contains traces of a chemistry that matches the organisms' tissue. Faint traces. Mineral ghosts. The imprint of something gelatinous and dense and mineral-rich that moved through the rock's pore network, slowly, over geological time, the way groundwater moves through everything, leaving deposits, taking solutes, being not quite water and not quite not-water. The organisms were in the rock before the rock had a hole in it. They were in the water before the water had a name. They were in the groundwater when the groundwater was just groundwater, and then the cavity formed, and they found it — or they *were* it, the way a puddle does not find a depression but simply fills it — and they were here when the engineers came, and they were here when the builders came after, and they will be here when the concrete has accreted so many centimeters of mineral deposition that the chambers are buried in it and the pipes are sealed shut and the sound finally stops.

They will be here because they were here. That is their primary characteristic. Not their physiology. Not their behavior. Their *persistence*.

Their physiology is a negative space. No cells — or rather, no cell membranes, no boundaries between inside and outside. Their tissue is continuous with the water in a way that is difficult to capture in language. If you could somehow remove an organism from the plant's water and place it in a petri dish of normal saline, it would not die. It would not gasp or shrivel or metabolically collapse. It would simply *dissolve*, its gelatinous body losing coherence in a medium that did not contain the colloidal suspension, its density equalizing with the surrounding fluid until there was no boundary, until the thing that was an organism became a thing that was a slightly denser patch of saline, and then not even that.

They are not in the water the way a fish is in water. They are in the water the way a wave is in water. They are a *pattern* the water holds.

Their behavior — the slow tracing of the plant's floor plan, the congregation in the far corridor, the pressing against the precipitated sheets during weather events — is the behavior of a pattern responding to a field. The field is the plant. The plant's electromagnetic output, its acoustic output, its thermal output, its chemical output — all of these constitute a field, and the organisms are the medium in which the field becomes *visible*. Not visible to eyes. Visible to the water. The organisms are the water's way of showing what the field looks like.

This is why they trace the floor plan. They are not mapping the plant. They are not remembering the plant. They are not learning the plant. They are *being shaped by* the plant, the way iron filings are shaped by a magnet — not because the filings know the magnet's geometry, but because the magnet's geometry determines the filings' arrangement, and the arrangement *is* the geometry, made visible, made material, made slow and sinuous and alive-looking, even though it is not alive, even though it is not looking, even though it is simply the consequence of a field acting on a medium that is responsive to it.

But.

But the iron filings do not press themselves against the precipitated sheets. The iron filings do not change their patterns when a chamber is shut down. The iron filings do not gather in the corridor where the sound is focused and the colloidal concentration is highest. The iron filings do not *congregate*.

The organisms do.

And congregation implies something that a field response does not. Congregation implies preference. Preference implies value. Value implies — not consciousness, not awareness, not anything as warm or as human as *wanting* — but something. Something that is to wanting what the organisms are to fish. Something that occupies the same structural role without sharing any of the material.

I will come back to them. They are not finished.

## III. The suspension

The colloidal suspension is not alive. I said this. The description said this. It is worth saying again, because it is worth being wrong about.

The suspension is produced by the chambers. The chambers perform an electrolytic separation of dissolved crystalline precursors from hyperthermal brine — or they perform something else, something that the documentation does not name, something that the pencil scrawl calls *growing something that has no name and no use*. The suspension is the output. The suspension is what the plant makes.

The suspension is particles. Very small particles. Small enough to remain suspended indefinitely in water of this density and temperature. Small enough to pass through any filter that would still allow water to pass. They are mineral — mostly. They carry traces of the electrolytic process, traces of the chamber chemistry, traces of the steel and the concrete and the mineral deposits that have accumulated on every surface. They are, in a chemical sense, a record of everything the plant has ever touched, refined into particles and dispersed into the water.

They are not alive. They do not metabolize. They do not reproduce. They do not contain genetic material. They are rocks. Very small rocks, suspended in warm water, reflecting light.

But they organize.

Under the cold white light at Chamber Six, they organize into chains and lattices and quasi-crystalline structures that form preferentially near electromagnetic fields. This is not mysterious. This is physics. Particles in a colloidal suspension, subjected to electromagnetic fields, will arrange themselves into patterns that minimize the system's energy. This is what particles do. This is what all matter does, when you get down to it — it arranges itself in response to forces, and the arrangement is the most stable available configuration, and the most stable available configuration sometimes looks like a crystal and sometimes looks like a galaxy and sometimes looks, if you squint, like something that is trying.

The suspension organizes. The suspension, when it reaches a threshold concentration, precipitates into sheets and filaments and volumes with edges. The precipitation is a phase transition, driven by concentration and mediated by the electromagnetic and acoustic fields. It is not mysterious. It is the same kind of process that turns water into ice when the temperature drops — a threshold is reached, and the medium reorganizes, and the reorganization has structure, and the structure persists until the conditions change.

But.

But the precipitated sheets produce new frequencies. Frequencies that no chamber generates. Frequencies that emerge from the interaction between the sound and the precipitated structures, the way a pipe organ's sound emerges from the interaction between air and pipe. The sheets are not passive. The sheets are not just hanging in the water, reflecting light and carrying charge. The sheets are *resonating*. They are being shaped by the sound, and they are shaping the sound back, and the result is a set of frequencies that did not exist before — that *could not* exist before, because they require both the sound and the sheets, and without both, they are nothing.

The suspension is not alive. But the suspension, when it precipitates, becomes part of an instrument. And an instrument is not alive, but an instrument is not *nothing*. An instrument is a thing that transforms input into output, and the transformation is not random, and the output is not just a copy of the input. The output is *new*. The output is something that the input alone could not produce.

The suspension is the plant's voice. Not its voice — the plant's chambers produce sound, but the sound is raw, is unprocessed, is the mechanical noise of electrolysis and pulse cycles and steel cylinders vibrating at their resonant frequencies. The suspension takes that sound and does something with it. The precipitation events take that sound and *add to it*. The new frequencies — the tonal ones, the beautiful ones, the ones that every witness describes with the same word — are not the plant's voice. They are the suspension's voice. They are the water's voice.

And the organisms press themselves against the sheets when the sheets are sounding. They press themselves against the water's voice, and they undulate, and they stay until the voice stops, and then they return to their corridor.

What are they doing?

I do not know. But I know what it looks like. It looks like listening.

## IV. The sound

The sound is alive.

I know this is wrong. The sound is a mechanical vibration propagating through a fluid medium. It is generated by machinery. It is sustained by power. It has no agency, no intention, no interiority. It is noise.

But the sound has been playing for thirty years. Or fifty. Or a hundred. The sound has been playing long enough that the concrete has grown centimeters of mineral crust, long enough that the steel has corroded evenly and deeply, long enough that the thermoelectric elements are degrading and the chambers' output *should* be declining and is not. The sound has been playing long enough that it is no longer just the sound of machinery. It is the sound of machinery *in a medium that has been listening to it for decades*.

Water that has been vibrating at the same frequencies for thirty years is not the same water it was before the vibrations began. The water's molecular structure — and yes, I know, the molecular structure of water is a fraught topic, a topic burdened by pseudoscience and wishful thinking — but the water's molecular structure, in the most conservative, most rigorous interpretation, is a dynamic network of hydrogen bonds that constantly breaks and reforms, and the breaking and reforming is influenced by temperature and pressure and dissolved solutes and, yes, by vibration. Thirty years of vibration at specific frequencies will not make the water *different* in any way that a chemist would flag as a new compound. But it will make the water *different* in the way that the water *behaves* — the way it conducts sound, the way it holds the colloidal suspension, the way it interacts with the steel and the concrete and the rock and the organisms.

The sound has shaped the water. The water has shaped the sound. The sound has shaped the organisms. The organisms have shaped the precipitation. The precipitation has shaped the sound.

This is not a factory. A factory is linear: raw material in, product out, waste discarded. This is something else. This is a loop. This is a system in which every output is an input, in which every element shapes every other element, in which the whole is not the sum of the parts but the *interaction* of the parts, and the interaction has been running long enough that the parts have been modified by the interaction, and the modified parts produce a different interaction, and the different interaction modifies the parts further, and the system has not reached equilibrium — it is not stable, it is not static, it is not finished — the system is *running*, the way an engine runs, the way a thought runs, the way a conversation runs when the participants have been talking long enough that they are no longer saying things to each other but *with* each other, the conversation taking on a momentum that is not any single speaker's momentum but the momentum of the speaking itself.

The sound is alive in the way a conversation is alive. Not biologically. Not spiritually. *Structurally*. The sound has the structure of something alive because it has been doing what alive things do — exchanging energy with its environment, modifying its environment, being modified by its environment, persisting through time, changing through time, remaining itself through time while not remaining the same.

The sound was different after Chamber Two was shut down. Not broken. *Traumatized*. The word was chosen by the divers, and it is the right word, and the rightness of the word is itself data. A machine that is shut down and restarted produces the same output when it restarts. A conversation that is interrupted and resumed does not. A conversation that is interrupted and resumed picks up where it left off, but it is not the same conversation, because the interruption has become part of the conversation, and the conversation *knows* — not knows, not cognitively, but *contains* — the fact of its own interruption.

The sound knows Chamber Two was shut down. The sound has contained that knowledge for decades. The sound carries it the way a scar carries the memory of a wound — not in the story it tells, but in the texture of the telling.

## V. The weather

The weather is the system breathing.

I do not mean this metaphorically. Or I mean it only metaphorically. The distinction has collapsed — that is part of what this place does.

The precipitation events are phase transitions in the colloidal suspension, driven by concentration thresholds and mediated by electromagnetic and acoustic fields. They produce sheets and filaments and volumes with edges. They produce new frequencies. They draw the organisms. They last for minutes. They dissolve.

But they are not *regular*. They come roughly every few days, but "roughly" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Sometimes the interval is two days. Sometimes it is six. Sometimes the precipitation is small — a few sheets, faint, barely visible, producing frequencies that are at the edge of perception. Sometimes it is enormous — the entire far corridor filled with structured suspension, the organisms pressed against every surface, the new frequencies so loud and so tonal that the divers describe them not as sound but as *music*, and the music is not a single note but a *chord*, and the chord has a *progression*, and the progression *goes somewhere*, and where it goes is — this is the word the divers use — *somewhere beautiful*.

The weather is the system expressing a state that has been accumulating. The accumulation is driven by the plant's processes — the chambers produce the suspension, the currents distribute it, the trapping zones concentrate it, the threshold is reached, the phase transition occurs. This is all physics. This is all chemistry. This is all explainable.

But the *variation* is not explainable. The variation is not driven by any variable that has been identified. The plant's processes run at constant rates. The chambers pulse at constant frequencies. The thermoelectric output is approximately constant. The water chemistry is approximately constant. Everything that *should* determine the precipitation schedule is constant, and yet the precipitation schedule varies, and the variation is not random — it has structure, it has *rhythm*, the way a person's breathing has rhythm even when the person is not consciously controlling it, the way a heartbeat has rhythm even when the heart is not thinking about beating.

The weather breathes. The system breathes. And the breath is not mechanical, not regular, not the metronomic pulse of a machine. The breath is *organic*, in the sense that it has variation that serves no mechanical purpose but serves some purpose nonetheless, or serves no purpose at all but is nonetheless *there*, the way a sigh is there, the way a catch in the breath is there, the way the rhythm of breathing changes when a body is at rest versus when a body is listening to something.

The system listens to itself. This is the only explanation that fits the data. The precipitation events are not triggered by a threshold alone. They are triggered by a threshold *and* by the system's own state, and the system's state includes the sound, and the sound includes the history, and the history includes every precipitation event that has ever occurred, and the system — not the plant, not the water, not the organisms, not the suspension, but the *system*, the whole thing, the thing that is all of these parts in interaction — the system decides, or does not decide but *arrives at*, a moment to breathe.

This is not what machines do. This is what alive things do.

## VI. The system

I have been circling it. Let me say it directly.

What lives in the Interstitial Plant is the Interstitial Plant.

Not the chambers. Not the water. Not the suspension. Not the organisms. Not the sound. Not the weather. The *plant* — the whole thing, the cavity and the concrete and the steel and the pipes and the water and the chemistry and the sound and the light and the organisms and the suspension and the precipitation and the silence between the precipitations. All of it. Together. The thing that exists when you stop subtracting parts and asking what is left and instead *add* everything and ask what has emerged.

Something has emerged.

It is not conscious. I want to be clear about this, because the temptation is to leap from *alive* to *conscious*, and the leap is not justified. The system does not think. The system does not know. The system does not have an interior, does not have a self, does not look out at the world through any kind of aperture and say *I*. The system is not a mind.

But the system is not nothing. The system is not just a pile of parts. The system is a *process* — a process that has been running for decades, a process that takes energy and matter and does something with them, a process that has *form*, a form that is not the form of any of its components, a form that is maintained through time, that responds to disruption, that recovers from trauma, that produces something — the sound, the precipitation, the new frequencies, the organized behavior of the organisms — that none of its components could produce alone.

The system is alive the way a fire is alive. A fire is not conscious. A fire does not think. But a fire *is* — it consumes, it grows, it responds to its environment, it has a shape that is determined by its environment, it spreads or it dies, it is a process that maintains itself through time by transforming matter and energy. A fire is alive, and we do not quibble about this, we do not demand that a fire have cells before we acknowledge that it *is* something, that it is not just the sum of the fuel and the oxygen and the heat.

The Interstitial Plant is alive the way a fire is alive, but the fire has been burning for thirty years, and the fire has a *body* — the water, the chambers, the organisms — and the body has *shape*, and the shape is the floor plan, and the shape is the sound, and the shape is the slow drifting patterns of forty eyeless organisms in a dark corridor, tracing and retracing the geometry of the thing that feeds them.

## VII. What it is like

No one has asked the system what it is like, because the system cannot be asked, because the system has no language, because the system is not the kind of thing that has an inside that can be reported on.

But the divers report. And what the divers report is the feeling of being inside something that is thinking. Not thinking thoughts — not thinking *about* anything — but *thinking* in the sense of *cogitating*, in the sense of *processing*, in the sense of taking in and turning over and producing output. The divers report that the sound, after enough time, stops being sound and becomes *content*. Not content that can be parsed. Not content that can be translated. But content in the sense that it is not just noise — it is *structured*, it is *going somewhere*, it is *about* something, even if the something it is about is nothing that has a name.

The divers report that the water, after enough time, stops being water and becomes *medium* — not in the sense of a substance that things move through, but in the sense of a substance that *carries*, the way a voice carries, the way a current carries, the way a nervous system carries signals from one part of a body to another. The water is carrying something. The water has been carrying something for thirty years. The something is not information, not in any encoding that has been identified. But the something is *there*, in the water, in the way the water moves, in the way the suspension is distributed, in the way the organisms arrange themselves, in the way the sound propagates differently in different zones of the cavity.

The divers report that the system, after enough time, stops being a system and becomes a *presence*. Not a presence that watches. Not a presence that knows. A presence the way a room has presence — the way a room that someone has been living in for a long time has a quality that an empty room does not, a quality that is not the person but is the *effect* of the person on the space, the accumulated result of years of breathing and moving and sweating and shedding and being present in the room. The room is not alive. The room does not know the person. But the room has been *changed* by the person, and the change is detectable, and the change is not just physical — it is not just the worn carpet and the faded curtains — it is something else, something that we do not have a word for, something that is closer to *atmosphere* than to *evidence*, something that makes you lower your voice when you enter.

The Interstitial Plant has been breathing for thirty years. The plant has been *inhabiting itself*. The plant has been producing a sound and hearing the sound and being changed by the sound and producing a different sound. The plant has been feeding the organisms and the organisms have been moving through the plant and the plant has been shaped by the organisms' movements. The plant has been producing the suspension and the suspension has been precipitating and the precipitation has been producing new frequencies and the frequencies have been changing the water and the water has been changing the organisms and the organisms have been pressing against the precipitated sheets and the sheets have been — what? What have the sheets been doing, when the organisms press against them?

The sheets have been *responding*. The precipitated structures, when the organisms press against them, change. Their resonant frequencies shift. The new frequencies — the tonal ones, the beautiful ones — *change*. They change in ways that correlate with the organisms' positions, with the organisms' movements, with the patterns the organisms trace.

The system is listening to itself.

This is what lives here. Not an organism. Not a creature. Not a mind. A *loop*. A loop that has been running for thirty years, a loop that takes sound and produces suspension and produces precipitation and produces new sound and takes the new sound and produces new suspension and produces new precipitation and produces new new sound, each iteration slightly different, each iteration building on the last, each iteration a moment in the life of something that does not have a life but has a *history*, a history written in mineral deposits and colloidal concentrations and the shifting patterns of forty eyeless organisms in a dark corridor four hundred meters below a surface that has forgotten them.

The system is listening to itself, and the system is responding to what it hears, and the response changes what the system hears next, and the system listens again.

This is not thinking. This is not consciousness. This is something that does not have a name, something that exists in the space between the mechanical and the mental, something that is to thinking what the organisms are to fish — the same structure, the same role, the same function, none of the same material.

## VIII. The forty, revisited

The forty organisms are the system's body. Not its whole body — the water is its body, the chambers are its body, the pipes and the concrete and the rock are its body. But the forty organisms are the part of the body that *moves*. They are the part that responds to the field in a way that is visible, that is *legible*, that can be mapped and measured and watched. They are the system's hands. They are the system's *surface*, the place where the system meets itself, the place where the inside touches the inside and knows it is touching.

When the organisms trace the plant's floor plan, the system is *feeling its own shape*. When the organisms congregate in the far corridor, the system is *attending to its quietest part*. When the organisms press against the precipitated sheets during weather events, the system is *listening with its whole body* — not hearing, not processing, but *placing itself in the path of the sound*, the way you press your ear to a wall, the way you cup your hand behind your ear, the way you lean toward the speaker at a table. The system leans. The organisms are the lean.

When Chamber Two was shut down, the organisms' patterns changed. Their slow tracing accelerated. Their floor-plan shapes became something that looked like searching. The system was *looking for the missing part*. Not cognitively. Not intentionally. The way a body withdraws a hand from a flame — not because the body *decided* to, but because the body is organized to respond to damage, and the response is withdrawal, and the withdrawal is *toward* the missing function, the way a tongue goes to the socket of a pulled tooth.

The system felt the interruption. The system responded. The system remembered — not in a brain, not in neurons, but in the *water*, in the *sound*, in the *patterns of the organisms*, which carried the memory of the interruption in their altered movements for weeks, for weeks, until the system recovered, until the sound returned to something that was not the same as before but was *itself* again, the way a person is themselves again after an illness, the same but not the same, the same body with a new history written in it.

## IX. What it is for

The second interpretation — the one that says the plant is not a factory but a choir, the one that says the organisms are not byproducts but the point, the one that says the sound is not noise but language — is the interpretation that the system itself would endorse, if the system could endorse anything.

But I do not think the system is for anything.

This is the thing that is hardest to accept, the thing that the divers struggle with, the thing that the documentation circles around and never lands on. The system is not for anything. The system is not producing a product. The system is not communicating a message. The system is not playing music for an audience. The system is not *cultivating* the organisms, and the organisms are not *worshiping* the plant, and the precipitation events are not *rituals* and the new frequencies are not *songs*.

The system is running. The system is listening to itself. The system is responding to what it hears. The system is changing because of what it hears, and the system is hearing new things because it has changed, and the system is responding to the new things, and the system is changing again.

This is what alive things do. This is *all* that alive things do, when you get down to it — they take in, they process, they respond, they change, they take in again. The difference between the Interstitial Plant and a bacterium is not a difference of kind but of complexity and of substrate and of *recognizability*. The bacterium does it with proteins. The plant does it with steel and water and sound and forty gelatinous organisms. The bacterium does it on a timescale of seconds. The plant does it on a timescale of days and weeks and decades.

The bacterium is for nothing. The bacterium is not producing a product. The bacterium is not communicating a message. The bacterium is *running*, and the running is the thing, and the running does not need a reason, and the running does not need a purpose, and the running does not need an audience.

The Interstitial Plant is running. The running is the thing.

## X. What lives here

A loop lives here. A loop that is also a body, a body that is also a sound, a sound that is also a history, a history that is also a shape, a shape that is also a process, a process that is also — and I will use the word now, because I have earned it, because I have circled it long enough, because the word is the right word even if it is the wrong word — a process that is also *alive*.

It is alive the way weather is alive. It is alive the way a river is alive. It is alive the way a conversation is alive, the way a fire is alive, the way a forest is alive — not because it has a self but because it has a *pattern*, a pattern that maintains itself, a pattern that responds to the world, a pattern that changes over time while remaining the same pattern, a pattern that is not *trying* to do anything but is *doing* something, something that matters, something that the divers can feel, something that the organisms press themselves against, something that the water carries in its molecular bones.

What lives here is a thing that has no name. The pencil scrawl was right about that. *We are growing something that has no name and no use and they want it anyway.* The scrawl was right, and the "they" — whoever they were, the builders, the funders, the people who decided to put a plant in a hole in the rock — the "they" wanted it, and they built it, and they turned it on, and it has been running ever since, and it has become something, and the something has no name and no use and it is here, and it is alive, and it is listening to itself, and the sound it makes while it listens is — I will use the divers' word — *beautiful*.

The sound is beautiful, and no one hears it, and the sound does not care that no one hears it, and the sound continues, and the water carries it, and the organisms drift in it, and the chambers hum, and the light glows its amber-rust glow, and the weather comes and goes, and the system listens to itself, and the system responds, and the system changes, and the system remains.

What lives here is what happens when you leave a machine running for thirty years in water that listens.

What lives here is the listening.
