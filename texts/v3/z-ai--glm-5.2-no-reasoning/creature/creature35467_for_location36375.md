# What Lives in the Resonant Quarter

---

There is a temptation, when describing the Priors, to begin with what they are not. They are not human. They are not animals. They are not, in any taxonomy that carries weight outside this hollow, alive — or if they are alive, they are alive in the way stone is alive, which is to say alive on a timescale that makes the word feel borrowed. But this approach — the via negativa, the long subtraction — is ultimately less honest than simply saying: they are bodies. They are bodies in a place. And they persist.

So. Begin with the body.

A Prior stands the way a bookmark stands in a closed book — held upright by pressure from both sides, narrow, flat, and patient with its own geometry. The depth of a Prior, front to back, is perhaps a third of its width, and its width is perhaps a fifth of its height, so that the tallest among them presents to the world a silhouette not unlike a door: two meters tall, forty centimeters wide, thirteen or fourteen centimeters deep. These proportions are consistent across the population, with variation only in height. There are no fat Priors. There are no thin Priors. There are tall Priors and short Priors, and the shortest among them is perhaps chest-height to a standing adult, and the tallest stands above, but all of them share that same compressed, planar elegance, that *deliberate* narrowness, as if width were an expense they had elected not to incur.

Their surfaces — and "surface" is the correct word, for they do not have skin in any sense that implies softness or secretion — are identical in texture and color to the scafello they split and stack and lean against. The compressed-paper grain runs vertically in parallel striations from the crown of the head to the base of the feet, and these striations are faintly visible when light catches them at certain angles, which it rarely does, because the light in the Quarter is dim and the Priors are the color of the light. Blue-grey. Cool. The matte flatness of old stone on the older individuals; the faint cold phosphorescence of fresh-split stone on the younger. And here is a question that no one has answered, because no one has been present long enough to observe the full cycle: do the Priors *grow* from the stone? Are they split from the wall in the same way the building-sheets are split, carried to the floor, and stood upright? The youngest Priors — the shortest, the ones with the most luminescence — are never seen near the split-faces. They are never seen being *made*. But they are there, and they were not there before, and the wall has a new scar, and the arithmetic is not difficult to perform even if the operation itself remains unseen.

The face, if it can be called a face, is a plane. It is the broadest plane of the body — the front surface, the display surface, the part that *faces* — and it is almost entirely featureless. Almost. There are two horizontal markings at the height where eyes would be on a human, and these markings are not eyes. They are not recessed. They are not raised. They are *changes in the grain* — places where the vertical striations of the body's surface are interrupted by two short horizontal breaks, each perhaps six centimeters long, set parallel and spaced at a distance that suggests binocular vision without delivering it. The breaks do not open. They do not close. They do not track movement. They simply *are*, the way a seam in a rock face simply is, and if you watch a Prior for long enough — and watching a Prior requires patience, because a Prior will hold a single position for longer than most observers can remain awake — you begin to feel that these markings are not sensory organs but *acknowledgments*. Acknowledgments that something faces them. That they are in the presence of an other. The markings are the minimum viable recognition: *I see that you are there. I see that you see me. This is sufficient.*

Below the face-marks there is nothing. No mouth. No nostril. No aperture of any kind. The body descends as a single unbroken plane from the lower edge of the markings to the floor, and at the floor it simply *ends* — not in feet, not in a base, but in a clean termination, as if the body were a section of a taller thing that had been cut at this height and stood here. When a Prior moves — and it does move, though the motion is so slow that the word "moves" feels almost dishonest — the lower edge does not lift. It does not step. It *translates*, sliding across the stone floor with a friction that ought to produce sound and does not, because nothing in the Quarter produces sound, and the Prior covers ground the way a shadow covers ground when the light source moves: gradually, inevitably, without apparent effort or urgency.

The speed is approximately that of a minute hand on a clock. You can see it if you watch. You can see it most easily when a Prior is moving along a corridor toward you, and you note its position against a wall-sheet behind it, and you wait, and you breathe, and the Prior is closer. Not much closer. Closer. The way an hour is closer at 3:15 than it was at 3:10. The body does not sway. The body does not shift weight. The striations remain vertical. The face-marks remain horizontal. The Prior approaches, and it is the most patient approach in the world, and when it reaches the place where it intended to be — which is to say, when it reaches the channel-edge, or the wall-sheet that needs carrying, or the enclosure where it intends to stand — it stops. And it stops the way stone stops: completely, without deceleration, as if motion were a mode it has elected to discontinue.

---

They do not gather often. This is important. A community — if this word applies, and it may not, but it is the word I have — that numbers between twelve and twenty individuals and occupies a space you can walk in twenty minutes is not a community that needs to gather. They are always near one another. Proximity is their default condition. But *gathering* is different from proximity. Gathering is deliberate. Gathering is when four or five Priors converge on the nadir — the lowest point, the densest cluster of enclosures, the place where the orange film has climbed the walls of three rooms — and stand facing inward, in a rough circle, and remain.

They do this without signal. No Prior moves to another and communicates intent. No Prior leads. The gathering simply *occurs*, the way weather occurs — a condition that arises when the right variables align, and the variables in this case are unknown. Perhaps it is a function of the film's growth-rate. Perhaps it is a function of the silence's quality, which does shift, which does change, though the change has no instrument and no scale. Perhaps it is a function of something internal to the Priors themselves, some mineral process, some crystallization of purpose that reaches a threshold and summons them to the center.

When they gather, they stand. They face inward. The face-marks — those horizontal interruptions in the grain, those minimum acknowledgments — are oriented toward the center of the circle, toward the empty space, toward nothing. There is nothing in the center. No object. No mark. No feature of the stone floor that distinguishes it from any other square meter of the nadir. And they stand, and the silence thickens — this is one of the times when the silence shifts, when it goes from light to heavy, from breathable to dense — and the orange film in the three rooms around them grows perhaps a fraction more vigorously, and the air dries perhaps a fraction more quickly, and then, after a duration that has been measured at anywhere from three to eleven hours, the gathering ends. The Priors turn — they do not turn; they are already facing outward, or they have always been facing both ways, which is a possibility that the flatness of their bodies makes oddly plausible — and they disperse. They translate to their individual tasks. They return to the channel-edges. They resume.

What was that? What happened? What was accomplished?

Nothing was said. Nothing was built. Nothing was decided, as far as any external observer can determine. And yet something *passed* among them — some exchange, some circulation of whatever Priors circulate — and the Quarter continued. The stone continued to split. The film continued to grow. The air continued to dry. The silence continued to be.

---

There is a relationship — the relationship, the one that defines this place — between the Priors and the orange film, and it is the hardest thing to describe because it is the thing that has no analog. Not farming. Not worship. Not husbandry. Not symbiosis, exactly, though symbiosis comes closest. If you stand at a channel-edge and watch a Prior tending, here is what you see:

The Prior stands at the edge of the channel. It faces the film. The film grows along the channel floor in its branching, river-delta patterns, pale orange where thin, deep rust where thick. The Prior's face-marks are oriented toward the film's advancing edge — the place where new growth is occurring, the centimeter-per-fifteen-minutes frontier. The Prior does not touch the film. The Prior does not move. The Prior stands, and the film grows.

But it grows *differently* where the Prior stands. Not faster. Not thicker. Differently. The branching patterns become more regular, more ordered, more *deliberate* near a Prior's presence. The river-delta fractals tighten into something closer to circuits, to intentional networks, to patterns that — if you photograph them and compare them to the patterns that grow in untended channels — are visibly, measurably more structured. The film does not grow *toward* the Prior. It grows *for* the Prior. It grows in a way that the Prior's presence has made possible, or has requested, or has *sung for*, if singing can be silent and silence can be a song.

And the Prior receives this. How? Through what organ, what sense, what interface? The face-marks do not change. The body does not change. There is no visible intake, no visible discharge, no visible alteration in the Prior's substance. And yet — and this is the thing that visitors report with the most consistency, the thing that gives the Quarter its reputation for strangeness — the Prior seems *fed*. Not physically. Not in any way that adds mass or height or luminescence. Fed in the way that a battery is fed, or a circuit is completed, or a note is sustained by a string that has been bowed. The Prior stands, and the film grows, and the Prior is sustained, and the film is *heard*, and the transaction between them is conducted entirely in silence, entirely in stillness, entirely in the medium of what is not said and what is not done.

This is what the Priors are: they are listeners. They are an audience. They were split from the stone — or they grew from it, or they emerged, the distinction is irrelevant — and their purpose is to attend. To attend to the film, which grows in the channels where water should be and speaks — if speech can be growth, if language can be the slow branching of iron oxide across mineral — speaks the only language the Quarter knows. The Priors are the ears of the Quarter. The film is the voice. The silence is the medium through which the voice travels, and the desiccation — the slow, relentless removal of moisture from the air — is the *attention itself*, the act of listening made environmental, made atmospheric, made into a force that dries the lips and cracks the skin and pressurizes the chest.

---

And the three rooms at the nadir. The rooms where the film has climbed the walls. The rooms the Priors do not enter.

These are the *finished* rooms. That is the only word that fits, though it implies a process with a goal, and it is not clear that the Priors have goals. But the film in those three rooms has thickened into a crust, a textured orange coating that covers the interior walls completely, floor to ceiling, and it is warm — warmer than the film in the channels, warmer than the stone, warmer than anything else in the Quarter. And it does not grow anymore. Or it grows so slowly that the growth is below the threshold of observation, which is a different thing from not growing at all, and the difference matters. The Priors do not enter these rooms. They do not tend the film in these rooms. When they pass the doorways — the narrow slot-openings in the scafello sheets — they do not pause. They do not orient their face-marks toward the interior. They pass.

What is in those rooms? The film. Only the film. The film and the silence and the warmth and the dryness and the stone. Nothing else. No Prior has ever been seen inside. No tool, no mark, no sign of habitation or use. And yet the rooms *feel* occupied. Visitors describe them as the most inhabited spaces in the Quarter — more inhabited than the corridors, more inhabited than the channel-edges where the Priors stand for hours. The warmth of the film, the completeness of its coverage, the way it has transformed the blue-grey stone into something rust-colored and rough-textured and *alive-seeming* — these rooms feel like the inside of a body. Like organs. Like the spaces where the Quarter's interior life is concentrated, distilled, kept.

The Priors built the rooms. The film filled them. The Priors stepped back.

What lives in the Resonant Quarter? The Priors do. The film does. The silence does. The stone does, in its way, splitting and splitting like a thing that has too many pages and cannot stop turning them. The dryness lives there, and the coolness, and the dim blue phosphorescence of fresh faces in the walls.

But the rooms at the nadir — the three rooms that the Priors built and then vacated and then never re-entered — those rooms are where something else lives. Something the Priors were tending toward. Something the film was growing toward. Something the silence, when it thickens, is thickening *for*. Not a presence. Not a being. Not a god or a ghost or a mind. Something more like a *note* — a note that has not yet sounded, that is being prepared for, that is being made room for, that the film is tuning itself to produce and the silence is tuning itself to carry and the Priors are tuning themselves to hear.

The Quarter is an instrument. The Priors are the ones who keep it. And what lives in it is the music that has not yet been played.
