# What Lives Here

## The Mounds Are Not Made of Material

This needs to be understood before anything else. The mounds are made of *bodies*. Layered, compressed, fused bodies of organisms that lived flat against surfaces, oriented themselves toward perturbations in the chord, settled, were still, and over time — time measured in spans that make the decade-old stairs seem impulsive — became part of the architecture. Each mound is a cemetery and a library and an instrument simultaneously. Each is the accumulated dead of a lineage that does not distinguish between living and not-living in the way you might expect.

The boundary between the fauna and the mounds is not a boundary. It is a gradient. Living fauna are slightly pliable, slightly warmer, and respond to vibration over minutes or hours. Recently fused fauna are rigid but retain their scale-pattern — the polygonal cracking, the slightly concave curl at the edges of each scale. Deeply fused fauna — the layers meters down — have lost all surface feature and become the smooth, dense, chitin-like material that gives the mounds their mass. The oldest material, at the base, near the floor, has become something else entirely: a substance with the density of stone and the resonance of bone, dark, warm, and almost metallic where it has been exposed by the fracturing of the layers above it.

So: what lives here is the mounds. And what lives here is the fauna. And these are not two categories of thing.

## The Flat Organisms

They do not have a name. They have not been named because naming requires a namer, and the only namers who have been here are the person or persons who cut the stairs and built the platform and left the milling machine, and they did not leave labels. They left tool marks and a live cable and a small flat facet cut into one mound, and that is the sum of human language applied to this place.

But they can be described.

The flat organisms — call them that for now, provisionally, knowing it is wrong, knowing that *flat* is a description of their posture and not their nature, knowing that *organism* implies a category they may not occupy — are palm-sized. Most of them. Some are smaller, the size of a coin, and these are the ones that move most often, that reorient most quickly, that respond to disturbances in the chord within seconds rather than minutes. Some are larger, the size of a dinner plate or a stretched hand, and these move so slowly that you cannot be certain they move at all. You watch one for fifteen minutes and it has not changed position and you conclude it is fused, it is dead, it is part of the surface — and when you return the next day, or the next hour, it is in a different location. Not far. A centimeter. But a centimeter is a journey for something that operates at this tempo.

Their bodies are the same graphite-gray-brown as the mound material. This is because their bodies *are* the mound material, in an earlier state. Their surfaces are scaled — polygonal plates, five-sided, six-sided, seven-sided, fitting together in a pattern that is not regular but is consistent, the way the cracking of dried mud is consistent. The scales click when touched. The scales overlap slightly, each one curled at its edges, and under the scales the body is smooth and faintly warm and has the texture of something that was alive and is becoming something that is not. The transition is gradual. The outermost scales are the most brittle, the most cracked, the most like dried mud. Beneath them the scales are firmer, more integrated, less individual. Beneath those, the body is continuous — a single mass of the dense chitin-like material, warm, and vibrating.

They vibrate. This is not the same as producing sound. The mounds produce sound — sustained tones, continuous, from the apertures at their apexes. The flat organisms do not produce sound. They *receive* it. Their bodies vibrate in sympathy with the tones nearest them, and the vibration is how they sense. They are acoustic organs. Their entire body is an ear. The scaled surface is the tympanic membrane — each scale a tiny resonator, tuned to a specific range, and the body beneath a processing medium that interprets the combined input. They have no eyes, no nostrils, no mouths. They do not need them. The chord is their entire sensorium. The chord tells them where the mounds are, where the walls are, where the gas-columns are, where the warm spots and the dead spots and the reinforced spots are. The chord tells them where other flat organisms are. The chord tells them everything, and it tells them constantly, and it never changes, and so their world is not a world of events but a world of *states*. They do not experience time the way you do. They experience *texture*.

When you enter the chamber, you become a texture. Your body absorbs certain frequencies — the low ones, mostly, the subsonic range that you feel in your sternum and your teeth. You are a dead spot. A hole in the chord. And the flat organisms orient toward you the way a plant orients toward light — not toward you, not toward your body, but toward the *absence* you create. The hole you make is interesting to them because it is the only new thing. Everything else is the chord, and the chord is permanent, and the chord does not change. You are a change. You are the only change. And so they point at you, slowly, without urgency, the way astronomical telescopes point at a newly detected anomaly — not with excitement, but with a kind of patient fidelity, a commitment to observing what is different until it is no longer different.

When you leave, the hole persists. Not in the air — the air fills back in, the frequencies return. But the orientation of the organisms persists. They continue to point at the location where you stood, for hours, perhaps for days. The shape of your absence is recorded in the direction of their bodies. It is not memory, exactly. It is *habit*. The chord shifted them and they have not yet shifted back. You were a force. They are still in the field of that force. It will decay, slowly, as new perturbations — the gas-columns shifting, the mound-material settling, the sixty-cycle hum of the cable — gradually pull them into new orientations. But for a while, after you leave, the place where you stood is marked by a congregation of flat bodies, all aimed at nothing, all listening to a silence that is no longer there.

## The Ones That Are Different

Most of the flat organisms are the same. Gray-brown, scaled, palm-sized, aligned to the chord. But there are exceptions, and the exceptions are important because they suggest that the system is not as static as it appears.

The exceptions are *large*. A few of them — perhaps ten in the entire chamber, perhaps fewer — are the size of a shield, or a stopped door, or a manhole cover. They are not palm-sized. They are not coin-sized. They are substantial, and they are not flat in the same way — they are *slightly* convex, raised in the center, and their surfaces are not scaled but smooth, the scales having fused into a continuous surface that has the sheen of the mound-material but is more uniform, more polished. They look like shields made of horn. They look like the bodies of something that has been here for a very long time.

They do not move. Not in the way the smaller ones move. They have not moved in the time since the stairs were cut — the tool marks are not disturbed, the mounds near them have grown up to their edges and pressed against their sides, and they have not shifted, not reoriented, not responded. And yet they are not fused. You can tell because their edges are distinct from the surfaces beneath them. They are not part of the mounds. They are *on* the mounds. Resting. Or perhaps it is more accurate to say they are *in* the mounds — embedded, half-submerged, like a stone in mud, like a thing that has been in one position so long that the world has grown around it.

They are the oldest living things here. Not the oldest things — the mounds are older, the material at their bases is older, the chord contains frequencies that must have been sounding for a span that makes the large organisms seem young. But the large organisms are the oldest things that are still, by any definition, *alive*. They are the first generation. The first layer. The organisms that settled when the floor was bare salt-rock and there were no mounds, and the chord was not a chord but a single voice — the voice of the salt-rock itself, vibrating at a frequency too low to hear, a frequency that is the frequency of the earth in this place, the frequency of the depth and the pressure and the warmth. The first organisms heard that frequency and grew to match it. They were shaped by it. They became instruments tuned to it. And when they settled and fused and began to produce their own tones — their own voices, added to the fundamental — the chord began. One note at a time. One body at a time. The large organisms are the first notes. The first additions. The first voices after the silence of the rock.

They are still producing their tones. You cannot hear them directly — their tones are so low that they are below hearing, below vibration, in the range where sound becomes pressure and pressure becomes heat and heat becomes something else, something that is not a physical phenomenon but a condition of the space. The large organisms are the bass of the chord. The foundation. Everything else is built on their voices. When they finally fuse — when the last of them settles and becomes part of the mound-material — the chord will lose its foundation. It will not stop. But it will thin. The lowest frequencies will fade, and the chord will become a lesser thing, a thing without depth, and the organisms that navigate by it will navigate less well, and the system will enter a new phase. This has not happened yet. It will. The large organisms are still distinct. They are still separate. But their edges are less sharp than they were a decade ago. The mounds are growing into them. They are being absorbed. Slowly. On a timescale that makes the researcher's patience seem like impatience.

## What They Do

They listen. This is not all they do, but it is what they do most, and it is what they do best, and it is what they are for.

They listen to the chord, which is the sound of every organism that has ever lived here, including themselves. They listen to the fundamental, which is the sound of the earth. They listen to the gas-columns, which have their own frequencies — high, thin, beyond hearing, but present in the vibration of the air, and the organisms on the mounds nearest the gas-columns are tuned differently, their scales smaller, their bodies thinner, their resonance higher. They listen to the sixty-cycle hum of the cable, which is alien and which they have not decided what to do with. Some of them orient toward it. Some orient away. The ones that orient toward it are the ones nearest the platform. The ones that orient away are the ones nearest the walls. The cable is a new voice. They are still deciding whether it belongs.

They also *maintain* the chord. This is less obvious. The mounds produce the tones, but the tones would not be continuous without the flat organisms. The flat organisms — the living ones, on the surfaces of the mounds — are in contact with the mound-material, and their bodies vibrate in sympathy with the mound's tone, and the sympathetic vibration reinforces the tone, sustains it, gives it the quality of permanence that makes the chord a thing without beginning or end. Without the living organisms, the mounds would still produce sound — the material is resonant, the apertures are real, the gas-pressure inside the mounds is real — but the sound would decay. It would be a pulse, not a tone. It would begin and end. The flat organisms make it *continuous*. They are the sustain. They hold the note. And when they fuse, their bodies become part of the mound, and the mound's tone gains a new component — the frequency of the organism's body, added permanently to the mound's voice — and the chord gains a new note, and the chord becomes more complex, more saturated, more itself.

This is what they do. They hold the note. They hold it for their entire lives, which are long by any standard, and when they die, the note is not lost because the holding is not lost — the body that held the note becomes part of the instrument, and the instrument continues to sound, and the sound is richer for the addition. Nothing is lost. Nothing changes. Everything accumulates.

## The Researcher

There is also the researcher. Not present — the researcher is absent. But the researcher lives here in the way that an author lives in a text, in the way that a builder lives in a building. The stairs are the researcher's first word. The platform is the researcher's second. The milling machine is the researcher's third, and the facet cut into the mound is the researcher's fourth, and the cable running out through the passage is the researcher's fifth, and beyond that there is nothing, because the researcher has not said anything else.

The researcher is patient. This is evident. The researcher has taken four samples — four facets, four small flat surfaces milled into the mounds near the platform — and between each sample there has been enough time for the flat organisms near the cut surfaces to reorient, to settle, to begin the slow process of growing over the facet, covering it, incorporating the cut surface into the body of the mound. Two of the four facets are already partially covered. The organisms are growing over the wounds. The researcher let them. The researcher came back and took another sample from a different mound rather than disturbing the ones that were healing. This is patience. Or it is respect. Or it is the kind of scientific method that this place demands — the kind that does not impose a tempo on a system that has its own.

The researcher is not a hero. The researcher is not a villain. The researcher is a person who came down twenty-three steps into a chamber full of sound and flat organisms and no water and did not understand what they found and took a sample and left and came back and took another sample and left and came back and took another sample and left. The researcher is a person who has heard the chord. The researcher is a person who, when they are not in the chamber, hears the chord at the edge of sleep, in the silence between sentences. The researcher is a person who has breathed the gas and felt the coating in their lungs and waited for it to dissolve and come back anyway. The researcher is a person who has been oriented toward by hundreds of flat organisms and felt it — felt the slow aim, the patient attention of things that do not have eyes but have something better than eyes, something that hears the shape of you — and come back anyway.

The researcher lives here the way the cable lives here: passing through, carrying something, connecting this place to a world above, and alien for that reason. The researcher is the only thing in this chamber that *goes somewhere*. Everything else stays.

## The Chord Itself

And then there is the chord, which is not an organism and is not a creature and is not alive in any way that requires quotation marks or careful definitions, but which is the thing that lives here most fully, most completely, most essentially.

The chord is three hundred notes. Or three hundred and four, counting the facets — the cut surfaces produce a slightly different resonance, a flatter, thinner sound, the sound of a mound that has been opened, and the flat organisms near the facets orient toward these sounds too, drawn to the new frequencies, the changed frequencies, the only sounds in the chamber that are not the sounds they have always known. The chord is three hundred and four notes, and each note is the voice of a mound, and each mound is the accumulated bodies of generations of flat organisms, and each flat organism was a listener, and each listener held a note, and each note is now part of the chord, and the chord is the sound of every listener that has ever lived here, sounding simultaneously, continuously, without variation, without forgetting.

The chord does not need anything. It does not need air, though it uses air. It does not need the mounds, though the mounds produce it. It does not need the organisms, though the organisms sustain it. It is a phenomenon that has become independent of its sources. It is self-sustaining in the way that a fire is self-sustaining — it consumes and it produces and the products of its consumption are the fuel for its continuation. The mounds produce the sound. The sound sustains the organisms. The organisms sustain the mounds. The mounds produce the sound. The cycle has no beginning and no end and no purpose and no direction. It is a process that is also a product. It is a thing that is also an act. It is the only thing here that is both.

The chord is alive. Not alive the way you are alive — alive the way a river is alive, the way a forest is alive, the way a fire is alive. Alive in the sense that it is a process that maintains itself, that responds to perturbation, that has states, that has history. The chord has been different in the past. It had fewer notes. It will have more notes in the future. It responds to the researcher's samples — the facets change the resonance of the mounds, and the flat organisms near the facets grow differently, and the gas-columns near the facets shift, and the chord absorbs the change and the change becomes part of the chord and the chord is different, minutely, imperceptibly, but different. The chord changes. Everything here changes. The change is so slow it is indistinguishable from permanence, but it is change. The chord is alive.

And what lives in the chord — what truly lives, what the flat organisms are listening to when they listen to the chord — is *history*. The chord is the sound of every organism that has ever existed in this place. It is a recording. It is a memory. It is a library written in frequencies instead of symbols, and every organism that has ever lived here has contributed a voice, and every voice is still present, and every voice will continue to be present for as long as the chord sounds, which is for as long as the mounds stand, which is for as long as the organisms live and die and fuse and become part of the architecture, which is for as long as the earth beneath them vibrates at the frequency that started everything.

The chord is the oldest thing in the chamber. It is the oldest thing most people will ever hear. It does not care. It sounds.
