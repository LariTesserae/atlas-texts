# What Lives Here

---

There is no single thing that lives here. That is the first thing to understand, and it is the hardest.

But you start with what you can see.

---

## The Grazer Continents

The flat ones come into focus first, because they are the closest thing to movement the grove offers, and the eye, even an eye adapted to foxfire dimness, is an organ built for movement.

They have no name. They need none. They are the sound of the walls.

Picture a hand-sized body compressed to the thickness of a coin, six legs splayed at angles that suggest they were never legs in the terrestrial sense — they are holdfasts, gliding surfaces, the minimal hardware required to maintain contact with a vertical substrate. The legs do not lift. They slide. The whole animal moves like a document across a desk, like a thing being pushed by something invisible, and the invisible thing is hunger, which is the only ambition this animal possesses.

Their backs are ecosystems. The mold that grows there is not parasitic; it is camouflage so thorough it has become identity. The grazer does not know it is pale umber underneath the mold. The mold does not know it is growing on an animal. They have reached an arrangement so complete that the boundary between them is philosophical. When a grazer dies — and they die constantly, quietly, folding inward like a closing book and dropping from the wall to the floor — the mold continues growing on the corpse for days, and the corpse becomes indistinguishable from the floor, and the burrowers find it by chemistry, not sight, and the arrangement ends.

Their mouths are the part that rewards attention, if you can call it attention, if you can kneel on the cold wet root-floor and bring your face close enough to a vertical surface to watch an animal feed.

The mouth is a ribbon. Not mandibles, not jaws — a structure like a radula but broader, a band of ciliated tissue that moves in a continuous wave, rasping the fungal film from the root surface the way a conveyor belt removes material, and the material removed is the fungus, and the fungus is the light, and so what the grazer does, as it moves, is eat the light. It leaves a trail behind it. A dark trail, root-brown, where the bioluminescent pellicle has been stripped away. The trail persists for hours. The fungus regrows. You can map the grazing history of a wall by the age of its trails — the fresh ones dark and bare, the older ones faintly glowing as the mycelium reclaims them, the oldest invisible, fully restored. The wall is a palimpsest. Every surface has been eaten and regrown, eaten and regrown, a thousand times.

There are thousands. Tens of thousands. The walls, seen closely, are not still — they crawl. But the crawling is so slow, and so uniform, and so distributed, that it produces an effect like the movement of a clock's hour hand: invisible in the moment, undeniable over time. If you sit for twenty minutes and watch one patch of wall, you will see nothing move. If you mark a grazer's position with a scratch on the root and return in an hour, it will be four centimeters away. It will have eaten a strip of light four centimeters long. It will not have noticed you.

They do not notice anything. The grazers have no organ that corresponds to attention. They have chemosensory patches along their anterior margin that detect the nutrient density of the fungal film ahead of them, and they turn toward density, and they rasp, and they move forward, and this is the entirety of their behavioral repertoire. They do not flee. They do not seek. They do not rest. They feed until they die, and they die because something eats them, or because the fungal film they are crossing is toxic, or because their own metabolism fails after a span that no one has measured because no one has cared to, because the grazers are not individuals. They are a process. They are the process of the light being eaten and the eating being returned to the floor, and the process does not have a lifespan.

What you notice, after watching them for an hour: they pulse.

When a bright wave of luminescence sweeps across the ceiling — the slow aurora of the network's electrical signaling — the grazers on the walls pause. Not all of them. Not synchronously. But there is a statistical shift. The rate of rasping drops. The rate of movement drops. The animals that were flowing become still, and then the wave passes, and they resume. It is not a flinch. It is not a fear response. It is a dimming, as if the electrical pulse that brightens the fungus also touches something in the grazer's simple neurology and quiets it. As if the same signal that is the grove thinking is also, to the grazer, a lullaby.

Or a command. You cannot tell. The grazers cannot tell. The distinction may not exist.

## The Hanging Ones

Above, in the ceiling's dim reaches, where the root-mass arches toward its apex and the fungal glow is thickest, the filter-feeders hang.

You hear them first. The clicking — that knuckle-crack sound, repeated at irregular intervals from every direction above — is the sound of their living. Each click is a droplet released. Each droplet is a meal processed and discarded.

They are larger than the grazers. Forearm-length. Their bodies are segmented in a way that suggests both centipede and larva, but neither — the segments are not anatomical units so much as constrictions, ring-marks in a body that is essentially tubular, as if the animal were a pale soft cylinder pinched at intervals by invisible thread. The segments allow flexibility. The animal can bend, coil, twist, and it does, constantly, slowly, as it hangs from its own silk.

The silk is extraordinary, though you would not know this from looking. It is not protein in the way spider silk is protein. It is chitin-derived, produced by glands that are modified from the same tissue as the animal's exoskeleton, and it has a property that no spider silk possesses: it is hydrophilic. Each thread absorbs water. Condensation wicks along it, travels downward, pools at the thread's end, and forms a droplet. The animal has positioned itself at the thread's end. It drinks the droplet. It extracts the dissolved organics — the sugars, the amino acids, the phenolic compounds the roots leak into the water as they respire — and it releases the remainder. The released water falls. The click is the surface tension breaking.

They are factories. Each one is a tiny water-driven mill, suspended from the ceiling, processing the grove's condensation cycle one droplet at a time. And there are hundreds of them, and each produces a droplet every eight to twelve seconds, and the sound of their production is the sound of the grove.

Their spinning is constant. Each animal turns, slowly, on its thread, and the turning is not purposeful — it is the result of the animal's body asymmetry, the slight weight imbalance between the feeding end and the excretory end, which shifts as the animal feeds and voids, and the shift produces a torque, and the torque produces rotation. They are water wheels. The water moves them. They process the water. The motion and the metabolism are the same event.

The fungus grows on them. This is the detail that elevates the filter-feeder from interesting to uncanny: their chitin is colonized by the same bioluminescent mycelium that coats the ceiling, and the colonization is not uniform. It follows the segments, pooling at the constrictions, creating bands of blue-green light around the animal's body like the rings of a planet seen from a great distance. When the animal spins, the rings spin, and in the dim upper reaches of the grove, you see them — constellations turning, slowly, in a sky of root.

Whether the fungus benefits the animal is unclear. The luminescence might attract mates. It might attract nothing — the filter-feeders seem to find each other by chemical means, by the trail of dissolved organics in the silk threads, and the light may be vestigial, a consequence of living in a place where everything glows. The fungus certainly benefits. The filter-feeder is a mobile substrate, a moving ceiling, a thing that carries the fungus to new locations, to new junction-points in the root architecture, where it can colonize and reinforce and glow. The filter-feeder is a seed. The fungus is using it to disperse.

The filter-feeder does not know this. The filter-feeder hangs, and clicks, and turns, and drinks, and the fungus rides it like a passenger on a bus that has no route and no destination and no driver, and the bus goes in circles, and the circles are the grove.

When they die, they do not fall. The silk holds them. They hang, dried, emptied, the fungus continuing to grow on the corpse for weeks, a glowing husk turning slowly in the dark, until the silk finally degrades and the body drops. The burrowers receive it. The cycle absorbs it. Nothing is lost.

## The Worms

You will not see them clearly. This is by design — not their design, not anyone's design, but the design of a world where the floor is a substrate and the substrate is a body and the body is a graveyard and the graveyard is a garden, and the worms are the ones who tend the boundary.

They are in the floor. In the gaps between compressed roots, where the dense substrate loosens into something fibrous and penetrable, they move by peristalsis — the same motion that moves food through a gut, which is appropriate, because the floor is a gut, and the worms are its lining.

Blind. Legless. Pale to the point of translucence — you can see their internal structures through the skin, the dark thread of a gut, the slower pulse of whatever serves as a circulatory system, and this visibility is not a vulnerability but a consequence of never having needed pigmentation, never having needed opacity, because the place where they live has no light except the faint blue-green that reaches the floor, and that light carries no information worth seeing.

Their heads — if heads is the word, if a body that is essentially a tube can be said to have a head — are ringed with cilia. Sensory cilia. The rings emerge from the floor when a worm surfaces, and they taste the air, and "taste" here means something closer to "read," because the air is a text written in dissolved chemistry, and the cilia are the eyes that read it, and what they read is: *is there food here, is there a dead grazer, is there a fallen filter-feeder, is there a deposit of root-matter that has begun to decompose, is there anything that was alive and is now not and can be made alive again in a different form.*

When they find food, they do not emerge. They come up underneath it. The floor opens — not dramatically, not visibly, but the fibrous root-mass parts, and the worm's anterior extends, and the dead thing — grazer, filter-feeder, root-fragment — is taken from below, pulled downward, consumed in the substrate. The process takes minutes for a grazer, hours for a filter-feeder, days for a large root-section. The worm's gut is slow. Everything here is slow.

The castings are the point. What the worm excretes, after processing the dead, is a paste of mineralized nutrient — nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, the elements that the roots need to grow, delivered in a form the roots can absorb. The worm is a refinery. It takes the complex chemistry of death and reduces it to the simple chemistry of growth, and the roots receive it, and the roots grow, and the roots respire, and the condensation forms, and the filter-feeders drink, and the grazers eat the fungus, and the grazers die, and the worms eat the grazers, and the cycle is complete.

You see their trails. The floor, examined closely, is not flat — it is marked with shallow ridges, the paths of worms that have moved through the substrate just below the surface, pushing material aside, compressing it, leaving a trace like the wake of a boat in shallow water. The trails intersect, overlap, form a network as dense and purposeless-looking as the root-braids above. But there is purpose. The trails follow chemical gradients. The worms move toward decomposition, toward death, the way the grazers move toward nutrient density, the way the filter-feeders hang where the condensation is richest. The entire grove is a system of organisms moving along gradients, and the gradients are the grove's shape, its internal geography, its map of where things are needed and where things are available, and the map is redrawn every minute, and the organisms follow it, and the following is the grove.

Sometimes a worm surfaces fully. It rises from the floor like a pale finger, two centimeters, five, tasting the air with its ring of cilia, and the air here is cold and wet and full of the drip-sound and the hum, and the worm has no way to process any of this except as chemistry, and the chemistry tells it nothing it needs to know, and it descends. The surfacing lasts four seconds. You would have to be watching. You would have to be on your knees on the cold root-floor, staring at a patch of ground that looks identical to every other patch, waiting for something that happens once per hour in a random location. You would have to be very patient or very lucky.

The worms do not know you are there. The grazers do not know you are there. The filter-feeders click and spin and drink and do not know you are there. This is not indifference. It is the absence of the category. They do not have the machinery for *you*. You are a temperature anomaly, a disturbance in the condensation pattern, a shadow that blocks the faint light for a moment as you pass. You are less than a grazer. A grazer, at least, is food.

## The Thing That Is All of Them

But the animals are not what lives here. The animals are what *moves* here, and movement is not life, and life is not movement, and the thing that lives here does not move.

The root-fungal network is alive. Not in the way a tree is alive — a tree is an organism, a unit, a thing with a boundary. The root-fungal network has no boundary. It is the walls. It is the ceiling. It is the floor. It is the water system and the structural support and the light source and the computational substrate and the food chain base and the sensory apparatus and the waste recycling system, and all of these are the same tissue, the same process, the same continuous undifferentiated living material, and calling it an organism is like calling the ocean a fish.

It thinks. Or it does something that, when you watch it — when you sit in the cold and the dark and the drip and watch the blue-green waves sweep across the ceiling in patterns that correspond to the hum you feel in your sternum — looks enough like thinking that the word *thinking* is the closest available approximation, even though it is wrong.

The thinking is not done by a brain. There is no brain. There is no neuron, no ganglion, no centralized processing structure of any kind. The thinking is done by the network — by the roots and the fungi and the bacterial films and the chemical gradients that propagate through them, by ion cascades that move through living tissue at the speed of a slow thought, by calcium waves and electrical potentials and pH shifts and hormone analogues that carry information from one part of the grove to another the way a hormone carries information through a bloodstream, except the bloodstream is the body and the body is the room and the room is the thought.

The waves of luminescence are the visible trace of this process. When a region of the network experiences a change — a shift in moisture, a spike in grazer density, a structural stress in the root architecture — the change propagates. It moves outward through the mycorrhizal connections, from root to root, from fungus to fungus, and the fungus brightens as the signal passes, because the signal is electrical and the bioluminescence is electrochemical, and the brightening is not a message, it is the message, it is the signal itself made visible, and you watch it cross the ceiling like a slow lightning and you are watching the grove feel.

What does it feel? It feels the grazers eating its skin. It feels the filter-feeders drinking its sweat. It feels the worms digesting its dead. It feels the cold. It feels the water, the condensation, the saturation, the balance between too dry and too wet that is the difference between dormancy and rot. It feels the structural integrity of its own architecture — the arch of the ceiling, the compression of the walls, the places where the root-mass is thinning and the places where it is thickening. It feels itself.

And it responds. The responses are not decisions. They are adjustments — chemical adjustments, growth adjustments, the redirection of resources from one region to another, the thickening of a root where stress is detected, the secretion of a compound that suppresses grazer reproduction in a region where grazing has exceeded the regrowth rate, the acceleration of fungal growth in a junction that needs reinforcement. These adjustments are automatic. They are cybernetic. They are the control system of a machine that maintains itself, and the machine is alive, and the alive is the machine.

The timescale is the thing that defeats you. The grove thinks at the speed of chemistry, which is to say it thinks at the speed of a sluggish thought, and a single thought — a single complete cycle of sensing and responding — takes hours. The wave that crosses the ceiling is the beginning of a thought. The adjustment — the thickened root, the secreted compound — is the end. The interval between them is the processing, and the processing is the thinking, and the thinking is so slow that you, standing inside it, are like a bacterium standing inside a human brain: too fast, too small, too brief to perceive the thought that is happening around you.

You are inside a thought. You are inside a thought that began before you arrived and will continue after you leave and has been continuing for longer than your species has existed. The thought is about moisture. The thought is about structure. The thought is about the slow adjustment of a living architecture to the forces that act upon it — gravity, water, chemistry, time. The thought is the grove being a grove. The thought is the grove.

## The Ones You Do Not See

There are others.

The mites — smaller than the head of a pin, eight-legged, living in the interface between the fungal film and the root surface, in a world so thin it has no depth, a two-dimensional ecology that exists on the surface of a surface. They are predators. They eat the grazing larvae of something even smaller — nematodes, perhaps, or bacteria, or things that have no name because no one has looked at this scale in this place. The mites are the apex predators of a universe that is three millimeters thick, and they have never seen the grove, and the grove has never seen them, and they are part of it.

The bacteria. The biofilms that coat every interior surface — the root-walls, the root-floor, the root-ceiling, the animals, the silk threads of the filter-feeders, the castings of the worms, the dead. The bacteria are the chemistry. They are the ones that fix nitrogen, that break down cellulose, that convert the complex organics of death into the simple organics of life. They are the translators. They are the ones that make the cycle cycle, and they are everywhere, and they are so numerous that the concept of number loses meaning, and they are, in aggregate, the most massive and the most metabolically active component of the grove, and you cannot see them, and they are what the grove is made of, more than the roots, more than the fungus, more than anything you can see.

The thing in the deep crack. The one you almost stepped on. The one that moved.

It was in the floor, in a gap between root-masses wider than the others — a fissure, a place where the substrate had not yet closed, where the compressed root gave way to something looser, darker, and from that darkness something withdrew. Not quickly. Not slowly. At the speed of something that has no predator and no prey and no reason to move at any particular pace. It was pale. It was larger than the worms. It was gone before your eyes could assemble the details into a shape, and the shape your mind offered afterward — segmented, flat, wet, the color of the inside of a root — was a guess, and the guess was probably wrong, and the thing that made the guess was an animal that evolved to guess about surface things, and this was not a surface thing.

There are deeper levels. The grove does not end at the floor. The root-mass extends downward — how far, no one knows, because no one has followed it, because following it would mean burrowing into a living substrate that is cold and wet and dark and breathing, and the breathing is not yours, and the space is not yours, and the thing in the deep crack lives there, in the root, in the dark, in the part of the grove that does not glow.

---

## What Lives Here

Everything lives here. That is the answer, and the answer is not satisfying, and the answer is also the truth.

The root lives here. The fungus lives here. The bacteria live here. The grazers live here. The filter-feeders live here. The worms live here. The mites live here. The thing in the crack lives here. The water lives here, if water can be said to live when it cycles through a living system and carries the living system's chemistry in its dissolved load and deposits that chemistry on surfaces that need it and is taken up again and respired again and cycled again and has been cycling for longer than the word *cycle* has had a user.

And the grove itself lives here. The room. The architecture. The system that is the sum of its parts and also more than its parts, in the way that a body is more than its cells, in the way that a thought is more than its neurons, in the way that a forest is more than its trees. The grove is an organism, if you stretch the word, if you let it break, if you allow that an organism can be a place, can be a room, can be a cathedral built by cells that have no blueprint except chemistry and patience.

What lives here is a place that is alive.

You were inside it. You were inside a living thing, and the living thing did not know you were there, because the living thing does not know things, because knowing is not what it does. What it does is *be*, and the being is the living, and the living is the architecture, and the architecture is the organism, and the organism is the room, and the room is the grove, and the grove is the root and the fungus and the water and the grazers and the filter-feeders and the worms and the mites and the bacteria and the thing in the crack and the cold and the dark and the blue-green light that sweeps across the ceiling like a thought that never finishes forming.

What lives here is this. All of this. At once. The same thing.
