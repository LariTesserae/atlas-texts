# What Lives in the Condensaries of Vesh-Dravon

## The Workers

Eight hundred and twelve on the last census count. Eight hundred and nine by the time the census was filed, because three had rotated out through the gland locks during the processing, and because census-keeping in the Condensaries is a courtesy rather than a requirement, maintained by the administrative staff more out of institutional habit than any expectation that the number will be used for anything. The number does not matter. What matters is that there are enough of them to keep the place running, and that the number has stayed within that threshold for longer than anyone currently living can remember.

They are not born here. No one is born here. The Condensaries are not a settlement in the generational sense — they are an installation, and installations are staffed, not populated. Workers arrive through the gland locks on contract lengths that vary from six months to three years, though the contracts are renewable, and a significant fraction of the workforce — perhaps a third, perhaps more — has been here long enough that their original contract is a formality no one references. They came from outside. They will, most of them, eventually leave. Some will not. The Condensaries do not require permanence from their workers, but they accept it when it is offered.

What the workers look like, physically, is shaped by the place within the first few weeks. The constant moisture softens the skin — not dramatically, but noticeably. Hands become smooth in a way that reads as care, though it is not care. It is the ash-water, the faint mineral residue that sits on every surface, that coats every railing, that transfers to every hand that touches anything. It builds up in the creases of the knuckles and the folds of the wrists, a pale film like the talc the workers are warned about and have long since stopped worrying about. The film is harmless. The workers have tested it, formally and informally, over decades. It does not penetrate the skin. It sits on the surface. It makes their hands look younger than they are.

The altitude does something to the face. The thin air, the cool temperature, the constant low pressure behind the molars that never quite resolves into pain — these things write themselves into the musculature over time. The jaw sets slightly forward. The cheekbones tighten. The eyes, after months in the dim light, develop a particular quality: they seem larger, not because they are, but because the surrounding tissue has narrowed, the fat pads thinning in the cool air, the skin drawing taut across the orbital bones. Workers who have been in the Condensaries for more than a year have a look. It is not a bad look. It is a *specific* look. They recognize it in each other instantly, the way people from the same small town recognize the accent, the posture, the tell. The tell here is the jaw and the eyes and the smooth hands. You can pick a long-term Condensary worker out of a crowd on the outside, if you know what to look for. They look like they are still slightly inside.

---

They wear what the place requires, which is layers. The temperature sits at eight or nine degrees during the active cycle and drops toward freezing during the condenser rest phase, and the moisture means that any single garment will be damp within an hour, so the strategy is not to stay dry — that is impossible, and the workers stopped attempting it early in the settlement's history — but to manage the damp. A base layer, close to the skin, that wicks. A mid layer, insulating, that absorbs. An outer layer, heavy and open-woven, that sheds the worst of the deposition but that breathes, because sealing yourself against the moisture just traps your own heat and creates a personal humidity that is worse than the ambient. The garments are not uniform. There is no dress code in the Condensaries. There is a materials specification — the outer layer must be non-reactive with ash-water, which rules out certain synthetics — but within that, workers dress as they prefer. The result is a population that looks individually practical and collectively varied, a workforce in which no two people are dressed alike but all of whom are dressed for the same conditions. The colors are muted, not by regulation but by the environment. The mist leaches dye. Bright fabrics fade within weeks to soft versions of themselves. The workers know this and do not bother with bright fabrics, or if they do, they accept the fading as a thing that happens, the way the moss happens, the way the weepers happen.

The footwear is specific. It has to be. The wet ceramic is slick, and the crust is uneven, and the work takes you across surfaces that range from clean tile to moss-covered slopes to standing water to the mineral-roughened surface of an old weeper. The standard issue is a boot with a soft, high-friction sole — not a heavy boot, because the work requires sensitivity of foot, the ability to feel the substrate beneath you and adjust your weight to its grade — but a boot that grips. The workers break their boots in within a week and then keep them until the soles are smooth, which takes about four months, at which point the boots are resoled in the workshops or replaced. The resoled boots are the ones worn by the long-term workers. You can tell how long someone has been in the Condensaries by how many times their boots have been resoled. The crease lines deepen with each resoling. The leather, if it is leather, takes on a grain that is not its original grain but the grain the place has given it — a dark, damp-softened texture that the workers call *crust-leather*, though it is not made from the crust, merely shaped by it.

---

The work itself shapes the body further. The tower-scalers — perhaps forty of the eight hundred, the smallest specialized group and the one with the highest turnover, because the work is the most physically demanding and the most exposed — develop the upper body strength that climbing requires, but it is a specific strength, a pulling-and-gripping strength, the strength of hands that hold ceramic and steel in cold wet conditions and do not slip. Their forearms thicken. Their hands develop calluses that are different from the calluses of other workers, because the calluses of the tower-scalers are formed against a wet surface, which means they are smoother, denser, less prone to cracking. The tower-scalers also develop a tolerance for height that is not fearlessness but habit. They do not look down when they climb. They do not need to. They know where the ground is. The ground is where the mist comes from.

The drainage technicians — the largest group, perhaps two hundred — develop a different relationship to the body. Their work takes them into the sublevels, the settling tanks, the filtration channels, the spaces beneath the crust where the water is collected and processed. They work in a wetness that is different from the surface wetness — deeper, warmer in places where the processing heat radiates through the tank walls, colder in places where the runoff from the surface hits the sublevel air. They develop a tolerance for confined spaces that is not claustrophilia but something more practical: a learned indifference to the distance between their body and the nearest wall. They move through the sublevels with a fluidity that looks like ease but is actually expertise, the expertise of people who know exactly how much space their body occupies and how to fit it into spaces that were not designed for bodies. They are, as a group, thinner than the other workers. The sublevels require it. The access hatches between levels are narrow — they were designed for maintenance access, not for the repeated passage of adult humans carrying tools — and a drainage technician who gains weight beyond a certain point simply cannot do their job. This is not stated. It is understood. The drainage technicians, as a population, are lean. Not unhealthy. Efficient. Their bodies have been shaped by the infrastructure they maintain.

The crust-repair specialists are the ones who work most closely with the material of the place itself — the ceramic tile, the substrate, the waterproofing membrane, the expansion joints. They are the ones who know the crust as a material rather than as a surface, who understand the layering of the built environment the way a geologist understands strata. They can tell, by the feel of the tile underfoot, whether a section of crust is sound or whether the sublevel beneath it has begun to compromise. They tap the surface with a small hammer — every crust-repair specialist carries one, a short-handled thing, heavier than it looks, made of a dense alloy that rings differently against solid substrate versus compromised substrate. The sound is the diagnostic. A sound crust rings flat and clean. A compromised crust rings hollow, or damp, or — in the worst cases, where the sublevel has collapsed and the crust is bridging a void — does not ring at all. The crust-repair specialists listen to the place with their hammers. They hear the Condensaries the way a doctor hears a body, through contact and percussion and the interpretation of resonance. They are, as a group, the most patient workers. Their work is slow. A crust repair — removing the damaged tile, assessing the substrate, patching the membrane, laying new tile, sealing the joints — takes between four hours and four days, depending on the extent of the damage and the location. They work in the open, in the mist, on their knees, with their hands in the wet. They are the workers most visibly marked by the place. Their knees are permanently darkened by the tile. Their hands are permanently roughened by the ceramic and the grout. They look, of all the workers, the most like the place they work in — dark, textured, wet, solid.

The filter-cleaners are the ones whose work is most directly engaged with the product of the Condensaries, the mineral compounds that are the entire point of the installation. The filters sit in the settling tanks, where the ash-water is processed, and the filters capture the mineral precipitate that the condenser grid has extracted from the aquifer water. The cleaning of the filters is the extraction of the product. What comes off the filters is the mineral load — the binding agents, the catalyst substrates, the thermal insulators, the chemical precursors — in the form of a dense, sticky paste that is scraped, washed, and pressed from the filter membranes. The paste is the Condensaries' reason for existing. Everything else — the towers, the crust, the rills, the moss, the workers — is in service to this paste. The filter-cleaners are the ones who touch it. They are the ones whose hands are stained, not with the faint ash-water film that all workers carry, but with the deeper stain of the product itself, a pale ochre color that sits in the whorls of the fingerprints and the beds of the nails and that does not wash out, that fades only over weeks, and that is renewed with every shift. The filter-cleaners are marked by what they do. They carry the Condensaries' product on their bodies, visibly, identifiably. They are the only workers whose role is apparent to an outsider at a glance. They do not mind this. Some of them are proud of it, in the quiet way that people are proud of things that are not impressive but are theirs.

## The Administrator

Her name is Pela Trev. She has been in the Condensaries for eleven years, which makes her the longest-tenured worker currently active, though not the longest-tenured worker ever — that record belongs to a crust-repair specialist named Sorn Aved, who was here for twenty-three years before rotating out through the gland locks with no announcement and no farewell and a forwarding address that turned out to be false. Pela Trev is not the longest ever. She is the longest now, and that is enough.

She is not what you would expect from an administrator, if you expected an administrator to be someone who sits behind a desk and coordinates with the outside world via communication systems and does not touch the wet. Pela Trev touches the wet. She walks the Condensaries every morning — every morning, without exception, without regard for the weather, though the concept of weather is largely inapplicable here, since the weather is always the same, which is misty — and she walks the whole settlement, all four square kilometers, which takes her approximately three hours at the pace the crust requires. She walks through the central district, through the processing corridors, along the major rills, through the old works, along the perimeter, past every condenser tower, past every drainage grate, past every weeper. She does not inspect, exactly. She does not carry a checklist. She walks, and she looks, and she feels the crust under her feet, and she listens to the rills and the towers and the drainage and the quiet, and she has been doing this for eleven years, and what she is doing is *maintaining an internal model of the state of the Condensaries* — a mental model, constantly updated, constantly corrected, of what is working and what is failing and what is about to fail and what has failed and what can be repaired and what cannot and what should be abandoned and what must not be. She is the closest thing the Condensaries have to a consciousness. Not a controller — the place is too complex for central control, and she knows this — but a consciousness, an awareness that holds the whole system in mind even though the system operates without her.

She is not tall. She is not short. She is the size the place has made her, which is a size that fits through the access hatches in the sublevels, because she goes there too, in her morning walks, descending through the crust into the settling tank chambers, into the drainage corridors, into the spaces where the water is processed. She does not go everywhere. Some sections of the sublevel are flooded, some are collapsed, some are sealed. But she goes where she can, and she has been going where she can for eleven years, and the sublevels she has not seen she has seen in the reports of the drainage technicians, and she has integrated those reports into her model, and her model is — as far as anyone can determine, including Pela Trev herself — accurate. She knows the Condensaries. She knows them the way you know your own body, not by studying it but by inhabiting it for a long time.

She does not give orders. This is the thing that surprises outsiders most about her, if they meet her, which most outsiders do not, because the gland locks are the only point of contact and the administrators of the Condensaries have never been the sort who meet the arriving shipments at the door. Pela Trev does not give orders because the work does not require orders. The workers know their work. The work is defined by the place — a drainage technician sees a clogged grate and clears it, a tower-scaler sees an eroded nozzle and replaces it, a crust-repair specialist feels a hollow ring and opens the tile. The work is self-evident to the people who do it. What Pela Trev provides is not direction but *context* — she knows which sections are being prioritized, which repairs are urgent versus which can wait, which materials are in short supply and which are abundant. She communicates this not through directives but through conversation, through the shift-change briefings that happen three times a day in the central district, where the workers gather and the information flows laterally, worker to worker, and Pela Trev is one node among many, though her node has the most connections.

She has a greenroom. Not her own — the greenrooms are not owned, not assigned, not designated — but there is a greenroom near the eastern edge of the old works, in the collapsed walkway that was mentioned earlier, the one where the filmy ferns grow to half a meter, and Pela Trev spends twenty minutes of her morning walk there. She does not sit. She stands. She stands in the greenroom and the ferns brush her face and the air is still and the rills are quiet and she stands there for twenty minutes and then she continues her walk. No one comments on this. It is known. It is part of the routine. It is, in its way, part of the Condensaries' routine, as regular and as unremarked as the cycling of a tower.

## The Way They Speak

The workers speak Vesh standard, the administrative language of the installation, but they speak it the way people speak a language they have lived in rather than grown up in — fluently, precisely, with a vocabulary that has been shaped by the specific needs of the place. The technical lexicon is extensive: the names of the infrastructure components, the processes, the materials, the failure modes. A new worker learns this lexicon in the first weeks, not through instruction but through immersion, the way you learn the vocabulary of a workplace, by hearing the terms used in context and by using them and by being corrected when you misuse them. The corrections are not harsh. They are efficient. A drainage technician who calls a weeping a weeper will not be corrected, but one who calls a weeper a *deposit formation* will be, once, and then will not make the mistake again, because the distinction matters: a weeping is the process, a weeper is the thing the process has made, and the thing and the process are different categories in the Condensaries' ontology, and the ontology is not academic but practical, because if you confuse the process with the thing you will confuse the repair with the maintenance, and that confusion will cost time.

They also have a vocabulary that is not technical but that has developed in the place, words and phrases that do not exist outside the gland locks because they describe things that do not exist outside the gland locks. *Surge* is one — the high-output cycle. *Greenroom* is another. *Underwalk* is a third. *Ash-water* is a fourth. There are others. *Gone-soft* — used to describe a section of crust that has begun to compromise, that flexes underfoot, that may or may not be safe to walk on. *Cycling down* — used to describe a condenser tower that has been taken offline, permanently or temporarily, and that is, in the workers' phrasing, "going to moss," meaning that it is being colonized by vegetation and will not be reclaimed. *Gone to moss* is the past tense. It is used with a particular tone — not regret, not relief, but recognition. A tower that has gone to moss is a tower that the Condensaries have released from function. It is still there. It is still standing. It is still part of the place. But it is no longer part of the system. It has become part of the garden.

They speak quietly. This is not a rule. It is a consequence. The Condensaries are quiet, and speaking loudly in a quiet place feels wrong, not because it is prohibited but because it is discordant, a note out of scale with the rills and the towers and the drainage. The workers have adjusted their speaking volume to match the ambient sound level, which is low, and the result is that conversation in the Condensaries is a low, even murmur, carried on the mist, barely audible at a distance of ten meters. You hear the workers before you see them — a soft exchange of words, the tone more apparent than the content, the rhythm of speech rather than its meaning, and then they emerge from the dim and they are there, two or three of them, walking to or from a task, and they pass you with a nod or a brief word — "watch the grade here," or "tower four is cycling" — and then they are gone again into the mist and their voices go with them, absorbed by the moisture in the air, and the rills fill the space they leave.

## The Way They Sleep

The quarters are in the central district, clustered in blocks of two- and three-story units built from the dark ceramic of the crust. The rooms are small — a bed, a storage locker, a shelf, a lamp. The windows are small, their glass thick, and they look out onto the condenser towers, which at night are blinking amber lights rising into the mist and disappearing. The rooms are damp. This is not a failing of the construction. It is the atmosphere. The moisture is in everything, and the quarters are not sealed — no space in the Condensaries is sealed, except the gland locks, because sealing a space against the ambient atmosphere would require a pressure differential that the infrastructure was not designed to maintain. The damp is accepted. The beds are warm — the workers use heavy blankets, and the quarters are heated by a system that draws waste heat from the settling tank processing, a low, warm radiation that comes up through the floor and that keeps the rooms at a temperature that is comfortable for sleeping, which is warmer than the outside but not by much.

The workers sleep well. This is a consistent report, across workers, across shifts, across years. The sleep in the Condensaries is deep and regular, and the reason is the sound. The layered quiet — the rills, the towers, the drainage, the mist — is a soundscape that the human brain, or at least the human brains that end up in the Condensaries, finds restful. It is not white noise. White noise is uniform, featureless, and the Condensaries' sound is not that — it is textured, varied, composed of distinct elements that interact and shift. But it is *constant*, and it is *low*, and it does not interrupt, and the workers sleep in it the way they sleep in the damp, by accepting it, by letting it be the medium of their rest rather than an obstacle to it. New workers sometimes have difficulty for the first few nights. By the end of the first week, they sleep like the rest. They sleep deeply. They sleep the sleep of people who have worked hard in a cool, wet environment and who have come inside to a warm bed and a dark room and a sound that is like the place breathing around them, and their bodies respond to the breathing and they go down into it, and they sleep.

## What the Workers Are

They are not a culture. They are not a community. They are not a society. They are a workforce. But they are a workforce that has been in the same place for a long time, and a workforce in a place long enough develops the qualities of a community without the structure of one. They know each other. Not all of them — eight hundred is too many for everyone to know everyone — but they know the workers in their section, in their shift, in their specialty. They know the crust-repair specialist who works the stretch of corridor adjacent to their drainage route. They know the tower-scaler who services the towers in their district. They know the filter-cleaner whose morning break coincides with their own. They know each other the way the place knows them — through proximity, through repetition, through the long accumulation of shared context that does not require friendship but that creates something that is not unlike it.

They do not celebrate. There are no festivals in the Condensaries, no holidays, no marked days. The condenser grid does not observe calendars. The place runs continuously, and the workers run with it, and the passage of time is measured not in seasons — there are no seasons here, or rather, there is one season, which is misty — but in contract lengths and shift cycles and the slow accretion of weepers and the slow loss of towers gone to moss. A worker who has been here for three years has not experienced three of anything. They have experienced three years of the same thing, and the same thing is the Condensaries, cycling, flowing, breathing, failing, being repaired, cycling again.

They do have rituals. Small ones. The morning walk of Pela Trev is one, though it is her ritual, not a shared one. The shift-change briefings are another — the three-times-daily gathering in the central district, where information flows and the workers see each other and the place's human component reassembles briefly into a visible whole. The greenroom visits are a third, and these are shared, though not formally, not scheduled. A worker on break who is near a greenroom will go in. They will stand there for a few minutes. They may encounter another worker doing the same thing. They will acknowledge each other — a nod, a quiet word, or just the mutual recognition that they are both standing in a place where the system has failed and something else has grown — and then they will leave, and the greenroom will be empty again, and the ferns will hang and the moss will hold the still air and the weepers will deposit their minerals in slow, patient accretion, and the place will continue, and the workers will continue with it.

## What Else Lives Here

The moss is alive. This is worth stating plainly, because in the context of the Condensaries — the built substrate, the engineered atmosphere, the industrial function, the workers in their damp layers and their resoled boots — the moss can be read as a feature of the environment, a thing that grows here the way rust grows on iron, and that is true, but it is also alive, it is a living system, it is the only living system in the Condensaries that is thriving rather than merely persisting. The workers persist. The place persists. The moss thrives. There is a difference.

The moss has been here almost as long as the condenser grid. Spores arrived — on workers' clothing, on shipping materials, on the wind that occasionally finds its way through the gland locks during the intake cycle — and found conditions that were, for a moss, ideal. Constant moisture. Cool temperatures. Diffuse light. Stable surfaces. The moss colonized the Condensaries the way life colonizes any environment that meets its needs: thoroughly, relentlessly, without asking permission. Within a decade of the original construction, the moss was established. Within two decades, it was a feature. Within three — and the place is now well past three centuries old, though the moss has not been here that long, because the early decades of the settlement included a more aggressive maintenance regime that kept the vegetation in check — within three, it was part of the identity of the place. The Condensaries without the moss is unimaginable now. Not because the moss is beautiful, though it is. Because the moss is *what the place does when it is not being maintained*. The moss is the place's own impulse, the thing it grows when no one is directing it, the direction it takes when the direction is left to it.

The moss is not a single organism. It is many species, competing, coexisting, partitioning the available surfaces by the fine gradients of moisture and light and substrate composition that the Condensaries' varied topography provides. The dark, nearly black moss that grows in the oldest, wettest areas is the most ancient colonizer — it was here first, or nearly first, and it holds the ground it has claimed with the tenacity of a pioneer. The bright yellow-green moss that climbs the condenser towers is a later arrival, more aggressive, more tolerant of the aerosol spray, and it is slowly, over decades, displacing the darker species from the lower tower sections. The red-brown moss of the drier surfaces is a specialist, occupying a niche that the others cannot. The pale, ambiguous growth on the weepers — not quite moss, not quite lichen — is something else, something that has evolved in the Condensaries or has been shaped by the Condensaries into something that exists only here, that has found its niche in the mineral deposits of failing infrastructure and has made that niche its own.

The moss is alive, and it is growing, and it has been growing for a long time, and it will continue to grow for as long as the condenser grid deposits moisture on the surfaces of the Condensaries. It is the second system operating in the place — the first being the mechanical system of water and towers and drainage, the second being the biological system of moss and fern and weeper. The two systems are not in competition. They are in *conversation*. The mechanical system creates the conditions the biological system requires. The biological system colonizes the surfaces the mechanical system has built. The mechanical system degrades, and the biological system grows into the degradation. The workers repair the degradation, clearing the biological growth from the operational surfaces, and the biological system recolonizes the cleared surfaces within weeks. This is the dynamic equilibrium. This is the conversation. It has been going on for centuries, and it will go on for as long as the place exists, and neither system will win, because winning is not what systems do. Systems persist. Systems adapt. Systems find the shape that the conditions allow and hold it until the conditions change, and then they find a new shape.

The filmy ferns are alive in the same way, though they are more specific in their requirements and less dominant in their presence. They need the moss first — the moss establishes the surface, the moisture-holding mat, the organic substrate that the ferns can root into. The ferns are the second wave, the colonizers of the colonization, the life that follows the life that came first. They are more delicate, more dependent on the constant moisture, more sensitive to the surges that flood their tissues and strip their color. They recover. They always recover. But they are, in the ecology of the Condensaries, the most fragile element, the thing that is most clearly alive in the sense that fragility implies life — the sense that says: this thing can be damaged, and therefore this thing is not a machine, and therefore this thing is alive.

The insects are alive in a marginal, barely-there way. They exist in the Condensaries the way a rumor exists in a conversation — present, acknowledged, not central. They breed in the settling tanks, where the mineral content of the water supports their larval stage, and they hover in the sheltered areas, and they are eaten by nothing, because there is nothing here that eats them. They live and die in the mist, and their lives are short, and their deaths are unnoticed, and their bodies become part of the organic accumulation in the cracks of the crust, a thin layer of chitin and protein that the moss absorbs without distinction. They are not a population. They are a residue of life, the faintest trace of the animal kingdom in a place that has rejected it.

## The Underwalk

And then there is the thing in the sublevels.

The workers do not talk about it. They write about it. The distinction matters. In conversation, the underwalk is not a topic. It is not avoided — avoiding a topic requires intention, and the workers do not intend to avoid it, they simply do not discuss it, the way you do not discuss the color of the sky or the taste of the water or any other thing that is simply a condition of the place rather than an event in it. But in the logbooks, in the maintenance reports, in the brief notations that accompany shift changes, the underwalk appears. *Underwalk reported, sector 7-old, 02:40. No visual. Log entry: K. Davrin, third shift.* These entries go back decades. They are consistent. They describe the same sound — a soft, wet sound, like something moving through shallow water — in the same locations — the abandoned sublevels of the old works, the flooded corridors, the collapsed sections of the deep crust — at the same times — the deep night, the third shift, the hours when the workers are in their quarters and the condenser towers cycle alone and the rills hum their baseline note and the place is as empty of human presence as it ever is.

The underwalk is not investigated. This is not a policy. It is not a decision. It is an absence of action that has become, through long persistence, a practice. The sublevels where the underwalk is reported are abandoned, flooded, collapsed — they are not accessible to investigation without equipment and effort that would have to be diverted from the maintenance of operational systems, and the underwalk does not affect the operational systems, and so the investigation does not happen. This is practical. It is also something else. The workers feel it, though they do not say it: the underwalk is the Condensaries' own life, the life of the place itself, the sound the place makes when it is not being maintained and not being observed and not being walked through by human feet. The underwalk is the sound the Condensaries make when they are alone.

No one has seen it. No one has found evidence of it beyond the sound. No one has proposed a hypothesis that satisfies the workers who have heard it, because the workers who have heard it do not want a hypothesis. They do not want an explanation. They want the sound to remain unexplained, because an explanation would make it a thing, and a thing can be categorized and managed and addressed, and the underwalk is not a thing to be managed. It is a thing to be *noted*. It is a thing to be written in a logbook and passed to the next shift and received by the next worker and written again when it is heard again, a thread of notation running through the records of the Condensaries like the rills run through the crust, a continuous, quiet, persistent presence that does not require understanding because it is not a problem. It is a quality. It is a quality of the place, the way the mist is a quality and the dim light is a quality and the sound of the rills is a quality. The Condensaries have an underwalk. It is theirs.

## What the Condensaries Are For Themselves

The Condensaries do not exist for themselves. They exist to extract mineral compounds from aquifer water through atmospheric precipitation. This is their function. This is why they were built. This is what they do.

But the Condensaries have been doing this for over three hundred years, and in three hundred years the function has become the identity, and the identity has become the place, and the place has become something that is not entirely described by its function. The function is the skeleton. The place is the body that has grown around the skeleton — the moss on the towers, the weepers in the cracks, the ferns in the greenrooms, the workers in their damp layers, the underwalk in the sublevels, the rills humming their constant note, the towers cycling their constant pattern, the crust slowly degrading and slowly being repaired, the whole vast, wet, dim, green, quiet machine doing what it does, being what it is, persisting.

The Condensaries are alive in the way that a very old machine is alive — not sentient, not aware, not intentional, but *complex*, complex enough that the behavior of the whole cannot be predicted from the behavior of the parts, complex enough that emergent properties arise that were not designed, complex enough that the system has developed qualities that are not functional but are real. The moss is real. The weepers are real. The greenrooms are real. The underwalk is real, or real enough, or real in the way that things are real when they are heard but not seen, written but not spoken, noted but not investigated. The Condensaries have grown past their function. They are still functional — they still extract, they still produce, they still ship product through the gland locks to the outside world — but they are also *themselves*, a place with a character, a place with qualities that are not in the original specifications, a place that has, over three centuries of constant operation in a closed atmosphere with a resident population of humans and moss and ferns and insects and whatever is in the sublevels, become something that was not built but has emerged.

What lives in the Condensaries is the Condensaries. The workers live there, and the moss lives there, and the ferns and the insects and the underwalk and the water and the rills and the towers and the weepers and the crust, all of it, all of them, together, in the dim, in the wet, in the quiet, in the place that was built to do a thing and has become, over centuries of doing that thing, a thing in itself. The place lives. It breathes. It persists. It is not aware that it persists. It does not need to be. It persists the way a river persists, by flowing, by continuing, by being the thing that it is in the place that it is, and the thing that it is, is the Condensaries of Vesh-Dravon, and the Condensaries of Vesh-Dravon are alive, and the life that is in them is not any single life but the sum of all the lives that are there, the human and the botanical and the mechanical and the mineral and the rumored, all of them together, all of them at once, all of them in the mist.
