# What Lives in the Rootworks

---

## The Rootwrights

They are not the first people here, and they know it. The gutters were worn smooth before their great-grandmothers were born. The Borer queen's chamber was excavated by hands they never met, for a queen they never chose, and the queen they tend now is that queen's daughter's daughter — they think. The records are good but not that good. There are gaps in the lineage that correspond to gaps in the town's memory, and those gaps correspond to the interregnums, the bad years, the times no one wrote anything down because everyone was busy staying alive.

The rootwrights call themselves rootwrights because someone else called them that first. The name stuck the way names stick in places where the thing named is too important to leave untitled. There are eleven of them at any given time — not by rule but by the practical limits of the chamber nearest the queen, which has eleven alcoves and no room for more. They sleep in shifts. Two are always awake. This is not paranoia; it is animal husbandry. The queen is an animal, and she is also the town's water supply and ventilation system and the reason the peat floor doesn't flood, and if she dies in the night someone needs to be there to begin the work of keeping her successor alive through the first fragile months.

The eldest rootwright is named Anner. She is fifty-three, which is old for the Hollow, and her hands are stained to the wrists with sap and Borer-milk and the black residue of the poultices she packs into the queen's abrasions. She has been a rootwright for thirty years. She was not chosen; she apprenticed, the way someone apprentices to a clockmaker, except that the clock is alive and the clock can kill you if you forget what it is.

Anner's mother was a rootwright. Her mother's mother was not. Her mother's mother was a gutter-woman, one of the children who walked the sap-gutters with scrapers, and her mother's mother's mother is the reason the sealed column at the north end exists — not the person who broke the rule, but the person who loved the person who broke the rule, and who is said to have stood at the barrier they built afterward and said nothing for three days and then went back to work. Anner tells this story differently than the town tells it. The town tells it as a warning. Anner tells it as a genealogy.

The rootwrights argue. This is the thing that is most audible across the Hollow — not the queen, who is nearly silent, but the rootwrights, who are not. Their arguments are technical and specific and heated to the point that visitors sometimes think they're fighting. They are not fighting. They are doing what engineers do when the stakes are high and the data is incomplete and the machine they're responsible for is made of meat. Anner argues with Tessen, who is thirty-eight and the queen's favorite handler, meaning the queen tolerates his hands on her hide with less lateral shifting than she tolerates anyone else's. Tessen argues that the queen's egg-laying has slowed in the past four hundred breaths, which means either she's settling into a new cycle or she's beginning to decline, and the difference matters because a decline means they need to begin stimulating a replacement egg-cluster within sixty cycles or risk an interregnum. Anner argues that the egg-laying hasn't slowed; the counting has gotten imprecise, because Breya, who keeps the count, has been distracted by her daughter's cough, which is the mold-cough that's been moving through the south columns, and Anner is arguing this not because she has evidence but because she has known Breya for twenty-five years and knows the quality of her counting and does not believe it fails because of a cough.

They argue like this for hours. The arguments are the sound of the town's engine being maintained. Outsiders hear contention. The town hears diligence.

## Breya, Who Counts

Breya is forty-one and has been counting the queen's breaths since she was nineteen. This is her job, and it is not a job in the way that most jobs are jobs; it is a vocation in the original sense — a calling, a thing she was called to, and she was called to it because she has a particular quality of attention that the previous breath-counter recognized in her when she was a child sitting in the rootwrights' stairwell, watching the lamp-oil settle after a rain, and counting the rings the light made on the ceiling without being told to.

She counts breaths. The queen's breaths, specifically — the eleven-minute cycles of airflow that ventilate the Hollow, that the town keeps time by. Each breath has a shape: the air rises warm from the deep tunnels, holds for a count that varies between forty and sixty depending on the queen's position and the season, and then sinks as she contracts and the lower vents open. Breya counts the shape. She has counted thirty thousand of them. She keeps the records in a wax-sealed chest in the rootwrights' column, on bark pages that she inscribes with a charcoal stylus that needs replacing every four hundred breaths, which she knows because she has measured its wear.

Her daughter, Lem, is nine, and has the mold-cough. The mold-cough is not lethal; it is the town's equivalent of a winter cold, something that passes through the children every few years and lingers for weeks and produces a sound that the Hollow has absorbed into its ambient register the way it has absorbed everything else. But it has distracted Breya, and Anner has noticed, and Tessen has noticed, and the argument about the egg-laying is partly about the egg-laying and partly about whether Breya should rest, which Breya will not do, because rest is a concept that requires a substitute counter and Breya has not trained one because she has been too busy counting.

Lem sits with her mother during the counting. She is learning the shapes of the breaths, though she doesn't know that's what she's doing. She thinks she's sitting with her mother because her mother is working and there's nowhere else to be. She is wrong. She is apprenticing.

## The Children Who Walk the Gutters

There are, at any given time, between fifteen and twenty children whose chore is the gutters. They range in age from six to twelve, and their work is this: walk the sap-gutters with flat-edged scrapers, clear the beetle-jams where Grinders have crowded at a narrow point or piled up behind a leaf-blockage, and keep the water moving toward the cisterns when the twice-daily rain comes.

They are children. They do this work because someone has to and they are the right size for it — small enough to walk the gutters without crowding them, old enough to follow the channels without getting lost, young enough that the Grinders don't process them, which is a joke the town makes and the children don't understand and the parents make anyway because the alternative to joking about it is something else.

A child named Osh is eight and has been walking the gutters since he was six, which is normal. He knows the gutter-map of the Hollow the way a street-child above knows the alleys — not as a diagram but as a sequence of turns, a path his body remembers, a route that exists in his feet and his hands and the particular way he leans when the gutter curves around the third column from the stairwell. He has a scraper his mother made for him from a Borer-shell segment, and it fits his hand the way a good tool fits a hand that has used it for two years, which is to say it fits like a part of him.

Osh has a friend named Teit who is seven and who is new to the gutters, meaning she started last season, and who is still at the stage where the Grinders frighten her. Not the Grinders themselves — the processions. The way the clicking swells when a procession rounds a corner toward you and you can feel the vibration through the floor of the gutter and the beetles come on like a tide. Osh remembers being frightened. He doesn't remember when he stopped. He tells Teit that you get used to it, which is true and also not the most useful thing he could tell her, which would be: you learn to feel the difference between a procession that's moving with purpose and a procession that's been redirected, because the freight-lines shift, and a redirected procession is the one that will walk over you if you're standing in its new path.

Teit will learn this. All the children learn it. The ones who don't learn it are the ones the town doesn't talk about, which is a small number, which is a number larger than zero, which is the body count behind the second rule.

## The House-Adjusters

The buildings settle. This is the fundamental fact of architecture in the Hollow, and it is the reason the house-adjusters exist. A house in the Hollow is built on a living column, and the column is growing — thickening, pushing outward, shifting its grain as the root swells with the seasons and the sap-rises and the rain softens the bark — and the house, which is dead wood and peg and lashing, does not grow. The house sits on the column like a hat on a growing head, and the hat must be adjusted, or it will crack, or it will tilt, or the door will seize, or the floor will separate from the wall and the whole structure will groan and sway and eventually fail.

The house-adjusters are four men and one woman, and their trade is this: they shim. They re-peg. They cut new notches in the columns where the old notches have been pushed out of shape. They hang new doors where the old doors no longer close. They tighten lashings that have loosened with the wood's expansion. They are, in the language of the world above, carpenters, except that their work is more like osteopathy — they are adjusting a structure to a body that is still changing, and the body does not consult them about its changes, and the changes do not stop.

The eldest house-adjuster is named Felle. He is forty-four and has been adjusting houses for twenty years, and his particular skill is that he can hear a house that needs adjusting before anyone who lives in it knows. He walks the town's walkways in the early breaths of the morning, before the lamps are fully lit, and he listens to the buildings the way a farrier listens to a horse's gait — for the catch, the creak, the particular sound a stressed joint makes when it's bearing weight unevenly. He has adjusted every building in the Hollow at least three times. He has adjusted his own house eleven times. His wife says this is because he can't leave work at work, which is a joke, and also true, and also the reason their door hangs perfectly.

Felle's apprentice is named Kir, who is sixteen and who came to the trade because he is good with his hands and bad with the beetles, which is a combination that limits a person's options in the Hollow. Kir is learning the sounds. He is not good at them yet. He can hear the big complaints — a cracked lintel, a separated floor — but he cannot yet hear the subtler ones, the way a lashing has loosened by a quarter-turn, the way a peg is beginning to push out. Felle teaches him by making him stand in a house and close his eyes and listen, and then describe what he hears, and then describe what he thinks the house wants, and Kir finds this frustrating because houses don't want things, and Felle finds this patient because houses do want things, and the difference between them is twenty years of standing in living rooms and hearing them speak.

## The Renderers

There is a building near the south end of the Hollow, built into a column that leans slightly away from the queen's chamber, where the Whirrs are rendered. This is the town's only industry that produces something the town exports, if export is the word for sending a thing up the stairwell to be traded above for things the Hollow cannot make: salt, metal, cloth that hasn't been oiled against mold, books. The renderers produce Whirr-oil, which is the Hollow's lamp fuel and its waterproofing and its trade good, and the rendering house is the loudest building in a loud place, which is saying something.

The renderers are six people, and they work in shifts, and their work is this: they harvest the Whirr-clouds. This is done with sap-baited nets hung in the migration paths between columns — the Whirrs follow the warmth of the lamps, and the renderers know the paths the way a fisherman knows currents, and they hang their nets where the clouds are thickest. The nets are oiled cloth, and the Whirrs collect in them by the thousand, and the rendering itself is a pressing: the Whirrs are pressed in a screw-press that one of the renderers maintains the way the rootwrights maintain the queen, with a devotion that is partly mechanical and partly devotional.

The press produces oil. It also produces a cake — a pressed flat disc of Whirr-bodies and chitin and the faintly phosphorescent powder that clings to the Whirrs' wings, which is what makes the oil burn with a clean amber light. The cake is fed to the Grinders, who process it into the peat, which feeds the roots, which feeds the trees, which is the loop, closed and humming.

The chief renderer is named Iytt. She is thirty-six and has been rendering since she was twenty and has a particular smell that she no longer notices and that her children notice and her husband pretends not to. The smell is not unpleasant — it is the smell of the Hollow concentrated and intensified, the yeast and the frying-insect and the amber and the warm and the something else, the something that is the Whirrs themselves, their particular biology expressed as an odor that gets into cloth and hair and the grain of the skin. Iytt washes in the cistern water every day. She still smells like the rendering house. Her children smell faintly like the rendering house. Her husband, who is a house-adjuster and works with living wood and smells like sap and peat, says that their household smells like the Hollow's whole economy and that this is either a complaint or a love letter.

Iytt's daughter, Sass, is twelve and beginning to learn the rendering. She is learning the nets first, which is the part of the work that requires patience and knowledge of the Whirrs' patterns, and she has not yet begun learning the press, which is the part that requires strength and a willingness to stand in a room that is the loudest room in a loud town, because the press groans and the Whirrs crackle and the oil hisses when it runs, and the rendering house is a place where the noise is so total that it becomes a kind of silence, which is the opposite of the Hollow's rule about silence and which Sass finds confusing and which Iytt finds restful.

## The Queen

She is not a person. She is a Borer — *the* Borer, the one whose body is the engine, whose tunnels are the town's lungs and veins, whose breath is the clock. She is thirty feet long and pale as the inside of a wrist and segmented and blind and patient as geology, and she does not know the town exists. This is important. The rootwrights know it is important. The town knows it is important. The queen does not know anything except temperature and humidity and the pressure of her own body in the chamber that the town excavated for her, and the presence or absence of the particular mites that live in her tunnels and clean her skin and which the rootwrights cultivate in shallow trays near her anterior end.

She lays eggs. This is her function, and it is continuous, and it is the reason the town has air and water and the capillary transport of sap up from the deep root-wells. Her eggs become the Borers that tunnel through the root-mass, and the tunnels ventilate the Hollow, and the ventilation is the breath, and the breath is time, and time is the thing the town runs on.

When she shifts her bulk — which she does rarely, perhaps once a season, perhaps less — the ground shivers and the peat floor compresses in a slow wave that travels from her chamber outward, and every conversation in the Hollow pauses. Not because of a rule. Because of the sound. Because the sound is the sound of the thing that keeps them alive reminding them that it is alive and that its aliveness is not their aliveness and that its patience is not their patience and that they are, in the end, living in the house of something that does not know they are there.

Anner says the queen is an animal. Tessen says the queen is an animal. This is not a point of argument between them. They agree on it. They agree on it the way you agree on the weather — it is not a question of belief; it is a question of what is true, and what is true is that the queen is an animal and they are her keepers and she does not know it and the not-knowing is the most important thing about her.

The last interregnum was fourteen years ago. The queen before this one died of a blockage in her lower intestine, which is the kind of thing that happens to an animal that is thirty feet long and eats continuously and lives in a chamber that is, however well-maintained, a chamber in a root-mass in a cave in a tree. The blockage was not detected because the signs were subtle and the rootwrights at the time were young and the eldest among them was not yet thirty, and the queen died on the night of the day that Tessen was born, which is a coincidence that the town has not assigned meaning to because the town does not assign meaning to coincidences; it assigns meaning to survival.

The successor was already in the tunnels — a juvenile Borer, not yet a queen, that the rootwrights found in a lateral runner near the south wall and relocated to the chamber. She was six feet long at the time. She is thirty feet long now. She will be longer. The rootwrights don't know how long, because there is no one to ask and the records from before the last interregnum are incomplete because the interregnum was bad and no one was keeping records, which is what an interregnum is: a time when the counting stops and the breaths become irregular and the town holds its breath because the thing that breathes for it is not breathing.

## The Predators

The floor hunters are real and the town does not like to talk about them and the town talks about them constantly, which is the way the town handles everything that is dangerous: by making it ordinary.

They are flat. They are armored. They are eyeless. They live in the deep peat and they surface rarely and they eat the Grinders, mostly, and they eat anything else that is on the floor when they surface, which is why the people build upward and the floor is a road and the rule about not standing in a groove exists. They are the size of a dinner plate. They are the size of a door. They vary. The town's few weapons are kept for them — spears with Borer-chitin points, oiled and sealed and stored in the rootwrights' column, taken out only when a procession stops clicking, which means a hunter is up, which means someone has to go down and drive it back into the peat.

This happens perhaps twice a year. It has happened eleven times in Anner's life. She has never gone down. The people who go down are young and fast and have done it before, and they go in pairs, and they carry the spears and they carry torches of Whirr-oil soaked rag on poles, and they make noise, because the hunters are blind and navigate by vibration and the noise of a torch-waving spear-carrying human is a vibration that says *not a Grinder, not food, not worth it*, and the hunters sink back into the peat, and the town goes back to what it was doing, and the procession resumes its clicking, and the silence that was the alarm ends, and the ordinary noise — the noise that is safety, the noise that is home — comes back.

A woman named Hen was killed by a hunter when Anner was twenty. Hen was crossing the floor between processions and she tripped — on what, no one knows, a root-knot, a groove-edge, her own foot, it doesn't matter — and she fell and she was on the peat and the silence came, which is how they knew, and by the time anyone reached the floor she was gone, which means the hunter had her, which means the hunter had pulled her into the peat, which is what the hunters do, which is why the town builds upward.

Hen had a daughter who was four. The daughter is thirty-four now. Her name is Maret and she is a gutter-woman, which is the adult version of the gutter-child's work — she supervises the gutters, the cleaning and the maintenance and the children, and she does this work because her mother died on the floor and the gutters are the floor's edge and the floor's edge is the place where the floor is most dangerous and most knowable, and Maret has decided to live there, which is her way of being in the world, which is a way of being in the world that the Hollow understands and does not judge.

## The Visitors

They come down the stairwell sometimes. Not often. The Hollow is not a place that welcomes visitors, not because the people are unwelcoming but because the place itself is not welcoming — the light, the noise, the rain, the breath, the floor that is a road, the silence that is an alarm. A visitor arrives and the first thing they do is stand at the mouth of the stairwell and stare, and the town watches them stare, and the town knows what they are seeing, which is: a cathedral in a root-mass, a hundred thousand beetles, a town stacked like bracket fungi on living columns, an amber light that makes everything look like a memory of itself.

The visitors stay a day, usually. They stay because they cannot leave, because the stairwell is behind them and the Hollow is in front of them and the noise is too loud for thinking and the breath is too strange for clocks and the food — Grinder-meat and peat-fungus and the faintly sweet water from the cisterns — is too foreign for palates that have eaten above-ground food their whole lives.

They leave and they do not come back, usually. Sometimes they come back and when they come back they come back for good, which has happened nine times in the town's memory, and each of those nine people is now a person of the Hollow, which means they count breaths and they walk between processions and they know the smell of the rain and they know the sound of the queen shifting and they know what it means when the clicking stops, and they are, in every way that matters, home.

## What Lives Here

What lives here is a town of two hundred people in a cathedral in a root in a tree, and a hundred thousand beetles, and a million Whirrs, and a queen who does not know they exist, and the predators that keep them honest, and the mold that keeps them oiled, and the rain that keeps them watered, and the breath that keeps them in time, and the loop, closed and humming, that keeps them alive.

What lives here is a place that predates its people by whole ages and tolerates them and employs them and is employed by them and holds them the way a fist holds a seed — completely, and without malice, and for reasons of its own.

What lives here is alive, all of it, always, and what lives here is fine.
