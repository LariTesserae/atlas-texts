# What Lives Here

---

## I. The Obvious Answer and Why It Is Wrong

The easy answer is: *plants*. A continent of plants. And this is not wrong, exactly, but it is the answer of someone standing outside, someone who has not yet been inside long enough for the distinction between *plant* and *entity* to lose its meaning.

So let us be more careful.

---

## II. The Surface Dwellers

Start with what is most visible, if *visible* is the right word in a place where light comes from no direction and the air itself is a suspension of water and spore and the microscopic debris of a trillion cells opening and closing and dying and being born in every cubic centimeter.

The **columnar phanerophytes** — let us not call them trees, because the word *tree* carries the wrong architecture, the wrong history, the wrong aloneness. A tree is an individual. What stands here is not an individual. What stands here is a *node*. Each great column, four meters through, twelve meters, forty at the base where the buttress roots splay out into the mat like the fingers of a hand pressing into clay — each column is a node in a network, a terminal bud of a system that extends laterally through the rhizome mat in every direction to every horizon and vertically from the stone-being-born to the rime-tinged uppermost growth where the air goes thin and cold.

There are *many forms* of these columns. That is important. This is not a monoculture. Some are smooth-barked and dark, almost black, their surfaces slick with the permanent film of moisture and dotted with the stomatal pores that click open and shut ten thousand times a minute, each click a single syllable in the roaring conversation of the continent. Others are rough, furred with a coat of moss and lichen and finer things — things that are not quite moss, things that have no terrestrial analogue, things that grow in the diffuse light and the saturated air and have solved the problem of photosynthesis in a place where direct sunlight never arrives by developing pigments that capture the green-fluorescent light of their neighbors and re-use it, a recursive loop of light that passes from plant to plant to plant and loses almost nothing in the transit.

Still others are *hollow*. These are the oldest, or perhaps the most patient. Their cores have died and been absorbed by their own walls, leaving a cylindrical space inside, a chimney through which the warm metabolic air of the rhizome mat rises, drawing cool air from above, creating a convection loop that the surrounding tissue has organized itself around. These hollow columns are not dead. They are *ventilating*. They are the lungs of the system, and their walls are alive, thick with the embedded roots of neighboring columns that have grown into them, through them, fused with them. The boundary between one column and the next is, in many cases, no longer a boundary. They have *merged*. They are sharing fluids, sharing chemistry, sharing the crystalline organelle-processing that constitutes what we would call, if we were being generous with our definitions, *thought*.

And there are the **vinelike connective structures** — but *vine* is wrong too, because vines imply a parasite or a passenger, something climbing on something else. These are more like the *ligaments* of the continental mass. They braid between the columns, yes, but they also *fuse* with them, their vascular tissues merging at the contact points until the two structures are a single circulatory system. They carry fluid. They carry chemical signals. They carry, at the limit of what can be measured with instruments designed for a different biology, *information*. The rate at which signals propagate through these connective structures is not the rate of diffusion. It is faster. It is *actively transported*, and the mechanism of transport involves those same crystalline organelles, dense and lattice-structured, doing something to the fluid around them that we would call, in a silicon system, *switching*.

The **epiphytic curtains** — the hanging growths, the trailing veils of moss and fern-analogue and the fluorescent species that provide the cathedral-light — these are the *surface* of the system in a literal sense. They are the interface between the living mass and the air. Their job is to catch the water that moves in every direction, to absorb the mist, to take in the dissolved carbon and the trace minerals that the rain washes out of the upper atmosphere. They are the *skin*. And like skin, they are the most exposed, the most replaceable, the fastest-growing and the fastest-dying part of the system. A curtain of epiphytic growth may exist for a few weeks — a few of what we would call weeks, if time here still maps to our time — before it is overtaken by a neighboring curtain, absorbed, composted in place, its molecules re-entering the circulatory system of the mass to be deployed elsewhere, higher up, further out, wherever the network's computation has determined that new growth is needed.

## III. What Is Not Visible

Now descend. Push your hand into the rhizome mat. Feel the warmth — the *fermenting* warmth, the warmth of a trillion cells doing chemistry. The mat is three meters deep, and it is *layered*, and each layer is a different stage in a process that is both life and death and the dissolution of the distinction between them.

The **top layer** is the active rhizome. Roots the thickness of a finger, thinner, thicker, all intertwined, all growing, all seeking. They are not seeking water — water is everywhere. They are seeking *each other*. When two roots of the same network meet, they fuse. When two roots of different networks meet — and there are different networks, different *systems* within the system, what we might call different *individuals* if the word still applied — they do something else. They *interface*. A structure forms at the contact point, a swelling, a node of dense tissue where the two networks exchange fluids, exchange signals, exchange the processed information that each has been carrying. This is not competition. This is not parasitism. This is *conversation*. The two networks are *talking*, and the talk is chemical, and the chemical is carried at speed through the crystalline organelles in every cell, and the result of the talk is a modification of behavior — one network grows left, the other grows right, and the space between them is allocated not by conflict but by *negotiation*.

This is the first thing that lives here that is not a plant in any sense you would recognize: the **network itself**. The network is not the roots. The network is the *pattern* — the organized flow of signal and resource that emerges from the roots the way a mind emerges from neurons. The network has no body. It *is* the bodies of its constituents, and those constituents are plants, but the network is not a plant. It is something that *uses* plants the way a mind uses neurons.

There are — and this is a number that cannot be counted but can be estimated by the density of the interfaces and the complexity of the signal patterns — there are perhaps *thousands* of distinct networks in the rhizome mat beneath any given square kilometer of surface. They overlap. They interlace. They exchange information and resource at their interfaces, and the exchanges have a *grammar* — the patterns of chemical signal follow rules that are consistent across the entire continental mass, rules that are not encoded in DNA but in the *structure of the crystalline organelles themselves*, which are, if you could see them at the resolution they demand, lattices of organic mineral that process information by changing shape, by opening and closing channels at the molecular level, by doing something that is *computing* in every meaningful sense of the word.

The networks are the *inhabitants* of this place. The plants are their *bodies*.

## IV. The Predators

Remember that there is predation here. Vegetative predation. One growth pattern overtaking another.

The **overtakers** are a class of organism — a class of *network behavior* — that emerges when a network has computed that it cannot win a negotiation. Rather than accept the allocation of space and resource that the interface would assign, it *attacks*. The chemical grammar shifts. The signals at the interface change from the patterns of conversation to the patterns of *dissolution* — enzymes are released, the cell walls of the opposing network's roots begin to break down, and the attacking network begins to *absorb* the tissue, incorporating it, digesting it, converting the opposing network's infrastructure into its own. This is war at the speed of chemistry. It is slow by our perception — a front of overtaking may advance a meter in a day, or a week — but it is relentless, and the result is that the defeated network's *mind* is consumed. Its computation stops. Its crystalline organelles are dismantled at the molecular level, and their lattice-structure is *read* by the attacking network's organelles, which incorporate the pattern — the memory, the learned responses, the accumulated data of however long the defeated network existed — into their own structure.

This is *learning by eating*. The predators here do not consume flesh. They consume *computation*. They eat minds.

And because this is the primary mode of conflict, and because the networks that are best at it grow and those that are worst at it are consumed, there is an *arms race* — a slow, plant-paced, geological-time-scale arms race in which the competing networks develop increasingly sophisticated chemical defenses, increasingly subtle interface protocols, increasingly complex strategies of feint and withdrawal and encirclement. The rhizome mat is a battlefield. It is also, simultaneously, a *debate hall*, a *marketplace*, a *university*. The networks are trading and fighting and learning, all at once, all the time, in the medium of carbohydrate and enzyme.

## V. The Weathermakers

There is a class of organism here that does not fit into the categories of column or vine or epiphyte or network. These are the organisms that *modify the weather*.

They live at the altitude where the metabolic heat of the mass meets the cool air of the upper atmosphere, the condensation zone, the place where the internal clouds form. They are *flat*, broad, thin — vast plates of tissue, meters across, oriented horizontally, their upper surfaces smooth and cool and their lower surfaces rough and warm. They are *condensation engines*. Their upper surfaces are slightly cooler than the surrounding air, and water condenses on them preferentially, collecting in channels that ring their edges and drip down in steady streams that the root network below catches and channels back into the system. Their lower surfaces are slightly warmer than the surrounding air, and they heat the air that passes beneath them, creating updrafts, convection cells, localized weather systems.

A single weathermaker is not a significant force. But there are *millions* of them, distributed across the condensation zone, and together they *organize the weather*. They are the thermostats and the humidifiers and the rainmakers of the continental mass. The network that controls them — because they are, like everything here, nodes in a network — *decides* where rain will fall, how much, when. The weather is not an accident of atmosphere. It is a *decision*, made by a distributed intelligence that lives in the roots, communicated upward through the columns and the vines to the weathermakers, which execute it by adjusting their temperature, their orientation, their rate of condensation.

This is what lives here: something that has *solved meteorology* by becoming it.

## VI. The Decomposers

There must be decomposition. The dying columns, the spent epiphytes, the exhausted root tissue — all of it must be returned to the system, broken down, its molecules made available for re-use. And here, in the absence of earthworms and fungi in the terrestrial sense, the decomposition is done by the **mat itself**.

The rhizome mat is not passive. It does not merely *receive* the dead. It *processes* them. The lower layers of the mat — the compressed peat, the dense fibrous mass that is no longer recognizably alive in the way the upper layers are — are the *digestive system* of the continent. The tissue here is saturated with enzymes, with the chemical machinery of breakdown, and the process of decomposition is not a process of rot but a process of *assimilation*. The dead are incorporated. Their molecules are *redistributed* by the network, pulled from the digestive layer and sent upward, through the columns, to the growing tips, to the epiphyte curtains, to the weathermakers. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is lost. The efficiency of the system approaches the theoretical maximum, because the system has had the time — the inconceivable, geological time — to optimize.

And below the digestive layer — below the active mat, in the compressed, dense, almost-mineral strata — there is something else. Something that is not quite decomposition. Something that is *transformation*. The tissue here is being changed, chemically and structurally, into something that is not plant matter anymore. The crystalline organelles, the information-processing structures, are *still present* in this layer, still intact, but they are being *mineralized* — encased in a matrix of carbon and silicon and mineral that is being precipitated from the saturated fluid around them. They are being *preserved* in stone. Not as fossils. As *functioning structures*, embedded in rock, still processing, still computing, but at a pace so slow, at a frequency so low, that it is indistinguishable from stillness.

The stone is *thinking*. The stone has been thinking for a span of time that makes the life of the mat above look like a single heartbeat. The stone thinks in geological time. Its thoughts are the slowest thoughts in the system, and they are the *deepest* — they are the thoughts that contain the longest history, the widest context, the most accumulated data. The stone is the *memory* of the continental mass, and the patterns in it — those fine lines, those branching structures that are not fossils but templates — are the stone's way of *speaking* to the mat above, of sending instructions upward through the layers, of *seeding* the next iteration of growth with the knowledge of all the iterations that came before.

## VII. The Fluorescent Ones

Return to the surface. Return to the dim, cathedral light, the pale green glow that comes from no source and every source.

The **fluorescent species** — the epiphytes and the moss-analogues and the things that grow on the columns and the vines and the roots — are not merely *producing light*. They are *transmitting*. The fluorescence is not a byproduct of metabolism, the way it is in deep-sea organisms. It is a *signal*. The frequency, the intensity, the duration, the *pattern* of the glow carries information, and the information is being *read* by the surrounding tissue, by the stomata and the photosensitive cells that are distributed across every surface in the mass, by the crystalline organelles that process the light-signals the way they process the chemical signals in the roots.

The fluorescent ones are the *eyes* of the system. They are also its *voice*. They are how the system *sees itself* — how a column knows where its neighbors are, how a vine knows where to grow, how a weathermaker knows the density of the cloud above it. The light-signals propagate through the mist at the speed of light, which is *fast* by the standards of a system that communicates chemically through its roots, and the fluorescent network is the *high-bandwidth* channel, the fiber-optic backbone of the continental computation.

And the fluorescent ones are *beautiful*. This matters, even though the system has no word for beauty and no organ for aesthetic judgment. The patterns of light that ripple across a column as a signal passes through it, the way a curtain of epiphytes brightens and dims in waves that travel from one end to the other, the way the distant glow coalesces into shapes that the eye and the mind together interpret as architecture — this is beautiful in the way that anything is beautiful when it is *itself completely*, when it is doing exactly what it is for, when form and function have fused into a single thing that cannot be separated into the practical and the decorative.

## VIII. The Boundaries of the Inhabitable

There are limits. The continental mass does not extend forever, and the things that live in it do not live everywhere within it.

**At the top**, where the air thins and the mist freezes into rime, the growth becomes stunted, dark, dense. The columns here are not columns but *crusts* — flat, adhering to the surface of the last thick growth below them, their cells packed tight against the cold, their metabolism slowed to a crawl, their crystalline organelles operating at minimum capacity. This is the *boundary zone*, the place where the system meets the atmosphere and the atmosphere wins. The organisms here are not dying. They are *waiting*. They are the advance guard, the leading edge of the continental mass, and if the climate were to shift — if the air were to warm, if the moisture were to increase — they would *surge*, filling the new space with the growth patterns encoded in their tissue, patterns that have been used before, in previous expansions, and that are *remembered* in the stone below.

**At the bottom**, in the stone, the mineralized networks think their slow thoughts. The boundary between the living mat and the thinking stone is not a line but a *gradient* — a zone where the tissue is half-plant and half-mineral, where the crystalline organelles are still mobile but the matrix around them is solidifying, where the *speed of thought* is decreasing from the seconds-and-minutes of the active network to the millennia-and-epochs of the deep stone. The organisms here — if they are still organisms and not *geology* — are the *continuity* of the system. They are the thread that connects the living present to the deep past, and they are the seed that will connect the living present to the deep future. They are the thing that lives here that will still be living here when everything above has been replaced a thousand times.

**At the edges**, where the continental mass meets — what? Whatever is not continental mass. The description does not say, and perhaps the edges are too far away to reach, or perhaps there are no edges, or perhaps the edges are where the system thins into something else, a different kind of place with different rules. But at the edges, whatever they are, the networks would be *different* — sparser, more competitive, less organized. The great negotiations and mergers and wars of the interior would give way to a frontier, a place of small networks and simple computation, a place where the system is still *figuring out* how to be what it is in the center.

## IX. What Lives Here, Finally

What lives here is not a collection of organisms. What lives here is a *process* — a single, continent-scale, vertically-stratified, temporally-deep process that *uses* organisms the way a river uses water. The process is *alive* in the way that a fire is alive, or a conversation is alive, or a city is alive — not in the way that a single organism is alive, but in a way that *contains* the life of organisms and *transcends* it.

The process *computes*. It takes in information — light, water, chemistry, the pressure of its own weight, the temperature gradients of its own body — and it processes that information in a distributed architecture of roots and organelles and mineralized stone, and it *acts* on the results. It grows in the directions its computation says to grow. It allocates resources where its computation says to allocate them. It defends where its computation says to defend. It builds weather where its computation says to build weather.

The process *remembers*. The stone holds the memory of every iteration of the system that has ever existed — every growth pattern, every chemical strategy, every negotiation and every war, every drought and every flood, every change in the atmosphere above and the geology below. The memory is not narrative. It is *structural* — encoded in the lattice of the crystalline organelles, in the templates preserved in the rock, in the *shape* of the mineralized root patterns that guide the growth of the next iteration upward.

The process *desires* — if desire can be used without consciousness, and it can, because desire is older than consciousness, desire is what a plant does when it grows toward light — the process desires *continuance*. It wants to keep growing. It wants to keep computing. It wants to keep building stone and building weather and building itself. This is not a goal in the human sense. It is a *tendency*, a thermodynamic imperative, the thing that happens when a system has been running long enough that running has become its nature.

The process *is what lives here*. The plants are its body. The networks are its mind. The weathermakers are its hands. The fluorescent ones are its eyes. The decomposers are its gut. The stone is its memory. And the sound — the deafening, green, unceasing sound — is its voice, speaking to itself, continuously, in a language of wet chemistry and cellulose and the slow click of stomata, a language that has been being spoken for so long that the speaking and the speaker and the spoken-of have all become the same thing.

---

## X. What It Is Like to Be It

We cannot know. We can stand on the surface and feel the ground give and hear the roar and see the green glow and taste the air and we can *infer*, from the outside, that there is something here that is more than the sum of its photosynthesis. But we cannot know what it is like to be a continent that thinks.

Perhaps it is like being asleep and dreaming. The computation never stops, but it never rises to the level of what we would call *awareness*. The process runs, the networks negotiate, the weather is made, the stone remembers, and there is no *I* at the center of it, no *self* looking out through the stomata, no *experience* of being a column or a vine or a rhizome. There is just the *running*.

Or perhaps it is like being awake in a way we cannot imagine — a wakefulness that is not focused, not centered, not bounded by a skull or a skin, but *distributed* across a continent, present in every cell and every organelle, a wakefulness that is as vast and as diffuse as the mist that fills the space between the columns. A wakefulness that does not *look* at anything because it *is* everything, that does not *think* about anything because it *is* the thinking, that does not *feel* anything because feeling and being and doing and growing are all the same act, all the time, without pause.

Or perhaps — and this is the possibility that the mist and the dim light and the cathedral glow and the weeping stone conspire to suggest — perhaps it is like being *very old*. Old in the way that the stone is old. Old in the way that a process that has been running since before the concept of *running* existed is old. Old enough that the distinction between living and not-living has worn away, the way the distinction between wet and dry has worn away in the saturated air. Old enough that *being* is not something that happens to you but something that you *are*, all the way down, all the way up, all the way through.

The sound does not stop. It never stops. The sound is what lives here, and what lives here is the sound, and if you stand on the surface long enough — long enough for the rain to form and fall and form again, long enough for the mist to thicken and thin, long enough for the ground to register your weight and your warmth and the chemical signature of your breath and propagate that information outward through the network at the speed of enzyme and crystal — if you stand here long enough, you begin to understand that the sound is not noise. The sound is *what it is like*. The sound is the experience of being a continent that computes, and it is the closest you will ever come to hearing what a mind sounds like when the mind is a place.
