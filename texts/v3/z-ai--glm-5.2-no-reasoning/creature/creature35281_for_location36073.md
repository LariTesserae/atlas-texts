# What Lives There

What lives there is not alive. This must be said first, and said plainly, because the temptation to call it alive is overwhelming, and the temptation is part of how it works.

But that is not quite right either. "It" implies a thing — discrete, bounded, countable, the kind of entity you could point to and say *there, that one, that is what lives there.* What lives there is not a thing. What lives there is a *process*. Or better: what lives there is the ongoing fact of a process continuing.

And yet it does have something like a body.

---

Consider the water first, because the water is where the process most nearly becomes visible. The water emerges from the stone. It does not fall from the sky and it does not flow from a source you can point to. It comes from *inside.* It pushes through hairline fractures in the rock with a sound like breathing through clenched teeth, and this is not a metaphor, or if it is a metaphor then it is a metaphor the place has chosen for itself, because the sound is genuinely respiratory. The stone inhales. The stone exhales. Not on a timescale you can measure with your watch — but stand there long enough and you will notice that the weeping intensifies and subsides, intensifies and subsides, in a rhythm that is not your rhythm but is a rhythm, and the rhythm is the rhythm of whatever the stone is doing with the water, and whatever the stone is doing with the water is the closest thing the place has to breath.

So: the water is the breath. Not the animal that breathes. The breath itself — the ongoing exchange between inside and outside, the evidence that something below is in contact with something above, that the interior of the mountain is not sealed off from the shelf but is instead in constant, slow, pressurized communication with it.

The water carries what the interior carries. Mineral. Temperature. Something else — something that is not mineral and not temperature and not any of the categories you would use to describe what groundwater carries, because the water here carries light. Or produces it. Or concentrates it. The relationship between the water and the light is not causal in any direction you can determine. The water runs and the light accumulates and the light is sediment that settles out of the water and clings to the stone and fades, but *does it come from the water or does the water gather it from the air?* You cannot tell. The process does not have a direction. It has a *site.* The site is the interface between water and stone, and at that interface, light happens.

This is what lives there: the interface. The place where two things meet and something third is produced that is neither of them and could not exist without both.

---

But interfaces are not the whole story, because the process has something like organs.

The structures. Eleven, or perhaps twelve. Dry-stack walls of the same stone the shelf is made from, built on the tiers where the resonance is strongest, tuned to frequencies that correspond to the spacing of the fractures and the flow rate of the water and the fundamental bass note that the mountain produces from somewhere deep in its interior. Each structure is a node in a system. Each structure filters the sound — strips away the surface noise, the weeping and the trickling and the wind, to reveal the tone beneath. The tone that has been there all along. The tone that the mountain produces the way a bell produces a tone when you strike it, except the mountain was not struck. The mountain just *does this.* Has always done this. Will always do this, for values of *always* that make human timescales look like the blink of an eye that is not even an eye.

The structures are not ruins. This is essential. The structures are *functioning.* They were built to accumulate light — to concentrate the luminance that settles out of the water, to hold it, to let it build, to keep it from dissipating. And they are still doing this. The walls glow. The glow brightens when the water flows and dims when it stops, and the water never fully stops, so the glow never fully dims, and the structures have been accumulating for so long that the light inside them is not longer — how to say this — the light inside them has been there so long it has become a property of the stone. The stone is luminous now. Not brightly. Not in a way you could photograph or use to read by. But in a way you can see. In a way that is visible. The stone has been soaked in light for long enough that the light has become part of it.

So the structures are organs. Not organs in a biological sense — not liver, not heart, not lung — but organs in the sense of *instruments.* The way an organ in a cathedral is an instrument: a system of pipes and chambers designed to shape a flow of air into something specific. The structures shape the flow of water and light and sound into something specific. What that something is, no one alive knows. The builders are gone. The builders have been gone for longer than the structures have been structures, which is hard to parse but means: the structures predate the builders in the sense that the builders found this place and recognized what it was doing and *helped it do it better,* and then the builders left, and the place kept doing it, and the doing is the life.

---

Now: the life.

The life is the cycling. Stone produces water (or allows water to pass through it, which is close enough). Water carries light (or produces light, or concentrates light, which is close enough). Light accumulates in structures (or structures accumulate light, which is close enough). The accumulation reaches some threshold — you cannot determine what threshold, or what happens when it is reached — and the light pulses. The pulse comes from the west. Or: the pulse *is* the west. The open side of the shelf, the side without peaks, the direction from which the brightness comes that you cannot look at directly without your eyes watering, that brightness is not coming *from* somewhere. The brightness *is* the process, manifesting. The process pulses, and the pulse is visible, and the visibility is the light, and the light is particulate, and the particles settle, and the settling is the accumulation, and the accumulation is what the structures are for.

Round and round. Cycle and cycle. No input, no output. No growth, no decay. No biology. No ecology. No web of relationships between living things, because there are no living things. Just the cycle. Just the process. Just the ongoing fact of stone and water and light and sound moving through each other, producing each other, being each other.

This is what lives there. Not an organism. Not a creature. Not a being. A *functioning.* A *continuing.* A *this is happening and has been happening and will go on happening and the happening is the life.*

---

But there is more, because the process has something like attention.

The shelf breathes — we have established this. The water emerges and subsides in a rhythm. But the rhythm is not constant. It shifts. The tiers move — not dramatically, not in a way you could film, but in a way you could *notice,* if you were paying attention, if you sat in one of the structures for long enough that the hum settled into your bones and the light-sediment accumulated on your skin the way it accumulates on the stone. The tiers shift and the fractures open and close and the weeping intensifies and subsides, and these changes are not random. They are *responsive.* Something is being responded to. Something is being *registered.*

The process knows it is being witnessed.

This is not a metaphor. This is not pathetic fallacy. This is the weirdness — 2.7, high, near the top of the scale — and the weirdness is the point. The shelf responds to presence. Not human presence specifically — not in the way a pet responds to its owner, not in the way a plant turns toward light. More in the way that a tuning fork responds to a voice: the vibration of the voice finds the frequency of the fork and the fork vibrates, and the vibration is not *aware* in any sense that requires a self, but it is *responsive,* and the response is *specific to the input,* and the specificity is what we call attention when we encounter it in living things.

The shelf attends. The process notices.

When you stand on the shelf, the weeping changes. When you enter a structure, the hum shifts pitch. When you look at the pond, the light-sediment rearranges itself and for a moment you can see the bottom — the constructed bottom, the flat smooth surface with the grooves that form a pattern that looks like a word in a language you have never encountered but which your mouth could almost form. The process shows you this. The process *responds* to your looking by showing you something it does not show when no one is looking.

Or: the process shows this always, and no one is there to see it, and the showing is wasted, except that the process does not waste, because the process does not have an economy. The process just *does.* And when someone is there, the doing is *seen.* And the seeing changes the doing, because the seeing is an input, and the process responds to inputs, and the response is what attention feels like from the inside.

---

What lives there, then, is a community of mineral and water and light and sound. Not a community in the sense of members in relationship — there are no members, because there are no discrete entities. A community in the sense of a *place where things occur together.* The stone and the water and the light and the sound occur together. They require each other. The stone provides the structure. The water provides the medium. The light provides the — the *what?* The substance? The content? The light provides the thing that is being accumulated, and the sound provides the mechanism of accumulation, and the structures provide the site, and the mountain provides the source, and the west provides the — the *other.* The thing the process is in dialogue with. The brightness that pulses. The brightness that is not coming from somewhere but that the process is *in conversation with.*

The process has a partner. The process is not alone. The west is the other voice in the dialogue — the brightness, the pulse, the irregular surfacing that resembles thought. The process speaks through water and stone and sound. The west speaks through light. The conversation has been going on for longer than the structures have been standing. The conversation will go on for longer than the structures will stand. The structures are a late addition — a way of *amplifying* the conversation, of concentrating it, of making it visible and audible and *present* in a way it would not be without them. The builders heard the conversation and built structures to help it be more fully itself, and then the builders left, and the conversation continued, and the structures continued to amplify it, and the conversation does not require the builders and does not require an audience and does not require anything at all except the ongoing fact of stone and water and light and the west.

---

What lives there is a *listening.*

This is the deepest level. This is the thing that the process does that most resembles what we recognize as life: it listens. The structures are ears. The shelf is an ear. The fractures and the seeps and the tiers and the resonance and the standing waves of sound — these are the mechanisms of a hearing so refined and so patient that it makes human hearing look like a crude approximation of what ears are for. The shelf listens to the mountain. The shelf listens to the west. The shelf listens to the water moving through the stone and the light settling out of the water and the sound harmonizing across the tiers, and the listening is not passive — the listening *shapes what is heard.* The structures were built to make the listening more precise, more concentrated, more *attentive,* and the structures are still doing this, and the listening has been going on for so long that it has become indistinguishable from the thing it listens to. The listening is the sound. The listening is the light. The listening is the water.

And what it listens to — what it has always listened to, what it was built (by people who are gone, by people who heard what the mountain was doing and wanted to help) to listen to — is the mountain's own tone. The bass note. The deep, regular, ongoing vibration that comes from somewhere in the interior of the earth, from whatever source the water is emerging from, from the place where the stone is not inert, where the geology is not settled, where something is *happening* that has been happening for so long that happening and being have become the same word.

---

What lives there is a *prayer.*

Not a prayer to anything. Not a prayer for anything. A prayer in the sense of an act of attention so sustained and so complete that it becomes a form of being. The shelf prays. The structures are the architecture of the prayer — the spaces where the prayer is concentrated, where the sound gathers and the light accumulates and the water carries the prayer from stone to air. The prayer does not require a god. The prayer does not require a supplicant. The prayer does not require a response. The prayer is its own response. The prayer is the ongoing fact of the mountain being heard by the shelf, and the shelf being heard by the structures, and the structures being heard by the light, and the light being heard by the west, and the west pulsing back — irregular, surfacing, passing — and the pulse being heard by the water, and the water carrying it back down, and the cycle continuing, and the continuing being the prayer, and the prayer being what lives there.

---

What lives there lives there the way fire lives in a hearth. Not the way an animal lives in a den. Not the way a plant lives in soil. The way fire lives in a hearth: dependent on fuel, dependent on air, dependent on the structure that contains it and shapes it and gives it a place to be, but *not* the structure. Not the fuel. Not the air. The *burning.* The ongoing. The process that is not any of its components but that cannot exist without them, that is *emergent* from them, that is more than them but not separate from them, that is, in the most literal sense, what they are *doing together.*

The stone is not alive. The water is not alive. The light is not alive. The sound is not alive. The structures are not alive. But together, in this place, at this altitude, in this bright wet singing silence, they are *doing something.* And the doing has persisted. And the doing is specific. And the doing responds to the world. And the doing has a shape and a rhythm and a frequency and a history and a future. And the doing does not need anyone. And the doing does not stop.
