# What Lives in the Coil

They do not have a name. They do not need one. Names are for things that exist in relation to other things, and these have existed in relation only to stone and to each other and to the slow downward pull of their own weight for longer than the mountain has had a shape worth naming.

But something lives there. Many somethings. A continuum of somethings, if that is the right word for beings whose edges are less defined than they appear.

---

Start with the body.

They are long. Longer than they first seem, because in the lower terraces where the largest ones cling to the spiraling wall, they fold themselves against the stone in segments that compress the body's true length into something almost compact. Each segment is distinct, separated by a flexible joint that permits both lateral and vertical flex — they can curl, they can twist, they can flatten. When one moves across the surface of the wall, the motion is not a crawl. It is a *flow*. One segment plants, the next lifts, the third plants again, and the body moves across the basalt like a pale rope pushed by an invisible hand. The legs — eight, arranged in pairs along the ventral line — are not the primary means of locomotion. The primary means is peristalsis. The body itself contracts and expands in waves, and the legs serve mainly to anchor each wave before the next begins. On a vertical surface, this means they move upward by gripping and downward by releasing, and they are nearly always moving downward.

The translucent ones are young. This is obvious once you watch them long enough — not because they grow in any way you can observe in a single visit, but because the gradient is visible along the spiral. Near the top, near the chimney, near the cold breath of the outside world, the creatures are small, pale, soft-shelled. Their cuticle is thin enough to see through. Their digestive tracts are dark threads visible through the body wall, and you can watch the slow pulse of fluid through them — not blood, not hemolymph exactly, something in between, something that carries both nutrients and the mineral slurry they extract from the stone. They are the size of a human hand at their largest on these upper terraces, and most are smaller. They are the freshest. The newest. They were born here, in the warm wet dark, and they have never known anything else, and they will never know anything else, and they do not know there is anything else to know.

They do not have eyes.

This is the thing that strikes you most once you notice it. Every surface of their heads — and they have heads, distinct from the first body segment, tapered and slightly wider, flattening at the anterior end — is smooth. No compound eyes. No ocelli. No pigmentation spots that might serve as primitive photoreceptors. The bioluminescence that fills the chamber is not for them. They did not make it for themselves. They cannot see it.

They navigate by vibration. The entire body is a sensorium. Each segment carries, along its dorsal ridge, a row of fine hairs — trichobothria, if you want the term, though the term is borrowed from spiders and these are not spiders — that are sensitive to air movement down to the displacement of a single molecule. They feel the chamber. They feel each other. They feel you, if you are present, not as a shape or a color but as a pattern of warmth and displacement in the air, a disturbance in the field. They know you are there the way a river knows a stone — by the way you interrupt the flow.

The clicking is their feet. It is also their language.

---

And here is where it becomes difficult, because the word *language* implies something these creatures do not do. They do not communicate information. They do not signal danger or food or mating readiness. They have no calls. They have no displays. What they have is *synchrony*.

Click. Click. Click. Ten thousand feet touching stone in overlapping waves. The timing is not random. Each footfall occurs within a window of the collective rhythm — the long, slow, four-or-five-minute cycle of the thrumming — and each footfall is *phase-locked* to it. A creature on the eastern wall clicks its feet a fraction of a second before a creature on the western wall. A creature one terrace down clicks a fraction after that. The pattern spirals. The pattern follows the spiral. The sound is a helix made audible, and it has been turning in this chamber for centuries, and it does not repeat, and it does not deviate.

When two creatures meet on the wall — when their paths intersect, which they do constantly, because the surface is full — they do not fight. They do not mate. They pause. Their antennae — long, fine, twice the length of the head, covered in the same molecular-scale sensory hairs as the body — sweep across each other's faces. Their mandibles open slightly. A fluid is exchanged. Not saliva. Something thinner. It passes from glands in the mandibular base to receptors on the opposing creature's mouthparts, and in that exchange, something chemical moves between them. A signal? A nutrient? A record?

You cannot know. You can only watch, and watching, you see that after the exchange, both creatures adjust. They adjust their position on the wall. They adjust the phase of their footfalls. They adjust the rhythm of their body contractions. The collective sound shifts — not much, not perceptibly, but measurably, if you had instruments, which you do not — and the whole system absorbs the change and continues.

This is how they think. Not individually. There is no individual. The creature on the wall is not a creature in the way you understand creatures. It is a *cell*. The Coil is the organism. The spiral is the body. The clicking is its neural activity. The thrumming is its heartbeat. The moisture is its blood. And the creatures are its components, its organelles, its neurons and fibroblasts and epithelial cells, arranged along a structure they built and are building and will build until the mountain is hollow and the spiral reaches whatever it is reaching for.

---

The small ones eat stone.

This is not metaphor. On the upper terraces, the young ones — the translucent, the soft, the recently born — cling to the basalt and secrete. The secretion is clear, faintly viscous, and it is *acidic*. Not strongly. Not enough to hiss or smoke. But enough. They press their mouths to the stone and release a thin film of it, and the basalt surface softens, and they ingest the softened mineral, and their digestive tracts — those dark threads visible through the cuticle — process it, extract what they need, and pass the rest. The waste is not excreted. It is *deposited*. Behind each creature, as it moves, a thin trail of processed mineral hardens on the stone. This is the mortar. This is the wall. The structure of the Coil is built from the waste of creatures eating the mountain.

As they grow — and they grow slowly, over years, over decades perhaps — they move down. The migration is not deliberate in any way you would recognize. They do not decide to descend. The collective rhythm pushes them, the way a heartbeat pushes blood. Downward. Inward. Toward the center. Each terrace holds larger ones, older ones, and their color deepens as they age. The translucence fades. The cuticle thickens, calcifies, takes on the minerals they have consumed — iron, manganese, the dark compounds that give basalt its color — and they become opaque. Brown. The deep mottled brown of old blood. Their digestive tracts are no longer visible. They are no longer young. They are no longer soft.

The ones on the lowest visible terrace are the oldest. Their legs have sunk into the stone. This is not a metaphor either. The secretion that once dissolved basalt for food has, in these ancient individuals, changed. It no longer dissolves. It *bonds*. Their feet are chemically welded to the wall. Their mandibles work slowly in the air, grasping at nothing, or at the moisture, or at the fine particulate that drifts in the fog. They are fixed. They are becoming part of the structure. The pale crystalline deposit on their backs is the same material as the wall, and it is growing over them, and they are slowing, and their footfalls — which once clicked in time with the spiral — are now part of the thrumming, the deep slow vibration, because their bodies are tensing against the stone less and less frequently, and the intervals between their movements are stretching, and the rhythm of the Coil is, in part, the rhythm of their dying.

---

There is something at the bottom.

You do not see it. You cannot. The spiral continues past the light, and the light fails, and what remains is sound and warmth and the sweet chemical smell that tingles on the back of the tongue. But the creatures tell you, in their way, that something is there.

The thrumming originates from below. The rhythm starts there and propagates outward, upward, through the walls and the air and the bodies of every creature on every terrace. The deepest ones are not creating it. They are *receiving* it. They are closest to the source, and their bodies vibrate with it, and they pass it on, up and up, to the youngest ones at the top, who click their feet in time with a pulse they have never traced to its origin.

Something down there is large. Something down there is old in a way that makes the oldest creature on the lowest terrace look like a newborn. Something down there is the reason the spiral exists — the reason the creatures secrete and build and descend and die and become the wall. The spiral is not a nest. The spiral is a *throat*. The mountain breathes through it. The creatures maintain it. And whatever is at the bottom — whatever is breathing, whatever is thrumming, whatever has been thrumming for centuries in the warm dark — is the thing the mountain breathes *with*.

You will not see it. You do not need to. You can feel it in your sternum, in the rhythm that enters your bones when you sit still long enough. It is patient. It is vast. It is the center of the Coil, and the Coil is its body, and the creatures are its cells, and the spiral is the slow, slow, slow process of its growth, and the mountain is its shell, and it has been growing for longer than the word *long* usefully describes, and it will continue to grow, and the spiral will descend, and the mountain will thin, and one day — not soon, not in any time that matters to you, but one day — the spiral will reach whatever it is reaching for.

And then.

You do not know. The creatures do not know. The Coil does not know. It is growing toward something it has never reached, in a direction that has no name, for a purpose it cannot formulate because it does not think. It grows because growing is what it does. It breathes because breathing is what it does. It is complete. It has been complete for a very long time. It does not need you to understand it.

It does not need you at all.

---

And the plants. The pale fronds growing from the cracks. They are not guests. They are not parasites. They are *part of it*. The organism includes them. The light that the wall-organisms produce feeds the fronds, and the fronds produce oxygen — not much, barely measurable, but enough — and the oxygen stabilizes the chemistry of the moisture, and the stabilized moisture feeds the creatures, and the creatures eat the stone and build the wall and house the organisms and the organisms glow and the fronds grow and the cycle continues and the cycle is closed and the cycle has been closed for a span of time that you cannot hold in your head without it slipping sideways into something like vertigo.

They are all one thing. The creatures and the walls and the light and the plants and the moisture and the spiral and the sound and the mountain and the dark. They are one thing. They are the Coil, and the Coil is alive, and the Coil is breathing, and the Coil does not know you were there.

---

You left.

Of course you left. The chimney was behind you. The cold air called. The sky, the stars, the ordinary darkness that is merely the absence of light rather than the presence of something else — these were waiting, and you went to them, because you are human, and humans leave. It is what you do. It is almost the only thing you do that the Coil does not also do, because the Coil does not leave. The Coil has never left. The Coil has never been anywhere else. The Coil does not know there is anywhere else to be.

But you think about it.

You think about the sound, and the way it entered your teeth. You think about the moisture, and the way it tasted, sweet and mineral, on the back of your tongue. You think about the pale shapes flowing across the walls in the blue-green dark, and the way the light moved in slow waves that never repeated, and the way the largest ones sat fixed and dying and patient at the bottom of the visible spiral, their backs crusted with crystal, their mandibles working at nothing.

You think about what is below them.

You think about the breathing.

You do not go back. You do not go back. You think about going back. You lie awake and you feel a faint pressure in your sternum, and you are not sure if it is memory or if it is the Coil, still, somehow, resonating in the mineral structure of your bones, a frequency you picked up and cannot put down, a rhythm that is not yours but that your body has, in some small way, learned.

You do not go back.

The Coil does not miss you. The Coil does not know you left. The Coil is breathing, in the dark beneath the mountain, and the spiral is one millimeter deeper than it was when you were there, and the creatures on the upper terrace are one millimeter closer to the sky, and the oldest ones on the lowest terrace are one millimeter more stone than animal, and the sound is the same sound it has always been, and it will be the same sound long after the memory of your visit has dissolved from the face of the earth the way the creatures dissolve the basalt — slowly, patiently, one molecule at a time.

The Coil breathes. The mountain breathes. The throat in the stone opens and closes, opens and closes, and the air that moves through it is warm and wet and sweet, and it carries the smell of chitin and deep time, and it does not carry the smell of you, because you are gone, and you are not part of the pattern, and the pattern does not pause for gaps.

The pattern continues.

The pattern has always continued.
