# What Lives Here

The first thing you notice about the people is how they move.

Not quickly. There is no urgency in the Springs because urgency belongs to places where the conditions change fast enough to demand response, and here the conditions change on a scale that makes human rushing look absurd. The springs shift over years. The channels migrate over seasons. The mineral crust builds over decades. A person who has lived here long enough has calibrated themselves to this tempo, and it shows in the way they cross the basin — deliberate, unhurried, each foot placed with a knowledge that is no longer conscious. They know where the crust holds. They know where it does not. They have broken through enough times, or they have watched others break through, and the knowledge has entered their legs the way the heat has entered the ground, from below, steady and invisible.

They walk the way you walk on ice, except the ice is white and warm and alive, and they have been doing it so long they no longer think of it as caution. It is simply walking.

---

There are perhaps three hundred, though the number is soft. The counting changes because people leave and return with the logic of the water itself — following channels that open and close seasonally. A woman who works the eastern basins in the cooler months moves to the western channels when the flow shifts in summer. A man who has been gone for six weeks on a supply run comes back and takes up his place as though the place had been holding it for him, which it had. The settlement does not track its people with precision because precision implies a fixedness that does not apply here. You are present or you are not, and if you are not you will be again, and if you are not again then someone else will be, and the basins will still need tending.

They are not old, most of them. Or they are old in the way that the place makes people old — not in years but in the mineral accumulation that coats everything. A person who has lived in the Springs for ten years has a quality about them that is difficult to name. Not weathered, not in the desert sense. More like encrusted. A faint residue on the skin that never quite washes off because the water itself carries it. A quality in the hair, which becomes stiff with mineral deposits and takes on the pale character of the landscape. The people who stay longest develop a faint sulfur scent that is not unpleasant but is unmistakable — not the rotten-egg sharpness of concentrated sulfur but a softer thing, a warm mineral smell that is simply the smell of the place having entered the body through the air and the water and the food and the years.

Their hands tell the story. The extraction work involves handling the crystalline residue, the channels, the valves, the pipes. The minerals build on the skin. The nails thicken slightly, or they become brittle, or they take on a faint opacity. The knuckles develop a roughness that is not from labor in the muscular sense — the work is not heavy — but from the constant contact with mineral surfaces, the crystalline residue, the rough crust that lines the channels. A person's hands in the Springs become, over years, something like the mineral terraces themselves: layered, textured, shaped by the slow deposition of what the water carries.

---

There is a record-keeper. There has always been a record-keeper, or the position has always been filled, which is not quite the same thing. The ledgers sit in the small office near the main spring, and the office smells of sulfur and old paper, which is a combination that should not work but does, somehow, because everything here exists alongside sulfur and the paper has accepted it the way the people have accepted the flies. The ledgers go back decades. The handwriting changes. The current record-keeper is a woman who came from somewhere else — they all came from somewhere else, originally — and she writes in a small precise hand that is nothing like the large loose script of her predecessor, which was nothing like the cramped angled writing of the one before that. She records the flow rates, the temperatures, the mineral content as measured by the crude tests they have — not laboratory tests but practical ones, tests that tell you whether the water is carrying more calcium this month or less, whether the silica has increased, whether the sulfur compounds have shifted. She does not understand everything she records. She has said this. She records it because it has been recorded, and because the pattern is in the recording, and because someone after her will need the record even if she cannot say what they will need it for.

She is not young. She is not old. She has been here — she thinks, though she does not always think about it — about seven years, or nine. The time does something strange here. The flat light, the absence of seasons in the dramatic sense, the sameness of the heat and the fog and the mineral landscape — these things compress time. A year feels like a month. A month feels like a week. She is not sure whether this is disturbing or restful and has settled on the conclusion that it is neither. It is simply how time moves here, the way water moves here: slowly, finding its path, not in a hurry to arrive.

---

There are children. Not many, but some. This surprises visitors, when visitors come, which is rarely. The children do not surprise themselves because they have nothing to compare it to, or their comparison is not a comparison but a texture — they have heard of other places, they have seen pictures, and these things exist in their minds not as alternatives but as stories, the way fairy tales exist, not quite real, not quite believed. The Springs is what is real. The white ground, the warm water, the flies, the flat light — these are the furniture of the world, and the furniture of the world is simply what it is.

The children play in ways that are specific to this place. They know the crust. They know which pools are too hot and which are merely warm, and they have a game that involves running along the narrow rims of mineral terraces that is terrifying to watch and apparently fun to do, and the falls when they come are into warm shallow water and soft mineral mud, and the children come up laughing and coated in white and their parents do not react because the parents did the same thing. The children catch flies in their hands — not to kill, just to hold, just to feel the impossible density of them, the way a handful of flies is like a handful of warm dust that moves — and they release them and the flies resettle and the children move on. They know the microbial mats by color. They know which colors mean which temperatures. They have names for the bacterial zones that are not scientific names but their own names, invented or inherited, names that sound like the place itself — short, plain, functional names that do not describe so much as point.

A child raised in the Springs has a sense of balance that children raised elsewhere do not have, because the ground here is not trustworthy and the child has learned this from before they could articulate it. They have a sense of temperature that is not learned from a thermometer but from the skin, from the hand dipped in the water, from the steam that rises at different rates from different pools. They have a sense of the landscape that is geological in a way most people's sense of landscape is not — they do not see the basin as scenery but as process, as something happening, as the earth doing something slowly and continuously and visibly, and they accept this the way children elsewhere accept the existence of sky.

---

There is a man who maintains the generator. Or there is a person who maintains the generator, and at this moment the person is a man, a heavy quiet man whose name is not important because the role is more durable than the person holding it, as the ledgers are more durable than the record-keeper, as the channels are more durable than the person who digs them. He comes from the north, like the road. He has been here — he counts, because he is the kind of person who counts — eleven years. He maintains the generator because the generator maintains the pumps and the pumps maintain the channels and the channels maintain the basins and the basins maintain the settlement and the settlement maintains the road and the road maintains the connection to the place that is not the Springs, which is to say the place where the fuel comes from, which is to say the world.

He understands the generator the way the record-keeper understands the ledgers — not completely, not theoretically, but practically, functionally, in the language of sound and vibration and the specific quality of the exhaust smoke that tells you the mixture is wrong before the gauges confirm it. He has a relationship with the machine that is closer to companionship than maintenance. He talks to it, sometimes, not in words but in the way he touches it, the way he listens to it, the way he sits beside it during the maintenance hours when it is silent and the broader silence of the basin opens up and he can feel the difference between the generator's absence and the generator's presence, which is the difference between the settlement functioning and not functioning, which is everything, and he knows this, and the weight of it sits in him the way the heat sits in the ground.

When the generator fails, and it does fail, and he knows it will fail permanently one day because everything mechanical fails permanently eventually, the settlement will need to decide something. He does not think about this. Or he thinks about it the way the record-keeper thinks about the patterns in the ledgers — as something that is there, something that will be there, something that someone will need to address, and that person may or may not be him. He maintains the generator. This is what he does. The future is a different kind of problem, and he is not working on it.

---

There are people who leave. This is not a failure or a rejection. It is a thing that happens, the way a spring shifts or a channel closes. A young person reaches an age — it is not a fixed age, it varies — where the mineral landscape becomes insufficient, where the flat light becomes not restful but oppressive, where the absence of plants and birds and the presence of flies and sulfur and the same faces in the same small grid of buildings becomes a weight that the body can no longer carry, and they leave. They take the road north. The road takes them to the place where the asphalt begins, and the asphalt takes them to wherever asphalt goes, which is everywhere, which is the world.

Some of them come back. This is the thing that the settlement does not advertise and does not hide. They come back because the world — the world of asphalt and plants and birds and weather that changes and people in numbers large enough to be a crowd — is too much, or too fast, or too various, and the Springs has recalibrated something in them that cannot be recalibrated back. They have been mineralized, in some small way. They have absorbed the pace and the patience and the flat light, and the world's bright directional light hurts them, and the world's noise is not the generator's low companionship but something sharper, and they come back, and the settlement receives them without comment because the settlement does not comment on such things, because coming and going are the same process, which is the process of the water, which finds its level.

The ones who come back are different. Not dramatically. Not in a way a visitor would notice, if visitors came, which they don't, often. But the ones who come back move differently from the ones who left. They move with a deliberateness that is not caution but intention, as though they have learned something in the world that has confirmed something about the Springs, and the confirmation has settled them. They are not here because they cannot leave. They are here because they have left and the leaving has told them where they are, and where they are is the Springs, and the Springs is where the water rises warm from below and the minerals build and the flies hum and the light is flat and the ground glows faintly at night and the process continues, and they are part of the process, and this is enough, and this has always been enough, and they did not know it was enough until they left and came back and found it still here, still running, still warm.

---

The flies live here.

It seems strange to say this, to set the flies alongside the people, but the flies have been here longer and the flies will be here after and the flies are, in a way that the people are not, the true inhabitants of the Springs. They are everywhere. They coat the ground near the water in densities that look like fabric, like a dark fabric laid across the white mineral crust, and when you walk through them they rise in a body and the sound is a sound that has no equivalent — not a buzz, not a hum, but a vibration that is felt in the chest before it is heard in the ears, and then they resettle behind you, and the fabric closes, and it is as though you were never there.

The people have made their peace. This was said before. But the peace is more specific than that. It is a peace that has developed its own customs, its own etiquette. You do not wave at the flies. Waving is useless and it marks you as a visitor and it disturbs them unnecessarily. You walk through them and you let them settle on you and you do not mind, or you have learned to not mind, which is the same thing. They are on your arms. They are on your face. They are in your hair and on your clothes and they do not bite and they do not sting and they are simply there, the way the heat is there, the way the sulfur is there, the way the mineral crust is there under your feet. They are a condition. The people have accepted the condition. The condition is: you are covered in flies. And then you are not, when you move indoors, and then you are again, when you go out. And this is life, and life is what lives here, and what lives here is the flies.

The children eat them. Not deliberately, not as food, but accidentally, the way you eat dust on a windy road, and the children do not mind this either, or they mind it the way children everywhere mind the small indignities of the world they live in, which is to say not at all, because the indignity is their world and their world is the only one they know.

---

The microbial mats live here. They are alive in a way that is visible if you look, and the people look, because looking at the water is part of the work and because the mats tell you things. The orange band has moved. The yellow zone is wider this month. The green mats at the cooler margins are thicker than they were last season. These are observations that the record-keeper records and that the basin-workers know and that the children see without knowing they are seeing, because the colors are simply part of the landscape, the way grass is part of a landscape elsewhere, and the colors change, and the change is normal, and the normal is alive.

The mats are the closest thing the Springs has to vegetation, and they are not vegetation. They are something older. They are communities of organisms that have been building and dying and building again in these waters for longer than the basalt ridges have been basalt ridges, for longer than the fault has been a fault in the sense that people understand faults. They are the original inhabitants. The people are recent. The flies are recent. Even the mineral terraces are recent compared to the mats, because the mats were here before the terraces, building their tiny structures, depositing their own minerals, producing the conditions that allowed the larger formations to begin. The Springs is their place. The people are living in it.

There is something in this that the people understand without articulating. Not reverence — the Springs does not produce reverence, it produces something flatter and more practical. But an awareness, maybe. A sense that the place they live in is not theirs in the way that places are usually yours, by construction or by conquest or by choice. It is theirs by proximity. They are here and the mats are here and the flies are here and the water is here and the heat is here and the flat light is here, and all of these things exist together, and the togetherness is not community exactly but co-presence, and co-presence is what the Springs offers, which is not much, which is enough.

---

The empty buildings live here too, in their way.

They are not abandoned. They are in a phase. The people know this because the people understand phases, because the water teaches phases — a pool is clear and then it is cloudy and then it is clear again, or it is not clear again, or it becomes a different kind of pool. The empty buildings are in the phase where people are not in them. The mineral dust accumulates. The sulfur smell settles. The windows develop their milky opacity. The walls acquire their crust. The buildings are slowly becoming geological, and this is not a death but a transition, and the people who live in the occupied buildings do not mourn the empty ones because mourning implies an ending, and there is no ending here, only a change of state, from architectural to mineral, from human-scaled to earth-scaled, and this is the process that governs everything, and the buildings are part of it, and the people are part of it, and the water is part of it, and the process does not need anyone to mourn it because the process does not end.

A person who has lived here long enough can stand in front of an empty building and feel the building passing through its phase. This is not mystical. It is practical, the way reading the water is practical. The building is becoming what the ground already is. The crust on its walls is the same crust that covers the basin. The dust inside is the same dust that the wind carries across the hardpan. The building is returning to the material it was made from, which is the material of the earth, which is what the springs bring up from below, and the building is not dying but being digested, slowly, by the same process that builds the terraces and the crust and the mats and the flies and everything that lives here, which is the process of the earth expressing itself through heat and water and mineral and time.

---

At night the people sleep. The ground is warm beneath them. The fog lies across the basin. The generator vibrates. The flies are quiet, or as quiet as flies get, which is a different kind of quiet than the daytime quiet, a lower density, a slower movement. The microbial mats glow, if they glow, or the mineral surfaces glow, or the darkness itself has a quality of light that is not light but the absence of total darkness, and the people sleep in this, and their sleep is the sleep of the Springs, which is warm and mineral and deep, and the dreams they dream, if they dream, are dreams that belong to this place and no other — dreams of flat light and white ground and water that moves slowly and the sound of the generator underneath everything, and the flies, and the colors in the pools, and the children running along the terrace rims, and the road going north and going nowhere after that, and the springs rising, always rising, from below.

They live here. They do not live here because it is beautiful, though it is beautiful, sometimes, in the way that indifferent things can be beautiful — not by trying, not by arranging themselves for the eye, but by simply being what they are under a light that does not care whether anyone is looking. They do not live here because it is easy, because it is not easy. They do not live here because they have nowhere else to go, because most of them do, and some of them have gone, and some of them have come back. They live here because the water rises and the minerals deposit and the basins need tending and the generator needs maintaining and the ledgers need recording and the channels need digging and the crust needs walking on and the flies need living with and the heat needs enduring and the place needs people, not in the way that a house needs people to be a home but in the way that a process needs participants, and they are the participants, and this is what they do, and they do it, and the Springs continues, and they continue, and the water rises, and it is warm.
