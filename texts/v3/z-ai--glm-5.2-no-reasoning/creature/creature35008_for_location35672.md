The midges do not know they are underground.

This is not a failure of cognition — they have no cognition to fail, no model of the world that includes "above" or "below" or "ground." They have only the surfaces they walk on and the air that touches them and the film they eat. The film is on the pipes. The pipes are in the gallery. The gallery is warm. Everything else follows.

But it matters that they do not know. It matters because their not-knowing is the shape of their lives, and their lives are the shape of this place, and this place is a marsh that has been folded underground and pressed into the geometry of industry and told to keep working. The midges kept working. They never stopped.

---

There are perhaps three hundred of them. Perhaps four hundred. The count shifts. Individuals are lost — they fall from the pipe fittings, they land in the collection channel, the water takes them down the conduit, and they do not come back. The conduit receives them the way it receives the water: without comment, without adjustment, without a change in the readings on the instruments in the gallery. A midge is immeasurable. A midge is below the threshold of what the system measures. And yet the midges are part of the system. Someone wrote this down. The note is still pinned to the wall. The pin is green.

They are small. Four millimeters from head to abdomen, another two of legs and antenna, the whole creature small enough to sit on a thumbnail with room to spare. Pale — not white, not translucent, but the color of old cream, of parchment held against a light, of skin that has never seen the sun because there is no sun to see and there has not been one for longer than the species can remember. Which is not a metaphor. The species does not remember. The species does not have memory in the way that implies nostalgia or loss. The species has *persistence* — the continuation of form and behavior across generations, the slow editing of genes against the pressure of an environment that does not change, the accumulation of traits that say: *this works, keep this, this is what we are now.*

Their wings are vestigial. This is the thing you notice first, if you look closely, which requires bringing your face to within a few centimeters of a pipe fitting in a dim gallery while condensation drips on the back of your neck. The wings are there — two pairs, flat against the abdomen, overlapping like the pages of a book that has been closed and pressed and forgotten. They are not useless. They are *finished*. They have served whatever purpose they once served and they have been set down. The veins in the wings are still visible, a branching pattern that suggests a structure capable of flight, capable of the long aerial search for mates that midges perform on the surface, in marshes, in the thin cold air above standing water. But the muscles that would drive those wings have atrophied to thin bands of connective tissue that do not contract. The wings are relics. The wings are a record of a different life, a surface life, a life in which these creatures flew.

They do not fly. They walk.

They walk with a deliberateness that seems at first to be slowness and then reveals itself as care. Each leg is placed. Each step is taken on a surface that is wet — the pipes are always wet, the gallery is always damp, everything in the Weeping Works is wet in some degree — and the midges move across wet metal with a precision that suggests they have learned, over evolutionary time, that wet metal is treacherous, that a misstep means a fall, that a fall means the channel, that the channel means the conduit, that the conduit means whatever is down there. They do not know this chain of consequences. But they move as if they do. They move as if each step is a decision, and each decision matters, and the surface beneath them is not to be trusted.

Trust, of course, is the wrong word. The midges do not trust or distrust. They *respond*. The wet pipe is a stimulus. The careful leg placement is a response. The response has been selected for, over generations, by the simple mechanical fact that midges which place their legs carefully fall less often and live longer and produce more midges which place their legs carefully. This is not intelligence. This is not instinct in the romantic sense — no inner voice saying *watch your step*. This is the residue of a thousand thousand falls, a thousand thousand losses, compressed into the architecture of a nervous system that is smaller than a grain of sand and that contains, in its minimal connections, the entire behavioral wisdom of a species that has lived on wet pipes in the dark for longer than anyone has been counting.

---

They eat the biofilm. Not the purple biofilm on the slag tiles — that is outside the gallery, in the basin proper, and the midges do not go there. The gallery has its own biofilm, thinner, paler, growing on the pipe fittings where the condensation gathers and the warmth of the pump motors creates a microclimate a few degrees above the basin average. This biofilm is not purple. It is colorless, or nearly so — a slight cloudiness on the metal, a slickness that the fingers can detect but the eye cannot easily parse. It is composed of the same kinds of organisms as the purple biofilm — iron-reducers, anaerobes, the patient chemists of wet surfaces — but without the community density, without the species interactions, without the critical mass of interaction that produces the emergent pigment. The gallery biofilm is a suburb. The basin biofilm is a city.

The midges graze. They move along a pipe fitting, stop, lower their heads, scrape at the biofilm with mouthparts adapted for exactly this kind of surface feeding — not biting, not piercing, but *scraping*, a raking motion that lifts the thin film of bacteria and their secretions from the metal and draws it into the mouth. They feed for a few seconds. They move on. They feed again. The pattern is: walk, stop, scrape, walk, stop, scrape. A rhythm as regular and as unvarying as the pumps. A rhythm that is, in its own way, a heartbeat.

They do not eat much. A few hundred midges scraping at a biofilm that regrows as fast as it is grazed — the impact is negligible. The biofilm does not notice the midges. The pipes do not notice the midges. The water does not notice the midges. The midges are, by any material measure, irrelevant to the function of the Weeping Works.

And yet.

The note says they are part of the system. The note is unsigned. The note is old. The handwriting does not match any other document in the gallery. Someone — at some point, for some reason — looked at these small pale flightless insects walking on the pipes in the collection gallery and decided that they mattered. Not that they were interesting. Not that they were worth preserving for scientific reasons. That they were *part of the system*. A component. A node in the network of interactions that makes the Weeping Works a marsh and not merely a wet basement.

What part? What node?

The midges walk on the pipes. Their feet touch the biofilm. Their mouths scrape the biofilm. Their waste falls — tiny deposits, invisible, dissolved by the condensation almost before it has settled — and enters the water that is being collected and tested and sent down the conduit. The midges are *processing*. They are taking the biofilm — which is itself processing the iron and the water and the darkness — and they are converting it, through the ancient chemistry of digestion, into something else. Something that enters the water. Something that goes down.

Is this what the note means? Is this what the midges contribute — the metabolic output of a few hundred small bodies, feeding on a suburban biofilm in a warm gallery, their waste dissolving into the collected water and riding the conduit down to whatever waits below?

Perhaps. Perhaps the contribution is that small. Perhaps the system is that sensitive — a marsh so precisely balanced, so carefully tuned, that the waste products of three hundred midges are a variable that matters, a term in the equation that cannot be zeroed out without changing the result. Marshes are like this. Marshes are sensitive to the presence of grazing invertebrates. Marshes are sensitive to the removal of any component, however small, because a marsh is not a machine but a *community*, and a community is the sum of its interactions, and the removal of one interaction changes all the others, and the change propagates through the system in ways that cannot be predicted because the system is too complex to model, too old to fully understand, too much a product of its own history to be reduced to specifications.

Or perhaps the note means something else. Perhaps the person who wrote it — standing in the gallery, looking at the midges, looking at the pipes, looking at the water going down — saw something that the instruments do not measure. A pattern. A behavior. A *relationship* between the midges and the biofilm and the water that is not metabolic but *structural* — a relationship in which the midges, by walking on the pipes, by scraping the biofilm, by *being present* on the surfaces where the water gathers, are doing something that has nothing to do with their waste and everything to do with their *touch*.

They touch the water. When they drink — and they do drink, drawing condensation from the pipe fittings through the same mouthparts they use to scrape the biofilm — they touch the collected water. They touch it with mouthparts that have been in contact with the biofilm, with the metal, with the air of the gallery. They introduce, with each drink, a trace of whatever is on their mouths into the water. A trace so small it is below the instruments. A trace that is, perhaps, above whatever threshold the system below uses to measure what it receives.

Or perhaps it is none of these things. Perhaps the note means only that the midges were here when the system was built, and the system was built around them, and removing them would be like removing a brick from a wall — not necessarily catastrophic, but a change in the structure, a violation of the form, a departure from the design. The midges are part of the system because they were part of the place, and the place was made into a system, and the system includes the place, and the place includes the midges, and this is circular, and circular is what ecosystems are.

---

They reproduce. This is the other thing — the thing that is easy to forget because the midges are so quiet, so careful, so small, so pale, so present and so unobtrusive that they seem to be not quite alive but rather *maintained*, like the pumps, like the lamps, like the vents. But they are alive. They eat and they excrete and they drink and they walk and they reproduce.

The reproduction happens in the corners of the gallery. The corners are darker than the rest of the gallery — the sodium lamps are mounted on the ceiling and the walls, and the corners are where the light angles meet and fail, where the dark brown of the basin's engineered darkness creeps in and claims a small territory. In these corners, where the pipe fittings converge and the condensation gathers in heavier drops and the biofilm grows slightly thicker, slightly more robust, the midges lay their eggs.

The eggs are laid on wet metal. Not in water — the midges are not aquatic, not at any stage of their life cycle, not anymore. The eggs are laid on the *surface* of damp pipe fittings, in the thin film of moisture that coats the metal, and they develop there, in that film, drawing oxygen from the air and moisture from the film and nourishment from their own yolk. They are laid singly or in small clusters of three or four. They are almost invisible — translucent, smaller than a grain of salt, adhered to the metal by a secretion that the female produces from her terminal segments. The secretion is clear. It dries to a film that is tougher than it looks — it holds the egg in place against the slow movement of condensation, against the drip and the trickle, against the vibration of the pumps transmitted through the pipe walls.

The females lay eggs and walk away. There is no care. There is no attention. The female does not return to the egg. The female does not check on the egg. The egg is *placed*, with the same care that characterizes every other movement — the leg placement, the feeding stops, the navigation of wet metal — and then the female walks away, and the egg is on its own, and the egg will become a larva or it will not, and the larva will become an adult or it will not, and the adult will walk on the pipes and scrape the biofilm and drink the condensation and fall into the channel or not fall into the channel, and the cycle will continue or it will not.

The cycle continues. It has been continuing. The midges are not numerous — three hundred, four hundred, the count shifting — but the count has been shifting within this range for as long as anyone has been counting, which is not very long, because the counting is not part of the maintenance schedule, it is a thing that curious workers do occasionally, standing in the gallery with a flashlight and a notepad, looking at the pipes, estimating. The estimates are always the same. A few hundred. Never more. Never fewer. The population is stable.

This stability is itself a kind of information. A population of three or four hundred, in a space this size, with this much biofilm available, with this much water, with no predators, no competition, no disease — the population *should* grow. It should explode. It should cover the pipes in pale cream bodies, should fill the gallery with the slow careful walking of ten thousand midges, should become a presence that cannot be ignored. But it does not. The population stays at a few hundred. The midges do not overpopulate. They do not expand their range. They do not colonize the basin. They stay in the gallery. They stay on the pipes. They stay at a few hundred.

Something is regulating them. Something in the system — in the temperature, in the humidity, in the quality of the biofilm, in the chemistry of the condensation, in some variable that the instruments do not measure and the maintenance schedule does not address — is holding the midge population at a few hundred. A carrying capacity has been set. Not by the midges. By the place. The Weeping Works has decided how many midges it needs, and it maintains that number, and the midges comply, not through choice but through the slow mechanical logic of birth and death in an environment that has been tuned to a specific pitch.

The system includes the midges. The note is correct.

---

They do not go into the basin. This is the boundary. The gallery opens onto the basin — there is no door, no barrier, no physical separation between the space where the midges live and the vast shallow bowl of slag and steel and falling water — and yet the midges do not cross. They reach the edge of the gallery, where the pipes converge and the wall meets the floor and the light from the basin's sodium lamps reaches in like a hand, and they stop. They turn. They walk back along the pipes.

Why? The basin is wet. The basin has biofilm — the purple biofilm, the real biofilm, the city and not the suburb. The basin is larger, more various, more *marsh*. Everything the midges need is in the basin. And they do not go.

The temperature. The gallery is warmer — the pump motors, the test equipment, the concentrated pipe runs that carry water at the temperature of deep earth. The gallery is two or three degrees above the basin average, and for an insect this small, two or three degrees is the difference between living and not living, between moving fast enough to feed and moving so slowly that the biofilm outgrows the scraping. The midges are thermal prisoners. They are held in the gallery by the warmth of the pipes, and the basin — vast, dark, purple-slicked, weeping — is a desert of cold that they cannot cross.

Or perhaps it is not the temperature. Perhaps it is the light. The gallery is brighter than the basin — more lamps, closer spacing, a maintenance space designed for human work, for reading gauges and checking fittings and filling in the maintenance schedule. The midges do not like light. They do not seek it. But they do not avoid it aggressively either — they walk across lit pipes and unlit pipes with the same careful pace. The light is not a barrier. But the darkness of the basin might be. The basin's darkness is *engineered*. It is a darkness with a luminous ceiling of less than two lux, a darkness designed to preserve the purple biofilm, to maintain the process, to keep the marsh in the conditions that a marsh needs. And the midges, who are part of the system, may also be part of the system's *tolerances* — calibrated to a brightness that exists in the gallery and does not exist in the basin, held in place by the same specifications that hold the biofilm in place, maintained by the same tradition of maintenance that maintains the pumps.

The midges are the system. The system is the midges. They were built together, or they grew together, or they found each other — the marsh and the insect, the wetland and the invertebrate — and they have been together ever since, and neither one is fully itself without the other.

---

The maintenance workers see them. The workers come down the shaft every — the schedule says every fourteen days, and the schedule is followed, and the workers arrive with toolkits and rags and a can of pump oil that smells like wintergreen and a sense of duty that has been transmitted through enough hands that it has the texture of ritual. They check the pumps. They check the gauges. They check the lamps. They wipe down the pipe fittings that need wiping and they tighten the fittings that need tightening and they replace the gaskets that need replacing and they sign the schedule and they climb back up the shaft.

They see the midges. Of course they see them — the midges are on the pipes, the workers are at the pipes, the encounter is unavoidable. But the seeing is not a *noticing*. The midges are part of the visual texture of the gallery, part of the expected scene, part of what the workers' eyes have learned to file as *normal* and therefore *invisible*. The midges are there in the way that the condensation is there, in the way that the orange light is there, in the way that the hum of the pumps is there. They are part of the room.

Occasionally a worker will pause. Will look at a midge on a pipe fitting, walking its careful walk, scraping its careful scrape. Will watch for a moment — five seconds, ten — and then will return to the work. The watching is not scientific. The watching is not curious. The watching is something else — a recognition, perhaps, that the small pale thing on the pipe is *alive*, is the only alive thing in this place besides the biofilm and the worker, is a living creature in a constructed space, and that this means something, though the worker could not say what.

They do not remove the midges. The note says not to. But even without the note, they would not remove them. You do not remove a thing that has always been there. You do not remove a thing that is part of the room. The midges are part of the room the way the bronze grate is part of the western wall — not because anyone can explain why, but because their absence would be a change, and the Weeping Works does not like change. The Weeping Works likes constancy. The pumps run at their rates. The water falls at its rate. The biofilm grows at its rate. The midges walk at their rate. Everything stays. Everything continues.

---

And the ones that fall. The ones that slip on the wet metal — a leg misplaced, a fraction of a millimeter off, the careful placement not quite careful enough — and tumble from the pipe fitting into the collection channel below. The channel is shallow. The water moves slowly. But the midge is small, and the water is cold, and the midge's legs are not adapted for swimming, and the current — gentle as it is — carries the midge along the channel toward the convergence point where the gallery's collected water joins the central channel's flow and enters the conduit.

The midge rides the water. It does not struggle. It may not be capable of struggle — the cold water may have already slowed its metabolism to the point where movement is impossible, where the careful leg placement that defined its life is no longer available, where the only thing left is to be carried. The water carries it. The water has been carrying things for a long time — condensation, biofilm particles, mineral traces, the dissolved waste of three hundred midges — and it carries this one midge with the same patient indifference, the same mineral patience, the same slow directional flow.

The conduit opens. The midge enters. The midge goes down.

What is down there? The workers do not know. The maps do not show. The maintenance schedule does not describe. The instruments in the gallery test the water before it enters the conduit, and the readings are normal, and the water goes down, and no one has ever followed it. The conduit goes down. That is all anyone knows.

The midge goes down with the water. A pale cream body, four millimeters long, with vestigial wings flat against its abdomen like closed books, with legs that are still extended in the posture of walking, with mouthparts that are still positioned for scraping. A body that was part of the system. A body that has left the system. A body that is now in the water, going down, to whatever is below.

Does whatever is below receive the midge? Does it notice? Does the midge — dissolved, decomposed, reduced to its constituent chemistry by whatever process occurs in the depths — become part of whatever the conduit feeds? Does the midge, in its final transit, contribute something that the water alone could not carry? Is this why the midges are part of the system — not because of what they do in the gallery, but because of what they become in the conduit?

The note does not say. The note only says: *Do not remove the midges. They are part of the system.* It does not say which part. It does not say how. It does not say what happens to the ones that fall, or how many fall, or whether the falling is a feature or a failure. It says they are part of the system, and the system is still running, and the note is still pinned to the wall with a brass pin that has turned green.

---

The midges walk on the pipes. This is what they do. This is what they have always done, for as long as there have been midges, for as long as there have been pipes, for as long as the gallery has been warm and the biofilm has been growing and the condensation has been falling. They walk. They scrape. They drink. They lay eggs in the corners. They do not fly. They do not go into the basin. They do not overpopulate. They do not disappear. They maintain themselves at a few hundred individuals, in a warm gallery, at the eastern edge of an underground marsh, in a space that someone built because they needed a marsh and did not have one.

The midges do not know they are underground. The midges do not know they are in a marsh. The midges do not know that the water they drink has been through the cycle — ceiling to floor to pump to pipe to gallery — a thousand times, ten thousand times, that it has tasted iron and slag and darkness so many times that it has become something other than water. The midges do not know that the water goes down. The midges do not know that some of them go down with it.

The midges know the pipe. The midges know the biofilm. The midges know the warm air and the wet metal and the careful placement of legs and the dark corners where eggs are laid and the faint vibration of the pumps transmitted through the pipe walls like a heartbeat that is not theirs but that they have lived with for so long that it might as well be.

The midges are alive. They are the only clearly, unambiguously alive things in the Weeping Works — the biofilm is alive too, but it is a smear, a slickness, a presence that is more chemistry than creature. The midges are *creatures*. They have bodies. They have legs. They have mouths that scrape and wings that do not open and eggs that adhere to wet metal with a secretion that dries to a film. They are small and pale and careful and they are part of the system, and the system is part of them, and they have been here for a long time, and they will be here for a long time, walking on the pipes in the warm gallery while the ceiling weeps and the pumps hum and the water goes down, down, down.

The water goes down. The midges stay.
