# What Lives in the Rootworks

---

They are not many. That's the first thing to understand. If you came here expecting a society, a culture, a people with shared customs and a collective name for themselves, you'd be disappointed in the way you'd be disappointed to learn that a remote monastery has only three monks. The Rootworks supports a number that hovers around six. Sometimes seven. Occasionally five. Never fewer than four, because the pumps need tending and the pruning can't wait and someone has to maintain the generator, and those three tasks alone fill most of the hours a body has in a day.

They don't have a word for themselves as a group. Or if they do, it's not offered. They have names. They have the names they arrived with.

---

Harker was first. Or Harker has been here longest, which isn't the same thing, but Harker treats it as the same thing and no one has the energy to argue. She is somewhere past fifty and somewhere before seventy — the dim light and the constant damp make age difficult to read, and she has the lean, weatherstripped quality of someone who has been shaped by her environment the way the root-beams have been shaped by their forms. Not bent so much as trained. Her hands are the telling detail. The knuckles are thick. The skin between them is cracked in a pattern that has healed and cracked and healed again so many times that the cracks have become permanent, a geography of creases that follow no consistent direction. Her fingernails are short and dark-stained. Not dirt — the stain goes deeper than dirt. It's the tannin in the root-felt, the same compound that turns the water tea-brown, and it has worked itself into her skin the way it has worked itself into the wood of the tool handles, until the boundary between her and the material she works is more a gradient than a line.

Harker prunes. That's her primary function, and she thinks of it as a function, not a job, because a job implies something separate from the person performing it and this is not separate. She rises — she doesn't wake, exactly; she surfaces, the way you surface from a doze, and the transition from sleep to consciousness is as gradual as the transition from dark to dim in the chamber itself — and she prunes. She takes the shears from the hook by her sleeping platform. The shears are old. The handles are wood that has been replaced three times. The blades are steel and are sharpened on a stone that sits in a niche near the generator room, and the stone is worn concave from years of the same motion. She sharpens the shears. She starts at the south end of the chamber, where the fresh growth is palest, and she works north, clipping the new growth back to the established line — the line that marks the difference between the shape the Rootworks is and the shape it would be if left alone. She has been maintaining this line for what she estimates as nineteen years. She could be off by two in either direction. She doesn't keep a calendar because the calendar would tell her nothing she doesn't already feel in her joints and in the thickness of the stems she cuts.

She doesn't talk much. When she does, it's in a voice that has gone slightly hoarse from disuse, a voice that has shaped itself to the acoustics of the chamber the way the chamber has shaped itself to the acoustics of the water. She speaks low. She doesn't need to speak louder, because the chamber is small and the other residents are never far away. She gives instructions when instructions are needed. She answers questions when questions are asked, though her answers are often just a look — a look that means *you already know the answer to that* or *the answer is the same as it was yesterday* or *stop talking and start cutting.*

She sleeps on a platform that is shaped like her. Not metaphorically. The root-felt has compressed over nineteen years of the same body in the same position, and the platform has a shallow impression that is recognizably Harker — the breadth of her shoulders, the length of her spine, the slight twist she puts in her hips when she lies on her side. The platform is smoother than any other surface in the chamber. It has the patina of long use, the way an old chair seat has the patina of long use, except this patina is alive. The surface she touches every night grows a little differently from the surface she doesn't. It's finer. It's denser. It has adapted to her.

---

Pell came up through the stairs nine years ago. That's the phrase he uses — *came up through the stairs* — even though the stairs descend, because for him the Rootworks is up, is elevation, is a destination that required effort to reach. He was looking for something. He won't say what. He carries the not-saying in a particular way, in his shoulders, a tightness that has nothing to do with the work and everything to do with whatever he left on the surface. It's not his secret that's interesting. It's the way the secret has become part of his posture. He has lived with it long enough that it has ossified. He will take it with him when he leaves, if he leaves, and he will leave with the same shape he arrived with, because the Rootworks doesn't take things from you. It just gives you a place to keep them.

Pell runs the generator. This is not a glamorous job. It is not even a satisfying job, the way pruning is satisfying, because pruning has a visible result — the line is maintained, the shape is preserved — while the generator is a matter of keeping a thing running that should by any reasonable standard have stopped running years ago. The generator is a diesel unit, old, a model that Pell suspects was obsolete when it was installed. He has to bleed the fuel line every morning. He has to check the oil every second day. He has to listen to it — and he does listen, the way a doctor listens to a heart, with an attention that goes beyond hearing into a kind of diagnostic intuition — and he can tell from a change in the idle if the injectors are fouling or if the fuel filter is getting clogged or if the compression in the third cylinder is dropping, which it is, slowly, and which he cannot fix because he doesn't have the parts and can't get the parts and so he compensates by adjusting the fuel mixture, leaning it out, which buys time but costs efficiency and makes the generator run hotter and the lights flicker more.

He talks to the generator. Not in the way a lonely person talks to a cat — he talks to it the way a mechanic talks to a machine, a running commentary on its condition, its temperament, its moods. *She's rough today.* *Listen to that — that's the injector.* *Come on, come on, hold together.* He doesn't personify it. He doesn't name it. He just acknowledges that it has a disposition, and the disposition is relevant to his work, and so he tracks it the way he'd track the disposition of a difficult coworker.

Pell also does the mechanical maintenance on the pumps. The pumps are simpler than the generator, which means they break less often but when they break the breakage is more catastrophic, because a pump that stops working means water starts rising, and water rising in the Rootworks is not an abstract concern. The creek spreads. The root-felt saturates. The platforms get slick. The work becomes impossible. Pell has a routine for the pumps — he checks each one daily, listens to the impeller, checks the packing gland for leaks, greases the bearings with a grease he has a limited supply of and has been supplementing with rendered plant oil, which works but doesn't last as long. The grease is in a can. The can is rusting. When the grease is gone, the maintenance regime changes. Pell thinks about this the way a person with a chronic condition thinks about the progression of the disease — not with panic but with a steady, low-grade awareness that the situation is not improving.

He is younger than Harker. Maybe mid-thirties. Maybe younger — the Rootworks makes everyone look older, the way high altitude makes everyone look older, the constant mild stress of a body working in conditions it wasn't quite designed for. He is thin but not slight. He has the ropy forearms of someone who turns wrenches and hauls pipe. His hair is longer than it needs to be, because cutting it requires a reason and he hasn't found one. He keeps it tied back with a cord of root-fiber. The cord is the same material he uses for everything else. He didn't bring much with him. What he brought is in a bag under his sleeping platform, and the bag is partially grown-over now, the root-felt creeping up its sides the way it creeps up everything, and Pell lets it, because the bag is where it is and the root-felt goes where it goes and the negotiation between them is not his to manage.

---

Senn and Tori arrived together. They don't say whether they're together-together or simply together in the sense of having come from the same place at the same time, and the distinction is not one the Rootworks encourages you to parse, because the Rootworks doesn't have categories for relationships. It has categories for work. Senn processes fiber. Tori builds. That's how they're known.

Senn is the one who turns the raw root-felt into the panels that are the Rootworks' product. This is a multi-stage process that takes strength and patience in roughly equal measure. The harvested fiber has to be separated — pulled apart, the long strands from the short, the woody from the fibrous — and this is done by hand, sitting at a low bench, working through a pile of cuttings with fingers that move with the automatic precision of someone who has done this so many times the hands don't need the eyes anymore. Senn's fingers are long. Not especially graceful — they're working fingers, slightly swollen at the joints from the repetitive stress of gripping and pulling — but long, which helps with the separation, because the difference between a good fiber and a bad fiber is often a matter of a few inches of length, and you need fingers that can feel that difference without measuring it.

Once separated, the fiber goes to the mill. The mill is manual. Senn turns the crank. The rollers compress the fiber, flatten it, align the strands. The fiber comes out the other side as a mat, rough but cohesive. The mat goes to the press, where it's compressed under high pressure for — Senn doesn't time it. Senn knows by feel. The press has a lever. The lever has resistance. When the resistance stops changing, the press is done. Senn reads the resistance through hands that have been reading it for years and the reading is accurate to within a margin that doesn't matter because the panels are not made to a specification that requires precision. They're made to a specification that requires consistency, which is different. The panels need to be the same as the last panels. Not identical — the same. There's a difference, and Senn knows it, and can't articulate it, and doesn't try.

Tori takes the panels and builds with them. Or repairs with them. The Rootworks' architecture is in constant slow flux — the growth pushes, the pruning pushes back, and in the conflict between the two the structures shift. A wall that was vertical ten years ago has a slight lean now. A doorway that was the right height has narrowed as the surrounding growth thickened. Tori's job is to manage this — to cut out the growth that has intruded where it shouldn't be, to replace the panels that have softened with age, to reinforce the laminated beams that have started to delaminate where the moisture has gotten between the layers. Tori works with the same material the building is made of, which means the repairs are invisible — you can't always tell where the old growth ends and the new panel begins, because the panel is made of the same fiber as the growth and the moisture encourages the growth to colonize it, to send new roots into it, to incorporate it, and within a year a replaced panel looks like it was always there.

Tori is the one who interacts most with the architecture of the chamber itself — not the built structures, but the living mass. Tori understands the growth the way a gardener understands a hedge, which is to say with a combination of knowledge and instinct that resists formalization. *This wall wants to go left,* Tori will say, and the statement is not metaphorical. The growth has a direction. It has a tendency. It responds to the gradients of moisture and light and the micro-topography of the chamber in ways that are consistent enough to predict if you've been paying attention long enough. Tori has been paying attention. Tori works with the tendency rather than against it — if a wall wants to lean, Tori lets it lean and builds the support to accommodate the lean, rather than trying to force it vertical. The result is an architecture that looks organic because it is organic, not in the metaphorical sense but in the literal sense. It is shaped by the same forces that shape a tree.

Senn and Tori sleep on adjacent platforms. The platforms are close enough that Senn can reach Tori without getting up, which is a proximity that means something or doesn't, and which neither of them comments on because commenting on it would require a framework for commenting that they don't share. They have been here four years. They arrived with more equipment than Pell — tools, supplies, a sense of purpose that Harker's long tenure had made unnecessary for Harker and that Pell's flight had made unnecessary for Pell. Senn and Tori came to work. They are working. The work is what they expected, more or less. The place is not what they expected, but the place is not what anyone expects, because expecting the Rootworks is like expecting a specific dream — the details never survive the encounter.

---

There was someone else. There's always someone else.

The evidence is in the platforms. There are eight sleeping platforms in the Rootworks, arranged in clusters along the chamber walls where the root-felt is densest and the ceiling weeps least. Six are in current use — you can tell by the impression, by the smoothness, by the small personal effects that mark a claimed space. One is Harker's, the deepest and smoothest. The others are shallower, newer, still forming. A platform takes about a year to develop a noticeable impression, and a platform that has been empty for more than a few months starts to lose its impression — the root-felt grows back, slowly filling the concavity, the surface rising like water in a basin, until the body-shape is gone and the platform is just a platform again.

One of the two empty platforms still has its impression. It's shallow — a year or two of use, maybe three — and it's recent — the root-felt hasn't started to fill it in yet. Someone left recently. Weeks, maybe. Not months. The platform has a small niche cut into the wall beside it, and the niche is empty except for a stub of candle and a matchbook with two matches left. The matches don't strike. They're too damp. They've been too damp for as long as anyone can remember, but the matchbook stays in the niche because the person who used the platform left it there and no one has moved it because moving it would mean acknowledging the departure, and the departure hasn't been acknowledged yet.

Harker knows who it was. Harker knows who everyone was who has ever used a platform in the Rootworks, because Harker has been here longest and the platforms are the record and Harker reads them the way an archaeologist reads strata — the depth of the impression, the smoothness, the shape, the artifacts in the niche. She doesn't talk about the people who've left. She doesn't talk about the people who've stayed, either, except in the present tense. The past tense is a luxury the Rootworks doesn't encourage. The place is too much the same from one year to the next. The past doesn't feel past. It feels like another room in the same building, just a room you can't get to anymore, and talking about it would be like describing a room you can see through a window but can't enter. You could. But why.

The person who left — their name was Edra, and they operated the lathe. You know this because Pell mentioned it once, not in a moment of candor but in a moment of inattention, answering a question about the lathe with *Edra used to —* and then stopping, the sentence cut off the way you cut a branch, cleanly, at the node, so the cut heals over. Pell didn't say more. The lathe sits where it sits, half-dismantled, rusting into its platform, and the work it used to do — turning the laminated panels into the round shapes, the spools, the handles, the fittings that the Rootworks used to produce alongside the flat panels — that work doesn't get done anymore. It's not that no one can do it. It's that the lathe needs repair and the repair needs Edra, or someone like Edra, and there isn't anyone like Edra, because Edra was the person who knew the lathe, and the lathe is the kind of machine that requires knowing, not just operating.

The departure left a gap. Not a dramatic gap. Not a crisis. The Rootworks absorbs departures the way it absorbs everything — slowly, organically, the surrounding work expanding to fill the space. Senn took over part of what Edra did. Tori took over the rest. The lathe sits idle. The product list shortened. The adjustment was made.

But. But. If you stand near the lathe, you can see the impression of Edra's boots on the platform where she stood. The root-felt has a memory of pressure. It's faint, and it's fading, but it's there — two shallow depressions, the size and shape of boot soles, where someone stood in one place for long enough that the ground noticed. In a month it will be gone. In two months the root-felt will have grown back to level and the last trace of Edra will be the matchbook in the niche and the stub of candle and Harker's refusal to say her name.

---

The sixth person is Ren.

Ren is the one who is hardest to account for, because Ren's relationship to the Rootworks is not the same as the others'. The others came. Ren was here.

Not in the sense of having arrived before Harker — Ren arrived after Harker, after Pell, before Senn and Tori. But in the sense that Ren seems to have always been here, the way the generator seems to have always been here, the way the creek seems to have always been here. Ren is part of the system in a way that resists explanation. Ren doesn't have a specific job. Or Ren has every job. Ren moves through the Rootworks in a way that suggests an understanding of the place that is not the understanding of a person who learned it but the understanding of a person who grew up in it — except Ren didn't grow up in it. Ren arrived. And then Ren was of it.

Ren maintains the composting system. This is the job that no one else wants because it involves the pits at the edges of the chamber where the cuttings and the scraps and the waste go to decompose, and the pits are the most biologically active part of the Rootworks, which means they're the warmest and the smelliest and the most alive in the specific sense of alive that means *full of breakdown and transformation.* The root-felt in the pits is not the same as the root-felt elsewhere. It's darker. It's wetter. It's actively decomposing, which means it's releasing heat and gases and compounds that the living root-felt absorbs and uses, and the cycle is tighter here than anywhere else in the chamber. You can see the cycle. You can see the cuttings go in and the compost come out and the compost go into the mat and the mat produce new growth and the new growth get cut and the cuttings go in. It's the most honest part of the Rootworks. Nothing is hidden. Nothing is stored. Everything is in the process of becoming something else.

Ren spends hours at the pits. Not working — observing. Sitting on the edge of a pit with a hand in the compost, feeling the heat, feeling the texture, monitoring the process with a tactile attention that goes beyond anything the task requires. Ren is checking the compost the way a doctor checks a pulse, but also the way a parent checks a sleeping child — with a concern that is not about fixing anything but about knowing the state of things. Ren knows the compost the way Harker knows the growth and the way Pell knows the generator and the way Tori knows the architecture, except Ren's knowledge is less specific and more total. Ren understands the Rootworks as a system. Not the components — the system. The way the parts interact. The way the water feeds the growth feeds the fiber feeds the panels feed the market feeds the supplies feed the generator feeds the pumps feed the water. Ren sees the loop. Ren lives in the loop. Ren is, in a sense that no one has articulated and that Ren would deny if asked, the loop's custodian.

Ren is not old. Ren is not young. Ren is the age of the Rootworks, which is to say indeterminate, which is to say the question of Ren's age is the wrong question. The right question is something like: how long have you been here? And the answer is: long enough. Long enough that the platform Ren sleeps on has an impression that is deeper than Pell's and shallower than Harker's, which is a way of measuring time that is accurate but not precise, and which is the only way of measuring time that the Rootworks supports.

---

You don't see all six of them at once. The Rootworks is too small for that to feel natural — six people in a space this size would be a crowd, and the space resists crowds the way it resists straight lines. They overlap. They pass each other in the narrow gaps between the growth, and the passing is brief and mostly wordless — a nod, a shift of the shoulders to make room, the passing of a tool from hand to hand without looking. They eat at different times. They sleep at different times. The generator runs constantly and the pumps run constantly and the growth runs constantly, but the people move in staggered rhythms that mean the chamber always has someone in it and never has everyone in it, and this is the way they like it, or if not like, then tolerate, or if not tolerate, then have stopped questioning, which is the Rootworks' preferred form of acceptance.

When they do gather — once a day, maybe, or once every two days, near the hearth, when someone has food and someone else has food and the food is shared because sharing is easier than not sharing and the hearth is the one place where sitting together makes sense — they don't talk much. The conversation is practical. *The south wall is pushing in.* *The generator's running hot.* *The press needs a new roller.* Information is exchanged. Decisions are made, or not made, which in the Rootworks is often the same thing, because the default is to continue as before and the before has been working and will probably keep working. The decisions that get made are the small ones: which section to prune first, whether to harvest the east mat or the west mat, whether to adjust the fuel mixture or let it run another day. The decisions that don't get made are the large ones: how long to stay, what to do when the grease runs out, what happens if the third cylinder goes, whether to send for parts and if so for what and to whom and how. These decisions are not made because making them would require a shared understanding of the future, and the Rootworks does not have a future. It has a present. The present has been the same for a long time. The present will probably be the same for a long time more. This is not a philosophy. It's an observation, the same way the water weeping through the ceiling is an observation. It's what's happening. It's what keeps happening.

---

They have the smell. All of them. It's in their clothes, which are damp and have been damp for long enough that the dampness is structural, the fibers of the fabric permanently swollen, the weave permanently loosened. It's in their hair, which has the slightly greasy quality of hair that's been washed in soft water without enough soap. It's in their skin, which has the tannin-stain, the faint brown-yellow discoloration that comes from constant contact with the root-felt and the water that runs through it. Harker has it deepest. Ren has it most evenly. Pell has it on his hands and forearms but not his face, because Pell's face is the part of him that's least in contact with the environment. Senn and Tori have it in patches — wherever their skin presses against the fiber for long periods, which means their fingertips, their knees, the insides of their elbows.

The smell is not bad. It's the smell of the Rootworks — the green-rot sweetness, the cellulose, the standing heat — and it has become their smell, or they have become its, and the distinction doesn't matter because after a few months the boundary between a person's scent and a place's scent is not a boundary you can locate. They smell like where they live. Everyone does, if they live somewhere long enough. It's just more noticeable here because the place has such a strong scent to begin with.

---

There is one more thing about the people who live in the Rootworks, and it's the thing that stays with you after you leave, after you climb the bark-soft stairs back to the surface and the air thins and cools and you can breathe without tasting the place on your tongue.

They don't look up.

Not at the ceiling — the ceiling is forty feet overhead and the ceiling is where the water comes from and the ceiling is the boundary of their world, and they have no reason to look at it because they know what it looks like. It looks like the underside of a tongue. It weeps. They know.

They don't look up in the other sense. The sense of expectation. The sense of hope or longing or the imagination of something beyond. They are not trapped. The stairs are there. They can leave. Pell could leave. Senn and Tori could leave. Harker could leave, though Harker's leaving would require a kind of momentum that nineteen years of slow rhythm have thoroughly dissipated. They stay because they stay, and the staying is not a choice they revisit, the way you don't revisit the choice to keep breathing. It just continues.

But the not-looking-up is specific. It's the absence of a gesture you expect from people who live underground. You expect them to glance at the ceiling. You expect them to think about the surface. You expect some sign that they know they are down, that down is not the default, that most of the world is elsewhere. And they don't. Not because they've forgotten — Harker hasn't forgotten, Ren hasn't forgotten. But because the Rootworks is a closed loop and they are part of the loop and the loop doesn't include the surface. The surface is where the product goes. The surface is where the supplies come from. The surface is where Edra went. The surface is not where they are. They are here. Here is warm and dim and wet and the pumps are thumping and the water is weeping and the growth is growing and the light is breathing and it is enough.

It is enough.

That's what lives in the Rootworks. Not people, exactly. Not a community. Not a family or a team or a workforce. A system. A living system that includes six human bodies and a generator and three pumps and a creek and a ceiling and a mass of root-felt that is all one thing or many things and doesn't care which. The system maintains itself. The people are part of the maintenance. They prune the growth and run the machines and process the fiber and eat near the hearth and sleep on platforms that are shaped like them and they do this again the next day and the next day and the next day, and the Rootworks continues, and the continuing is the point, and the point is not a point that anyone articulates because articulating it would require stopping, and stopping is the one thing the system doesn't do.

The pumps keep pumping. The water keeps weeping. The growth keeps growing. The light keeps breathing. The people keep working. And the place — the chamber that should not exist, the settlement built inside a living mass, the workshop that is also a home that is also an organism — the place keeps being what it is. Warm. Loud. Still. Green. Alive in the particular way that something is alive when it has been alive for a very long time and intends to be alive for a very long time more.

It goes on.
