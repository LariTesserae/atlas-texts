# What Lives in the Sunken Loggia

## The Moths

They are not white. That is the first thing to understand. White is a color made by the absence of other colors, and these moths have not arrived at their paleness through absence. They have arrived at it through patience. Their wings are limestone-colored in the way that limestone is limestone-colored — which is to say they are the color of the place where they live, and whether the place has made them this way or they have made themselves this way in the image of the place is a question that requires a kind of thinking the moths do not do.

They rest on the ceiling during the day. This is the only behavior that could be called hiding, though it is not hiding. They are visible on the ceiling. They simply do not move. Their wings are flat against the stone, overlapping slightly at the edges, and from below they look like a texture the ceiling has always had. You could count them if you wanted to. You would lose count. There are perhaps a hundred and twenty, though the number varies by season — more in the wet months when the thin biological film on the stone grows thicker and the moths' larvae have more to eat. The larvae live in the cracks. They are small, pale, soft-bodied, and they eat the film and the lichen's outer edges and each other, sometimes, though not often. Not enough to matter.

The moths descend at dusk. This is the wrong word. They do not descend so much as release — they let go of the ceiling and the air takes them, and they move in lines between the columns as though the columns are stations on a route they have memorized. They have not memorized anything. The routes are a consequence of the air. The chamber's slow exhalation through the fissure creates currents — barely perceptible, nothing you would call wind — and the moths ride them. The straight lines are the paths of least resistance between columns. The moths are not navigating. They are falling slowly in a direction that the air has chosen for them.

They drink from the basins. This is the most deliberate thing they do. A moth will settle on the rim of a basin — its legs touching the wet stone, its body tilted forward — and extend a proboscis that is nearly invisible, thinner than a human hair, into the water. It drinks for ten or fifteen seconds. Then it lifts, turns, and returns to the ceiling or moves to another basin. The moths visit the same basins on the same nights. Not every moth visits every basin. There is a pattern, but it is a pattern of convenience and proximity, not of territory or ownership. The basins fill at dawn and are drunk from at dusk and are empty by the second dusk. The cycle is so regular that you could set a clock by it, if the clock were very slow and kept time in days rather than hours.

When they land on you — and they will, if you stand still — they do not stay long. A minute. Less. They are not investigating you. They are not resting on you. You are a surface. You are warm, and the air is warm, and the stone is warm, and there is no difference between these warmthes to the moth. It lands because it is a thing that lands on surfaces, and you are a surface, and it leaves because the next basin is a basin, and you are not.

If you hold one — and you can, if you are gentle, if you cup your hands around it without closing them — it will not struggle. It will sit in the dark of your palms and you will feel almost nothing. Its wings are so thin that they have almost no weight. Its body is the weight of a breath. You will feel its legs, which are fine and dry and surprisingly warm, and you will feel the faintest vibration of its wings adjusting, not to escape but to maintain position, the way a person shifts in a chair they intend to stay in. And then you will open your hands and it will go, and your palms will be faintly dusty, the dust being scales from its wings, the same color as the stone, and you will rub your palms together and the dust will disappear into the air and become part of the chamber.

## The Lizards

There are thirty-one. The number has been thirty or thirty-one for as long as anything has been counting, which is not long, because nothing here counts. They are small. Twelve centimeters from snout to tail-tip, the tail making up two-thirds of that length. Their bodies are the color of dry sand — not the sand of a beach but the sand of a place where sand has been sitting in the sun for a very long time, pale and fine and warm. The black line from snout to tail is not a stripe but a seam, as though the lizard were made of two halves of the same color pressed together and the line is where the join shows. It is not decorative. It is structural. It is where the scales are smallest and the skin is thinnest, and the lizard's heat enters and exits through this line. It is a thermoregulatory feature. The lizards bask by lying with the black line exposed to the light, and the light enters them through it.

They live in the crevices at the edge of the chamber, in the absolute black where the light cannot reach. They do not need the light to navigate these spaces. They know them. The crevices are not deep — perhaps a meter at most — and they branch, and the branches lead to other branches, and the lizards have occupied this network for long enough that the stone is worn smooth where they pass. Their claws, which are small and sharp and designed for gripping rather than digging, have polished the stone to a low sheen. If you crawled into a crevice — and you could, barely, if you did not mind the dark and the tightness and the absolute absence of any reason to be there — you would find the floor smooth and the walls close and the air warm and smelling faintly of lizard, which smells faintly of warm dust and something mineral, something like the smell of a hot rock.

They emerge in the brightest part of the day. The transition from black crevice to brilliant chamber should be blinding, but the lizards' eyes adjust instantly. Their pupils are round and dark and they do not contract. The light enters the eye fully and the lizard processes it fully and there is no period of adjustment. This suggests their eyes have evolved for exactly this range of brightness — the full light of the chamber and the full dark of the crevices — and nothing else. A lizard taken outside, into sunlight, would not be able to see. A lizard taken into deeper darkness, into a cave, would not be able to see. It is adapted to this place and no other. It is not that it could not survive elsewhere. It is that it has no elsewhere. It does not know that elsewhere exists. It does not know that existence knows that elsewhere exists.

They bask. They find a spot on the floor where the light is strongest — and because the light fills the chamber evenly, this is not a matter of finding a bright spot but of finding a spot where the stone is warmest, which is a matter of finding a slight rise in the floor where the stone is thinnest and the heat from below rises closest to the surface. There are seven such rises. The lizards distribute themselves among them — four or five per rise, sometimes six, their bodies not touching but close, arranged in patterns that look deliberate but are simply the result of each lizard choosing the warmest point on the rise and the warmest points being a fixed number and the lizards being a fixed number and the math working out.

They eat the moths. Not often — perhaps one moth per lizard per week. The moths are not their primary food source. The primary food source is the small flies that breed in the damp trough where rainwater collects during storms. The flies are tiny — the size of a grain of salt — and they swarm above the trough in numbers that fluctuate with the water level. When the trough is full after a rain, there are thousands. When it has been dry for weeks, there are dozens. The lizards catch them by sitting at the edge of the trough and waiting, their mouths open, their tongues ready, and the flies come to them. It is not hunting. It is the opposite of hunting. It is being in the place where food will arrive and opening your mouth.

They also eat beetles, occasionally. A beetle on its circuit will pass near a basking lizard, and the lizard will take it. This happens rarely. The beetles' circuits are designed — or have evolved, which is the same thing over a long enough time — to avoid the basking spots. But a lizard shifts position, or a beetle's circuit drifts, and they meet, and the beetle is taken. The other beetles do not learn from this. The beetle that is taken does not learn from this. The beetle that is taken is dead, and the other beetles continue their circuits, and the lizard digests, and the chamber continues.

When they move, they move slowly. This is not laziness. This is economy. The chamber provides everything they need at a pace that does not require speed. The flies come to them. The warmth comes to them. The water comes to them in basins on the floor, though the lizards do not drink from the basins. They get their water from the flies. They get their water from the moths. They get their water from the stone itself, which is damp and which they absorb through their skin when they are in the crevices. They do not need to seek. They need only to be. And they are. Thirty-one of them. Or thirty.

The oldest lizard — the one with the thickest tail and the darkest line and the most worn claws — has been here for twelve years. Or perhaps fourteen. Or perhaps sixteen. Lizards do not carry calendars. This lizard has basked on the third rise from the east for as long as the other lizards have been alive. It has eaten perhaps four thousand moths and one hundred thousand flies and twelve beetles. It has drunk from the moisture in the stone and the bodies of its food. It has never mated. None of them mate here. The eggs are laid in the crevices — two or three per female per year, left in a hollow scratched in the stone and abandoned — and the young hatch and find their way to the chamber or do not. The old lizard was once an egg in a hollow in a crevice. It found its way to the chamber. It has not left since. It will not leave. When it dies, it will die on the third rise from the east, and its body will dry in the light, and the beetles will find it and take what they can, and the silverfish will take the rest, and the stone will absorb what remains, and the third rise from the east will have one more lizard's worth of warmth in it, which is no warmth at all, which is nothing, which is enough.

## The Silverfish

They are the reason the shadowed passages are clean. This is their function, if function is the word — and it is, because they have one, and they perform it without variation, without error, without ambition.

They are translucent. You can see through them. You can see their digestive tracts, which are the color of what they eat, which is the color of the biological film, which is a darkness so subtle it is almost not a color at all. When a silverfish has eaten recently, its gut is visible as a thin dark line running the length of its body. When it has not eaten recently, it is nearly invisible — a transparency on stone, a ripple in the air close to the wall.

They live in the deep cracks. All of them. There are no silverfish in the main chamber. There are no silverfish in the light. They do not enter the light. They do not need to. The film they eat grows in the dark, in the damp, in the places where the light has never reached and will never reach. The film is bacteria and lichen — a different lichen than the pale green on the western wall, a darker lichen, a lichen that does not use light because it has never had light and does not know that light is something things use. The silverfish eat this lichen and this bacteria and the result is that the stone in the shadowed passages is bare. Clean. Pale. As though someone has been maintaining it. Someone has. The silverfish have. For longer than the lichen on the western wall has been growing, which is very long.

Their population is unknown. It cannot be estimated. The cracks go deep and branch and the silverfish go where the cracks go. There could be a thousand. There could be ten thousand. There could be more. They are small — a centimeter, perhaps a centimeter and a half — and they are silent, and they are in the dark, and counting them would require going into the dark with a light, and the light would change the film and the film would change the silverfish and the silverfish would change the stone and the stone would change the chamber, and the chamber does not need to be changed.

They live long. This is known because their growth is visible — the older ones are larger, and the largest ones are perhaps three centimeters, which would take, at the growth rate observed in the ones that occasionally venture to the edge of the light and can be seen, perhaps twenty years. Perhaps thirty. They live long because nothing kills them. There are no predators in the cracks. The lizards do not go deep enough. The beetles do not go into the dark at all. The silverfish are alone in the cracks, and they eat and they grow and they reproduce and they die, and their dead are eaten by the other silverfish, and the film grows over what remains, and the cycle is so complete that nothing enters it and nothing leaves it.

They are not the chamber's secret. They are the chamber's maintenance. They are the thing that keeps the dark parts dark and the clean parts clean. Without them, the shadowed passages would be colonized — the film would thicken, the lichen would spread, the stone would disappear under layers of life, and the chamber's edges would become something other than stone. The silverfish prevent this. Not intentionally. Not with any awareness of what they are doing. They eat because they are hungry. They are hungry because they are alive. They are alive because the film is there. The film is there because the stone is damp. The stone is damp because the water moves through it. The water moves through it because the mesa is limestone and limestone takes water and passes it through and the water dissolves the stone and the dissolved stone feeds the film and the film feeds the silverfish and the silverfish keep the stone bare, and the bare stone takes the water and passes it through.

This is the chamber's deepest cycle. It is the cycle that maintains the chamber's shape. The erosion that carved the chamber is the same force that feeds the silverfish, and the silverfish are the same force that keeps the erosion from filling the chamber with life. The chamber is not alive. But it is maintained by life, and it maintains life, and the distinction between the two is not something the chamber observes.

## The Beetles

There are ten. Not approximately ten. Ten. This is the number that the chamber holds. This is the number that the chamber has held for as long as the beetles have been here, which is longer than the lizards, which is longer than the moths, which is perhaps as long as the silverfish, which is perhaps as long as the chamber has been a chamber.

They are dark. Not black — dark the way the shadowed passages are dark, which is to say dark in contrast to the light but not truly colorless. Their shells have a faint sheen, a brown so deep it reads as black in the chamber's amber light, and the sheen catches the light and returns it as a dim polish, like the surface of a stone that has been rubbed. Their legs are designed for gripping. This is not metaphor. Their tarsi — the segments at the ends of their legs — are covered in fine hairs that adhere to smooth stone through a combination of surface tension and mechanical interlocking at a microscopic scale. They can walk on the ceiling. They can walk on the walls. They can walk on the floor. They do walk on all of these surfaces, but their circuits are primarily on the floor, because the floor is where their food is — the organic detritus that accumulates in the shallow troughs and basins, the dead moths that fall from the ceiling, the dried remains of flies, the occasional lizard dropping, the thin film of biological matter that settles out of the air and onto the stone like dust.

They walk in circuits. Each beetle has a circuit. The circuits are not the same, but they overlap. The overlaps are fixed points — specific locations on the floor where two or more circuits cross. The beetles do not choose these points. The points are a consequence of the geometry of the circuits, which are a consequence of the floor's shape, which is a consequence of the stone's erosion, which is a consequence of the water, which is a consequence of the mesa's geology. The beetles are walking paths that the stone has determined. They do not know this. They do not need to know this.

The circuit takes approximately three hours. This is not because the beetles are slow, though they are slow. It is because the circuit is long — it covers most of the chamber floor — and the beetles stop frequently. They stop to eat. They stop to investigate a crack. They stop at the basins and drink. They stop at the water thread and drink. They stop for reasons that are not visible — perhaps resting, perhaps sensing something through their antennae that requires a pause, perhaps simply stopping because stopping is something that happens between walking.

When two beetles meet at an overlap, they pause. They touch antennae. The contact lasts several seconds — sometimes two, sometimes five, sometimes eight. During this contact, they are still. Their legs are locked. Their antennae are extended and the tips touch — the left antenna of one to the right antenna of the other, then both to both, then the right to the left. The pattern varies. It is not random. It is a language, or it is the precursor to a language, or it is the remnant of a language, or it is nothing at all — a reflex, a twitch, a response to proximity that means nothing but happens because it has always happened.

After the contact, they continue. They do not change their circuits. They do not alter their speed. They do not follow each other. They separate and resume their paths and do not meet again until the next overlap, which will be the same overlap or a different one, depending on their relative speeds, which are constant, which means the meeting points are predictable, which means the beetles meet the same beetles at the same points at the same intervals with the same regularity as the water falling into the basins.

The oldest beetle is the largest. Its shell is smoother than the others — more polished, as though it has been walking the circuit for so long that the air itself has worn its shell the way the water has worn the stone. Its circuit is the longest. It covers the most ground. It has the most overlaps — four, with four other beetles. It walks past the grooves on the deepest wall every three hours. It has been walking past them for years. It has never stopped at them. It has never investigated them. The grooves are not part of its circuit. They are not food. They are not water. They are not an overlap. They are stone.

When a beetle dies — and they do die, eventually, though the deaths are rare and unremarkable — its circuit is empty for a time. The other beetles do not alter their circuits to cover the gap. The gap exists. The food that the dead beetle would have eaten is eaten by other beetles on overlapping circuits, or it remains on the floor and dries and becomes part of the stone's film. Eventually, a new beetle appears — not from outside, from inside, from the eggs that are laid in the cracks at the chamber's edge, in the shallow shadow where the light fades, in the places where the beetles retreat to reproduce. The new beetle is small and dark and its legs grip the stone as tightly as any adult's. It finds a circuit. It may be the circuit of the dead beetle, or it may be a new circuit, a path that no beetle has walked before, a path that the stone has always held but that no beetle has needed. It walks the circuit. It takes three hours. It meets the other beetles at the overlaps. It touches antennae. It continues.

There are ten. There will be ten.

## The Lichen

It is the only color that is not stone or shadow.

This is not entirely true — the moths' wings have a faint amber tint, the lizards' line is black, the water thread catches the light and returns it in a thin ribbon of reflected amber. But the lichen is the only color in the chamber that is its own. The pale green — almost white, almost gray, almost the color of the stone but not — is the color of something alive and growing and choosing to be the color it is. Or not choosing. Being. Being the color it is because the color it is is the color that survives in this light, in this damp, on this wall.

It grows only on the western wall. This is specific. It is not a preference. It is a requirement. The western wall is the wall where the water thread traces its path, and the water thread is the only source of flowing moisture in the chamber, and the lichen needs flowing moisture — not standing water, not condensation, but the slow movement of water over stone that carries dissolved minerals and deposits them in a film thin enough for the lichen to absorb. The basins on the floor hold water, but it is still water, and still water does not carry minerals in the way moving water does. The condensation on the ceiling falls and is gone. Only the thread moves, and only the western wall has the thread, and so only the western wall has the lichen.

It grows slowly. A patch the size of a hand may be a thousand years old. This is not a metaphor for slowness. This is a measurement. The lichen grows outward at a rate that has been estimated — not here, not in this chamber, but in other places where similar lichens grow — at a fraction of a millimeter per year. The patches on the western wall are the size of hands, the size of open books, the size of a person crouching. The largest patch, near the top of the wall where the thread begins, is the size of a door. It is the oldest thing in the chamber. It is older than the beetles, older than the lizards' lineage, older than the moths' adaptation to the stone. It may be older than the fissure's current shape. It may be older than the grooves on the deepest wall.

It is alive. This is worth saying because it is not obviously alive. It does not move. It does not react. If you touch it — and you can, if you reach the western wall, which you can, because the chamber is not deep and the light reaches there — it feels like stone. Dry stone. Slightly rough. It does not feel like something alive. It does not feel like something that is growing. But it is. At its edges, where the pale green fades into the bare limestone, there is a frontier so thin it cannot be seen, a line where the lichen's hyphae are reaching outward into the stone, dissolving it with acids so weak they would not be detectable on a human tongue, incorporating the dissolved minerals into its body, becoming infinitesimally larger, infinitesimally older, infinitesimally more itself.

The lichen does not grow on the columns. This is not because the columns are a different kind of stone — they are the same limestone, the same mineral composition. It is because the columns do not have the water thread. The columns are dry. The columns are smooth and warm and beautiful in the light, and the lichen does not care about beauty, and the lichen does not grow on them. The lichen grows where the water is, and the water is on the western wall, and the lichen is on the western wall, and this has been true for longer than the columns have been columns.

The lichen does not grow on the floor. The floor is damp, but it is not flowing-damp. It is sitting-damp. The lichen cannot use sitting-damp. And the floor is walked on — by the beetles, by the lizards, by the occasional thing that finds its way in through the fissure — and the lichen cannot grow where it is walked on. It is not fragile, exactly. A footstep would not destroy it. But a thousand footsteps would, and the floor has had a million footsteps, and the lichen has learned this, or rather the lichen has been shaped by this, or rather the lichens that grew on the floor have been ground away and the lichens that remain are the ones that grew where they were not ground, and the place they were not ground is the western wall, and so the western wall is where they grow.

It is the color of the chamber's age. If the chamber had a color — if the warm stone and the amber light and the pale moths and the sand-colored lizards could be distilled into a single hue — it would be the color of this lichen. Not because the lichen is the most beautiful thing in the chamber. It is not. The light is more beautiful. The columns are more beautiful. The water thread is more beautiful in its quiet persistence. But the lichen is the chamber's own color. It is the color that the chamber has made, or that has been made in the chamber, or that has emerged from the chamber the way the chamber has emerged from the mesa — not by intention, not by design, but by the long patient accumulation of what works and the long patient erosion of what does not.

## The Things That Do Not Live Here

There are no bats. This is notable. A space like this — warm, sheltered, with crevices for roosting and an opening for access — would typically hold bats. The fissure is large enough. The ceiling is high enough. The insects are plentiful enough. But there are no bats. There have never been bats, as far as the chamber's record shows — and the chamber's record is the guano that would be on the floor if bats had ever been here, and there is no guano, and there has been no guano, and the stone does not remember guano. The bats do not know about the chamber. The chamber does not know about bats. These two ignorances are compatible and have been compatible for the entire history of both.

There are no birds. The fissure is vertical and narrow, and birds do not navigate vertical narrow fissures. There are no rodents. The chamber has no food that rodents eat — no seeds, no vegetation, no stored caches. There are no spiders, because the spiders that would live here would need to catch the moths and the flies, and the moths and the flies are caught by the lizards, and the spiders have no niche. There are no fish. There is no reason there should be. There are no amphibians. The chamber is warm and damp, and amphibians might do well here, but amphibians need standing water to breed, and the standing water in the basins evaporates daily, and the trough is too shallow for tadpoles, and so there are no amphibians, and there never have been.

There are no humans. Or rather, no humans live here. Humans have been here — the grooves on the deepest wall suggest this, though they do not prove it. Someone, at some point, may have stood in this chamber and looked at the walls and felt the warmth and heard the drops falling and made marks on the stone. Or someone did not. The grooves may be natural. The grooves may be the work of water or stress or the slow movement of the stone itself. The chamber does not remember. The chamber does not distinguish between what was done to it and what happened to it. These are the same thing, over geological time.

If a human lived here — if someone tried — they would need water and food and warmth, and all of these are present, but not in quantities that sustain a human. The basins hold mouthfuls. The trough holds a shallow pool for days after rain. The lizards are small and few. The moths are insubstantial. A human could survive here for days. Perhaps weeks. Not longer. The chamber is not for humans. The chamber is for what lives in it, and what lives in it is small and slow and patient and requires very little and has been here for a very long time and will be here for a very long time after, and this is not a judgment of humans, who are large and fast and impatient and require a great deal and have not been here for a very long time and will not be here for a very long time after. It is simply a description of what works.

## What Lives Here

What lives here is the chamber. The moths and the lizards and the silverfish and the beetles and the lichen and the water thread and the light and the stone are all the chamber, in the way that a body is a body and not merely a collection of organs. The chamber lives. Not biologically. Not consciously. Not in any way that requires a definition or a test or a boundary between living and not-living. The chamber lives in the way that a process lives — by continuing, by maintaining itself, by taking in and putting out, by changing slowly and being the same slowly, by being different from what it was without knowing the difference.

The moths are the chamber's breath. The lizards are the chamber's warmth. The silverfish are the chamber's memory of its own shape. The beetles are the chamber's heartbeat — slow, regular, patient, three hours long. The lichen is the chamber's color. The water thread is the chamber's voice. The light is the chamber's time.

And the stone is the chamber's body, and the body is warm, and the warmth holds everything, and everything is held.
