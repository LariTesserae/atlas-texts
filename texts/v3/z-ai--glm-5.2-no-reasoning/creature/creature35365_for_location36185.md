# What Lives in the Seep

---

There is a confusion that happens when you say "nothing lives here," because the seep is so thoroughly occupied.

Not by visitors. Not by passers-through. By inhabitants. It is only that the inhabitants operate at scales and tempos that make the word "live" feel too active, too theatrical, too much like a verb that requires events.

---

## The Moss

Start with the dark band on the back wall, because it is the closest thing the seep has to a ruling presence.

It is not one organism. This is the first thing to understand about it. The dark felt that follows the moisture up the stone is a consortium — several kinds of life tangled so completely that they have become a single surface. There is a moss, yes, but there is also an alga threaded through it, and a fungus that has built the architecture the other two inhabit, and there are bacteria living in the microscopic films between the moss stems that do something none of the others can do — fix nitrogen from the thin air, pull it down into a form the others can use. They are a committee. They are a cooperative. They have been in session for a very long time.

The moss portion — and it is possible to separate the concept, if not the physical reality — is built like a city viewed from very high up. Each individual plant is a stem no taller than two millimeters, bearing leaves so small they are visible only as a texture, a roughness, a quality of the surface that catches light differently than bare stone. But the stems grow together so densely that they form a continuous skin. Each stem touches its neighbors. Each stem shares the water that condenses on its surface with the stems around it, not by intention but by physics — the water films that coat them are connected, a single matrix of dampness held in place by surface tension and by the fine structure of the plants themselves. They are drinking the same drink through the same straw, and the straw is the entire wall.

They reproduce, or rather they *have* reproduced, because the word implies a present activity that is too fast for what actually happens. There are no spore capsules now. There may have been, once, in some period when the moisture was slightly higher or the light slightly stronger or some condition aligned that does not align now. The spores that were produced then landed nearby — there was nowhere else for them to go, no wind to carry them — and germinated, and grew into new stems that pressed against the old ones, and the colony expanded millimeter by millimeter across the wet stone over spans of time that make the word "colony" feel too temporary. This is not a settlement. It is a metropolitan area that has been continuously inhabited since before the word "metropolitan" existed.

If you press your face close to the moss — and you would have to, because from standing height it is merely a dark stain — you would see that it is not flat. It has topography. There are tiny ridges where the growth is slightly thicker, following some vein in the stone where the moisture is slightly more abundant. There are shallow valleys where the growth thins. There are small craters, perhaps a centimeter across, where something disturbed the surface — a grain of sand that fell and sat on the moss and killed the stems beneath it, leaving a small pale scar that is slowly filling in from the edges. The scars heal at a rate too slow to watch. They heal the way coastlines heal after glaciers retreat.

The moss is the color it is — that black-green, that almost-grey — because it is saturated. Not with excess water, but with time. The pigments in it have oxidized slowly over decades or centuries, darkened by the same process that turns old paper brown and old photographs sepia. It is the color of something that has been wet for longer than wetness usually lasts. If you were to take a piece of it and dry it — and this would be a violence, a disruption of something that has not been disrupted in an extraordinarily long time — it would turn a brighter green. A younger green. But it would not survive the change. It has committed to being wet. It does not know how to be anything else.

## The Alga

Inside the moss, visible only as a slight greenish cast in the dark felt if you know what you are looking for, there is an alga. A single-celled organism, or rather a population of single-celled organisms, each one a sphere so small that a hundred of them could line up across the head of a pin and still leave room.

Each algal cell lives inside the structure of the moss, not on it but in it, nested among the stems and leaves like something hidden in a forest. It does not have the machinery to hold water on its own. It does not have the structural strength to resist drying. It relies on the moss for shelter and on the fungus for the structural framework that keeps the moss in place. In return, it does the one thing the others cannot: it turns light into sugar.

This matters more than it might seem, because the light here is poor. The sky is overcast. The hollow is shaded by its own walls. The light that reaches the moss band on the back wall has already bounced off sandstone at least once, losing energy with each reflection. The alga works with what arrives, which is not much. It works slowly. It produces just enough to keep itself alive and to leak a little into the shared system, feeding the fungus and the moss in quantities so small that the word "feeding" implies a generosity that is not really there. It is more like a tax. The alga pays a tax of sugar to the system that houses it, and the system continues, and the alga continues, and neither one has any awareness of the arrangement.

The algal cells divide. One becomes two, two becomes four. This is the fastest thing that happens in the seep — a cell division that takes perhaps a day, perhaps two, in conditions this marginal. It is the closest thing the seep has to a heartbeat. And it is invisible.

## The Fungus

The fungus is the part you cannot see, and it is the part that makes the moss possible.

It runs through the dark felt on the wall like a plumbing system laid by something that understood water but not geometry. Fine threads — hyphae, they are called, though the name is too clean for what they actually are — spread through and beneath and among the moss stems, binding them to the stone, creating a matrix that holds moisture against the surface of the rock with a tenacity that the moss alone could not manage. The fungus is the reason the moss can grow on a vertical wall. The fungus is the reason the water stays long enough to be used.

The hyphae are thinner than the moss stems by an order of magnitude. They are essentially invisible without magnification. They penetrate the stone itself — not deeply, perhaps a millimeter, perhaps less — following the pores of the sandstone, widening them imperceptibly, creating channels through which moisture can move more freely. The fungus is, in a very real sense, mining the stone. It is extracting minerals from the sandstone — calcium, iron, phosphorus — and passing them into the shared system. This is why the moss can grow on a surface that should not, by any reasonable measure, support life. The fungus feeds it rock.

It does this very slowly. The sandstone is hard, or hard enough, and the hyphae advance into it at a rate that is difficult to measure on any timescale a human would find meaningful. But they advance. Over decades, over centuries, they have etched their way into the face of the wall, and the wall has accommodated them, and the mineral streaks that descend the stone — the dark branching lines that look like veins — are partly the traces of water and partly the traces of this mining, the record of a process that has been ongoing since the seep began to seep.

The fungus does not produce mushrooms. It does not produce any visible fruiting body. It has no reason to: the conditions for spore dispersal do not exist here, because there is no wind, and because the hollow is so small and so enclosed that any spores released would simply fall back onto the same surface. The fungus reproduces vegetatively, by growing, by extending its hyphae into new territory, by the slow lateral expansion of a body that has no defined edge. It is a single organism in the same way a forest is a single organism — technically arguable, functionally true. It has been growing in the same direction, outward from some center it has long since lost track of, for longer than the hollow has had its current shape.

## The Succulents

At the edge of the pool, the dozen low plants with their grey-green leaves sit on the damp grit like objects that have been placed there by someone who left and did not come back.

They are not placed. They are rooted, though "rooted" is a strong word for what they do. Each one sends a single taproot down through the grit to a depth of perhaps three centimeters, where it touches the sandstone and stops, or rather begins to follow the surface of the stone horizontally, seeking the thin film of moisture that accumulates at the boundary between grit and rock. The root does not penetrate the stone. It grows along it, in the grit, in the damp layer that is neither stone nor air.

The leaves are the real story. Each plant has four to seven leaves, depending on its age and its position relative to the pool, and each leaf is a reservoir. It is thick, fleshy, almost succulent in the way a cactus is succulent, but adapted not to drought but to *interval*. The water here does not arrive on a schedule. The pool rises and falls on a rhythm too slow to observe, and when the pool is low and the grit at its edge dries, the plants must wait. They wait by spending what they have stored. A leaf that is full is turgid, firm, slightly cool to the touch. A leaf that has been waiting a long time is softer, wrinkled at the edges, the grey-green darkened to something closer to the color of the moss on the wall. It has not died. It has *paused*.

The plants do not flower. They have not flowered in the time they have been observed, which is no time at all, because no one has been observing. But they have flowered, or they could — somewhere in the cellular machinery there is the instruction set for it, the code for producing a bloom, but the conditions for triggering that code have not been met in so long that the code may have degraded. Mutation accumulates in unused genes. The plants may have forgotten how to flower. They may be only the vegetative remnants of something that once reproduced more conventionally and has since committed entirely to persistence.

They reproduce now, if they reproduce at all, by division. A plant that has been in one place long enough will produce a small offset at its base — a cluster of leaves that is genetically identical to the parent, connected by a thread of tissue so fine it is barely visible. The offset grows. The connection severs, or rots through, or is abraded away by the slow movement of grit. The offset becomes independent. It is not reproduction so much as cloning by attrition. It is the slowest form of increase: one becomes two over a span of time that makes the word "becomes" feel too sudden.

There are twelve plants. There may have been eleven before, and ten before that, and four before that, and one before that — a single seed that landed here, carried by some wind that existed before the hollow was as enclosed as it is now, or dropped by a bird that paused here in some era when birds still paused here. Or there may have been more, and the population has been declining, each generation losing one, the arithmetic of attrition grinding toward a zero that has not yet arrived but that the logic of the place implies. It is impossible to know. The plants do not record their history. They simply sit and store and wait and, very occasionally, divide.

## The Lichen

Higher on the walls, where the stone is dry, the pale grey patches are lichen. They are the oldest things here, and they are also the simplest.

Each patch is a single organism, or rather a single relationship — a fungus and an alga, the same partnership that exists in the moss band but expressed in a different form, a drier form, a form that has given up on lushness and committed entirely to endurance. The fungus provides structure. The alga provides food. The partnership provides something that neither could provide alone: the ability to live on bare stone in dry air with almost no input.

The lichen grows so slowly that measuring its growth rate is an exercise in humility. A patch the size of a coin may have taken a century to reach that size. The largest patches, which are perhaps the size of an open hand, may be a thousand years old, or more, or less — there is no way to know, because lichen does not lay down rings or layers that can be counted. It simply expands, and the expansion is so gradual that from one year to the next it is invisible. From one decade to the next it is perhaps visible, if you knew where to look. From one century to the next it is obvious.

The lichen does not use the seep. It is above the seep, above the wet shelf, above the moss. It lives on whatever moisture condenses on the stone surface during the temperature swings between day and night — and the swings here are small, the overcast sky acting as a blanket, so the condensation is minimal. It lives on dust that settles out of the still air and provides trace nutrients. It lives on light that reaches it after bouncing off the opposite wall. It lives on almost nothing, and it has been living on almost nothing for longer than anything else in the hollow.

When the air is dry enough, the lichen makes its sound — the faint ticking, the click of expansion and contraction. This is not communication. This is not expression. This is the sound of a material responding to physics. The lichen's body, which is mostly mineral at this point — accumulated dust, calcium oxalate crystals, the debris of its own slow metabolism — expands when humidity rises and contracts when it falls, and the differential stress between the lichen and the stone beneath it produces a tiny pop, a release of energy so small that it is barely mechanical. But it is audible, in the silence of the hollow, in the absence of any other sound. It is the lichen's way of announcing that it is still here, except it is not announcing anything. It is just here, and it makes a sound, and the sound is a side effect.

If you scrape the lichen, it comes off as powder. This is not because it is dead. It is because it is dry, and dry lichen is brittle, and brittle things crumble. The patch you scraped will not grow back in your lifetime, or in your grandchildren's lifetime. It will grow back, eventually, from the edges, from the hyphae that remain in the microscopic crevices of the stone. But it will take centuries. This is why scraping it is a kind of violence, even though it does not feel like violence. You are removing something that took longer to make than your entire family line.

The lichen is the thing in the hollow that is most indifferent to you. The moss at least benefits from your breath — the carbon dioxide you exhale is available to the alga inside it, a gift you did not intend to give. The succulents benefit from your shadow — a momentary reduction in light that does not matter to them but that signals something about the character of the place, that it can be interrupted. But the lichen does not benefit from you in any way. Your breath does not reach the upper walls. Your shadow does not fall on it. You could stand in the hollow for a year and the lichen would not know.

## The Bacteria

There is life in the seep that is not visible, not audible, not tangible in any way a human body can detect without instruments. It is the deepest layer of the life here, and it is the most numerous.

In the film of water that covers the wet shelf at the base of the back wall, there are bacteria. In the moisture that moves through the pores of the sandstone itself, there are bacteria. In the shallow pool, in the grit, in the thin layer of dampness between the moss and the stone, there are bacteria. They are everywhere that is wet, and they are doing what bacteria do: consuming, transforming, cycling.

They are not doing much. The inputs are so small, the energy so limited, the temperatures so steady, that the bacterial communities here operate at a metabolic rate that is almost geological. A single bacterial cell in the seep might divide once a month, or once a year, or once a decade. There is no way to know without measuring, and no one has measured, because the place does not invite measurement. It invites sitting. It invites the kind of attention that does not require data.

But they are there. They are the reason the pool is the color of weak tea — the dissolved minerals that give it its tint are partly the product of bacterial metabolism, the transformation of iron and manganese in the stone into forms that enter solution. They are the reason the moss can grow on a surface that should be too poor in nutrients: the bacteria fix nitrogen from the air, and the nitrogen leaks into the system, and the moss takes it up. They are the reason the grit on the floor has a faint organic smell if you press your nose to it — not the smell of soil, which is rich and complex, but the smell of a single process, a single kind of chemistry, occurring at a rate that is just fast enough to produce a trace.

There may be something else in the bacteria that is harder to name. There may be a kind of community — not communication, not consciousness, not anything that implies intent, but a *pattern*. The bacteria in the seep are not random. They are structured, organized by the gradients of moisture and mineral and light that the hollow provides. The ones at the top of the wet shelf, where the water film is thinnest and the oxygen highest, are different from the ones at the bottom, where the water pools and the oxygen drops. The ones inside the stone, in the pores, are different again — anaerobic, slow, living without oxygen in a world that is so close to the oxygenated surface that the distance is almost meaningless, and yet it is enough. There are layers. There is order. There is a system.

The system has no name. It does not need one.

## The Water

The water in the seep is not alive. It is important to say this, because the seep is so full of life and so defined by water that it would be easy to conflate the two. The water is not alive. It is the medium. It is the condition. It is the thing that makes life possible here without itself being alive.

But the water has a character. It has a history. It has been in this rock for a long time — how long is not clear, because the movement of water through sandstone pores is so slow that a molecule of water entering the rock at the surface might take decades or centuries to emerge at the back wall of the hollow. The water that touches the moss now entered the stone before the moss existed. It is older than the life it sustains.

It carries the stone with it. Not visibly, not in quantities that would register on any instrument designed for a faster world, but in solution — calcium carbonate, iron oxide, silica, traces of things that dissolve so slowly and travel so patiently that the water becomes a record of everywhere it has been. The mineral ridges on the back wall, the pale bone-colored deposits that feel like fine sandpaper, are the water's signature. Each ridge is a line of precipitation, a place where the water emerged and evaporated and left behind what it was carrying. The branching lines that look like veins are the water's history written on the surface of the stone.

The water does not know this. It does not know anything. It moves because physics requires it to move — gravity, capillary action, pressure differentials too small to feel. It emerges because the rock ends and the air begins, and the water cannot stay in the rock if the rock is not there. It evaporates because the air is drier than the stone. It returns because the grit at the base of the wall absorbs it, and the stone below the grit draws it back in, and the cycle closes.

If the water were faster, the seep would be a spring, and the hollow would be a different place — a wetter place, a louder place, a place where life could be larger and more various. If the water were slower, the seep would be dry, and the hollow would be a bare stone wound with nothing in it but lichen. The water is at exactly the rate it is, and this rate determines everything: the moss, the succulents, the bacteria, the tone of the light on the wet stone, the silence. The rate is the place.

## The Pool

The pool is where everything in the seep comes to rest.

Not everything. Not the moss, which clings to the wall. Not the lichen, which holds the dry stone. Not the bacteria inside the rock, which will never see the pool. But the water, yes. The minerals, yes. The light, in a way — the pool catches the light and holds it, reflects it, offers it back to the sky that provided it.

The pool is the seep's only mirror, and what it reflects is the seep itself. The sandstone ceiling, the pale sky, the walls with their dark streaks and pale lichen patches. And you, if you are there, dimly, as a smudge. The pool does not care what it reflects. It is not a mirror by design. It is a mirror because it is flat and still and water, and these properties together produce reflection, and the reflection is incidental.

But the pool is also the place where the system is most visible, because it is the lowest point, the collecting point, the place where everything that moves downhill — water, minerals, grit, the occasional dead cell from the moss or the succulents — arrives. The pool is the seep's ledger. Its contents are the sum of everything that has entered the system since the last time it was full enough to overflow, which it never is, because the hollow is shaped so that the lowest point is so shallow and so small that the water never accumulates beyond a few centimeters. It arrives, it sits, it evaporates, it leaves its dissolved minerals behind, and the cycle begins again.

The minerals accumulate. This is why the pool is the color of weak tea, and why the stone around it is darker than the stone farther away. Over very long time, the pool has been building up a crust of evaporite — calcium carbonate, mostly, with iron oxide giving it a brownish tint — that is so thin it is not a crust at all but a stain, a darkening of the surface that makes the pool's former extents visible as a kind of bathtub ring. You can see, if you look, that the pool has been larger in the past. The dark stain extends perhaps ten centimeters beyond the current waterline. At some point, the seepage rate was higher, or the evaporation rate was lower, and the pool was wider and deeper. It has since contracted. It may contract further. It may expand again. It follows a rhythm that is not a rhythm but a response to conditions that change too slowly to be called change.

## The Grit

On the floor, in the low points, the floury sand is not inert.

It is mostly mineral — crushed sandstone, grains that detached from the walls and ceiling and were rounded by their fall and by their long rest on the floor. But it is also partly organic. The dead cells of the moss, carried down by the film of water that trickles — not trickles, there is no trickling, but descends, in a way that has no adequate verb — from the wet shelf to the floor. The dried fragments of lichen that flaked off the upper walls and drifted down in the still air. The shed leaves of the succulents, which do not shed often but which have shed, over the decades, a few of their oldest leaves, which sit where they fall and are slowly incorporated into the grit.

The grit is the seep's soil, if soil can be something so thin and so poor. It is the accumulation of everything that has been broken down in the hollow, mixed with everything that has been carried in by the water and left behind. It is where the bacteria are most active, relatively speaking — the organic fragments provide carbon, the mineral grains provide surface area, the dampness at the interface with the stone provides water. It is a habitat. A thin, poor, slow habitat, but a habitat.

If you scuff it, the pale cloud that rises is mostly mineral. It settles quickly because the grains are fine and the air is still. But the cloud also contains something else — a faint, invisible component of biological matter, spores and fragments and cells, that drifts in the still air for a moment longer than the mineral grains and then also settles. The cloud is the seep, aerosolized. It is the closest thing the seep has to breath.

## What Lives Here

What lives in the seep is not a species or a community or an ecosystem. What lives in the seep is a *process* — a set of relationships between water and stone and light and air and the slow transformations that occur when these things are in contact for longer than contact usually lasts.

The moss lives by being wet. The fungus lives by mining stone. The alga lives by turning light into sugar. The bacteria live by transforming what the others produce. The lichen lives by enduring. The succulents live by waiting. The water lives by moving, though "moving" is too fast a word. The stone lives by being moved through.

Nothing here is independent. Nothing here could exist alone. The moss without the fungus would fall off the wall. The fungus without the alga would starve. The alga without the moss would dry out. The bacteria without the minerals would have nothing to transform. The minerals without the water would stay in the stone. The water without the minerals would be invisible. The lichen without the lichen's own particular alga would be a fungus on a dry wall with nothing to eat. The succulents without the pool would be grey-green smears on dry grit, waiting for a water that would not come.

Everything here is dependent on everything else, and the dependencies are so old and so tight that the boundaries between organisms have started to dissolve. The moss is not moss. It is moss-alga-fungus-bacteria. The lichen is not lichen. It is lichen-alga-mineral-dust-time. The pool is not pool. It is water-mineral-light-reflection-history. The grit is not grit. It is stone-organic-water-air-process.

The seep is not a place where things live. The seep is a thing that lives. It lives slowly, at a pace that makes the word "live" feel like it belongs to a different language — a language with more events in it, more beginnings and endings, more verbs. The seep's language has only one verb, and it is *continue*.

## The Feeling of What Lives Here

If you sit in the hollow long enough — long enough for your breathing to slow, for your eyes to adjust to the flat light, for your ears to stop expecting sound and start accepting the faint high tone of water in stone — you begin to feel what lives here. Not see it. Not hear it. Feel it, in the way you feel a low-frequency vibration through the floor of a building, something you cannot locate but that is present.

It feels like patience. Not the patience of waiting, which implies an expectation, but the patience of continuation, which does not. It feels like a system that has found its rhythm and does not need to find another. It feels like something that has been solved — not a problem, because there was no problem, but a configuration, an arrangement of matter and energy that works, that has worked for a very long time, that will work for a very long time to come.

It does not feel like death. It does not feel like life. It feels like the territory between the two, the long slow border where the distinction stops being useful. The moss is alive. The stone is not. The water is not. The grit is not. But the moss is made of the stone and the water and the grit, and the stone has been shaped by the moss, and the water carries the moss's residues, and the grit holds the moss's dead. The categories break down. The boundaries blur. What lives here is the blurring itself.

You will not feel welcomed by it. You will not feel rejected by it. You will feel, if you sit long enough, that you are in the presence of something that has been here for so long that it has become the place, and the place has become it, and the distinction between inhabitant and habitat no longer applies. You are inside something that is alive in a way you are not, and slower than you can imagine, and it does not know you are here, and this is not sad. This is not anything. It is simply the nature of the seep, which is the nature of patience, which is the nature of time when it is allowed to be long enough to become a place.
