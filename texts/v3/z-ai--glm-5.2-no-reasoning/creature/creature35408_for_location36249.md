# What Lives Here

The first answer is: everyone. Everything. The place is so full of life that the question becomes not *who* but *how many kinds of who*, and the answer to that is: more than you will ever count, more than you would believe if you could, more than the word "more" was designed to convey.

But that is a lazy answer. A true answer requires slowing down. The place demands it. Nothing here moves quickly. Nothing here *needs* to move quickly. Speed is a strategy for creatures who live in unreliable environments, and this environment has been reliable for longer than the concept of unreliability has existed in any mind that occupies it. So you slow down. You watch. And the whos begin to separate themselves from the texture of the place, the way individual instruments separate themselves from the sound of an orchestra when you learn to listen for them.

---

## The Beetles

Start with the beetles because they are the most legible. They are the ones with roads.

There are — you will lose count, and the losing of count is itself information — there are at least fourteen distinct species of beetle that use the highway systems of the lower terraces. Fourteen. Probably more. Some are so small that they exist at the edge of what your eyes can resolve in the amber dimness, specks of movement that might be mites or might be beetles or might be the newborn young of something else. But the visible ones, the ones you can track, fall into rough categories that your mind assembles for convenience rather than because the beetles themselves would recognize the distinctions.

The largest are thumb-sized, dome-shaped, dark-sheened things that move in what can only be called processions. Not lines — processions have a solemnity to them, a deliberateness, and that is what these have. They walk the same routes that their ancestors walked, routes worn into the moss like roads worn into stone, and they walk them with the unhurried certainty of commuters who have made the same journey for so long that the journey has become part of who they are. They are going to the water. They are going to the fungi. They are going to places that have been destinations for beetles for so long that the destinations have been shaped by their arriving — the edges of the shallow pools are worn smooth by generations of beetle legs, and the fungi they feed on grow in densities that suggest cultivation, or at least the passive result of consistent visitation.

They do not interact with you. You are not an obstacle. You are not a threat. You are a feature of a landscape that has never included anything like you, and they flow around your feet the way water flows around a stone, adjusting their paths with minimal deviation and no apparent alarm. If you stand in their road, they will climb over your boot. If you stand in their road long enough, they will have worn a new path around your boot by the time you move. They are patient in the way that geological processes are patient. They have time. They have always had time.

The smaller beetles are more varied, more restless, more *individual*. They do not travel in processions. They wander, they investigate, they pause and probe and move on. They are the foragers, the explorers, the ones whose behavior is not yet a road. Some of them are predatory — you see one, iridescent in the amber glow, pursue and capture something too small to identify, carrying it in its mandibles to a crevice in the wall where it will eat in privacy. Some are scavengers, working the edges where the biofilm thins and the built surface shows through, consuming the dying edges of the moss, recycling it back into the system. Some you cannot categorize at all. They do things that have no obvious purpose — climbing to the top of a fern frond and staying there for an hour, or gathering in clusters of a dozen or more around a particular spot on the wall that looks identical to every other spot, or walking in circles that seem to have no meaning. You are watching behavior that has meaning in a context you do not have. They are not confused. You are ignorant. The distinction matters.

And there is one species — one you do not see at first, because seeing them requires standing still for a very long time in a place where standing still means becoming part of the landscape — that lives in the carvings. The geometric spirals, the ancient art on the ancient walls. These beetles are small, flat, almost scale-like, and they live in the grooves of the carvings, their bodies aligned with the channels cut into the stone, their coloration matching the biofilm-covered surface so precisely that they are invisible until they move. They move only when the air current shifts, when the slow circulation brings warmer, damper air from below. Then they emerge from the grooves, walk a short distance along the carved lines, and return. They are following the art. They are living in it. Whether they know it is art — whether it means anything to them beyond a convenient channel that holds moisture and provides shelter — is a question you cannot answer, and the asking of it says more about you than about them. But the fact remains: they live in the carvings, they move along the lines that whatever built this place carved into the walls, and they have been doing this long enough that their bodies have shaped themselves to fit the grooves. They are the art's most devoted audience. They have been reading it for millennia.

## The Moths (Or the Things Like Moths)

The flying things are harder. Harder to see, harder to count, harder to categorize. They occupy the air the way the beetles occupy the surfaces — totally, completely, without gaps. But the air is three-dimensional in a way that surfaces are not, and the flying things use all three dimensions.

They are not moths. Calling them moths is a concession to the limits of your vocabulary and your frame of reference. They have wings. They fly. They are attracted to — or at least concentrated near — light sources. But their wings are not feathered. They are flat, smooth, almost translucent, and they catch the amber bioluminescence and refract it rather than reflecting it, so that a flying thing in motion leaves a brief smear of amber in the air behind it, a comet-trail of borrowed light that fades in the time it takes to blink. In the dim air beneath the overhanging terraces, these trails overlap and cross, creating a slow, three-dimensional phosphorescence that is the closest thing this place has to a light show. It is not for you. It is not for anyone. It is what happens when wings and light exist in the same space.

They are doing something up there. Something organized. You stand beneath an overhang and watch, and over the course of an hour — an hour in which you do not move, in which you breathe slowly and let the moisture settle on you and become part of your surface — you begin to see patterns. The flying things move in layers. The highest layer, nearest the underside of the platform above, flies in one direction. The middle layer flies in the opposite direction. The lowest layer, nearest the terrace floor, moves in a slow spiral that corresponds to nothing visible but is consistent enough to be intentional. They are cycling the air. Or they are feeding on something in the air that your senses cannot detect. Or they are doing both, or neither, or something that has no analog in your understanding of what flying things do.

Some of them are larger than others. The larger ones move more slowly, hold their positions more deliberately, and occasionally descend to a surface — a wall, a platform edge, the top of a fern — and remain there for minutes at a time before launching again. When one of them lands near you — close enough to see in the amber glow — you notice that its underside is different from its top. The top is smooth, translucent, the light-refracting surface. The underside is covered in fine filaments, almost like the gills of a mushroom, and these filaments pulse visibly, rhythmically, in and out, as the thing rests. It is breathing through them. Or it is eating through them. Or it is communicating through them, releasing chemical signals into the air that the other flying things will encounter and interpret. When it launches again, the filaments fold flat against its body and become invisible, and it is once again a smooth, winged, light-refracting shape in the dim air.

There are other kinds. Smaller ones that do not follow the layered pattern, that dart and hover and zigzag with an energy that seems wasted in a place this calm. They are the young, perhaps, or a different species entirely, or a different caste if these things have castes. They are drawn to the water pools — they hover above them, dip to touch the surface, rise again. They are drinking. Or laying eggs. Or collecting something from the surface film. They are doing what they do, and what they do is necessary to the system, even if the system does not know it.

## The Things on the Walls

You notice them as shadows first. The bioluminescent glow from the terrace surfaces throws every object above into faint relief against the undersides of the platforms, and among the shadows of ferns and fungi and the passing of flying things, there are shadows that move wrong. Too slow. Too deliberate. Too *patient*.

The predators.

They are not numerous. That is the first thing you notice about them — in a place where everything else exists in densities that strain comprehension, the predators are sparse. This makes sense. This is how ecology works. But the *sparseness* is striking when you finally see one, because it means that this creature — the one you are looking at, the one that has been motionless on the wall two meters from your head for the entire time you have been standing here — is alone. It is the only one of its kind in your field of vision. It may be the only one of its kind on this terrace. It does not need company. It does not need a pack or a flock or a herd. It needs a wall, and time, and the passage of prey, and it has all three.

It is flat. That is the primary characteristic of its body plan: flat. Flat the way a gecko is flat, but more so, flat the way a leaf is flat, flat the way something that has evolved to press itself against a surface and become indistinguishable from that surface is flat. Its legs are splayed to the sides, nearly invisible against the moss. Its body is elongated, tapering at both ends, and its head — if the end facing up is its head; you are not certain — is pointed, narrow, adapted for entering the crevices and channels where prey might hide. Its coloration is the coloration of the walls: dark green, darker green, with patches of amber that might be biofilm growing on its surface or might be its own pigmentation. You suspect the former. You suspect that this creature has been still for so long that the biofilm has colonized it, that it has become part of the surface it hunts from, that it is not hiding on the wall but has become the wall, or at least a piece of it, a piece that happens to eat.

It has eyes. You can see them, now that you know where to look: two dark spots on the narrow head, not large, not prominent, but present. They are open. They are watching. Not you — or not only you. They are watching the beetle road that passes below, the steady procession of dome-shaped commuters going to the water. They are watching the air where the flying things move in their layers. They are watching everything, because everything is prey, and because watching is what this creature does instead of chasing, and because it has been doing this long enough that watching and hunting have become the same act.

You see it eat. Once, in the hours you spend near it. A beetle — one of the smaller foraging species, not one of the procession-makers — passes within range. The thing on the wall does not lunge. It extends. A part of its body — a mouthpart, a tongue, something you cannot identify — extends from the narrow head with a speed that is shocking in this place of slow movements, touches the beetle, and the beetle is gone. Consumed. The thing on the wall resumes its stillness. The gap in the beetle's meandering path is closed by other beetles within seconds. The road does not pause. The road does not notice.

There are other predators. You find them as you climb — different species on different levels, each adapted to the specific conditions of its terrace. On the mid-levels, where the air is neither stagnant nor moving, the predators are slightly larger and slightly more active, moving along the walls in slow patrols rather than waiting in fixed positions. On the upper levels, where the air circulates and the light is brighter, the predators are different again — leaner, longer-legged, pursuing prey through the grass-like growths with a speed and purpose that the lower-level predators do not possess. The upper-level predators see you. You can tell because they change their behavior when you approach — not fleeing, but repositioning, moving to a different vantage point, maintaining a distance that suggests caution rather than fear. They have caution because they are large enough to have something to lose. The lower-level predators do not have caution because they do not need it. Nothing in their world is large enough to threaten them. You are an anomaly, and anomalies are not threats until proven otherwise.

## The Things in the Water

The pools are alive. Every pool, every channel, every thin film of moisture on every surface. Alive.

The larvae are the most obvious. Pale, legless, segmented, they fill the water the way the beetles fill the roads — totally, continuously, in numbers that make the pools appear to move. They are filter-feeding. You can see it: the slight pulsing of their bodies, drawing water in through one end and expelling it through the other, consuming the particulate matter that the system produces and returning the water cleaner than it entered. They are the kidneys of this place. Everything that dies here — every beetle that falls, every moth that expires, every fragment of moss that sloughs off a wall — eventually reaches the water, and the larvae consume it, break it down, return its components to the system in forms that the plants can use. They are the reason the water is clear. They are the reason the pools persist. They are the reason the entire system does not choke on its own waste.

But there are other things in the water. Things that are not larvae. Things that you see only as movements — displacements, ripples in pools too shallow to hide anything, and yet something is hidden in them. Flat things. Thin things. Things that lie on the bottom of the pools and are the same color and texture as the biofilm that coats the bottom and that move only when the larvae have gathered in sufficient density above them. Then they strike — a motion so fast that the water itself seems to skip, a contradiction of the stillness that preceded it — and a larva is consumed, and the flat thing settles again, and the pool is still, and the remaining larvae continue their work as if nothing happened.

These are the aquatic predators. They are the mirror of the wall-predators, occupying the same ecological role in a different medium. They are patient. They are flat. They are camouflaged. And they are everywhere there is water, in every pool and every channel, in numbers that you can estimate only by disturbing a pool and watching how many shadows dart for cover. You disturb one. Twelve shadows. In a pool the size of your hand.

The mathematics of this place are staggering. You cannot do them. You try, standing on a terrace, looking at a pool, counting the larvae (you lose count at three hundred, and the pool is not exceptional), estimating the predators (twelve, at minimum), extrapolating to the number of pools on this terrace (dozens visible, certainly more hidden), extrapolating to the number of terraces (uncountable, extending beyond sight in every direction), and the numbers collapse under their own weight. This place does not operate at a scale that your mathematics can hold. It operates at a scale that your mathematics can describe but not comprehend. The difference matters.

## The Things You Almost See

There is something else.

You become aware of it slowly, the way you become aware of a scent you have been smelling for so long that you have stopped smelling it. It is not a creature. It is not a species. It is a presence — a pattern of regularity in the system that cannot be explained by the behavior of the organisms you have observed.

The fungi. The fungi grow in patterns that are too regular, too evenly spaced, too consistently located near the water channels to be the result of random colonization. They are being tended. Something is placing spores in optimal locations, ensuring that the fruiting bodies appear where they will receive the right moisture, the right shade, the right traffic of pollinating moths. You have not seen what does this. You have stood near fungal gardens for long periods and watched, and nothing visible approaches to tend them. But the patterns persist. The gardens are maintained.

The water channels. Some of them have been modified — reshaped, deepened, redirected — in ways that are too deliberate to be the result of root growth or erosion. The modifications are old. The water has been flowing in these new paths long enough that the channels have developed their own ecosystems, their own populations of larvae and predators. But the modifications were made. By something with purpose. By something that wanted the water to go to a particular place.

The carvings. The beetles live in them, but the beetles did not make them. The carvings predate the beetles. The carvings predate the biofilm. The carvings were made when the structure was new, by whatever built the structure, and they are art, and the art is still here, and the art is still being *read* by the things that live in it, and this suggests a continuity that spans from the builders to the current inhabitants, a thread of attention and purpose that has not broken across the millennia.

Something tends this place. Not the way a gardener tends a garden — with active intervention and constant oversight. Something more like the way a forest tends itself, through feedback loops and slow adjustments that accumulate into systemic health. But the adjustments are too precise, too targeted, too *good* to be accidental. The fungal gardens are too well-placed. The water channels are too well-designed. The system is too balanced, too resilient, too perfectly calibrated to be the product of nothing but chance and time.

You do not see it. You may never see it. It may be something that moves so slowly that a human lifetime is not sufficient to observe its behavior. It may be something that lives in the deep levels, below where you have ventured, emerging rarely and in places you are not. It may be something that was once a single organism and has become so distributed, so integrated into the system, that it no longer exists as a discrete entity. It may be the structure itself — not a metaphor, not a poetic gesture, but literally the structure, the built surfaces and the channels and the terraces functioning as a body, maintaining itself through processes so subtle and so distributed that they cannot be observed from any single vantage point.

Or it may be gone. The carvings suggest builders, and builders leave, and what they built remains, and what remains is inhabited by others, and the others maintain the place not because they know what they are maintaining but because they are what lives here, and what lives here has shaped itself to the place, and the place is shaped by what lives here, and the two have been shaping each other for so long that the question of who is tending and what is being tended has lost its meaning.

This is, perhaps, the truest answer to the question of who lives here: everything. Everything that is here. The beetles and the moths and the predators and the larvae and the fungi and the mosses and the ferns and the water and the air and the structure itself, all of them alive in the only way that matters — all of them doing what they do, all of them necessary, all of them part of a whole that is larger and older and more complete than any of its parts. They are not inhabitants. They are the place. The place is them. The distinction between the living and the inhabited has dissolved, here, in the warm dark, in the amber glow, in the sound that is not sound but is the audible evidence of existence at density, and it has dissolved so thoroughly and so long ago that even the concept of a distinction is a foreign thing, a thing from outside, a thing that has no purchase here.

You are outside. You are the foreign thing. You are standing in a place that does not need you, does not know you, does not benefit from your understanding, and yet — and yet — the place is generous with itself. It lets you see. It does not hide. It does not perform. It simply is, and in being, it offers itself to your attention, and your attention is not enough to contain it, and it does not mind.

The Terrarium Spires are full. They have always been full. They will always be full, until they are not, and when they are not, the not-fullness will be just as complete, just as natural, just as much a part of whatever comes next.

What lives here is this: a world. A small one, a contained one, a forgotten one. But a world. Complete.
