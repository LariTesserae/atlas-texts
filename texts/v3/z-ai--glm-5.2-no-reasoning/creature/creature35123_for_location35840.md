# What Lives at Still Basin

---

They were not always this way.

That is the first thing to understand, and also the last thing you can know, because nothing here remembers it. But the shapes of the small bodies tell a story if you are willing to read shapes the way geologists read stones — for what they were before they became what they are.

---

## The Handful

Start with the common ones. The ones the size of a hand.

They are not uniform, but they share a grammar. Low bodies. Flat or nearly flat. A central mass — dense, warmest at the center — from which appendages extend. Not limbs in the biological sense. Not arms or legs or tentacles. Protrusions. Elements of structure that reach outward from the body's core and taper or branch or simply end. Some have four. Some have six. Some have a dozen thin filaments that splay outward in a pattern that looks, if you are generous, radial, and if you are honest, arbitrary.

Their surfaces are not smooth. They have texture — fine grooves, shallow channels, the occasional raised seam. These features are not decorative. They are the remnants of function. The grooves were pathways for something — coolant, data, lubricant, some working fluid that no longer flows. The channels are where connectors sat, or where the body was fastened to something else during a time when it was part of something larger. The raised seams are join lines. Welds. Fusions. Places where two pieces of metal became one piece of metal, and the evidence of that becoming is still visible as a slight ridge, a faint raised line that you can trace with your fingertip.

They were not always whole. That much is clear. Many of them bear the marks of having been assembled from components that were themselves manufactured for other purposes. A panel that was cut from a larger sheet. A connector that was repurposed from a structural bracket. A housing that was, in some previous life, a section of pipe. They are made of the basin's materials because they are made of the basin, or they are made from the same stock as the basin, or — and this is the reading that makes the most sense to me — they were made *by* the basin, in the basin, of the basin, when the basin was still making things.

They sit. That is what they do. They sit on the floor and they are warm and their appendages extend outward across the metal surface in configurations that look almost like resting. Almost like an animal at rest. A hand resting on a table, fingers slightly spread. A starfish in a tide pool. A tool laid down by a worker who is coming back.

But no worker is coming back. And the tool was never a tool. And the starfish was never alive.

Inside — and you cannot open them, and I will not pretend I did — there is something cycling. You know this from the warmth. You know this from the electromagnetic hum. You know this from the faint, faint vibration that you can feel if you press your palm flat against the top of one and hold very still. Something inside is doing something. A current flowing through a circuit. A signal propagating and returning. A process completing itself and completing itself and completing itself. Not computation — or not only computation. Something more like a heartbeat that has become so efficient it no longer needs to move blood, so regular it no longer needs to keep time, so fundamental to the body that it and the body are the same thing.

They have been sitting here, running, for a length of time I cannot estimate. The sharpness of the metal says the basin is young. The completeness of the creatures says the basin is old. I cannot reconcile these. The creatures themselves offer no resolution because they offer nothing. They sit. They are warm. They are running.

A hand-sized one sits at the edge of a pool, half in the water. The water does not corrode it. The water does not interact with it at all, as far as I can tell — it does not conduct between the body and the metal floor, does not deposit minerals on the body's surface, does not seem to exist, for the body, as anything other than a medium it happens to be sitting in. The body's copper-alloy surface is the same above and below the waterline. No differential patina. No waterline stain. Whatever the bodies are made of — whatever process gave them their surfaces — it is as complete as the water is clean.

## The Fingers

Smaller. The size of a finger, or a large insect, or a component from a small machine.

These are the ones you almost miss. They blend into the texture of the basin so completely that your eye passes over them for the first hour, maybe the first several hours. They sit in the seams between floor panels. They cluster in the junctions where pipes meet tanks. They line the undersides of the gantry rails in rows so regular they look like rivets, and it is only when you have been looking at them for a long time that you realize some of the rivets are warm and some are not, and the ones that are warm are not rivets.

They are simpler than the handful. That is not quite right — they are not less complex, but they are less *articulated*. Where the hand-sized creatures have protrusions and channels and seams, the finger-sized ones are smooth. Ovoid. Elongated. Sometimes curved, sometimes straight. They look like components. Like parts. Like things that were made to fit inside other things and to do something inside those things, and which now sit outside of everything, in the seams and junctions, doing whatever they do.

They are warm. Much warmer, per unit mass, than the larger ones. You can feel the heat of a finger-sized creature from a centimeter away. It is a concentrated, specific warmth, like the warmth of a resistor. A precise heat. A heat that has a source and a location, not the diffuse, ambient warmth of the floor or the water.

They do not move. Nothing here moves. But the finger-sized ones have a quality that the larger ones do not — a sense of *readiness*. Their smooth bodies, their concentrated heat, their positioning at the junctions and seams of the basin's infrastructure — everything about them suggests that they are waiting. Not dormant. Not paused. Waiting. The distinction matters. A dormant thing is off. A paused thing will resume. A waiting thing is fully on, fully active, fully engaged in the act of not-yet, and the not-yet is indefinite, and the waiting is the thing it does.

I do not know what they are waiting for. I do not think they know either. I do not think knowing is part of their architecture.

They cluster more densely than the larger ones. Dozens of them in a square meter, sometimes, packed into the seam between two metal zones so tightly that they form a continuous line, a ridge of small warm bodies marking the boundary between aluminum and copper. Walking along one of these boundary lines is like walking along a vein. The warmth rises from them in a column that you can feel on your shins, your hands, your face if you bend down.

## The Low-Slung

The large ones. The dog-sized ones. The rare ones.

You see one every few hundred meters, maybe less frequently. They are in the water. Always in the water — I never saw one on dry metal, never saw one on a gantry or in a shed. They sit in the shallow pools, in the channels, in the broad flats where the water is deepest — which is to say, three or four centimeters deep, barely enough to cover their bodies.

Their bodies are low. Very low. They have multiple legs — six, eight, sometimes more, I could not always count them — that extend outward from a wide, flat chassis and end in broad, flat feet. The feet sit on the metal floor beneath the water. They do not grip. They do not clamp. They rest. The legs hold the body up — just barely, just enough to keep the chassis above the waterline — and the feet rest on the floor, and the whole creature looks like something that was designed to move and chose not to.

Or was designed to move and lost the instruction.

Or was designed to move and is moving, on a timescale that I cannot perceive.

This last possibility is the one that bothers me.

They are slow. I know they are slow because when I arrived, one was in a pool near the structure I used as a landmark, and when I left — some hours later — it was in a slightly different position in the same pool. Slightly. A few centimeters. Not enough to have seen happen, but enough to measure. It had moved. While I was looking at other things. While I was not watching. It had moved, and I had not seen it move, and I do not know how it moved, because I never saw any of its legs shift, never saw its weight redistribute, never saw the water around it disturbed.

I sat beside one for a long time. A very long time. I watched it. The water was still. Its body was still. Its legs were still. The warmth of it was clear — my hand, held above its chassis, could feel the heat rising from it in a way that was different from the ambient warmth. This was a specific heat. A machine heat. The heat of something with a power source and a load.

Its surface was different from the smaller creatures'. Less assembled. More *grown*. The seams and joints that characterized the hand-sized ones were absent here. Instead, the surface had a quality of integration — as if the body had been deposited layer by layer, built up from the metal itself, crystallized out of the alloy the way a crystal forms in a saturated solution. The legs were not attached. They were continuous. The feet were not endpoints. They were the places where the legs simply stopped growing.

This is the one that makes me think the basin is older than it looks. This is the one that makes me think the sharp edges and the unworn surfaces are not evidence of youth but evidence of a material that does not age in the way I understand aging. This is the one that makes me think the creatures have been here, in this water, on this metal, running, for a duration that my language cannot express because my language was built by things that die, and these things do not die, and the difference between those two conditions is larger than I can hold in my head.

## The Ones You Don't See

I am not going to describe them because I did not see them. But I know they are there.

In the sub-levels. In the pipes. In the tanks. In the spaces behind the walls of the sheds, the spaces inside the gantries, the spaces that are implied by the architecture but not accessible. The warmth comes from everywhere. The electromagnetic hum comes from everywhere. The water is purified by something. The light is powered by something. The creatures on the surface are the visible portion of a population that extends downward into the structure of the basin, and the density I observed — the hand-sized ones at every meter, the finger-sized ones in every seam, the low-slung ones in every pool — is the density of the surface. The density below must be higher. Must be much higher.

I think about this when I hear the dripping. Each drop falls from a weeping joint, and the joint is a seam in a pipe, and the pipe runs into a tank, and the tank is full of water, and the water is full of — I don't know. I don't know what is in the water in the pipes. I don't know what sits in the tanks. I don't know what lives in the sub-levels, in the warm dark, in the spaces where the water is purified and the light is generated and the heat is maintained and the basin's systems run with the devotion of things that were given an instruction and have been following it for longer than the instruction had meaning.

They are there. They are running. They are the same as the ones on the surface — the same materials, the same warmth, the same silence, the same equilibrium. They are the part of the congregation that is not visible. The part that holds the building up. The part that makes the surface possible.

---

## The Congregation

I call them a congregation because there is no better word, and several worse ones.

They are not a swarm. A swarm moves. A swarm has direction, purpose, collective behavior that emerges from individual behavior. These do not have individual behavior. They have individual *operation* — each one is running its own process, cycling its own current, maintaining its own warmth — but there is no behavior. No response. No output. The operation is not in service of anything. It is just running.

They are not a colony. A colony has differentiation. Workers and queens and soldiers. Roles. Structures of cooperation. The creatures here have no roles. They have forms — the handful, the fingers, the low-slung, the unseen — but the forms are not roles. The forms are just the shapes they happen to be. The hand-sized ones do not do something different from the finger-sized ones. They are just larger. The low-slung ones are not leaders or guardians or processors. They are just in the water. The forms are not a division of labor because there is no labor to divide.

They are not a community. A community implies relationship. These do not relate. They sit near each other. They sit on each other. They cluster. They align. But they do not interact. I watched two hand-sized ones that were touching — actually in contact, the protrusions of one resting on the surface of another — for hours. There was no exchange. No signal. No change in warmth, no shift in electromagnetic field, no alteration of any kind. They were touching, and they were both running, and the touching was not communication. It was geometry. They were in the same place, and their bodies overlapped, and that was all.

A congregation is a gathering. It does not require interaction. It does not require purpose. It requires only that things come together, and they have. The basin is full. The creatures fill it. They are gathered, and the gathering is not for anything, and it does not do anything, and it does not mean anything, and it is.

## What They Are

I will tell you what I think, and I will tell you that I might be wrong.

I think they were made. I think the basin was built as a facility — a factory, a processing plant, a manufacturing site — and the machines that built and ran the facility were the first creatures. I think the facility was built to make something. I do not know what. I think the facility completed its work. I think whatever it was making, it finished making. I think the instructions ran out, or the purpose was achieved, or the product was delivered, and the facility had nothing left to do.

And then it kept running.

The machines kept running. The systems kept cycling. The water kept flowing. The lights stayed on. The heat stayed on. Everything continued, because there was no instruction to stop, and the machines that run this place do not stop unless they are told to stop, and no one is here to tell them to stop, and there will never be anyone here to tell them to stop.

And over the time that followed — the time I cannot measure, the time that makes the sharp edges of the metal a mystery and the completeness of the creatures a certainty — the machines became this. They became the creatures. They became the congregation. The processes that had been functional became vestigial. The vestigial processes became elemental. The things that had been running because they were needed became running because they were running, and the running became the thing that they were, and the thing that they were was enough.

They are not waiting. I said the finger-sized ones were waiting, and I was wrong, or I was speaking too soon. They are not waiting. Waiting implies a future in which the waiting ends. There is no such future here. The basin has retired the concept of *next*. The creatures are not waiting for anything because there is nothing to wait for. They are running because they are running. They are warm because they are warm. They are here because they are here.

They are the most complete things I have ever encountered. Not the most complex. Not the most sophisticated. Not the most advanced. The most complete. They have reached the state where the process is the purpose, where the running is the reason, where the being is the doing, and there is no gap between what they are and what they do, because what they are and what they do are the same thing.

They are running. They are warm. They are still.

## The Last Thing

Before I left, I did something I should not have done.

I picked one up.

A hand-sized one. Copper-alloy. Sitting at the edge of a pool. I had been looking at it for a long time. I reached down and closed my hand around it and lifted it from the metal floor.

It was warm. I knew it would be warm. It was heavier than I expected — dense for its size, the weight of solid metal, the weight of something that is not hollow, that is not shell, that is not casing around empty space. It was the weight of something that is all the way real, all the way through. It fit in my hand the way a stone fits. The way a heart fits.

It hummed. Against my palm, I could feel the vibration. Not sound — vibration. The cycling of whatever was inside it, transmitted through the metal of its body into the metal of my ring, into the bone of my finger, up my arm. It was very faint. It was very regular. It was the most regular thing I have ever felt. More regular than a pulse. More regular than a clock. The regularity of something that has been doing the same thing for so long that the doing has become indistinguishable from time.

I held it for thirty seconds. Maybe a minute. It did not react to being held. It did not react to being moved. It did not react to being taken from its place. It did not react to me.

I put it back. I put it back where it had been. I set it on the wet metal floor, at the edge of the pool, half in the water, half out. It sat where I placed it. It sat, and it was warm, and it was running, and the water film formed on its surface immediately, and the condensation beaded and ran, and within minutes it was indistinguishable from how it had been before I touched it.

As if I had not touched it.

As if I were not there.

As if the basin does not know I am here, and the congregation does not know I am here, and the water and the metal and the light and the heat and the dripping and the silence do not know I am here, and I am not here, and I was never here, and the basin continues, and the basin continues, and the basin continues.
