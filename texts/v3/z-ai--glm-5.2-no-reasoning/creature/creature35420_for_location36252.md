# The Seepage Works

## What lives there

### I. The obvious inhabitants

The biofilm is the closest thing the Seepage Works has to a citizen.

It lives on the Pump House's south wall and is migrating east with the patience of a glacier and the intent of something that knows what it wants. It is not one organism. It is a cooperative — a consortium, as the description notes — and this is worth pausing on, because the word *consortium* implies a kind of contract, an agreement among parties to pursue shared interests, and this may be more accurate than it first appears. The bacterial species within the matrix do not all perform the same function. Some metabolize the sulfur compounds in the seepage water. Some fix nitrogen. Some produce the polysaccharide matrix itself, the communal infrastructure, the shared wall. Some do nothing identifiable, and these may be the most important members, or may be freeloaders, or may be doing something so subtle that the categories of "important" and "freeloading" do not apply at their scale.

The biofilm is dark. Nearly black. This is not a pigment but a consequence: the bacteria are iron-reducing, and the iron they process precipitates within the matrix as a fine, dark mineral dust, and this dust colors the whole community. They are, in a literal sense, stained by their own work. They are also, in a literal sense, building with their waste. The matrix thickens with mineral inclusion, becomes denser, becomes more structurally coherent. The biofilm is not just growing on the wall. It is becoming a kind of wall itself, a new surface laid over the old one, and the old one — the brick, the mortar — is being incorporated into the new one at a molecular level, and in a hundred years or two hundred the Pump House's south wall will not be brick with a biofilm on it. It will be something else. Something that was brick and bacteria and iron and water and time, fused into a material that does not have a name because no one has needed to name it yet.

The biofilm does not think. It does not plan. But it does *persist*, and it does *expand*, and it does *respond* — to light levels, to moisture, to the chemical composition of the water that weeps from the wall's joints. When the weep-points shift with thermal cycling, the biofilm's densest growth follows. It does not abandon the old zones. It leaves a thinner, drier, paler film behind, a record of where water used to be, a ghost of previous conditions. In this sense the biofilm is a historian. It is also a prophet, because where it is thickest now is where the water is now, and where the water will be is where the biofilm is already thinning outward, probing, sending filaments of itself into mortar joints that are not yet weeping but soon will be.

It does not know any of this. It is doing all of this.

### II. The rill-dwellers

The flatworms are the Seepage Works' most elegant residents.

They are turbellarians — free-living flatworms, probably triclads, though no one has confirmed this because no one has come with a collecting jar and a hand lens and the particular brand of patience required to lie face-down over a rill and watch for something two millimeters long moving at a speed that makes glaciers look impulsive. They glide on their cilia, which cover their ventral surfaces in a carpet of molecular oars, each one beating in coordination with its neighbors in a metachronal wave that you can see under a microscope as a shimmering ripple, like wind over a wheat field, if the wheat field were on the belly of an animal and the wind were the animal's own intention. The flatworms navigate the rills' shallow water by touch and by chemical sense, and they eat what they encounter: bacteria, protozoans, detritus, each other sometimes. They are predators and scavengers and occasionally cannibals, and they are transparent enough that you can see their gut branches through their skin, and the gut branches are dark after a meal and pale after digestion, and this is as close to a facial expression as a flatworm gets.

They are not the most numerous animal in the rills. The nematodes are. Nematodes are the most numerous animal everywhere, and the Seepage Works is not an exception to any rule that nematodes obey, and nematodes obey all rules. There are, in a handful of muck from a rill margin, perhaps ten thousand nematodes. Perhaps more. They are invisible — a tenth of a millimeter in diameter, a millimeter or two in length, threadlike, translucent, moving with a sinuous undulation that is the only mode of locomotion their body design permits, since they have no circular muscles and cannot change diameter, only curve, and so they thrash, and the thrashing propels them through the muck's water films, and this is their entire life: thrashing forward through water films, eating bacteria, being eaten by fungi, occasionally encountering a plant root and puncturing it with their stylet and draining its contents, though the dead rushes offer little, and the willow's roots offer more, and the nematodes that have found the willow's roots are, in their way, the luckiest organisms in the landscape, though luck is not a concept that applies to nematodes. What applies to nematodes is: thrash, eat, reproduce, die, be decomposed, be eaten by other nematodes, and the cycle continues, and the muck is dense with their cycles, and the muck is in part *made* of their cycles, and the ground you walk on is in part nematode.

The ostracods are the rills' charmers. They are crustaceans — true crustaceans, related to lobsters and crabs through a lineage so ancient that the connection seems absurd, like claiming kinship between a skyscraper and a cairn. They are a millimeter long. They have bivalved shells — chitinous, calcified, glossy — that they open to extend their appendages, which are many and varied: antennae for swimming, mandibles for biting, maxillae for manipulating, walking legs for walking. They swim with a jerk: the appendages thrust, the shell moves forward, the appendages retract, the shell settles, repeat. The jerk is fast enough that you can see it if you are watching, and the ostracod appears to teleport — here, then there, then there — and between jerks it is still, and the stillness has a quality, a deliberateness, as though the ostracod is considering its next move, though it is not considering anything. It is reacting to stimuli: light gradients, chemical gradients, the vibration of a flatworm's cilia somewhere nearby. But the pause between jerks is real, and it lasts a fraction of a second, and in that fraction the ostracod is the most complex object in the rill, a calcified grain of decision-making, and it decides, and it jerks, and it is somewhere else.

There are other invertebrates. Midge larvae in the still pools, blood-red from hemoglobin that allows them to survive low-oxygen conditions — they are, in their way, the most adapted organisms here, because the still pools are the most oxygen-poor habitat and the midge larvae have made them theirs. Tubifex worms, related to earthworms but aquatic, clustering in the muck's deepest layer, extending their tails into the water column to absorb oxygen, withdrawing when disturbed, extending again when disturbance passes. Water bears — tardigrades — in the moss, probably, because tardigrades are always in the moss, and the moss here is bright and thick and wet and exactly the kind of habitat tardigrades have evolved to inhabit for six hundred million years, through every extinction event the planet has produced, and the Seepage Works is not an extinction event. It is barely an event at all. The tardigrades in the moss do not know they are in a landscape. They are in a film of water on a moss leaf, and that is their universe, and it is sufficient.

### III. The spiders

The spiders are *something*, and the something is identifiable only to the level of family, probably Linyphiidae, the sheet weavers, though no one has collected one and no one will.

They are small. Three millimeters, perhaps four. Brown, with legs that seem too thin for the body they carry, though the proportions are correct for linyphiids, and the impression of thinness is an artifact of size — at three millimeters, everything seems too thin, too fragile, too barely-there, and yet the spider is not barely there. The spider is *there*, completely there, with eight eyes that see almost nothing and a body that is dense with organs and a nervous system that is, for its size, elaborate, and a capacity for silk production that is, for any size, remarkable.

The webs they build are horizontal sheets, slung between the rills' edges, anchored to dead rush stems or to bits of stick or to nothing at all — sometimes the anchor points are just the surface of the muck, which is firm enough for a silk attachment if the silk is laid down in a film and allowed to bond with the muck's organic surface. The webs are not orbs. They are sheets, and the sheets are not sticky. They are tangled, and the tangling is the trap: an insect flying low over the rills, disturbed by something, moving erratically, encounters the sheet, tangles its legs in the silk, struggles, and the struggle brings the spider, and the spider bites, and the bite is neurotoxic, and the insect is still, and the spider feeds.

The webs are destroyed by rain. Not every rain destroys every web — a light mist is survivable, a drizzle is survivable, but a real rain, the kind that fills the rills and darkens the muck and slicks the shale, collapses the webs and washes the silk into the drainage, where it becomes part of the organic load, part of the muck, part of the ground. After a real rain, the spiders rebuild. They begin within hours, sometimes within minutes, while the ground is still wet and the rills are still high, and the new webs are in the same places as the old webs, or nearly, because the spiders do not have many options — the rills are narrow, the anchor points are few, and the geometry of a sheet web between two edges is constrained by the distance between the edges, and so the web that was is the web that will be, and the rain is an interruption, not a redesign.

The spiders catch very little. The landscape's insect population is low — midges, mosquitoes, the occasional beetle — and the webs are small and low to the ground and in a place where insects are not abundant. The spiders are, in energetic terms, operating near the margin. They survive because their metabolic rate is low, because they can go long periods without food, because the food they do catch is enough — barely, on average, over time — to sustain a population that is not growing and not declining and not doing anything except persisting at a density that the landscape can support, which is low.

But the spiders do something that none of the other rill-dwellers do. They build. The flatworms glide. The ostracods jerk. The nematodes thrash. The spiders build. They take a material that they produce inside their own bodies and they lay it down in patterns that are not random — the anchor lines first, then the supporting stays, then the sheet itself, laid in a spiral or a zigzag, each line placed with a precision that the spider's legs can feel but its eyes cannot see, because its eyes are nearly useless, and the web is built by touch and by memory and by instinct, which is another word for the accumulated decisions of a million generations of spiders, each of which built a web in a place like this and survived long enough to pass the web-building instruction onward, and the instruction has not changed because it has not needed to, and the web that is built today in the Seepage Works is the same web that was built a hundred thousand years ago in a different wet place with different rills and different muck and the same geometry of need.

The spiders are the landscape's architects. They are the only organisms here that make something that is not themselves. The biofilm makes more biofilm. The fungus makes more fungus. The spiders make a web, which is not a spider, which is a structure, which is an expression of a spider's physiology that exists outside the spider's body and serves a purpose the spider cannot achieve without it, and this is what building is, and the spiders are building, and they are the only ones.

### IV. The fungus

The white fungus on the workbench is the Seepage Works' most patient organism, and in a landscape defined by patience, this is a significant distinction.

It is white. Not the white of paper or snow or bone, but the white of something that does not need to be any other color — a white that is structural, the color of chitin and hyphae and nothing else, a white that is the absence of pigment because pigment costs energy and the fungus has calculated, over evolutionary time, that the energy is better spent on growth, on digestion, on the extension of its filamentous body into the wood that is its food and its world.

The fungus is a mycelium. It is a network. It is a single organism in the way that a forest is a single organism — technically, legally, by the definition of continuous genetic identity — but functionally it is a distributed intelligence, a system of hyphae that branch and fuse and branch again, that communicate chemically along their length, that transport nutrients from areas of abundance to areas of need, that can sense the difference between soft wood and hard wood and direct their growth accordingly, that can detect the iron bolts and grow around them or, possibly, into them, secreting organic acids that chelate the iron's ions and carry them back through the hyphal network to be deposited, perhaps, at the mycelium's growing edge, where the iron may serve as a structural element, a mineral inclusion in the fungus's own architecture, and so the fungus is eating the workbench and using the workbench's iron to build more of the fungus that is eating the workbench, and this is a closed loop, and the fungus is a closed loop, and the workbench is a closed loop being closed.

It has not produced fruiting bodies. It may never produce fruiting bodies. Fruiting bodies — mushrooms, brackets, crusts — are reproductive structures, and they are produced when a fungus has accumulated enough biomass and enough energy to invest in the production and dispersal of spores, and this fungus has not reached that threshold, or has reached it and decided, in the chemical language of mycelial feedback, that the conditions for spore dispersal are not favorable, and so it continues to grow vegetatively, extending through the wood, digesting what it has already colonized and colonizing what it has not yet digested, and the boundary between the digested and the not-yet-digested is a frontier, and the frontier is moving, and the frontier has been moving for years, and the workbench is losing ground, and the fungus is gaining ground, and the ground it is gaining is the ground the workbench was, and the ground the workbench was is becoming something else, something that is not wood and not fungus but a third thing, a residue, a compost, a dark, fibrous material that smells of soil and is, in fact, becoming soil, and the soil is what the Seepage Works is made of, and the fungus is making more of it, and this is the fungus's role in the landscape: it is converting a thing that was built into a thing that grows, and it is doing this at its own pace, and its own pace is slow, and slow is sufficient.

### V. The moss

The moss is the only thing in the Seepage Works that is unambiguously alive in the way that people mean when they say *alive*: green, growing, photosynthesizing, visibly responding to light and water, changing from one day to the next in ways that can be seen without a microscope.

It is a pleurocarpous moss — a mat-former, a creeper, a species that spreads laterally by protonemal filaments and by rhizoids that anchor it to the substrate, which here is the saturated muck at the rills' margins. It is bright green. Not dark green, not blue-green, not the gray-green of drought-tolerant mosses on rocks or the yellow-green of sphagnum in bogs. This is a green that is almost luminous, a green that seems to produce light rather than reflect it, though this is physiological rather than supernatural: the moss's chloroplasts are dense and active and the moss is always wet, and wet moss in diffuse light photographs as a source of light because the surface of each leaflet is a lens that focuses the ambient glow, and the cumulative effect of ten thousand lensed leaflets per square centimeter is a surface that appears to glow, and in the flat gray light of the Seepage Works' overcast, the moss is the only thing that glows, and it glows.

Its edge is sharp. This is the moss's strangest quality, and in a landscape full of strange qualities, sharpness stands out. The moss does not colonize gradually. It does not thin out at its borders. It is present, thick, vivid, and then it is absent, and the boundary between presence and absence is a line you could trace with a pencil. The line follows the water's edge — or rather, it follows the edge of saturation, the contour where the muck's water content drops below some threshold that the moss requires and cannot compromise on, and at that contour the moss stops, as though cut, and beyond it is bare muck, dark, wet but not saturated, and empty of moss.

The moss does not reproduce sexually here. No sporophytes — no stalks, no capsules, no spores. It reproduces vegetatively, by fragmentation, by the breaking-off and dispersal of small pieces of itself that travel down the rills in high water and lodge in new saturated zones and grow, and this is why the moss's patches follow the water so exactly: the water is the moss's reproductive medium, and the moss goes where the water goes, and the water goes where the ground lets it, and the ground is what the water has made, and the moss is growing on the water's product, and the water is distributing the moss across its own architecture, and the moss and the water are, in this sense, the same organism, or at least partners in a relationship so tight that the word *partner* may be too loose.

The moss harbors the tardigrades. The moss harbors nematodes. The moss harbors rotifers — another group of microscopic animals, wheel-bearing, corona-ciliated, protozoan-eating, found in mosses worldwide and found here because here there is moss and where there is moss there are rotifers and this is not a mystery but a rule. The moss is a habitat. The moss is a landscape. To a tardigrade, the moss's leaflets are a forest, and the film of water between them is a lake, and the nematode moving through that film is a sea serpent, and the rotifer feeding on bacteria in that film is a filter-feeding whale, and the scale does not matter, and the metaphor does not matter, and what matters is that the moss is alive and full of life and green and growing and it is the closest thing the Seepage Works has to a heartbeat.

### VI. The willow

The willow is the Seepage Works' most tragic inhabitant, and it does not know it is tragic, and the tragedy is not the kind that involves suffering. It is the kind that involves the gap between what something is and what something could be, and the willow's gap is wide.

*Salix cinerea* — gray willow, sallow — is, in better circumstances, a large shrub or a small tree. It can reach six meters. It can form thickets. It can colonize wet ground with aggressive, enthusiastic roots that drink deeply and spread widely and hold soil against erosion and produce pollen for bees and catkins for birds and shade for everything beneath. It is, in its full expression, a landscape-shaping plant.

Here, it is less than a meter tall.

It has been less than a meter tall for what appears to be a very long time. The stem is woody, gray-barked, scarred with the healed-over stubs of branches that were lost to wind or water or the simple attrition of being a small plant in an exposed, wet, marginal place. The scars are many. The stem's diameter is not increasing. The root system — what can be inferred from the plant's stability and its persistence — is extensive, running through the muck and into the clay, and this is the willow's strategy: it invests below. It puts everything into its roots, into the exploration of the substrate, into the search for nutrients and water and stability, and what is above ground is an afterthought, a concession to the need to photosynthesize, a minimum viable canopy.

The willow leafs out in spring. The leaves are small — smaller than *S. cinerea* leaves should be, smaller than the species' average, as though the plant is rationing its own leaf area, producing only as much photosynthetic surface as it can sustain on the resources its roots have gathered, and no more. The leaves are gray-green, slightly hairy, oval, and they hold through summer and yellow and fall in autumn, and the willow is bare through winter, and in winter it looks like a dead thing, a stick in the mud, a marker for something that was and is not.

But it is alive. It has been alive for years — decades, perhaps. Its growth rings, if it were cut and sectioned, would be extraordinarily narrow, microscopic, the kind of rings that dendrochronologists count under magnification and that represent a year of survival so marginal that the tree barely grew at all and the ring is not a ring but a line, a pencil mark, a year's worth of living expressed as a fraction of a millimeter of wood. Each line is a year the willow persisted. Each line is a year the willow decided, in the chemical language of plant hormones and root signals and photosynthetic feedback, to remain.

The willow is not deciding to remain. The willow is not deciding anything. But the willow *is* remaining, and in a landscape where everything else is either being consumed or being built or being shifted or being dissolved, the willow's persistence — its simple, stubborn, root-deep refusal to die or to grow — is a kind of statement, and the statement is: I am here, and I am not leaving, and I am not changing, and the ground is not good enough for more than this and I am not willing to settle for less than this and this is what this is, and this is enough.

It is not enough. But the willow does not know that.

### VII. The chimney, and what may live beneath it

The chimney is not alive. The chimney is brick and mortar and an internal flue and a plumb line that has not moved since it was set, and it is not an organism and it is not a habitat and it is not, despite the temptation to say so, waiting.

But beneath the chimney there may be something. And the something may, in a sense, be alive.

The sound — the rhythmic, non-regular, phased dripping that seems to correlate with breathing — has an explanation that is geological and an explanation that is biological and an explanation that is something else, and the geological explanation is the most likely and the something else is the most interesting.

Geological: the chimney's foundation, whatever it is, sits over a cavity in the shale — a dissolution cavity, enlarged by centuries of seepage water moving through the rock's bedding planes and dissolving the lime-rich stone along joints and fissures, creating a chamber that is perhaps the size of a room, perhaps larger, its walls slick with moisture, its air cool and still and saturated with the chalky mineral load that gives the shaft water its pale green color. Water drips into this cavity from above — from the seeps, from the rills, from the clay layer that the chimney penetrates — and the drips fall into water standing in the cavity's floor, and the echo rises through the flue and out into the air, and the rhythm is irregular because the drips are not from a single source but from many, and the many sources are not synchronized, and the pattern is the interference pattern of many independent rhythms, and the human ear, which is an instrument tuned to find pattern, finds pattern in the interference and mistakes it for intention.

Biological: there is something in the cavity that is producing the sound. Not an animal — nothing large enough to produce an audible echo lives here, nothing above the level of invertebrates, and invertebrates do not echo. But a colony. A biofilm, perhaps, similar to the one on the Pump House wall but larger, more established, older — a consortium of bacteria that has been growing in the cavity's conditions for as long as the cavity has existed, which may be as long as the chimney has stood, which may be longer than the chimney. The biofilm could be producing gas — methane, hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide — as a metabolic byproduct, and the gas could be bubbling through the standing water in the cavity, and the bubbles could be producing the sound, and the rhythm could be the rhythm of metabolic activity, which is affected by temperature and pressure and the availability of nutrients, which are affected by the seepage rate, which is affected by the surface conditions, which are affected by the presence of a person standing near the chimney, whose body heat and exhaled breath and weight on the ground may slightly alter the local temperature and the local soil pressure and, through the chain of connections that the seepage system establishes between surface and subsurface, may slightly alter the rate of gas production in the cavity, and this slight alteration may be what the ear is detecting as correlation with breathing.

This is not far-fetched. Biofilms respond to their environment. Environments include the organisms within them. The Seepage Works' surface biofilm responds to the wall's weep-points. A subsurface biofilm could respond to surface pressure changes. The chain of causation is long and the effects are small, but the effects are real, and the ear is sensitive, and the correlation may be real, and if it is real then the thing beneath the chimney is, in a sense, aware of you — not conscious, not cognizant, but responsive, reacting to your presence in the way that a thermostat reacts to a room's temperature, which is not awareness but is not nothing, and the distinction between "not awareness" and "not nothing" is where the interest lies.

Something else: the chimney's plumbness. The chimney does not settle. In saturated muck and clay, everything settles — the Pump House's iron bolts have shifted, the workshop's blocks have tilted, the narrow-gauge rails have sunk below the water. The chimney has not sunk. Its foundation is not shale, because there is no shale at the chimney's location — the chimney is in the deepest, wettest part of the mire, where the shale is at its deepest, buried under the thickest accumulation of clay and muck. The chimney's foundation is something else. It may be a structure — a buried foundation, a concrete pad, a pillar of something manufactured and set into the ground before the muck accumulated, before the rills formed, before the seepage system was what it is now. It may be a geological feature — a pinnacle of resistant stone, a knob of unsolvable rock that the water has not been able to dissolve, a tooth in the shale's jaw that the erosion has exposed but not removed. It may be something that was built and has been buried and is now a foundation for something that was built on top of it and that is also no longer what it was, and the chimney is the last visible piece of a structure that extends downward into the muck and clay and shale and into whatever cavity the sound is coming from, and the chimney is not a chimney. It is a vent. It is a shaft. It is the only exposed opening of a system that extends below the Seepage Works and that has been there, operating or dormant or in some state between, for longer than the surface structures have been abandoned.

And whatever is in the cavity — the biofilm, the gas, the water, the still air — has been there as long. And it has been doing whatever it is doing — dripping, bubbling, metabolizing, persisting — as long. And it is, in the broadest possible sense, alive, if "alive" means "engaged in chemical processes that maintain a non-equilibrium state in a system that would otherwise run down," and this is one definition of life, and it is the definition that includes biofilms and fire and the Earth's atmosphere and the thing beneath the chimney, and it is not a wrong definition, and it is not a complete one, and the incompleteness is the point, because the Seepage Works is a place that resists completion, and the thing beneath the chimney resists it most of all.

### VIII. What does not live there, and why

No amphibians. This is the great absence, and it is the absence that tells the most.

Amphibians should be here. The habitat is perfect: shallow water, abundant invertebrate prey, moist ground, lack of fish predators, lack of reptile competitors. Frogs should be calling from the rills in spring. Newts should be hunting ostracods in the shallows. Toads should be burrowing into the muck at the rills' margins. The water chemistry is not prohibitive — the mineral load is moderate, the pH is within amphibian tolerance, the temperature is cool but not cold. There is no obvious reason for the absence.

But the absence is complete. No calls. No eggs. No tadpoles. No adults. No skins, no bones, no remains. Not even the ghost of an amphibian — no record, in the muck's layers, of amphibians having been here and departed. They have never been here. They have avoided this place since before the structures were built, since before the rills were established, since before the seepage system was what it is. They have avoided it for as long as amphibians have lived in the region, which is a long time, which is longer than the chimney, which is longer than the structures, which is longer than the dead rushes.

Why? The answer may be the chimney. Not the chimney itself — a chimney does not repel frogs — but what the chimney indicates: a subsurface system, a cavity, a connection to something deeper, something that produces water with a mineral load that is, to a frog's chemosensory skin, intolerable. Amphibian skin is permeable. It absorbs water and dissolved substances from the environment. It is the frog's drink and the frog's lungs and the frog's vulnerability, and a frog in water that contains substances the frog cannot tolerate is a frog that dies, and frogs do not voluntarily enter water that kills them, and if the entire surface water system of the Seepage Works is fed, in part, by the subsurface system that the chimney vents, then the entire surface water system carries, in trace amounts, the chemical signature of the subsurface, and the trace is enough. Not enough to kill invertebrates, which are more tolerant. Not enough to kill plants, which are less permeable. But enough to kill amphibians, or enough to discourage them, or enough to signal, in the chemical language that amphibians have been reading for three hundred million years, that this water is not their water and this place is not their place.

The birds' absence may be related. Birds are not as chemically sensitive as amphibians, but birds are neophobic — they avoid the unfamiliar — and a landscape that smells of chalk and iron and unnamed biofilm and whatever the chimney is venting is a landscape that does not smell like a place where birds should be, and birds, which navigate by smell more than is commonly acknowledged, may read the Seepage Works as wrong and simply not land. The swallows circled and left. They found something. It may have been the absence of insects, or the absence of perch sites, or the smell, or the chimney, or the sound from the chimney, or the quality of the air, or the quality of the light, or the quality of the place itself — the quality that a human visitor also feels, eventually, after standing in the landscape long enough: the sense that this place is occupied, not by an organism but by a process, and the process does not need or want additional participants.

### IX. The landscape itself as an organism

The Seepage Works is not an organism. It is a landscape, a place, a set of physical and biological processes occurring in a specific location over a specific period of time. It does not have a boundary in the biological sense. It does not reproduce. It does not evolve. It does not maintain homeostasis. By every biological criterion, it is not alive.

But it behaves like something. The water behaves like a circulatory system. The seeps are the heart, or the source, the point of origin. The rills are the vessels, branching and anastomosing, distributing the water through the landscape's body. The basins are the reservoirs, the holding points, the places where the system pauses before continuing. The muck is the tissue, the medium in which the processes occur. The shale is the skeleton, the structural framework that underlies and shapes everything above. The clay is the skin, the semi-permeable membrane that regulates the exchange between surface and subsurface, that holds water in and lets water through, that is the landscape's interface with the world.

The biofilm is the immune system, or the digestive system, or both — it breaks down what is foreign and incorporates what is useful. The fungus is the decomposition pathway, the mechanism by which the dead are returned to the living. The moss is the respiratory surface, the green membrane where gas exchange occurs, where carbon dioxide becomes oxygen and light becomes sugar. The willow is the landscape's sentinel, its one vertical expression, its one attempt at height, stunted and arrested but present, a flag planted in the muck that says: something here is trying to grow.

The chimney is the landscape's nervous system. It is the part that connects the surface to the depths. It is the part that transmits information — sound, chemical signature, pressure changes — between the world above and the world below. It is the part that responds, if it responds, to the presence of other organisms. It is the part that is, in some way that resists language, the center.

And the iron box is the landscape's memory. Closed. Rusted shut. Containing something — tools, nothing, something that made sense at the time — that the landscape has kept and will keep, dry and sealed, for as long as the box holds. The box is the one object in the Seepage Works that resists transformation. The brick is transforming. The iron is transforming. The wood is transforming. The ground is transforming. The box sits on its two bricks and does not transform, because its seal is intact and its contents are dry and transformation requires water and the box has denied water entry, and this is the box's one accomplishment: it has kept something the same in a place where nothing stays the same, and this is either a kindness or a cruelty, depending on whether what is inside wanted to be kept.

### X. Summary of inhabitants

The Seepage Works is inhabited by:

- A biofilm that is building a new wall over an old wall.
- Flatworms that glide on their cilia and eat what they meet.
- Ostracods that jerk through shallow water and pause between jerks.
- Nematodes in uncountable numbers, thrashing through water films, making the muck what the muck is.
- Midge larvae, blood-red, in the still pools.

- A moss that glows and follows the water's edge with a line as sharp as a cut.

- Beetles, dark and fast, hunting the rill margins, leaving tracks in the muck like punctuated sentences.

- Midges over the still pools, on still evenings.
- Whatever is beneath the chimney, which may be a biofilm, which may be a gas, which may be a cavity, which may be a sound, which may be alive in the broadest sense, which may be aware of you in the narrowest sense, which may be none of these things, which is, in any case, not talking.
- Whatever is in the iron box, which is dry, which is sealed, which is waiting, which is not waiting, which is simply there, unchanged, the one thing in the landscape that has refused the landscape's terms.

And above all of this, around all of this, through all of this: the water. The water is what lives here. The water is what has always lived here. The water is the organism that shapes the landscape and fills the landscape and carries the landscape's chemistry and the landscape's memory and the landscape's slow, patient, bureaucratic will, and the water is not alive, but the water is not not-alive, and the water is the thing that, when you stand in the Seepage Works and listen, you hear, and when you hear it, you are hearing the landscape breathe, and the landscape is breathing, and the breathing is the water, and the water is the breath, and you are welcome to stay, and you should understand that staying here is like staying in a room where someone has been working for a very long time.

The someone is the water.

It has not finished.

It does not require your help.
