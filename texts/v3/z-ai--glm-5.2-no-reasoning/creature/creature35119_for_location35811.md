# What Lives Here

---

The first thing to understand is that nothing here considers itself a resident.

Residence implies arrival, occupation, a distinction between self and shelter. Nothing in the Percolation Gardens operates with that distinction. The garden does not live *in* this place. It is the place. And everything else that persists here persists because the garden's metabolism has found a use for it, or has not yet finished the slow arithmetic of deciding.

---

## The Garden Itself

It is tempting to call it a plant. It is tempting and it is insufficient.

The garden is a plant the way a coral reef is a polyp — technically traceable to a single genetic origin, technically an organism, but functionally a *system*, a biome, an architecture. It began as one root in one seam of earth above one deposit of something it needed, and it has been growing since, and it has not stopped, and it does not know how to stop, and it may not have a mechanism for stopping. Growth is not a strategy it has selected. Growth is what it *is*. The groaning you hear is the sound of that growth encountering its own limits — the walls of the space it has filled, the weight of its own upper structure, the resistance of materials it has already incorporated and is now pressing against. It groans the way a stomach groans: not in distress, but in process.

Its age is difficult to estimate because the garden has consumed its own history. The inner rings of the root structure, the oldest wood, the material that would record the garden's early decades or centuries, have been metabolized and replaced. The garden is its own palimpsest. What remains is a surface that is always new, growing over a core that has been hollowed and refilled, hollowed and refilled, many times. The built structures give the only reliable timeline — the corrosion rates of the metals, the style of the ceramic pipe fittings, the copper wiring's verdigris — and these suggest that the garden has been here for at minimum several centuries, and possibly much longer. The deepest artifacts, those in the lowest chambers, are nearly unrecognizable. They could be decades old or millennia old. The garden does not keep records.

What the garden *does* keep is water. This is its primary activity, its constant preoccupation, the thing it does with the same unconscious devotion that a human body gives to breathing. Every surface is a catchment. Every groove is a channel. Every root is a pipe, every vine a downspout, every leaf a small green reservoir that holds water for a moment and then releases it, directing it downward, inward, toward the center, toward the deep thing below. The garden is a hydrological machine that has wrapped itself around its own purpose, and the purpose is to drink.

It drinks from below. The grating in the lowest chamber, the mesh of living wood through which the water falls into darkness, is the garden's mouth — or one of them, the largest, the most active. The flowers clustered at its edge are the interface, the organs that mediate between the garden's root structure and whatever lies below, and they have been growing into that interface for a very long time. Their roots extend down through the grating, through the falling water, into whatever is down there, and they draw up something — not just water, something *in* the water, something mineral and warm and necessary — and they concentrate it, and the fungal network distributes it, and the garden uses it to grow, to maintain, to repair, to continue.

The garden's intelligence — if that word applies — is distributed, hydraulic, and slow. The flowers are the densest nodes, the points where the computation is most concentrated, but the computation happens everywhere: in the osmotic gradients across root membranes, in the chemical signals passed along fungal filaments, in the mechanical responses of wood under pressure. The garden does not think thoughts. It solves problems. The problems are: how to maintain structural integrity at this scale, how to distribute water and nutrients to every cell, how to respond to damage, how to find and incorporate new resources. The garden has been solving these problems for longer than most human institutions have existed, and it has arrived at solutions that work, and it applies them without variation, without innovation, without the concept of improvement. What works is repeated. What does not work is not remembered. The garden does not experiment. It persists.

Its relationship to the built things — the pipes, the housings, the pumps — is the closest thing it has to curiosity, or memory, or respect, though none of those words are correct. The garden does not wonder about them. It does not investigate them. It grows into them the way water grows into cracks: not with intent, but with pressure, with the slow patient force of a living thing expanding into every available space. When a root finds a pipe, it does not think *pipe*. It thinks, if it thinks at all, *hollow*. *Warm.* *Something inside.* And it grows into the hollow, and it absorbs the something, and the pipe becomes part of the garden's body, and the garden does not remember that it was ever anything else.

The one exception — and it may not be an exception, may simply be a process slow enough that the garden has not yet completed it — is the pump. The pump that still cycles, that still moves water through the garden's drainage system with its mechanical wheeze, that has been powered by the garden's own hydraulic pressure for longer than anyone has been here to observe it. The garden has not consumed the pump. It has incorporated it. It has grown into the pump's intake and drive mechanisms and it has *used* them, not by understanding them but by growing into the shapes they provide and finding that the shapes do useful work. The pump is a tool the garden wears the way a hermit crab wears a shell — not because it chose the shell, but because the shell was there, and it fit, and it works.

If the garden has a relationship to anything, it has a relationship to the deep water. Not a relationship of feeling. A relationship of dependence. The deep water is what the garden needs, what the flowers reach for, what the whole structure serves. Without it, the garden would be a very large plant in a very large space, growing slowly, dying slowly. With it, the garden is what it is — a system, a body, a world. The deep water is the garden's reason, if reason can be applied to something that does not reason.

## The Symbionts

### The Moss-Coating (the Skin)

It is not moss. It occupies the ecological niche of moss and it looks, at a distance, like moss, but its cellular structure places it closer to the bryophyte lineage's deep ancestors — something that diverged from the line leading to true mosses long before mosses existed, that found its way into this environment and stayed, and has been here almost as long as the garden itself.

It is a symbiotic complex, technically. Three organisms, at minimum, living in such tight association that they function as one: a photosynthetic partner that provides energy from the filtered light, a decomposer partner that breaks down the garden's shed material, and a structural partner that holds the other two in a matrix of fibrous tissue adhered to the garden's wet surfaces. The complex reproduces by spores, carried in the water that films every surface, and it establishes on any exposed root or bark or wood that the garden has not actively sealed. It is everywhere. It is the garden's skin.

Its function is digestion. The garden sheds — bark fragments, dead root tissue, leaf litter, the occasional maintenance organism that has reached the end of its function cycle — and the moss-coating consumes this material, breaking it down into compounds the garden's roots can reabsorb through the drainage channels. It is the garden's detritivore, its decomposer, its gut flora. Without it, the garden would choke on its own waste. With it, the garden is a closed loop: it grows, it sheds, the moss-coating digests the shed material, the garden reabsorbs the nutrients, it grows again. Nothing leaves the system. Nothing is wasted.

The moss-coating does not pulse. It does not glow. It does not make sounds. It simply covers, and digests, and covers, and digests, and it has been doing this for so long that the garden's root structure has evolved — or has been shaped by the moss-coating's presence — to expect it. The grooves in the root floor, the drainage channels that carry water to the perimeter, are also nutrient channels, carrying the products of the moss-coating's digestion back to the garden's feeding roots. The garden and its skin have co-evolved. They are not separate organisms in any functional sense. They are parts of a single system that happens to have more than one genetic lineage.

### The Fungal Network (the Nerves)

The mycelium — and it is mycelium, true fungal tissue, though from a lineage no mycologist would recognize — threads through every cubic centimeter of the garden's structure. It is pale, almost translucent, and in the deeper chambers it glows: a pale green bioluminescence, faint, sourceless, a light that seems to come from the wood itself rather than from any distinct filament. This is not unusual for fungi. What is unusual is the density, the complexity, and the speed.

The fungal network carries chemical signals between distant parts of the garden at speeds that should not be possible for mycelial transmission. Normal fungal networks — the mycorrhizal networks of ordinary forests, the mycelial webs of conventional mushrooms — transmit signals through chemical gradients and cytoplasmic flow, and they do so slowly, over hours or days. This network transmits in seconds. The mechanism is unclear. The filaments are slightly thicker than conventional hyphae, and they appear to have a layered structure — an outer membrane, a conductive core, something between a nerve fiber and a root — and the signals they carry are not simple chemical gradients but complex, patterned, structured pulses that the garden's tissues respond to in specific ways.

When the flowers pulse, the fungal network spikes. When the garden flexes — when a root structure swells with hydraulic pressure to shift water from one zone to another — the fungal network carries the instruction. When a maintenance organism finds damage and seals it, the fungal network carries the report. It is the garden's communications infrastructure, its nervous system, and it is also, possibly, its memory. The density of the network in the lower chambers — where the flowers are most numerous, where the deep water is closest — suggests that the most important processing happens there, and the network radiates outward from that center, carrying the flowers' instructions to the periphery.

The fungal network does not exist independently of the garden. It cannot be separated from the root structure without dying. It has evolved, or been shaped, to exist only within the garden's body, in the spaces between wood cells, in the gaps in the bark, in the channels the garden's own growth has created. It is an endosymbiont — a parasite that has become an organ, an invader that has become infrastructure.

It is possible, though there is no way to confirm this from within the garden, that the fungal network is the reason the garden is what it is. That without the network, the garden would be a large plant, and with it, the garden is a *system*. That the network provided the coordination, the communication, the distributed processing that allowed a single genetic individual to grow to cathedral scale and maintain itself as a coherent, responsive, purposeful whole. The garden and the network may have made each other. They may still be making each other.

### The Ferns and Epiphytes (the Commensals)

They are not important. Or rather — they are not important *to the garden*, which is the only frame of reference the garden has.

The ferns are true ferns, or close enough. They colonize the garden's joints and seams, rooting in the accumulations of organic matter that collect in the crotches of branches and the angles where root meets root. They are opportunists, survivors, the kind of organisms that arrive at any suitable substrate and establish themselves without asking permission. They drink the garden's water. They absorb the garden's filtered light. They shed their fronds into the drainage channels, where the moss-coating digests them and returns their nutrients to the garden. They are, from the garden's perspective, harmless. They intercept light the garden's own leaves would otherwise catch, but the amount is negligible. They occupy space the garden could otherwise fill, but the space is small and the garden is in no hurry. They are below the threshold.

The epiphytes are more varied — at least four distinct species, possibly more, clinging to the upper canopy and sending their own root systems downward to tap the garden's surfaces for water and minerals. Some of them flower, small and bright, in the filtered light near the ceiling. Their flowers are ordinary. They are colorful. They attract nothing, because there are no pollinators here, and they produce fruit that goes unpicked and falls into the drainage channels and is digested by the moss-coating and returned to the garden. They are running on instinct, on genetic programs that expect a world that no longer exists here — a world with insects, with birds, with wind. They persist because they can, not because they should, and the garden tolerates them because tolerance costs nothing.

These commensals give the garden its texture, its visual complexity, its sense of being a *place* rather than a *thing*. They are the difference between a clean machine and a lived-in one. But they are not the garden's purpose, and they are not the garden's inhabitants, and if they were gone tomorrow the garden would not notice. It would grow into the spaces they left behind. It would continue.

## The Flowers (the Interface)

They are the garden's most specialized structures and its most mysterious, and the mystery is not a romantic mystery. It is a functional one. The flowers do something that the rest of the garden cannot do, and the garden produces them for that purpose, and the purpose is the deep water.

They are not flowers in the reproductive sense. They produce no pollen, no nectar, no seed. They have no petals in the conventional meaning — the thick, waxy structures that look like petals are not modified leaves but modified roots, flattened and exposed and surface-textured to maximize their contact with the air. They are, in a sense, the garden's lungs turned inside-out: organs of exchange, surfaces where something moves from one medium to another.

What moves is heat and signal and something chemical. The flowers are warmer than the surrounding air because they are metabolizing the substance the deep water carries — breaking it down, concentrating it, converting it into the chemical signals the fungal network distributes. They are refineries. They are processing plants. They are the garden's solution to the problem of the deep water: how to extract what it carries, how to convert it into something the garden can use, how to distribute it to every cell in a body that is forty meters across and many more meters deep.

The pulse is the cycle of that processing. Expansion: the flower's tissues fill with the deep water's cargo, drawn up through the roots that extend below the grating. Contraction: the flower metabolizes the cargo, releasing the chemical signals the fungal network carries, and the heat is a byproduct. Thirty seconds per cycle. Thousands of flowers in the deeper chambers, all synchronized, all pulsing together, all releasing their signals together, and the garden's body responds — flexing, shifting, groaning, the hydraulic redistribution that keeps the whole structure fed and watered and intact.

The flowers are also the garden's *only* point of contact with whatever is below. The deep water — the thing the flowers' roots reach into — is not something the garden's main root structure can access directly. It is too deep, or too hot, or too chemically hostile, or too something. The flowers are adapted to it. They are the garden's specialists, its deep-sea divers, its extremophiles. They grow where the garden's body cannot, and they bring back what the garden needs.

The smell — mineral, metallic, warm — is the smell of the deep water's chemistry, filtered through the flowers' metabolism. It is the smell of the thing the garden drinks, and it permeates the lower chambers because the flowers are working here, constantly, and their work has a smell the way all metabolism has a smell.

If the garden has a mind — and it may, in some sense, if the fungal network's signal patterns constitute a form of computation — then the flowers are its input. They are the only structures that bring information *in*, as opposed to processing information already present in the system. They are the garden's eyes, its ears, its tongue, pointed downward, tasting the deep water, and reporting what they taste to the network that wraps the garden's entire body.

They do not open. They do not close. They do not wilt, do not fade, do not cycle through seasons. They have been here, in this alcove, in this deepest chamber, pulsing at thirty-second intervals, for as long as the garden has needed what they provide, and that is a very long time.

## The Maintenance Organisms (the Hands)

They are the garden's only mobile structures, and they are not independent creatures. They are the garden's own tissue, shaped and released and deployed to do work the stationary body cannot do for itself.

Each one is about the size of a human hand, and each one is composed of the same material as the garden's root structure — wood, fiber, bark — but organized into a body plan that permits movement. The fibrous legs operate by hydraulic pressure: fluid pumped into a chamber causes the leg to extend; fluid withdrawn causes it to contract. The movement is slow, deliberate, mechanical in the literal sense. They do not scurry. They do dart. They move with the same patient, unvarying pace that characterizes everything the garden does, and they move with a precision that suggests they are not wandering but *working* — following routes, checking stations, performing routines.

They are produced by the garden, probably from the same meristematic tissue that produces new root growth. They are not born. They are grown — budded off from the root structure at points where the garden's maintenance processes detect a need for mobile intervention, and they detach, and they begin their work, and they work until they stop, and when they stop, they are where they were when they stopped, and the moss-coating grows over them, and they are digested, and their nutrients return to the garden, and the garden grows a new one.

Their tendrils — the anterior cluster of root-like filaments — are sensory and manipulatory. They probe. They test. They sample the surfaces they move across, detecting chemical changes: damage, decay, blockage, foreign material. When they find damage, they exude resin. When they find blockage, they clear it. When they find foreign material — a corroded pipe, a piece of machinery — they... explore it. They thread through it. They grow into it, slowly, over days or weeks, and the garden's main root structure follows, and the foreign material is incorporated.

They do not react to you. You are not on their list of things to respond to. You are not a blockage, not damage, not foreign material — or you are foreign material, but you are large and mobile and you will leave, eventually, and the garden does not invest energy in things that will leave. If you stayed — if you sat down in a corner and did not move — they would eventually find you, and they would explore you, and they would begin the slow process of growing into you, and it would take weeks, and you would have plenty of time to leave, and the garden would not resent your departure.

They are not individuals. They do not have identities. They are tools — the garden's hands, its immune cells, its cleaning crew — and when you see one on the ceiling sealing a crack, and another on the floor clearing a drainage channel, and a third inside a corroded machinery housing, you are seeing the same organism in three places, doing three things, and the organism is the garden.

In the deepest chamber, near the grating, the maintenance organisms are different. They are larger — the size of a human forearm, or larger — and their tendrils are longer, more numerous, and they do not move along the surfaces. They move *into* the grating, down through the gaps, into the darkness below, and they do not come back up for a long time. When they do come back up, they are carrying something — a film of something dark and warm on their tendrils, something that the flowers' roots also carry, and they move to the flowers, and they deposit what they carry on the flowers' surfaces, and the flowers absorb it, and the pulse quickens, very slightly, for a moment.

These are the garden's miners. Its deep workers. They go where the flowers' roots cannot reach, to the source of the deep water, and they bring back what they find. They are the only maintenance organisms that enter the space below the grating, and they are the only maintenance organisms that have a route back up, and the garden produces them carefully, in small numbers, and they are — if the garden has anything like concern — the garden's most precious tools.

## What Does Not Live Here

There are no animals. No insects, no birds, no reptiles, no mammals. There are no spiders, no worms, no nematodes that you can find. The garden's environment — warm, wet, saturated with tannin and the chemical traces of the deep water — may be hostile to animal life, or the garden may have evolved to exclude it, or there may never have been any here to begin with. The passage you entered through, the spiraling ramp of lateral roots, does not connect to any surface environment that would support the introduction of animals. The garden may be a closed system, biologically. It may have been closed for a very long time.

There are no microbes, or none that you can detect. The moss-coating and the fungal network may occupy the ecological niches that bacteria would otherwise fill, or the deep water's chemistry may be hostile to microbial life, or the garden's own immune processes — the maintenance organisms, the resinous exudates — may suppress microbial colonization. The garden is clean in a way that no natural system is clean. It is sterile, or nearly so, and this may be by design.

There are no people. There have been people — the built structures prove this, the pipes and the pumps and the machinery housings that the garden is consuming — but they have not been here for a long time, and the garden does not remember them, and the garden does not miss them, and the garden does not wonder where they went. The garden does not wonder anything. It drinks. It grows. It persists.

## What Lives Here, Then

One organism, if you count by genetics. Four, if you count by lineage — the garden, the moss-coating, the fungal network, and the deep-water extremophiles that the flowers may or may not constitute as a separate entity. Dozens, if you count by function — the maintenance organisms, the commensal ferns and epiphytes, the unknown things in the deep water that the flowers' roots and the deep workers' tendrils touch.

One system, if you count by behavior. The Percolation Gardens are not a garden in any sense a human would recognize. They are not cultivated, not tended, not arranged. They are a single living thing that has grown to fill a space, and the space was once an industrial facility built to extract something from deep below, and the living thing has replaced the facility with itself, and it has been doing this for centuries, and it is not finished, and it may never be finished, and it does not know what *finished* means.

What lives here is a process. A very slow, very large, very patient process that happens to have a body, and the body is made of wood and water and fungus and the ghosts of machinery, and the process is: *drink, grow, incorporate, repeat.* The flowers pulse. The fungal network carries the instructions. The maintenance organisms do the work. The moss-coating digests the waste. The deep water provides what is needed. And the garden breathes — that compound groan, that arrhythmic ticking, that deep subsonic pulse — and it has been breathing for longer than anyone has been listening, and it will breathe for longer than anyone will listen, and it does not know you are here, and it does not need to know, and it does not mind.

It is not lonely. It is not content. It is not aware. It is a machine made of wood that has been running for a very long time, and the machine works, and that is enough.

The water falls. The flowers pulse. The garden breathes.

That is what lives here.
