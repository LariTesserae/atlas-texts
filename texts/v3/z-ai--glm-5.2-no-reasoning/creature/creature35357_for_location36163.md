# What Lives Here

---

## The Gold

Start with the walls, because the walls are where you first understand that this place is occupied.

The organism — if it is one organism, and this is not settled — adheres to the amber panels in patches that have no clear beginning or edge. Where one colony ends and another begins is a question the colonies do not appear to have asked. They are the color of the amber they cover, which raises a question that no one who has visited this place has been able to answer: did they adopt this color because they live on the amber, or was the amber chosen — by the builders, by the water, by the organisms themselves — because it matched? The question implies intention. The question implies a kind of aesthetics. It is not clear that anything here is capable of aesthetics, and it is not clear that anything here is not.

They are soft. This is the first thing you learn when you touch them, if you touch them, which you should not do but which you will do, because the color invites it. They are soft the way a lung is soft — yielding but dense, wet but not slimy, warm but not hot. They are the temperature of the water, which is the temperature of a body, which is the temperature of something alive. When you press them, they compress. When you release, they do not spring back. They refill. Slowly. The water moves back into the space your finger made, and the organism re-expands, and in a minute there is no evidence you were there. This is the closest thing to forgiveness the place offers.

They breathe. Not with lungs or gills. They breathe through their entire surface, which is their entire body, which is also their entire interface with the world. The water moves through them. Not past them — *through* them. The saline enters their tissues at the upstream edge, during each fourteen-minute current cycle, and exits at the downstream edge, carrying with it whatever the organism has added to it and whatever the organism has taken from it. They are filters. They are also, possibly, digesters. The mineral content of the water that exits them is different from the mineral content of the water that enters them, and the difference is consistent, and the consistency suggests a process, and the process suggests a purpose, and the purpose is: they are eating the water. Or they are eating what the water carries. Or they are eating the water's history, one century at a time, and incorporating it into their tissue, and excreting the rest onto the wall behind them, where it becomes the calcareous deposit — the ridged, patterned, almost-textual layer that covers the amber like a second skin.

The patterns are the thing. The patterns are why you stay, if you stay, watching the walls in the faint honey-light that the organisms themselves produce. The patterns are regular but not repetitive. They change from section to section. They are not language — not in any sense that maps to any language you know — but they are not random. They are structured. They have a grammar of sorts, a syntax of density and spacing, of ridge-height and trough-depth, and the grammar changes across the wall in a way that corresponds, if you map it carefully enough — and no one has mapped it carefully enough — to the changes in the mineral content of the water over time. The organisms are recording. They are writing the water's history in calcium on the walls of a room that was built by hands that may have been human and that are now dissolved into the same water the organisms are writing about.

This is what lives here, primarily. A writing thing. A thing that eats its subject and excretes its record. A thing that is gold and darkens to near-black every fourteen minutes when the current shifts, and then lightens, and then darkens again, in a rhythm that has no audience and needs no audience and has been happening for longer than anything with eyes has been watching.

## The Readers

The shelled organisms are harder to love. This is not a judgment. This is a recognition. They are small — the largest is the length of a thumb, the smallest barely visible, and size does not appear to correlate with age in any way you can track. Their shells are not the shells of mollusks you know. They are not spiral. They are not bivalved. They are single, cap-shaped structures, dark as the grout they live in, and they are rough with mineral deposit — the same mineral the wall organisms excrete, the same mineral the water carries, the same calcium that makes the patterns on the walls. The shells are, in a sense, made of the same thing the record is made of. The readers are made of the text.

They have no eyes. This is important. This is not a detail. The absence of eyes in a creature that lives in a place with light — faint light, biological light, but light — is a statement. It says: sight was not needed. It says: sight was never needed here. It says: this place was built, or evolved, or settled into, by creatures for whom the visual was not a primary channel, and the wall organisms' bioluminescence is not for them, and the patterns on the walls are not for them, and the honey-glow that suffuses the water is not for anything that lives here. The light is for no one. The light is a byproduct. The light is waste.

What they have is a foot. A muscular, climbing foot, broad and flat, that secretes a mucus which adheres to the stone and the amber and the calcareous deposit and allows them to move with a tenacity that is difficult to parse as either determination or instinct. They move at one body-length per hour. This is not slow to them. This is their speed. This is the speed at which their life happens, and at this speed, a journey across a single amber panel takes days, and a journey from the floor to the ceiling takes weeks, and a journey around the entire chamber takes — it has never been measured. No reader has been tracked long enough to know.

They follow the veins. This is the behavior that defines them. They press their mouthparts — a rasping structure, not unlike a radula but more complex, with serrations that are themselves serrated — against the surface of the amber, where the mineral veins run, and they move along those veins, and they consume. What they consume is the mineral deposit that the water has laid down in the amber's permeable channels over centuries — iron, calcium, silica, traces of compounds that have no names because they have only been found here and have not been studied enough to name. They are eating the water's history. They are eating the same history the wall organisms are writing.

If the wall organisms are the scribes, the readers are the — what? The students? The archivists? The editors? They are consuming the record, and in consuming it, they are changing it. They are editing the text by eating it. The veins they have followed are eroded, deepened, opened further to the water, which then deposits new mineral in the channels they have cleared, which the wall organisms then record, which the readers then eat. It is a cycle. It is a conversation. It is a thing that has been happening for so long that the participants have become the conversation, and the conversation has become the place, and the place is what it is because of what is eating it and what is writing it and what is reading it, and none of these are separate activities, and none of these are separate creatures.

There are thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Maybe more. They are in every joint, every seam, every gap. They are on the walls and on the floor and on the ceiling, though the ceiling is harder to reach and fewer of them attempt it. They are in the grout channels, where the eroded compound has made room for their bodies, and they are in the amber veins, where the mineral deposits are richest, and they are on the calcareous deposits, where the wall organisms' patterns are deepest and most complex. They are everywhere that a surface meets water, which is everywhere that a surface exists, which is everywhere.

They do not interact with each other. They do not avoid each other. They do not seek each other. When two readers meet on the same vein — and this happens, because the veins branch and converge and the readers follow them — they pass each other without acknowledgment. Their mouthparts continue rasping. Their feet continue gripping. They do not pause. They do not deviate. They are alone in a way that is not loneliness, because loneliness requires the concept of company, and company requires the concept of other, and it is not clear that the readers have this concept. They have veins. They have mouthparts. They have the next centimeter of mineral. This is their world, and it is a complete world, and it is enough.

## The Bodies

The things in the center of the chamber are the hardest to write about, because they are the hardest to see and the hardest to understand and the hardest to place in any taxonomy that was built for creatures that live in light and air and the linear time of seasons and days.

They are the size of a human forearm. This is a comparison that would mean nothing to them — they have never seen a human forearm, and if they had, they would not have the capacity to register it — but it means something to you, because it gives you a scale, and scale is what you need when you are trying to imagine something you cannot see. A forearm. A cylinder. Gelatinous, but not weak. The gelatin is dense, structured, with the notochord running through it like a spine through a body that has forgotten what spines are for. The notochord is not bone. It is not cartilage. It is something else — a stiffened column of tissue that has more in common with the amber panels than with any skeletal structure you know. It is the same cloudy gold, seen dimly through the dark gelatin that surrounds it, and it flexes, and the flexing is movement, and the movement is how they hold their position.

They do not hold their position well. They drift. The current, even in the center of the chamber where it is weakest, is stronger than their ability to resist it, and over the course of each fourteen-minute cycle, they drift — north, then south, then north — in a range of perhaps a meter. They have been drifting in this meter for as long as anyone has been watching. They do not drift further. They do not drift less. They exist in a meter of water, and the meter of water is their territory, and their territory is defined not by boundaries but by the limits of their ability to resist.

What are they doing? This is the question that has been asked by everyone who has spent time in this place, and the answer is: nothing visible. They are not eating. They are not reproducing. They are not interacting with the walls or the floor or the ceiling or each other. They are not producing light. They are not producing sound. They are not producing anything that can be measured or collected or named. They are present. They are warm. They are dark. They are there.

But they must be doing *something*. Everything does something. Everything that lives has a function, and the function is not for itself — the function is for the system, and the system is the place, and the place is the water and the stone and the amber and the organisms and the readers and the bodies, and the bodies must have a role.

This is what has been proposed, by the few who have spent enough time in the chamber to think about it: the bodies are the heat source. The bodies are the reason the water is warm. The bodies are the reason the current moves, the reason the fourteen-minute oscillation exists, the reason the thermal differential between the center and the edges produces the slow breathing of the water through the chamber. The bodies generate heat. They generate it the way any living thing generates heat — through metabolism, through the slow burning of something — and they have been generating it for so long, and they are so many, and the water is so still, that the heat has become the dominant fact of the place, and the current has become the breath of the place, and the place has become the body of the place, and the bodies are the heart.

If this is true, then everything here exists because of them. The wall organisms grow where the water is warmest, which is where the heat from the bodies has diffused to the edges. The readers follow the mineral veins that the water has deposited, and the water has deposited them because the current has moved them, and the current has moved because of the heat, and the heat is the bodies. The amber panels are permeable because the water has been passing through them for millennia, and the water has been passing through them because the current pushes it, and the current pushes because the bodies are warm. The place is what it is because the bodies are what they are.

And what are they? They are not fish. They are not jellyfish. They are not tunicates, though they may be related to something that once was, in some shallow sea that no longer exists, in some time before the builders found this stone and began to cut. They are their own thing. They are the thing that lives in the center of a drowned cathedral and makes it warm by being alive.

They do not know they are the heart. They do not know they are the heat source. They do not know that the current exists, or that the wall organisms write, or that the readers read, or that the amber holds a record, or that the stone was cut by hands. They do not know any of this. They are warm and dark and they drift in a meter of water, and they have been doing this for longer than the water can remember, and the water cannot remember anything, and they cannot remember anything, and the place remembers everything, and no one is reading the place except the readers, and the readers are reading only the veins, and the veins are only the water, and the water is only the heat, and the heat is only the bodies.

## The Absent

The builders are gone. They are not here. They are the most important residents of this place, and they are not in it.

They left the stone. They left the tool marks. They left the amber, shaped and cured and paneled with a care that has outlasted whatever reason they had for paneling it. They left the floor tiles, polished to a sheen that the water has not yet dulled. They left the channels in the joints — deliberate, the evidence suggests, designed for water to pass through, which means they knew the water would come, or they brought the water with them, or they built in water and for water and never expected anything else.

They left no bones. This is worth noting. In a place where bone tools may have been used, where the tool marks are consistent with hand tools, where the scale is human and the patience is human and the impulse — to build, to make, to shape a room in a deep place and fill it with warm salt water — is an impulse that is recognizable if not explicable, the absence of bones is either meaningful or meaningless, and the place does not tell you which.

They left no images. No carvings, no representations, no figures on the walls. The patterns on the calcareous deposit are the work of the organisms, not the builders. The builders left only the structure itself — the room, the stone, the amber, the floor — and the structure is not an image. The structure is a frame. It is a frame for the water, and the water is a frame for the life, and the life is a frame for the heat, and the heat is a frame for the bodies, and the bodies are a frame for — what?

For time. The bodies are a frame for time. They have been here for so long that they have become the clock of the place. The current moves when they move it. The organisms write when the current moves. The readers read when the organisms write. The amber veins when the readers eat. The stone settles when the amber veins. Everything happens because something else happened, and the first thing that happened — the first thing in the chain, the thing that started the clock — was the builders, cutting stone, placing it, shaping amber, laying tile, making a room.

They are gone. They are the ghost in the machine. They are the hand that wound the clock and walked away. They are the reason the place exists and the reason the place has no reason and the reason the question "why" echoes in the water like a sound that was retired.

## What Lives Here, Finally

What lives here is a system. Not a community — community implies members, and members imply boundaries, and the boundaries here are not where the organisms end but where the water ends, and the water does not end. Not an ecosystem — ecosystem implies a relationship between living and non-living, and the living and non-living here have been negotiating for so long that the distinction has dissolved, like the grout, like the boundary between colony and colony on the wall.

A system. A process. A thing that is happening, has been happening, will continue to happen. The wall organisms write the water's history. The readers eat the water's history. The bodies generate the heat that moves the water that carries the history. The builders built the room that holds the water that carries the heat that moves the water. The stone holds the room. The amber holds the veins. The veins hold the record. The record holds the time. The time holds the place. The place holds everything.

And everything is alive, and everything is not alive, and the difference is a question that was asked once and then retired, like sound, like sight, like the concept of outside.

The current shifts.

The gold darkens at the upstream edge.

The readers rasp.

The bodies drift.

The water remembers nothing.

The walls remember everything.

The place continues.
