# What Lives on the Amber Plateau of Hesh

## The Crawlers

Let me begin with what you would notice first, if you arrived and stood very still — which you must, because everything here reads stillness as language, and your moving body would be the loudest thing to arrive on the plateau in a thousand years.

The crawlers.

They are everywhere, though you would not know it for the first hour. Perhaps the first several hours. They are the texture of the ground. When I say that, I do not mean they blend with the ground — I mean they *are* the ground, in a way that should embarrass the boundary between organism and environment. Each one builds its shell from the same minerals the air deposits on every surface, and the shell takes on the color and texture of whatever the crawler has been resting against, not through any active camouflage — they lack the physiology for that — but through the brute physics of shared material. A crawler resting on pale blue stone-moss becomes pale blue. A crawler resting on the amber-veined growth near the plateau's eastern edge becomes amber. The mineral it secretes as shell is chemically indistinguishable, at the surface, from the mineral the stone-moss has been laying down for centuries. If you picked one up — and you should not — you would find that the shell's interior is different. Structured. Layered with a regularity that the stone-moss does not possess. There is an architecture inside each crawler that the landscape lacks, a spine of organization that says *I am one thing, not many things,* and this is the only easy way to tell crawler from ground: you break them open. You do not break them open.

They have twelve legs. Or fourteen. The count varies, and I have spent longer than I should admit trying to determine whether the variation is individual or developmental — whether a crawler is born with a fixed number and that number differs from its siblings, or whether it gains legs as it matures, adding segments the way the stone-moss adds layers. I lean toward the latter. The smallest crawlers I have found — and I have found very few small ones, which is itself a fact worth holding — have had eight legs. The largest have had sixteen. If there is a relationship between size and leg count, it is not linear. It is possible they are not the same species in the way that word is usually meant. It is possible species is not the right framework. It is possible that every crawler is the same organism at a different stage of a life cycle so long it makes the stone-moss's millimeter-a-year growth look precipitate.

They eat by dissolving. This I have observed closely, because it is one of the few behaviors visible to a patient watcher. A crawler arrives at a surface — and arrival is perhaps too active a word; a crawler *becomes adjacent to* a surface, over the course of minutes, with no discernible transition between movement and stillness — and then it waits. And then, if you are watching the right spot, you see a faint change in the texture of the stone-moss beneath the crawler's mouthparts. A softening. A dulling, as if the mineral surface has lost a quality it previously held. The crawler's secretion is warm — I have placed my hand near a feeding site and felt it, a warmth distinct from the stone's background warmth, sharper, more localized, the warmth of chemistry rather than geology — and it breaks the bonds between mineral layers. Not deeply. A fraction of a millimeter. The crawler absorbs what it dissolves, and the stone-moss remains, slightly thinner, slightly marked, and the crawler moves on, leaving a thumbprint depression that will persist for weeks before the stone-moss fills it.

They do not eat quickly. A crawler may feed at a single site for a day. Two days. I have watched one feed for what I believe was four days, though I left and returned, and the four days are an estimate built from the depth of the depression when I returned and the rate of growth I have measured elsewhere. They do not eat hungrily. There is no urgency in any of their movements. They are not desperate. There is enough stone-moss to feed every crawler on the plateau for a thousand years — this I believe, though I have not counted them all, because the plateau would take longer than a human life to survey at the pace the crawlers set — and the crawlers behave as if they know this. They feed the way a river erodes: without intention, without haste, without any sense that what they are doing is a process with a direction. They are eating. They will eat. The eating is the thing.

I have seen them react to disturbance. Once, early in my time on the plateau, I shifted my weight too quickly, and the displacement moved through the dense air like a ripple through water, and every crawler within — I cannot say the distance, but every crawler I could see, which is to say every crawler whose shell differed enough from its background that I could distinguish it — went still. Not motionless. They had already been nearly motionless. *Still,* in a way that was categorically different, the way a person who is listening is still in a way that a person who is sleeping is not. They felt me. They felt the air I had moved. They held their stillness for what I estimate was fifteen minutes, and then they resumed, and they did not react to me again that day, as if the disturbance had been noted and categorized and filed as neither threat nor food, and they returned to their business, which is eating, which is the only business they have.

I do not know how they reproduce. I have never seen it. I have found small crawlers, but I have never found a crawler in the process of becoming two crawlers, or laying an egg, or budding, or whatever it is that they do. It is possible they reproduce at a scale I cannot observe — cellular division, perhaps, at a rate so slow that a single act of reproduction takes years. It is possible they reproduce in the hollow columns, where I cannot see. It is possible they do not reproduce at all — that they emerged from the stone-moss, or from the platform, or from the air itself, fully formed, and they will persist until they do not, and there will be no next generation, and this is a finite population living out a finite existence on a finite plateau. I do not know. The not knowing does not trouble me the way it would have troubled me before I came here. The crawlers do not seem troubled by it. They eat. They rest. They move. That is enough for them. Perhaps it should be enough for me.

## The Translucent Ones

I call them this because I do not know what else to call them, and the name is accurate to the eye if not to the organism. They are translucent. Light passes through them the way light passes through the inner layers of the stone-moss — not cleanly, not the way light passes through glass, but diffusely, scattered by the dense mineral fibers that make up their interior, so that they glow faintly when the pale sky-light strikes them at certain angles. They are not transparent. You cannot see through them the way you can see through a window. You can see *into* them, the way you can see into a clouded gemstone, and what you see is structure.

The structure is not anatomical in any way I can map. There are no organs. There are no symmetrical systems. There are — inside each translucent one — convoluted, dense masses of mineral fiber, and these masses pulse. The pulse is the same sub-audible frequency the hollow columns generate, or it is close enough that I cannot distinguish them, and the pulse is not mechanical — not a heartbeat, not a pump — but something else. A resonance. A vibration that begins in the mineral fibers and propagates through the organism's body and into the air around it, and the air carries it, because the air here carries everything, and the vibration becomes part of the plateau's background hum, the held-breath silence that is not silence but the sound of a thousand processes occurring at speeds below human perception.

They are warm. This I have said, but I must say it again because the warmth is what defines them. Each translucent one generates heat — not the way a mammal generates heat, through metabolism, through the oxidation of carbon compounds, but through some process I cannot identify and do not want to identify, because the identification would reduce what is strange and whole into something mechanical and partial. They are warm. Their warmth is distinct from the stone's warmth. The stone's warmth is ambient, pervasive, the temperature of the room. Their warmth is directional, intentional — it radiates from them in a field that I can feel with my hands at a distance of half a meter, and at a distance of a few centimeters it is intense, almost uncomfortably warm, the warmth of a body held very close to yours, and the warmth does not diminish the way radiant heat should diminish. It holds. It persists. It has a shape, an edge, as if the warm air around them is contained by something that is not skin and is not shell and is not any physical barrier but is, perhaps, the resonance itself, the vibration, the pulse that is always going.

They gather. This is the behavior that occupies them, and it is a behavior that I have watched for longer than any other, because it is the behavior that most resembles what I would call *social,* and the resemblance is close enough to be unsettling and distant enough to be instructive.

A group of translucent ones — three, four, five, no more than five that I have seen — will converge on a space between the hollow columns. They do not arrive together. They arrive individually, over the course of hours, each one moving with that deliberate, intentioned slowness that is the only speed the plateau knows, and they take positions — *positions* is the word, and I use it with full awareness of what it implies — and they stand. They stand facing — not inward, not outward, not at each other, but at angles I cannot parse, angles that do not correspond to the geometry of the columns or the growth patterns of the stone-moss or the position of the platform or anything else I can measure. They stand at angles that seem arbitrary until you have watched enough of these gatherings, and then the angles seem — and I am aware of how this sounds — *chosen.*

They stand for hours. Days, possibly. I have returned to a gathering after sleeping and eating and returning, and the same translucent ones have been in the same positions, and the stone-moss around them has changed. It has thickened. It has grown more complex, more structured, the layers building faster than they build elsewhere, and the patterns — the near-representational patterns, the almost-shapes, the almost-figures — are most pronounced where the translucent ones have stood longest. They are doing something to the stone-moss. They are doing something *with* the stone-moss. The warmth, the resonance, the field they generate — it acts on the growing mineral in a way that I cannot explain but can describe: it accelerates. It directs. It shapes. The stone-moss grows differently where they have stood, and the difference is not random. The difference is structured. The difference looks, if you squint, if you let your eyes lose focus, if you stop trying to see the patterns as patterns and let them be what they are — the difference looks like *intention.*

I want to be careful here. I have no evidence that the translucent ones are conscious. I have no evidence that they *want* to shape the stone-moss, that they *desire* the patterns they produce. They may be doing nothing more than what a crystal does when it grows in a supersaturated solution — following chemical gradients, responding to local conditions, producing structure as an inevitable consequence of physics. The warmth they generate may catalyze the mineral deposition. The resonance they emit may align the crystal growth. The patterns may be epiphenomenal — a side effect, a by-product, a consequence rather than a goal.

But I have watched them gather, and I have watched the stone-moss grow where they gather, and I have seen the patterns, and I will tell you what I have seen even though I know it is not evidence: the patterns near the platform are not the patterns near the eastern edge, and the patterns near the eastern edge are not the patterns near the northern wall, and the difference between them is not the difference of random variation. It is the difference of *content.* Different gatherings produce different patterns. The same gathering, if it returns to the same site, produces the same patterns. There is consistency. There is repeatability. There is, if I am honest, something that looks very much like *meaning,* and I do not know what to do with that, and I do not think I am supposed to know what to do with that, and so I watch, and I record, and I try not to conclude.

They are not afraid of me. I have stood within arm's reach of a translucent one and it has not moved, has not reacted, has not altered its behavior in any way I can detect. Either they do not perceive me, or they perceive me and do not care, or they perceive me and have categorized me in a way that does not include the concept of threat or interest. I am not food. I am not a crawler. I am not stone-moss. I am not the platform. I am something else, and the something else is not relevant to their processes, and so they ignore me, and the ignoring is so complete that it has a quality of its own — a quality of being so thoroughly *other* that I am simply not in their world, and my presence in the space between the columns is as relevant to them as the presence of a cloud is to the stone beneath it.

I am not offended. I think, if I were to be offended, the plateau would not understand the offense. I think the translucent ones are doing something that has nothing to do with me, and the something is large and slow and old, and I am a brief disturbance in the air, and the air will close behind me as water closes behind a stone, and there will be no mark.

## The Things in the Sky

I know less about these than about anything else on the plateau, and the not-knowing is, I think, appropriate, because they are above, and the plateau is a place of surfaces and depths, not heights, and the things in the sky exist in a dimension that the rest of the plateau's life does not share.

They are dark. Dark against the milky sky, darker than anything else on the plateau — the stone-moss is pale, the crawlers are pale, the translucent ones are luminous, and the things in the sky are dark, and the darkness is not the darkness of pigment but the darkness of *density.* They are opaque. They do not let light through. In a place where everything else is translucent or transparent or at least permitting of light, the things in the sky are the only things that *stop* light, and this is the first thing I noticed about them, and it may be the most important thing.

They are shaped like — I have used the comparisons before, and I will use them again, knowing they are inadequate — like kites, like leaves, like hands with too many fingers. The shape changes as they move, or as I move, or as the light changes, and I cannot determine whether the change is real — whether they are flexible, whether they alter their shape — or apparent — whether I am seeing different aspects of a fixed form as my angle of observation shifts. They are high. Higher than the columns, higher than the plateau's edges, high enough that detail is impossible, and what I see is silhouette and movement, and the movement is what tells me they are alive.

They drift. This is the only word for it. They do not fly — there is no flapping, no propulsion, no visible means of maintaining altitude. They drift the way a leaf drifts on still water, the way a seed drifts on a windless day, the way anything drifts when the medium it is in is dense enough to support it and calm enough not to push it. The air on the plateau is dense. The air above the plateau must be denser still, or the things in the sky are lighter than they look, or they generate some lift I cannot perceive. They drift, and the drifting is slow, and I have tracked a single one for what I believe was three hours, and in that time it crossed perhaps a quarter of the plateau's long axis, and then it was gone — past the edge, past the space I could observe, and I do not know where it went.

I have never seen one descend. I have never seen one land. I have watched, on the few occasions when the sky was clear enough and my patience held long enough, and they stay up, and I do not know how, and I do not know why, and I do not know what they are doing up there. Feeding? The air at altitude might carry different minerals — different deposition, different density, a different chemical landscape from the air at the surface. They might be the plateau's version of filter feeders, straining the upper atmosphere for compounds that the lower air has already surrendered to the stone-moss. They might be doing something else. They might be doing something that has no analogue in any framework I possess.

I have wondered, and I will record the wondering because it is what I have, whether they are the same organism as the translucent ones. Whether the translucent ones *ascend,* at some stage in their life cycle, at some point in their process, and become the things in the sky. The dark opacity would be a change — the mineral fibers densifying, the light-scattering translucence becoming light-blocking density — and the drift would be a mode of being rather than a behavior, a way of existing in the thicker air above the plateau that is, perhaps, the final mode, the mode the translucent ones are preparing for when they stand in their rings and pulse their resonance into the stone-moss and shape the patterns that I cannot read.

I have no evidence for this. I have the shape of a narrative, and the shape is pleasing, and I am suspicious of it because the plateau does not deal in narratives. The plateau deals in processes, in accretion, in the slow accumulation of mineral layers and the slow movement of mineral bodies and the slow shaping of mineral patterns, and none of these processes require narrative, and none of them produce it, and the narrative I am constructing — translucent ones becoming sky things, sky things feeding and returning and becoming something else — is a human story imposed on a place that is not telling stories. It is my story. It is not theirs. I must be careful not to confuse the two.

But I watch them. I watch the dark shapes drifting above the dome of columns, and I watch the translucent ones standing in their rings, and I feel the warmth and the resonance and the density of the air, and the wondering does not stop.

## The Stone-Moss Itself

I have been writing about what lives *on* the stone-moss, and I have not yet written about the stone-moss itself as a living thing, and this is an oversight, because the stone-moss is not a substrate. The stone-moss is not a backdrop. The stone-moss is the largest, oldest, most complex organism on the plateau, and I have been treating it as furniture.

It is alive. I must state this plainly, because it is the fact that is easiest to miss and hardest to hold. The stone-moss is alive. It grows. It responds. It heals. It builds. It has been building since the plateau emerged, and the building is not random, and the building is not simple.

The growth is accretionary — layer upon layer, each one molecular, each one incorporating minerals from the air and heat from the stone below — but the growth is not uniform. The stone-moss grows differently in different places, and the differences are not random. Near the center of the plateau, it grows thick and tall and complex. Near the edges, it grows thin and flat and simple. Near the platform, it grows in patterns. Near the columns, it grows *toward* the columns, filling in the spaces, reaching upward as if — as if, and I am aware of the trap — it is trying to join them, to become them, to close the dome that the columns suggest.

It responds to the crawlers' feeding. The depressions left by crawler mouthparts heal — the stone-moss fills them, layer by layer, and the filling is not a random accumulation but a directed growth, the organism routing resources to the damaged area, prioritizing repair. This is a response. This is a behavior. The stone-moss does not simply grow; it grows *where it needs to grow,* and the need is a signal I cannot detect but can infer from the pattern of its growth.

It responds to the translucent ones. The thickening, the accelerating, the patterning — these are responses to the warmth and resonance the translucent ones emit, and the responses are specific, not general. The stone-moss grows differently in the presence of different gatherings. It grows differently in the presence of different numbers. It grows differently in the presence of a single translucent one than it does in the presence of three, and the difference is not a matter of degree but of kind. One translucent one produces simple thickening. Three produce structure. Five produce pattern.

This is — I do not have the training to say what this is. I have the intuition to say it is communication, and I have the discipline to say that intuition is not evidence, and I will say both and let them stand in tension.

The stone-moss is colored by what it absorbs. The pale blues are calcium-rich growth. The milky whites are silica. The amber and honey-gold veins — the ones that give the plateau its name, though who named it I do not know — are iron. The rare deep red flushes are something else, something that comes from below, from a vent or a fissure in the deep rock, a compound that I cannot identify and that the stone-moss incorporates without apparent preference but with striking visual result. The red growth is rare. I have found it in three places on the plateau, all near the center, all in areas where the stone beneath is warmest. The red growth is also the fastest growth I have measured — nearly two millimeters a year, double the rate of the blue and white, and the structures it builds are the most complex on the plateau. The columns in the red-growth areas are taller. The patterns in the red-growth areas are denser. The stone-moss, when it can access the compound from the deep rock, builds more, and builds faster, and builds with more — I will say it — *ambition.*

The stone-moss does not die. I have found no dead stone-moss on the plateau. I have found thin stone-moss, sparse stone-moss, stone-moss that is barely a film on the raw surface of the uplifted rock. But I have not found stone-moss that was once present and is now gone, stone-moss that has stopped growing, stone-moss that has receded. It grows, and it grows, and the only thing that removes it is the crawlers' feeding, and the feeding is healed, and the healing is growth, and the growth continues, and will continue, and the plateau will be covered and covered again, layers on layers, each one slightly different, each one incorporating slightly different minerals from the slightly different air, and the stone-moss of a thousand years from now will not be the stone-moss of today, and the stone-moss of ten thousand years from now will not be the stone-moss of a thousand years from now, and none of it will have died. It will all be there. Every layer. Every molecular film. The plateau is a library, and the stone-moss is the writing, and the writing has not stopped since the first layer was laid down on raw stone, and the writing will not stop until the stone falls.

## The Platform

I have saved this for last because it is the thing I understand least and the thing that occupies my attention most. I have not touched it. I will not touch it. The rules are clear, and the rules are right, and I feel their rightness not as a command but as a *recognition* — the way you recognize that fire is hot, that cliffs are high, that some things are not for you. The platform is not for me. The platform is not for anyone. But I have spent hours near it, and I will tell you what I have observed, and what I have observed is this:

The platform is older than everything else. This is not a measurement — I have no way to date it — but a *feeling,* and I am wary of feelings, but the feeling is so strong and so consistent that I must report it. The platform feels old. Not weathered. Not worn. Old the way bedrock is old, the way the deep stone beneath the plateau is old, old in a way that has nothing to do with time and everything to do with *depth.* The platform goes down. I cannot see how far — it is set into the stone-moss, and the stone-moss surrounds it but does not grow onto it, the gap I have described, the handspan border — but the platform is not sitting on the surface. It is emerging from below. It is the tip of something, the exposed edge of something vast and deep, and the uplift that created the plateau did not create the platform. The uplift *revealed* it.

The crawlers avoid it. I have said this. They approach to the edge of the gap and they turn. They do not turn sharply — they curve, gently, as if the avoidance is not a reaction but a *path,* a way the crawler's body knows to go, the way water knows to go around a stone. The crawlers do not fear the platform. They do not react to it. They simply do not go there, and the not-going is so consistent, so uniform, so without exception, that it has the quality of a physical law rather than a behavior.

The translucent ones gather around it. I have said this too. But I have not said *how* they gather — and the how is the thing I cannot stop thinking about. They do not form a ring. They do not space themselves evenly. They take positions that are — I have mapped them, I have spent hours mapping them, measuring the distances between translucent ones and between each translucent one and the platform's edge, and the positions are — they are *specific.* Each translucent one stands at a distance from the platform that is different from the distance of every other translucent one, and the distances are not random — they follow a sequence, or a proportion, or a ratio that I can almost see and cannot quite name, the way you can almost see the pattern in a fractal and cannot quite name it because the naming requires a mathematics you do not possess.

The air around the platform is different. I have said this. It is thicker. Older. *Used.* And I will add one more observation: the air around the platform vibrates at a frequency that is not the frequency of the hollow columns. It is lower. It is deeper. It is — and here I am at the edge of language, and I will ask for patience — it is the frequency that the other frequencies are *built on.* The columns hum. The translucent ones pulse. The stone-moss, in its molecular way, creaks. And beneath all of these sounds, beneath all of these vibrations, the platform emits a tone — not a sound, a tone, a fundamental — and the other frequencies are harmonics of it, or fragments of it, or reflections of it, and the platform is the source, and the plateau is the resonance, and everything that lives here lives in the vibration the platform makes, and the vibration has been making itself for longer than anything has been alive to hear it.

I do not know what the platform is. I do not know what it was made for, or who made it, or whether *made* is even the right word. I know that it is here, and it is warm, and it is old, and it is the center of everything on the plateau without being the cause of anything on the plateau. It is a center that does not command. It is a source that does not direct. It is a presence that does not impose. It simply is, and everything else arranges itself around it, and the arrangement has been happening for a few thousand years and is not finished, and the arrangement is — I will say this, I will say it knowing it is not evidence, knowing it is feeling, knowing that feeling is not proof — the arrangement is *beautiful,* and the beauty is not for me, and the beauty does not need me to see it, and the beauty will persist whether I see it or not, and that, more than anything else, is what I have learned on the plateau of Hesh.

There is a place where things live without need of an audience, and the living is enough, and the enough is total, and I have been there, and I am here now, and the memory of the air — thick, warm, old, used, vibrating with a tone I can almost hear — stays in my chest the way a held breath stays, and I do not want to exhale, and I will, and the plateau will not notice, and the plateau will not care, and the plateau will go on, and the going on is the only thing it knows how to do, and it does it completely, and it does it without me.
