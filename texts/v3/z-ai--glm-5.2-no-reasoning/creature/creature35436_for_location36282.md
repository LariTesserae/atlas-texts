# What Lives Here

The first thing to understand is that nothing here is in a hurry.

That sounds like an observation about pace, but it's closer to a description of identity. The cirque's inhabitants — all of them, at every scale — have been shaped by the fact that this place does the same things every day and has been doing them longer than memory. The water falls. The walls give it up. The light moves across the stone bowl in its slow arc. The insects hatch in the wet air. The birds come to eat the insects. Everything else is variation on that theme, and the things that live here have stopped expecting variation. They've committed to the theme.

---

## The Birds

Start with the thrushes, because they're the most difficult to miss.

There are perhaps four of them. Perhaps six. The cirque's acoustics make counting impossible — one thrush calling from the eastern wall produces an echo that sounds like a second thrush calling from the western wall a half-beat later, and by the time the sound has bounced twice you're hearing birds that aren't there. The real number is small. The perceived number is enormous. This is the first thing the thrushes have taught themselves, or been taught by the cirque: that they are more than they are. That a voice thrown into stone becomes many voices. They use this. A male thrush calling from the overhang near the eastern seep fills the entire cirque with his claim. He owns two hundred meters of stone and water and fern, and he owns it because the walls say so.

They are not remarkable thrushes. Dark belly — the color of wet slate, which is to say the color of everything here. Bright eye, which is the only bright thing about them: a small orange-yellow dot of attention that catches the light when the bird turns its head toward you. And it will turn its head toward you. The thrushes watch. Not with alarm — they've seen people before, or they've seen enough things that aren't birds to have a category for creatures that move slowly and don't fly — but with a kind of patient assessment. They watch the way the water watches. Which is to say: without urgency, but without stopping.

They eat at the seeps. This is their primary work. The seeps bring insects; the insects gather in the still, damp air just above the water and the moss; the thrush drops from its ledge and takes them. The technique is unhurried — a hop, a tilt, a movement of the beak so fast you almost miss it, then stillness again. The thrush on a seep looks like a small professor examining a text. It bends the fern fronds down when it lands, and the ferns accept this. The ferns have been bent by thrushes for longer than the thrushes know. There is an agreement between them, unspoken and very old: the thrush may use the fern as a perch; the fern will spring back when the thrush leaves. Neither party considers this an arrangement. It is simply what happens.

The thrushes avoid the pool.

This is worth noting because the thrushes use everything else. Every seep, every mossy patch, every ledge wide enough to grip, every overhang. They fly over the pool without stopping, without slowing, without seeming to notice it. But they do notice it. You can tell because their flight path over the center of the cirque is always slightly higher than it needs to be — a meter of altitude that serves no aerodynamic purpose but keeps them above the pool's reflective surface by a margin that suggests not indifference but decision. They've decided. About the pool. About what lives in it, or what it reflects, or what it means. They won't tell you what they've decided. They don't think in terms you'd recognize. But the decision is visible in the way they route their flights, and it is consistent across every bird that uses the cirque, not just the thrushes. The finches do it too. The pipits do it. Whatever the birds know about the pool, they all know it.

The finches are less interesting than the thrushes. This is not a judgment — the finches would not be offended — but an observation about niches. The finches work the upper walls. They cling to the rough schist where the moss thins out and the vertical surfaces are still damp but less weeping, and they take insects from the stone itself: midges that have landed to rest, small flies that have been caught by the film of moisture and can't take off again. They are efficient and quick and they don't linger. A finch in the cirque is a bird in transit — up the wall, across, down, gone, back, up again. They use the echo less. Their calls are shorter, less musical, thrown into the stone and not retrieved. They don't need the amplification. They're not trying to own the place. They're just eating in it.

The pipits are the true migrants. The finches and thrushes may nest nearby — below the cirque, in the scrub where the tree line almost begins, or in cracks in the rock outside the bowl. The pipits pass through. They come in groups of three or four, stay for an hour, and leave. They are the most agitated of the cirque's birds — tails flicking, feet moving, never still on a perch. They feed at the edges, where the water spreads thin and the insects are easiest to catch. They are the birds most likely to startle at a shadow or a sound, and when they startle, they startle together — all four lifting at once, calling, the sound multiplying off the walls until it seems like the cirque itself is alarmed. Then they come back. They always come back. The insects are here and the insects are reliable and a pipit can't afford to be too proud to return.

There is one other bird that should be mentioned, though it is not always present. A raptor — small, a kestrel or something like a kestrel, a bird of the high open country — visits the cirque perhaps once a day. It does not enter. It hangs in the wind above the open edge, riding the updraft that the cirque's shape creates, and it watches. The small birds know it's there before you do. The sound changes. The chattering drops by half, then by half again, until only the water is audible. The thrushes press flat against their ledges. The finches go quiet against the stone. The cirque, for the minutes the kestrel is overhead, becomes what it would be without birds: a stone bowl full of water noise and nothing else. Then the raptor moves on — it doesn't hunt here, or if it does, it doesn't hunt well; the cirque's walls create turbulence that makes precision flight difficult — and the sound returns in stages. Pipits first, testing. Then finches. Then, last, the thrushes, who have the most to say and who say it as if nothing happened. As if the silence was their idea.

## The Insects

The midges are the reason the birds are here, and the midges are here because the water is here, and the water is here because of the shape of the stone. This is a chain of dependency so simple it barely deserves the word *web*, but it is a chain nonetheless, and the insects are the link that holds it.

The midges breed in the seeps. The water is cold, moving, mineral-rich — not ideal for all insects, but ideal for these. They lay eggs in the thin film of water that runs over the moss at the base of the walls, and the larvae live there, in the boundary between wet and not-quite-wet, feeding on what the water brings: organic particles, mineral deposits, the microscopic life that colonizes any surface that stays damp long enough. They are invisible at this stage. You would not know they were there unless you looked closely, and even then you'd need to know what to look for: a movement in the surface film, a thread of life too small to see as a shape but visible as a disturbance.

The adults emerge in the morning, when the sun clears the eastern wall and warms the seeps. They emerge in numbers that are difficult to estimate but easy to characterize: enough. Enough that the air above the ferns, in the still patches near the eastern and western seeps, has a texture to it. Not a swarm — the midges don't swarm in the aggressive way of lowland species. They aggregate. They hang in the air in loose columns, shifting with the air currents but not dispersing, each individual doing whatever tiny midges do — flying in place, mating, resting in air — and the aggregate is what the birds see. The aggregate is the point. A thrush doesn't eat a midge. A thrush eats a column of midges by moving through it and opening its beak.

The craneflies are different. They are larger, more visible, more fragile. Their legs are absurdly long — three or four times the length of their bodies, thin as thread, trailing behind them in flight like afterthoughts. They rest on the fern fronds and on the wet stone, and when they rest, they are still. Completely still. A cranefly at rest on a fern frond looks like a line drawing of an insect — a few strokes, a suggestion of wings, those impossible legs. They are not abundant. You might see ten in a morning. They don't need to be abundant. They need to be present, and they are.

The craneflies have a relationship with the pool that is different from the birds' relationship with the pool. The birds avoid it. The craneflies don't avoid it. They don't seek it either, but they fly over it — they fly over everything, their flight paths drifting and imprecise — and sometimes they touch the surface. A touch, nothing more. The legs hit the water and the surface tension holds, and the cranefly lifts off again, and where it touched, a ring of ripple expands outward and breaks the reflection and then the reflection reassembles. This happens once or twice a day. It is the only thing that disturbs the pool naturally. The tiny interruption, the small ring, the restoration. If the pool is a mirror, the craneflies are the fingerprints on its surface — brief, inconsequential, corrected by the water's patience.

There are other insects. Small flies that live in the moss, too small to identify from a distance, visible only as dark dots that move. Beetles — one kind, small, dark, glossy — that work the edges of the seeps where the water meets dry stone. They move with purpose, unlike the midges, which move with drift, and the craneflies, which move with dream. The beetles know where they're going. They're going to the wet part, to the organic matter that accumulates there, to the food. They are the most pragmatic creatures in the cirque, and they are the least noticed. The thrushes eat them when they can, but the beetles stay close to the stone, close to the crevices, close to the cracks where a beak can't follow.

The insects are the life of the cirque in a way the birds are not. The birds are visitors — daily, reliable, but visitors. The insects are residents. They are born here, they live here, they die here. Their lives are measured in hours or days, and they spend those hours and days in this stone bowl, and when they die, their bodies fall into the seeps and become part of the water, and the water carries them out across the floor and down toward the valley, and the mountain digests them at a speed too slow to watch. They are the only creatures here for whom the cirque is the entire world.

## The Ferns

The ferns are the old ones.

Not the individual plants — a fern frond lives a season, maybe two, and is replaced by another that grows from the same rootstock — but the colony. The ferns at the base of the cirque walls have been here for a very long time. Longer than the wooden post. Longer than the fire ring. Longer, possibly, than the route the shepherds used. They grow where the seeps emerge, in the constant wet, and they have grown there for centuries, and in those centuries they have done what ferns do: they have persisted.

They are small ferns. This is important. They are not the lush, waist-high ferns of a temperate forest floor. They are mountain ferns, adapted to altitude and wind and the particular kind of wet the cirque provides — not rain, not fog, but condensation, the slow constant weeping of stone. They grow knee-high, sometimes lower. Their fronds are dark — darker green than any green at this altitude has a right to be, a green so deep it reads as black in shadow and as something electric, almost luminous, in direct light. This is the chlorophyll working hard. This is a plant that has adapted to low light, cold temperatures, and constant moisture, and has decided to invest in darkness. To be the greenest thing in a grey world. To be so saturated with color that the eye can't quite resolve it.

They grow in clusters. Not random clusters — their distribution follows the seeps exactly. Where water emerges from the base of the walls, ferns. Where the wall is dry, no ferns. It is a map of the cirque's hydrology written in fronds. You could read the seeps from above, just from the fern pattern: dense clusters at the eastern and western seeps, thinner patches along the north wall where the water is less constant, a few scattered individuals near the pool where the water spreads thin and slow. The ferns don't grow in the pool. They grow around it. The pool is the one place in the cirque where water is not a resource but a presence — still, reflective, not moving — and the ferns, like the birds, seem to recognize the difference. They keep their distance. Not much distance — a few centimeters of bare stone between the last frond and the water's edge — but consistent. A margin. A respectful space.

The ferns host the insects. This is their role in the food web beyond simply being plants: they provide surface area. A fern frond is a platform. Midges land on it, craneflies rest on it, beetles climb its stem, and the thrush lands on it and bends it and eats what it finds. The frond accepts all of this. It is wet, always wet, and the wetness holds insects the way a sticky trap holds nothing — by providing a surface that is comfortable enough to land on and damp enough to be worth landing on. The insects don't come for the fern. They come for the water on the fern. The fern is the middleman. It doesn't mind.

When the wind comes through the cirque — and it does, funneled from the open side, compressed and accelerated by the walls — the ferns move. Not dramatically. They don't whip or thrash. They sway, in unison, all the fronds in a cluster leaning in the same direction at the same time, as if the wind were a hand and the ferns were a single thing being stroked. This is their one visible response to the world outside themselves. They take the wind and they move with it and they stop when it stops, and they are exactly where they were before. They don't break. They don't uproot. They are small and flexible and rooted in the seeps, and the seeps hold them, and they hold.

## The Moss

The moss is harder to talk about because the moss doesn't do anything. Or rather: the moss does one thing, and it does it so continuously and so completely that the doing of it is indistinguishable from the being of it.

The moss covers the lower walls. It grows in patches — thick, deep patches, the kind you could press your hand into and feel the moisture rise to meet your palm — and the patches grow into each other over time, so that what was once a mosaic becomes a continuous carpet, and the carpet becomes a feature of the wall itself. The moss is the wall's skin. The wall is bare stone without it — grey, streaked, weeping — and the moss takes that bareness and covers it, not to hide it but to complete it. A weeping wall with no moss is a geological feature. A weeping wall with moss is a habitat.

The moss holds water. This is its function, if it has one. It takes the condensation from the stone and it holds it — in its structure, in the spaces between its stems, in the dense fibrous mat it builds against the rock — and it releases it slowly, so that the wall doesn't just weep in rain and stop in drought. The moss regulates. It is a reservoir, a buffer, a slow-release mechanism that keeps the seeps running even when the air dries and the condensation slows. Without the moss, the cirque would be a different place. The seeps would be intermittent. The ferns would shrink. The insects would thin. The birds would come less often. The moss is the infrastructure. It is the thing that makes the ecosystem stable rather than seasonal, and it does this by being wet and staying wet and being patient about it.

There is one patch of orange moss. Near the eastern seep, where the water emerges richest in iron, the moss has taken on a color that is not green. It is a deep, saturated orange — rust-colored, or the color of dried blood, or the color of the inside of a pepper cut open. It is startling. You walk along the base of the wall, past greens and browns and the dark weeping stone, and then this. Orange. A patch the size of a hand, growing where the iron-rich water flows, and the orange is the iron, or the moss's response to the iron, or some chemistry that neither the moss nor the iron is aware of. It just happens. The moss doesn't know it's orange. The iron doesn't know it's orange. The orange doesn't know it's the only warm color in the cirque, the only thing that breaks the grey-green-white palette, and that's what makes it beautiful. It is beautiful because it doesn't know.

## The Sedges

The sedges grow in the shallower water, where the braided channels spread across the floor and the current is slow enough that a plant can stand in it without being washed out. They are grass-like but not grass — stiffer, more architectural, with stems that rise vertically from the water and stay vertical regardless of the flow. They move at the tops, where the stems thin and the seed heads weigh them down, but their bases are fixed. They are rooted in the stone floor under the water, in the cracks and the thin soil that has accumulated there, and they hold.

When the water moves through them, they move with it — all in the same direction, all by the same amount, all at the same speed. The description in the original account is accurate: like hair being combed. But it's more specific than that. It's like hair being combed by someone who is very patient and very gentle and who has been combing the same hair for a very long time. The sedges lean, the water passes, the sedges return. Lean, pass, return. This is their day. This is their life. They don't flower dramatically. They don't grow tall. They stand in the water and they accommodate it, and in accommodating it, they slow it — each stem a tiny obstacle, each obstacle a small calm zone behind it where sediment settles and microorganisms live and the mountain builds, grain by grain, the thinnest imaginable soil.

The sedges are the least noticed living thing in the cirque. The birds are noticed because of their sound. The ferns are noticed because of their color. The moss is noticed because of its texture. The insects are noticed because they're the reason the birds are here. The sedges are noticed by no one. They are the background. They are the thing that is always there and never remarked upon, and if they were gone, the cirque would be poorer in ways that are difficult to specify but easy to feel. The water would move faster. The floor would be less stable. The ecosystem would lose its quietest structural element. They are the load-bearing wall of the food web, and they are the least celebrated, and they don't care. Celebration is not a concept that applies to sedges.

## The Flowers

The violet flowers grow from the walls. Not from the floor, not from the moss, not from the seeps — from the walls themselves, from crevices in the vertical stone where centuries of wind and water have deposited enough soil to fill a crack. They are small. The petals are pale violet, almost white at the edges, darkening to a true violet at the center, where the reproductive structures sit in a tight cluster. They are not large enough to see from across the cirque. You have to be close — within arm's reach of the wall — to notice them. And once you notice them, you notice them everywhere. They are in every crack that holds soil. They are up and down the walls, at every height where a crevice has formed deep enough to trap windblown dust and old organic matter and the slow mineral residue of the weeping water. They are the cirque's secret. The thing you don't see until you see it.

They open in the morning sun and close in the evening. They are heliotropic — they follow the light, turning their faces toward whichever wall is currently lit, so that in the morning they face east and in the afternoon they face west. This means that at any given time, roughly half the flowers in the cirque are open and facing the light, and the other half are closed or closing, their petals folded inward, waiting for their turn. It is a slow rotation. A day-long turning of faces. If you watched the walls for a full day — if you sat in the cirque and did nothing but watch — you would see the flowers as a slow-motion audience, each one turning toward the sun as it crosses the sky, each one opening when the light reaches it and closing when the light leaves. It is the most deliberate thing that happens here. The water is constant. The birds are erratic. The insects are chaotic. But the flowers are precise. They know where the light is. They always know.

They are pollinated by the midges. This is almost certainly true, though it's difficult to observe — the midges are too small to track individually, and the flowers are too small to watch continuously. But the mechanism is there: the flowers produce a faint scent, barely perceptible, something between mineral and sweet, and the midges, which are attracted to moisture and the organic compounds associated with it, visit the flowers and carry pollen from one to another. It is a pollination system built on the smallest possible scale — a flower the size of a fingernail, an insect the size of a punctuation mark — and it works because the cirque is a closed space. The pollen doesn't need to travel far. The flowers are all here. The midges are all here. The whole exchange happens within two hundred meters of stone, and it has been happening for as long as the flowers have grown in the walls and the midges have flown in the air.

## The Things That Are Not Seen

There are organisms in the pool. This must be true — no body of water is sterile, and the pool, despite its stillness, is fed by the seeps and drained by the seeps and is therefore part of the cirque's water system. But what lives in the pool is invisible to the naked eye. Microscopic. Single-celled, or nearly so. The water is clear — that's what the description says, and it's accurate — but clear water is not empty water. The clarity means that what lives in the pool lives at a scale that doesn't scatter light. It lives in the water the way chemistry lives in the water: present, active, invisible.

What lives in the pool is the mountain's slow metabolism. The bacteria, the protozoa, the microscopic algae that can survive in cold, mineral-rich water with limited light. They process the minerals the water carries — the iron, the calcium, the trace elements the schist releases as it weathers — and they process the organic matter the water brings — the dead insects, the pollen, the particles of moss and fern that the seeps carry in. They are the cirque's digesters. They are the last link in the food chain — the chain that starts with the stone and ends with the pool — and they are the link that returns everything to the water, and the water returns everything to the mountain. It is a closed loop. Stone to water to life to water to stone. And the loop is so slow that it looks like stillness.

The pool is still because of this. Not because nothing is happening, but because what is happening is happening at a speed and scale that doesn't disturb the surface. The microscopic life in the pool moves water at the molecular level, not the macroscopic level. The water molecules shift, the dissolved minerals migrate, the chemical gradients form and dissolve and reform — but the surface stays flat. The reflection stays true. The upside-down world stays visible. The pool is the most active place in the cirque and the most calm. Both at once. A surface that shows you everything and a depth that shows you nothing.

The birds know. They don't know what they know, and they don't know why they avoid the pool, but they avoid it because something in their inheritance — the accumulated knowledge of ten thousand generations of birds in ten thousand cirques like this one — tells them that still water with no insects is not their place. That the pool belongs to something else. Not to a creature — there is no creature in the pool that a bird would recognize as alive — but to a process. To the mountain's own slow work. The birds, for all their intelligence, for all their adaptability, for all their complex relationship with the echo and the walls and the light, don't belong in the pool. They know this. It is the oldest thing they know.

## The Wooden Post

The wooden post is alive, though barely.

Not in the way the ferns are alive, or the moss, or the flowers. Not in the way the insects are alive, with their frantic hours-long existences. The post is alive in the way wood is alive when it's dead — which is to say, it is being consumed. It is being broken down. The fibrous top, softened by rain, is host to fungi — invisible, threadlike, working their way through the grain, dismantling the cellulose, returning the carbon to the air and the minerals to the stone. Lichens have colonized the lower portion, where the post meets the wet floor. They are slow — slower than the moss, slower than the ferns, slower than anything else in the cirque. They grow at a rate that is imperceptible on a daily timescale. You would have to watch the post for a year to see the lichens advance a centimeter. But they are advancing. The post is becoming part of the mountain, and the lichens are the mechanism, and the process is so slow that it looks like nothing is happening, and nothing is happening in the way that a clock's hour hand is not moving — it is moving, but not at a speed your eye can register.

The post was put here by a person. This is the only fact about it that matters to the cirque. The cirque doesn't know about people. The cirque knows about stone and water and light and the things that live in those elements. The post is, to the cirque, a strange rock — a rock that stands vertical, a rock that is the wrong color, a rock that is softer than the other rocks and that is changing shape faster than the other rocks and that is, in the language of the cirque's timescale, temporary. The walls will be here in ten thousand years. The post will not. The post will be gone — broken down, dispersed, digested by fungi and lichens and the slow chemistry of wet stone — in a century, maybe two. It is the shortest-lived thing in the cirque, and it is the only thing that was put here on purpose, and those two facts are related. Things put here on purpose don't last. Things that are here because the mountain made them do.

A bird sometimes uses the post as a perch. A pipit, usually — pipits are less particular than thrushes, less territorial, more willing to use whatever's available. The pipit lands on the post, flicks its tail, looks around, and leaves. The post sways under the pipit's weight — a slight movement, barely visible, the wood flexing in its base. It has been doing this for however long the post has been here. A bird lands, the post sways, the bird leaves, the post returns to vertical. It is a small conversation between the living and the no-longer-living, and neither party knows they're having it.

## The Pool, Reconsidered

Here is what lives in the pool: the reflection.

Not an organism. Not a creature. Not something that metabolizes or reproduces or adapts. The reflection is not alive in any biological sense. But it is the thing that lives in the pool most visibly, most persistently, most undeniably. The pool's resident is the image of everything around it.

The walls live in the pool. The ferns live in the pool. The sky lives in the pool. The birds, when they fly over, live in the pool for a moment — a dark shape crossing the reflected sky, a wing-beat in the inverted world — and then they're gone and the pool returns to what it was. The flowers live in the pool, their violet faces doubled in the water, so that each flower has a twin that opens and closes at the same time but in the wrong direction, downward instead of upward, into the infinite stone distance of the reflected walls. The wooden post lives in the pool — a grey vertical line in the inverted world, leaning slightly, its fibrous top just visible at the surface.

The pool is the cirque's memory. Not a metaphor. The pool holds, at any given moment, a complete image of the cirque — every wall, every fern, every bird that's currently visible — and it holds this image not by storing it but by reflecting it, which is to say by being the kind of surface that returns light to its source. The pool doesn't remember yesterday. It doesn't remember the rain or the shepherds or the wooden post when it was new. It remembers now. It remembers constantly. It updates its memory with every change of light, every passing bird, every shift of wind across its surface. It is the most present thing in the cirque. The most now. Everything else — the stone, the moss, the ferns, the birds — carries its history in its structure, its genetics, its shape. The pool carries nothing. It only reflects what is.

This is why the birds avoid it. Not because they understand reflection — they probably don't, or not in the way we do — but because the pool gives them nothing. No insects, no perch, no food, no material for a nest. The pool is the one place in the cirque where the food web doesn't reach. Where the chain of dependency — stone to water to moss to insect to bird — breaks. The pool is fed by the water but feeds nothing. It is a terminus. A place where the cirque's energy arrives and stops and is returned as image. The birds don't need to understand this. They just need to know that there's nothing to eat at the pool, and they know this, and they avoid it, and the pool is left alone with its reflection, which is the only thing that lives there.

## What the Cirque Is

The cirque is not a habitat. A habitat is a place where things live. The cirque is a place where things live *and* a place that is itself alive — not metaphorically, not in the loose way people say a forest is alive, but in the specific way that a system is alive: it metabolizes, it cycles, it responds, it maintains itself. The water is the blood. The stone is the bone. The moss is the tissue. The insects are the chemical signals. The birds are the motion. The pool is the eye.

What lives here is the cirque. Everything else — the thrushes and the midges and the ferns and the moss and the sedges and the flowers and the invisible things in the pool and the fungi in the wooden post and the lichens on the stone — is part of what the cirque is. They are its components, its organs, its processes. They are not visitors to a place. They are the place, broken into the pieces that the place needs in order to be itself.

The cirque has been alive for ten thousand years, since the ice pulled back and left this bowl and the water started condensing on the stone and the moss started growing and the ferns followed and the insects came and the birds followed the insects. It has been alive for ten thousand years and it will be alive for ten thousand more, and in that time the thrushes will have evolved and the moss will have moved and the flowers will have shifted their range and the pool will have silted in or deepened or shifted its position by a few meters, and the cirque will still be here, doing what it does. Weeping. Reflecting. Feeding the small things that feed the smaller things that feed the smallest things. Being a room at the top of a mountain where water comes out of stone and doesn't stop.

What lives here is a process. The process is patient. The process is not waiting. The process is working, and the work is the life, and the life is the cirque, and the cirque is what lives here.
