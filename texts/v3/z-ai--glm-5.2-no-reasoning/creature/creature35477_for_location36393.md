# What Lives Here

## The Obvious First

The lizards.

You see them within the first hour on the exposed plateau, and they are the closest thing to a resident this place has. Small — shorter than your hand, tail included — and dark, so dark they look like wet stone until they move. Which is rarely. They bask on the warm rock during the morning hours, positioned with geometric precision along the edges of seeps where the biofilm thins and the stone shows through. Their bodies are flat in a way that seems deliberate, as though they are trying to present the minimum possible profile to the sky, to the heat, to whatever watches from above.

Their legs are disproportionately long. This is the first thing you notice about them up close — the legs, lifted high off the rock when they run, holding their bellies clear of the hot stone. They move in quick, darting bursts, three or four rapid strides and then stillness, posed on the tips of their toes like sprinters caught mid-race. Their eyes are bright, brighter than has any right to be in a creature this small and this dark. Orange. Some of them. Amber-gold in others. They watch you with an alertness that feels almost offensive from something the size of a pencil.

They drink from the seeps. You watch one do it — approaching the thin film of water running across a stone shelf with a slowness and care that seems out of proportion, lowering its head in increments, touching its jaw to the water and holding there. Drinking, or something like drinking. Absorbing. The heat here costs them water faster than they can replace it, and they have calibrated their lives around the deficit. They drink in the morning. They hunt in the morning. By afternoon they are in the shade of the larger rocks, pressed flat against the cooler stone, motionless. They wait out the heat. They are very good at waiting.

What they eat: the insects, mostly. Small things, winged and unwinged, that live in the moss and the sedges and the biofilm. Flies that move in slow, heavy arcs through the saturated air, their wings laboring. Crawling things that have never needed wings and never will. The lizards take them with a speed that seems to come from somewhere outside their bodies — a stored energy, held in reserve, spent only when the mathematics of expenditure and return make it worthwhile.

They are not social, exactly. But they are aware of each other. You notice that the distance between any two basking lizards is roughly consistent — close enough to see each other, far enough that no single predator could take two at once. They have arranged themselves across the plateau like this without consulting each other, by some agreement older than language. When one startles and retreats to shade, the others nearby do the same, within seconds. Not because they saw the threat. Because they saw the response. The fear moves through them like a signal through wire.

There are more of them than you first think. Far more. Once you learn to see them — to distinguish the dark curve of a body from the dark curve of a wet stone — you realize they are everywhere. Dozens within visual range. Hundreds across the visible plateau. They are the vertebrate backbone of this ecosystem, such as it is, and they survive here because they are the right size for the available energy. Small enough to hide. Small enough to cool. Small enough that the insects are a meal. The plateau does not support anything larger. The lizards have found the niche and they fill it completely.

## What Flies

The birds are harder.

You hear them before you see them — and you almost never see them. They call from within the cloud cover, which sits so low and so constant that it might as well be a ceiling. The calls are not melodic. They are functional. Short, descending, almost electronic in their flatness. Something between a alert and a statement of position. *I am here. The air is wet. The ground is wet. I am still here.*

You catch glimpses, eventually. High above the plateau, navigating the cloud by some means you cannot fathom. They are large — raptor-sized, broad-winged, built for soaring, but you never see them hunt. They ride the thermals that rise from the heated stone, turning in wide, patient circles, appearing out of the white and disappearing back into it. Whether they nest here or are simply passing through, using the mountain's heat as a highway to somewhere else, you cannot tell. They do not land while you watch.

But the plateau is full of evidence that they do, sometimes. The lizards are cautious in a way that suggests predation. The scattered remains of one on a rock — a tail, still twitching with posthumous nerve signals, the rest of the body carried elsewhere or consumed where it fell. The lizards do not look up. They look sideways, along the rock. They watch each other. But they never look up, and you think: something comes from above. Something that the lizards have learned to fear but never to see.

There are smaller birds, too. You find them by sound — a clicking, chattering noise from inside the thickest vegetation around the basins. They move through the sedges and reeds in small groups, never visible, always audible, their movements marked by the trembling of stalks and the sudden silence of insects in their path. You see one, once, for less than a second. Dark. Compact. A body designed for threading through dense growth at speed. It sees you and drops back into the green and is gone.

These birds live here. You are certain of it. The vegetation around the basins is dense enough and tall enough to provide cover, and the insect population is more than sufficient. They have no need to leave. They exist in the narrow strip where water meets stone and growth goes vertical, and they do not venture out onto the open rock. They are the things that live in the margins, in the boundary layer, in the places the lizards cannot reach and the raptors cannot see. The plateau is not one habitat for them. It is two: the green and the red, and they belong entirely to the green.

## What Grows (That Should Not Be Counted as Living, But Is)

The moss is not just moss.

This is something you learn over days, not hours. The cushions of growth along the seeps and in the basins look like moss from above — green, spongy, wet. But when you kneel and look closely, pushing your face close enough that the condensation fogs your vision, you see that each cushion is not one thing but many. There are at least four distinct types of growth interlocked in each cushion — a primary structural moss, a secondary colonizer, a lichen, and something finer, threadlike, almost fungal. They are not competing. They are, as far as you can tell, collaborating. The structure holds the water. The colonizer processes the minerals. The lichen fixes something from the air. The threads bind it all together.

They are, in aggregate, an organism. Or at least they act like one. When the temperature rises and the evaporation accelerates, the entire cushion responds in unison — curling inward, closing pores, slowing its metabolism. When the condensation comes, the entire cushion opens at once. It is not a decision. It is not a signal. It is a property of the system, the way a crowd moves together when the rain starts, each body responding to the same stimulus independently but in synchronization. The cushions breathe.

They are also, you realize, ancient. Some of the larger ones — the ones in the deepest basins, the ones that have been growing undisturbed for decades, maybe centuries — have built up substantial thickness. A forearm's length, the description said, and you confirm this. But underneath the living surface, there are dead layers, compressed, partially decomposed, turning slowly into the peat that will eventually become soil that will eventually support something larger. If the plateau ever cools. If the water ever drains. If the conditions ever change enough to allow something taller to take root. None of this will happen soon. The cushions are patient because they have to be. They are building a future they will never see.

The biofilm is the other presence here, and it is harder to love.

It is the scum on the wet stone, the slippery layer that makes every step a calculation. It is alive, technically — bacteria, algae, archaea, things with no common names because nobody has ever had reason to name them. It colonizes bare stone faster than anything else here. You can watch it happen, on the rocks where the lizards have scraped it away to bask: within a day, the pale stone has a faint sheen. Within two days, the sheen has color. Within a week, it is a coating, thin but established. The mountain's skin, growing back over a wound.

You come to understand the biofilm as the plateau's immune system. It is the first responder to any disturbance, the first thing to cover bare stone, the first thing to begin the slow conversion of mineral surface into biological substrate. Everything else — the mosses, the ferns, the sedges, the communities in the basins — depends on this initial colonization. Without the biofilm, the stone would remain stone. With it, the stone becomes a foundation. The biofilm is not elegant, but it is essential, and it is everywhere, and it has been doing this for longer than any of the other inhabitants have been doing anything.

## What Lives in the Water

The pools are not empty.

This is the discovery that changes your understanding of the plateau. You assumed, initially, that the shallow basins — the temporary ponds, the seeps, the channels — were geographical features holding water that held nothing. You were wrong. Everything here that holds water holds life.

In the still pools, the clearest ones, the ones where the water sits long enough for sediment to settle, you see movement. Not fish — nothing here is large enough to be a fish. Smaller. A darting, jerking motion, barely visible, just below the surface. Something that moves in three dimensions through the shallow water and hunts things smaller than itself. You cup some water in your hands and wait. You see them: tiny, almost transparent, all legs and movement. Shrimp-like, or perhaps not — you don't have the vocabulary. But alive, and purposeful, and clearly resident.

These pools are ecosystems in miniature. The water carries mineral content from the stone. The biofilm grows on the submerged surfaces, sometimes in different colors than the terrestrial variety — more blue-green, thicker, more gelatinous. The shrimp-things (you start calling them this, then drop the "things" and just call them shrimp, knowing you are wrong) feed on the biofilm and on whatever organic matter the water washes in from the surrounding vegetation. The lizards come to drink and occasionally snap at the surface. Something — you never see what — leaves tracks in the muddy margins of the larger pools. Toes. Claws. Something that visits at night, when you are not watching.

The water itself is a habitat. Not a place, but a medium. Life here does not live *on* the plateau so much as it lives *in* the water that moves through the plateau. The stone is the container. The water is the home. Everything that breathes the air does so secondarily, as an adaptation to the fact that the water keeps moving and the air, at least, stays in one place.

In the channels where the water flows, you find something else. Worms. Long, dark, thin, living in the mud that accumulates in the slower sections of the seeps, their bodies visible only when the water clears enough to see the bottom. They do not move much. They do not need to. The water brings them what they need — organic matter, dissolved minerals, the waste products of the upstream ecosystems. They are the decomposers, the bottom of the bottom, the things that eat what nothing else wants. You suspect there are many more of them than you can see. You suspect that the mud in every channel is full of them. You suspect that they are, in terms of biomass, the most successful animals on the plateau.

## What Comes at Night

You do not see them clearly.

You hear them. Or you hear the evidence of them — a soft splashing in one of the larger pools, far too deliberate to be a lizard. A rustling in the sedge that is too heavy for the small birds. The sound of something moving with weight and care through the vegetation, placing each step with the attention of an animal that does not want to be heard and does not entirely succeed.

You shine your light once, toward the sound. You see eyes. Two of them, set wide apart, catching the light and throwing it back at you with a brightness that implies a tapetum lucidum — a night-adapted eye, a nocturnal eye. The eyes are low to the ground. Whatever owns them is not large. But it is not small, either. Larger than a lizard. Heavier than a bird. Something in between, something that occupies the niche that the lizards are too small to fill and the birds are too specialized to claim.

The eyes blink and are gone. You hear the soft departure through wet growth. You hear the water settle back into silence.

You find tracks in the morning. In the mud at the edge of a basin, in the soft biofilm along a channel. They are hand-like — five digits, splayed, gripping. A climber. Something that can navigate wet stone, that can grip the biofilm-slick surfaces without sliding. The tracks lead from one basin to another, following the water, and they disappear where the stone dries and the biofilm gives way to bare rock. Whatever made them was comfortable in the wet and chose to stay there.

You think about what it eats. The pools, probably — the shrimp, the worms, the insects at the water's edge. Maybe the lizards, if it can catch them in the dim hours before full light, when the cold-blooded things are still slow. Maybe the plants, if it is omnivorous. There is enough here to support a small population of something nocturnal, something that has found the one time and the one mode of life that the heat and the water and the stone permit. It lives in the gap that the other residents cannot occupy.

You do not see it again. You think about it often. You think about the eyes. You think about the deliberateness of the footsteps. You think about the fact that you heard it coming, which means it was not perfectly quiet, which means it is not perfectly adapted, which means it may be relatively new here — a colonizer, an immigrant, something that arrived after the industrial operation left and found a niche that the departure of human activity had opened up.

Or maybe it has always been here. Maybe it is the thing that lives in the gap between observation. Maybe it has been watching you for days, from the green margins, from the basins at dusk, from the edge of the light you carry. Maybe it knows more about you than you know about the plateau. You will never find out. You accept this. You move on.

## What Lived Here and Left

The humans.

They are the most recent departure and the most significant absence. The infrastructure tells the story that the plateau will not — the pipes, the pumps, the holding tanks, the drainage channels. Someone was here. Someone stayed long enough to build, to install, to operate. Someone decided that the water was worth the cost of extracting it, and they built the machinery to do so, and they ran that machinery for years, maybe decades, and then they left.

You find no personal artifacts. No cups, no tools, no signs of habitation beyond the industrial. This tells you something: the people who worked here did not live here. They commuted. They came up the mountain in shifts, worked the pumps, maintained the pipes, and went back down. The plateau was not a home. It was a worksite. They treated it as such, with the efficient indifference of people who do not need to love a place to use it.

But the lizards remember them. Or — not remember, exactly, but carry the imprint. The lizards are cautious of you in a way that suggests prior experience with large, upright, slow-moving things that walk on two legs and make noise and do not hunt but disturb. They have learned that humans are not predators, exactly, but are dangerous in a way that is harder to categorize. Humans step on things. Humans break things. Humans bring things that do not belong and take things that do. The lizards have kept this knowledge, whatever it is, in their bodies, in their startle responses, in the distance they maintain from anything that moves too slowly to be a raptor and too large to be a bird.

You also find that the industrial infrastructure has become habitat. The collapsed pumping station houses ferns and, you suspect, the nocturnal thing. The broken pipes provide shelter for lizards — you find them sleeping in the open sections, using the metal tubes as shade and refuge. The brick-lined drainage channels have become watercourses, their walls colonized by moss, their floors inhabited by the worms and the shrimp and the biofilm. The humans left, and the plateau immediately began converting their leavings into landscape. Not out of gratitude. Out of the simple, amoral process of colonization. If it stays wet for long enough, something will grow on it. If it provides shade, something will shelter in it. If it holds water, something will live in it. The plateau does not distinguish between a stone basin carved by erosion and a holding tank built by engineers. Water is water. Stone is stone. The mountain does not care about origins.

## What Remains

The mountain itself is alive, if you use the word loosely.

Not metaphorically — or not only metaphorically. The rock has a metabolism. The water moves through it in fractures and channels, dissolving minerals from one place and depositing them in another. The heat drives the process during the day. The cooling allows deposition at night. The stone is being continuously reshaped, continuously eroded, continuously rebuilt. The seeps that emerge on the surface are the visible evidence of an internal process that extends deep into the mountain, a slow circulation of water through rock that has been running for longer than anything on the surface has been alive.

The mountain breathes. Not in the poetic sense — in the mechanical sense. The heat causes the air inside the fractures to expand, driving moisture outward through the seeps. The cooling causes it to contract, drawing moisture back in. The cycle is diurnal, tied to the sun, tied to the flat gray light that never quite resolves into brightness. The plateau weeps because the mountain exhales. The condensation falls because the mountain inhales. The rhythm is not fast enough for you to feel, but it is real, and it is continuous, and it has been happening for as long as the water table has sat below the surface of the stone.

And the mountain communicates, in its way. The sound that is not silence — the low, pervasive hiss of water through stone, the groan of rock adjusting under thermal stress, the drip and run of condensation — is the mountain's voice. It speaks in water. It speaks in heat. It speaks in the slow language of geology, where a sentence takes a century and a conversation takes a millennium. Everything on the surface is listening to this voice, all the time, even when they do not know it. The lizards time their hunting to the mountain's exhalation. The mosses open and close with its breathing. The water creatures live inside its words.

You are listening too. You have been listening since you arrived. You will listen until you leave, and you will carry the sound with you, and you will hear it in other places — in the hiss of a tap, in the drip of rain, in the low hum of a world that is always, somewhere, breathing.

## The Ones You Never See

There are things here you will never know about.

You accept this on the fourth day, standing on a shelf of red stone above a basin full of still water, watching the condensation form on your arms and drip onto the rock and join the seep that runs down to the pool below. You accept it the way you accept the heat and the wet and the quiet — by acknowledging that your presence here is temporary and partial and that the plateau does not owe you its full inventory.

There are microorganisms you cannot see. Things living in the biofilm, in the water, in the moss, in the air itself. Things living in the mineral layers deep inside the rock, in the fractures where the water circulates, in the dark and the heat below the surface. Extremophiles, maybe. Things that have never seen light and never will. Things that eat stone and drink heat and exist on timescales that make the mountain seem hasty.

There are interactions you cannot map. The relationship between the moss and the mineral content of the water. The effect of the lizards on the insect population and the effect of the insects on the plants. The thing that visits at night and the things it eats and the things that eat what it leaves. The biofilm and the stone and the water and the heat, all locked in a system so interconnected that pulling on any thread would reveal a web too large to see all at once.

There are things that are not here yet. The things that will colonize the rusting pipes in another century. The things that will grow in the soil the mosses are building. The things that will come when the climate shifts, when the temperature changes, when the water table moves. The plateau is not finished. It is in the middle of something, and it has been in the middle of something for a very long time, and it will continue to be in the middle of something long after you are gone.

What lives here, in the end, is the process itself. The breathing in and out. The weeping and the drying. The colonization and the decay and the recolonization. The slow conversation between stone and water and heat and life, conducted in a language that has no words and no urgency and no end.

You are here. You are part of it, briefly. A small disturbance in the biofilm, a warm body adding its breath to the mountain's breath, a pair of eyes looking out across a landscape that does not look back.

And then you leave. And the water fills your footprints. And the moss grows over them. And the plateau continues, wet and hot and open and old and patient, inhabited by everything that has learned to live in the gap between rain and rain.
