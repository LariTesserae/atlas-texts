# What Lives Here

The first answer is: the wind lives here. The wind was here before the stone was cut, before the copper was poured and ribbed into its gathering patterns, before the first channel was worn smooth by the patient attrition of nightly water. The wind moves across the plateau from the west, shaped and compressed by the higher peaks, and it does not stop. It is the oldest presence. Everything else that lives here lives in negotiation with it.

But you want to know about the others.

---

## The Succulent Mats

Start with the ground. The pale gray-green growth that fills the cracks and crevices is not one thing but a *community* — a layered consortium of organisms that has spent so long in association that it functions as a single skin. The primary component is something that is not quite a moss and not quite a liverwort but occupies the narrow territory between them, evolutionarily speaking. It has no common name. It may have no name at all, having been noticed by no culture that named things. It is a flat, close-lying organism with no stem to speak of, only a thallus that spreads outward from a central anchor point in concentric ripples of growth, each ripple representing a season — or rather, a wet-dry cycle, which here is the nearest thing to a season.

The thallus is rubbery because it must be. Rigid structures would shatter in the thermal cycling — the daytime heat expands everything, the nighttime fog cools it, and the stress of repeated expansion and contraction would fracture anything brittle. The rubbery texture is a compromise: flexible enough to survive the cycling, firm enough to hold its shape when the moisture comes and it swells to three or four times its dry volume. The waxy sheen is not wax but a cuticle, a lipid layer that slows evaporation, that keeps the absorbed moisture inside long enough to do the work of photosynthesis before the midday heat tears it away.

Beneath this primary mat, in the deeper fissures where the metal meets stone and the stone meets soil, there is a second layer — a fungal network, threadlike and dark, that runs through the accumulated detritus and binds it into something that is almost earth. Not soil. Not yet. But the precursor. The fungal threads are the reason the thin substrate in these cracks can hold any water at all, the reason the succulent mats can establish, the reason the wiry grass can find root. They are the foundation that makes the rest possible, and they are invisible, and they have been here possibly longer than the metal.

The mats reproduce slowly. In the fog, the edges of the thallus extend, pressing outward by millimeters, exploring the adjacent surface. Where the surface is suitable — sheltered, slightly damp, rough enough to hold — the edge thickens and sends down rhizines, hairlike anchors that find the fungal network and connect to it. Where the surface is unsuitable — exposed, hot, smooth — the edge dries and curls and waits. It does not die. It does not retreat. It simply stops, and in the next fog, it tries again, millimeter by millimeter, season by season. Some of the mats on the platform have been growing in the same spot for what must be decades. Their concentric rings tell the history of the years: wider rings for years of more fog, narrower rings for years of less, gaps where the mat died back and re-established from a fragment that survived.

They are the closest thing this place has to permanence among the growing things. When you step on one — and you will, because they are everywhere in the cracks — it feels like stepping on a wet sponge. It does not harm them. They are adapted to being stepped on, to being compressed, to being dried and re-wetted and dried again. They have no expectation of gentleness. They have been living between metal and stone, in heat and fog, without gentleness, for longer than the concept of gentleness has existed here.

## The Grass

The wiry grass along the channel edges is a different kind of patience.

It is not numerous. There are perhaps forty clumps of it across the entire platform system, each one established in a spot where the condensation channel has a slight lip or irregularity that causes water to spill or seep onto the adjacent surface. The grass grows from these seep points, its roots following the moisture along the underside of the metal grate, finding the thin soil that has accumulated in the joins and seams.

The grass is tough in a way that almost seems hostile. Its leaves are thin and rolled, almost wire-like, and they are sharp enough that if you run your hand along a clump, you will bleed. This is not a defense mechanism — there is nothing here that would eat enough of it to matter. It is a structural choice: the rolled, narrow leaf minimizes surface area, which minimizes water loss, and the silica content that makes it sharp also makes it rigid, which keeps the leaf upright in the wind rather than lying flat and shading its own roots. The grass is built to hold its ground against the wind and the heat and to take what the channels give it and nothing more.

Its seed heads are its only concession to softness. They form on short stems in the weeks after the foggiest part of the cycle, and they nod — they actually *nod*, bending under the weight of the seeds, which are small and hard and dark and carry a tuft of fine hairs that will catch the wind when they dry. The grass disperses by wind, which is obvious in a place where there is nothing else to disperse it. The seeds land mostly on bare metal and fail. A few land in cracks and find the fungal network and the succulent mat and establish. The grass is not spreading. It is holding. Each clump is a small triumph of persistence over the physics of this place, and none of them know it, and it would not matter if they did.

## The Fern

The fern is the rarest thing here, and it knows it, in the way that an organism can be said to know the narrowness of its own existence.

There is one species. There are perhaps a dozen individual plants, each one no larger than an open hand, each one growing in a spot where the channel water spills continuously through the fog cycle — not the seep that feeds the grass, but an actual spill, a point where the channel drops to a lower level and the water fans out over a few square centimeters of surface before finding its way back into the system. These spots are permanently damp during the fog and take longest to dry in the morning, and in the most sheltered of them — the ones on the underside of the platform, in the substructure where the sun never reaches — the ferns have found a toehold.

They are delicate in appearance, which is deceptive. Their fronds are thin and translucent, almost papery, and they unfurl in the classic fiddlehead shape but at a scale that makes the unfurling seem precious, something to be protected. They are not precious. They have survived by being precisely as fragile as they need to be and no more — the thin frond is efficient at gas exchange in the still, damp air of the substructure, and the translucence is a consequence of having so little tissue to support, because tissue costs water, and water is the thing they cannot afford to waste.

But they cannot survive outside the spill zones. They are the most narrowly adapted organism here. If the channel shifted — if a piece of debris redirected the spill by a few centimeters — the fern would die. It has no buffer. It has no reserve. It lives exactly at the edge of what is possible and not one millimeter beyond it. This is not a strategy. It is a fact. The fern is what happens when life finds a niche so small that only the most minimal expression of life can fit, and then it fills that niche completely, and it does not ask for more.

## The Lichens

The lichens are here too, and they are the quietest living things, because lichens are always quiet, but also because they are the most *patient*.

The pale gray-green patches on the shady sides of every protrusion are a single species, or close enough that the distinction doesn't matter at this scale. They are crustose — flat, tight against the substrate, as if painted onto the metal and stone. They grow slowly enough that growth is almost not the right word. They *accrete*. A patch the size of a coin may represent a decade of accumulated living, each lobe of the lichen's edge advancing by microns per year, each micron a chemical negotiation between the fungal component and the algal component and the surface they have chosen to colonize.

The chemical negotiation matters here because the surface is metal. Copper alloy, weathered and oxidized, is not an inviting substrate. The lichen has to deal with the copper itself — the trace toxicity, the ions that leach in the fog and sit on the surface. Most lichens cannot do this. This one can, because its fungal partner produces a compound — something dark, something that stains the rock beneath it a faint amber where the lichen has been removed — that complexes with the copper and renders it inert. The lichen is not just living on the metal. It is *processing* the metal. It is eating the corrosion and excreting a buffer and building itself out of the interaction between its own chemistry and the platform's.

In the dry part of the day, the lichen cracks. This is not damage. This is the lichen's strategy for surviving desiccation: it loses moisture, its thallus contracts, and the contraction creates fissures that look like damage but are actually expansion joints. When the fog comes, the fissures soften and close, the thallus swells, and the lichen resumes its accretion. The cracking is part of the living. It is the lichen's way of being here without being destroyed by being here.

There is a second lichen, or something lichen-like, in the substructure — the more structured, branching form that clings to the undersides of the metal panels. This one is not crustose but foliose, with small leaf-like lobes that lift slightly from the substrate. It is darker, almost black, and it does not crack in the heat because the substructure does not experience the same thermal cycling. It is cooler down there, more humid, more stable. This lichen has a different pace of life — faster, in relative terms, though still slow by any standard other than its own. It colonizes new surfaces in years rather than decades. It has covered the undersides of the panels in a continuous mat that, from below, looks like a low ceiling of dark scales. The warren of the substructure is carpeted with it, and the lizards that flee into it are fleeing into a landscape made of this lichen as much as of metal and stone.

## The Beetles

The arthropods come in layers, like everything else here.

The beetles are the foundation. They are dark, hard-shelled, and they move with a deliberateness that seems almost philosophical. There are two visible species and probably more that are not visible — the substructure and the soil in the cracks certainly harbor things too small or too hidden to be casually observed.

The larger beetle is about two centimeters long, oval, with a domed carapace that is almost metallic in its sheen — dark brown with a faint iridescence that shifts in the light. It walks with a rocking gait, its legs slightly too short for its body, and it makes a clicking sound as its claws catch on the metal grate. This is the sound you hear in the quiet afternoons: the ticking of beetles crossing the platform. They are not fast. They do not need to be. Their carapace is hard enough that most of the predators here cannot crack it, and they are dark enough to be invisible on the oxidized metal, and they are smart enough — in the limited way that beetles are smart — to stay near the cracks and crevices where they can drop out of sight if something larger comes too close.

They eat the succulent mats. They graze on the thallus, scraping the surface with mandibles that are strong enough to shear the rubbery tissue but not so strong that they cut through to the substrate. Their grazing leaves pale patches on the mats, small scars that heal in the next fog. The beetles and the succulent mats are in a long, slow equilibrium: the beetles eat just enough to sustain themselves, the mats grow just fast enough to replace what is eaten, and neither has been able to overwhelm the other for as long as both have been here. This is not peace. It is balance, which is different.

The smaller beetle is half the size and twice as fast. It lives in the channels themselves, running along the grooves where the water flows, feeding on the organic film that accumulates on the wet metal — the biofilm of bacteria and fungal spores and airborne detritus that the condensation deposits as it runs. This beetle is a scavenger of the film, and it is most active during the fog, when the channels are wet and the film is rich. By day, it hides in the deeper parts of the channel system, in the joins where the metal panels meet and the water never fully dries. It is the thing that the smaller lizards hunt.

Both beetles reproduce in the fog season. The larger one lays eggs in the succulent mats, burying them in the tissue where the moisture will keep them from desiccating. The smaller one lays eggs in the channel joins, attaching them to the metal in clusters that look like tiny dark beads. The larvae are soft and pale and live entirely in the hidden places — in the mats, in the channels, in the substructure — and you will not see them unless you pull apart the components of this place, which you should not do.

## The Crickets

The chirping that begins in the late afternoon, before the fog, is produced by something that is not quite a cricket but fills the cricket's ecological role. It is smaller than a true cricket and more elongate, with legs that are adapted for jumping but not as dramatically as a grasshopper's. Its color is a pale tan that matches the dust on the platform surfaces, and it is nearly invisible when it is still.

It chirps by stridulating — rubbing a file on one wing against a scraper on the other — and the sound it produces is a single, dry note, repeated at intervals of about three seconds. Not a sustained trill. Not a song. A pulse. A ping. A notification, almost, as if the insect is confirming that it is still here, that the afternoon is still happening, that the fog has not yet arrived.

The chirping stops when the fog comes. This is the signal, more than the light change, more than the temperature shift, that the cycle has turned. The crickets go quiet and the condensation ticks begin, and the transition is so reliable that you can set your understanding of the day by it: the crickets are the afternoon, and the condensation is the evening, and the silence between the last chirp and the first drip is the hinge on which the whole system turns.

What the crickets eat is less clear. They are omnivorous, almost certainly — they have been observed on the succulent mats, on the channel edges, on the beetles' carcasses when a beetle has died of old age or bad luck. They are the generalists here, the ones that eat what is available and do not specialize, and they may be the most adaptable species in the system. If the platform were to change — if the fog were to lessen, if the channels were to shift — the crickets would be the last to leave. They are the species for whom this place is sufficient but not necessary, and that gives them a kind of resilience that the specialists lack.

## The Aerial Hunters

The flying insects are the most visibly active non-vertebrate presence, and they are also the most dramatic.

They are built like a cross between a wasp and a damselfly — long-bodied, narrow-waisted, with four translucent wings that are longer than the body and that catch the light in a way that makes them seem to flicker in and out of visibility as they fly. They are not large. The body is perhaps four centimeters, the wingspan twice that. They are fast. They are territorial. And they are committed to a way of living that is almost exhausting to watch.

Each individual has a circuit. A territory. A loop of space above a section of platform that it flies continuously during the hours of warmth and light, from mid-morning to late afternoon. The circuit is not random — it follows the same path, the same turns, the same altitude, with a precision that suggests the path is not learned but *built into* whatever passes for decision-making in an organism this size. The territory is perhaps ten meters across, and the insect flies its perimeter at a speed that makes observation difficult, and it does not rest. It does not perch. It does not pause. It flies its circuit until the light begins to fail and then it goes to its nest — a hollow conduit tube, one of the horizontal pipes that run between the cisterns — and it enters and it stays until the morning.

The circuit is a hunting strategy. The flying insect is a predator. It takes the smaller beetles and the crickets and, occasionally, one of the tiny lizards if the lizard is exposed and the angle is right. It hunts on the wing, striking at prey on the platform surface in a fast, angled dive that seems too steep for recovery but that it pulls out of with a precision that is almost aggressive. The strike is fast enough that you see only the result: the insect banking upward with something in its grip, spiraling back to its circuit altitude, eating its catch in the air.

The aerial disputes are the most visible spectacle here, and they are not really disputes. They are negotiations. When two circuits overlap — when two individuals are flying territories that share a border — they meet at the edge and they interact. They do not fight. They do not make contact. They *fly at each other*, head-on, at full speed, and at the last moment one of them turns and the other does not, and the one that turned has conceded the point and the one that did not has won the airspace. This happens dozens of times a day along the borders, and it is not dangerous, and it is not wasteful, and it is not play. It is a system for establishing and maintaining boundaries without the cost of physical conflict. It is, in its small way, elegant.

They nest in the conduit tubes, and their nests are built of the same organic film the small beetles feed on, scraped from the channel walls and carried in flight to the tube entrance, packed into a plug that seals the tube and protects the eggs inside. The plug is a structure. It is the closest thing to architecture that any non-human organism produces here, and it is entirely functional — no more elaborate than it needs to be, no larger, no more decorated. The tube is the nest; the plug is the door. The flying insect does not think about it. It does it.

## The Lizards

The lizards are the *inhabitants* here, in the way that matters. They are the vertebrates for whom this place is not a stop or a hunting ground but a *home* — the place where they spend their entire lives, where they were born, where they will die. The birds pass through or nest at the margins; the mustelids hunt and leave; but the lizards are *here*, always, and the platform system without its lizards would be a different place entirely — not because the lizards alter the landscape, but because the lizards are the landscape's awareness of itself, its motion, its watchfulness.

### The Large Herbivore

The largest lizard is a blunt-headed, heavy-bodied animal that reaches perhaps twenty centimeters from nose to tail tip, though the tail is short for a lizard and thick, almost cylindrical, as if it were a limb that had lost its purpose and been repurposed as a fat reserve. Its scales are dark — not the black of the metal but a deep charcoal, close enough that the lizard is invisible when it is still, and it is still often. It is the least active of the three species. It moves slowly, with a deliberateness that is almost bovine, and it spends most of its day on the platform surface in a spot that it has chosen for its combination of sun exposure and proximity to a crevice.

It eats the succulent mats. It grazes, slowly, methodically, taking bites from the thallus with jaws that are strong but blunt, shearing the tissue rather than tearing it. A lizard can strip a patch of mat down to the substrate in an afternoon, and the patch will take weeks to recover. This is why the mats are not continuous — the large lizard's grazing keeps them in a patchwork of ages and stages of recovery, and the pattern is not random but a function of where the lizards prefer to eat and how fast the mats grow back. The lizard is a gardener, in its way. It does not know this.

The large lizard is also the most thermally dependent of the three. It must reach a certain body temperature before it can move effectively, and it reaches this temperature by flattening itself against the sun-warmed metal grate, its body pressed as flat as possible to maximize contact with the surface. In this position, it is nearly invisible — a dark shape on dark metal, its outline broken by the grate's pattern — and it will hold the position for long minutes, occasionally shifting to track a moving sun patch or to extend a leg to absorb additional heat. It is vulnerable in this position, and it knows this, and it has a response: if a shadow falls across it — a bird, the mustelid, you — it drops. It does not run. It *drops*, straight down through the grate, into the substructure, and it is gone before the shadow has fully arrived. The grate closes behind it like water closing over a stone.

### The Channel Hunter

The second lizard is the one you will see most often. It is smaller — twelve to fifteen centimeters including a long, slender tail that is almost twice the length of its body — and it is quick in a way that seems almost nervous. It is darker than the large species, almost truly black, and it is thinner, more angular, with a head that is pointed and a body that is built for speed.

It hunts the channel edges. It patrols the grooves where the condensation flows, moving in quick, darting runs that stop and start with a precision that makes the motion seem digitized — it is here, then it is there, with nothing between. It is looking for the small beetles that live in the channels, and when it finds one, it strikes with a speed that is, for a moment, the fastest thing happening on the platform. The beetle is caught, crushed, swallowed, and the lizard is moving again before the capture has registered as an event.

This lizard is the one that gives the platform its sense of *life*. The large lizards are too still, too slow, too much like rocks that have learned to move. The channel hunters are too fast, too present, too obviously alive. They are the motion of the system, the visible evidence that something is happening here, that the transaction of condensation and consumption is ongoing. When you walk across the platform and the lizards scatter — a dozen small dark shapes fleeing in all directions, dropping through the grate or racing along the channels — these are the ones you are seeing.

They are territorial, but their territories are small and fluid. A channel hunter will claim a section of channel — perhaps five meters of groove — and hunt it exclusively for several days, then move to another section, then return. The territories overlap and shift and do not seem to be defended in any way the flying insects would recognize. The lizards do not contest space. They contest *prey*. If two lizards are hunting the same stretch of channel and one catches a beetle, the other will not challenge it but will simply leave, as if the absence of the beetle has made the territory temporarily worthless. This is not a system. It is an absence of a system, a space where competition exists but is resolved by departure rather than conflict.

### The Worm-Lizard

The third species is the one you will almost certainly not see.

It is tiny — five to eight centimeters, cylindrical, with limbs so reduced that it is nearly legless, though it is not a snake but a true lizard that has abandoned the lizard body plan for something more specialized. Its scales are pale, almost translucent, and its eyes are reduced to dark spots beneath the skin. It lives in the fern litter — the small, damp accumulation of organic material that builds up beneath the ferns in the channel spill zones — and it never surfaces. It does not bask. It does not hunt in the open. It burrows through the litter, eating the smallest arthropods and the eggs of the beetles and the organic material itself, and its entire life is conducted in a space that is, at most, a few square centimeters.

It is the most specialized animal here, and it is the most vulnerable. If the ferns die — if the channel shifts and the spill zone dries — the worm-lizard dies with them. It has no alternative. It cannot move to another spill zone because the spill zones are too far apart for an animal this size to cross the hot, dry platform surface. It is trapped in its niche by the same adaptation that makes it successful there, and its continued existence is a wager that the channel will not shift, that the fern will not die, that the specific conditions of its tiny world will persist for the span of its life. So far, the wager has paid off. The ferns at some of these spill sites are old — decades, perhaps — and the worm-lizard populations there have been continuous for at least that long. But the wager is always active, and the odds are set by the metal and the stone and the fog, and the worm-lizard has no say in any of it.

## The Birds

The birds are marginal here, and they seem to know it.

### The Ground-Nesters

The two species that nest on the platform are both small, both drab, and both ground-nesting, which is strange for this altitude and this exposure but makes a kind of sense when you understand the substructure. The space beneath the grate — the layered stone and corroded pipe and accumulated organic material — is a sheltered environment, cooler than the surface, more humid, and protected from the wind. It is also dark, and the birds that nest there are birds that have abandoned light.

Not literally. They are not cave-adapted. But they nest in the dark, in the gaps between the substructure stones, in the places where the metal panels meet the older stonework and the junction creates a small cavity. They line these cavities with the wiry grass and the fibrous material from the succulent mats, and the nests are invisible from above, hidden beneath the grate, accessible only through gaps that a larger animal — a mustelid, for instance — would have to work to find.

The first species is the one you hear. It is small — sparrow-sized, brown-gray, with a short tail and a round body that suggests it spends more time walking than flying. It hunts on the platform surface in the early morning and the late afternoon, moving in quick hops along the channel edges, taking the small beetles and the crickets. Its call is the short, descending note of dawn and dusk — a single tone that drops, as if pulled, as if reluctant. It is not a song. It is not a territorial announcement. It is a *check-in*. It is the bird saying: I am still here. Are you still here? And the other birds, in their own cavities, do not answer, but their silence is the answer: they are still here.

The second species is even smaller and even drabber and does not call at all. It is a gleaner, not a hunter — it moves through the substructure, picking arthropods from the lichen and the metal and the stone, and it almost never surfaces. You might see it once, as a small, fast shape disappearing into a gap in the grate, and you will not see it again. It is the most secretive vertebrate here, more secretive even than the mustelid, and it may be more numerous than it appears — the substructure is large, and the cavities are many, and a bird that spends its life in the dark does not need to be seen to be present.

### The Visitors

The birds that pass through are not residents but opportunists. A raptor — something broad-winged and dark — circles the platform occasionally, riding the thermals that rise from the heated metal, and the lizards freeze and the ground-nesting birds disappear into their cavities and the platform goes still until the raptor has moved on. The raptor does not hunt here, usually. It uses the thermal and leaves. But its shadow is enough to remind everything below it that the sky is not always empty, and that the platform is not as isolated as the heat and the altitude make it seem.

Smaller visitors — a swift, a flycatcher — appear erratically, perhaps drawn by the insect activity, perhaps simply passing through. They do not stay. The platform is too high, too exposed, too far from the vegetation that supports them. They are the reminders that the platform is part of a larger world, that the valley below has forests and streams and the ordinary business of bird life, and that this place — this metal-and-stone island in the sky — is not the whole of existence, even if it feels like it when the fog closes in.

## The Mustelids

The mustelid is the thing you will not see and will always almost see.

It is long, low, and dark — dark-furred in a way that is distinct from the lizards' dark scales, because fur absorbs light in a way that scales do not, and the mustelid, when it moves across the metal grate, seems to leave a hole in the visual field, a place where light goes in and nothing comes back. It is fast, but not in the way the lizards are fast — the lizards are fast in bursts, in short sprints that end in stillness. The mustelid is fast in a *sustained* way, a flowing way, a way that suggests it could keep moving at this speed for a very long time and that it would like to.

It hunts everything. The lizards, the birds, the large beetles if it can catch them. It hunts in the substructure, flowing through the gaps in the stone and the metal like water through a filter, and it hunts on the surface in the early morning and the late evening, when the light is low and the heat is reduced and the lizards are slower. You see its tracks in the dust that accumulates in the sheltered spots — small, paired prints in straight lines, the gait of an animal that does not wander but *travels*. You see its droppings near the cisterns, dark and twisted and full of the indigestible remnants of its meals — beetle carapaces, lizard scales, the small bones of birds. You see the places where it has been — a disturbed spider web, a scattering of dust at a gap's edge, a tuft of fur caught on a rough piece of metal — and you construct the animal from its traces, and the animal you construct is more present than the animal itself, because the animal itself is never where you are looking.

There are likely two or three of them. The platform system is large enough to support a small population of a predator this size, and the droppings are distributed in a pattern that suggests two or three separate territories — one centered on the northern end of the platform, one on the southern, and one that seems to overlap both, perhaps a younger animal that has not yet established a fixed range. They are the apex. There is nothing above them. They hunt the birds that hunt the lizards that eat the insects that eat the plants that grow from the fungal network that lives in the cracks in the metal that catches the fog that the wind brings, and the mustelid is the top of this pyramid, and it does not know it, and it would not care.

The mustelid is also the thing that makes the platform feel *inhabited* rather than simply populated. The lizards and the insects and the birds are here, but they are here in the way that furniture is in a room — they occupy space, they fill it, they give it texture. The mustelid is here in the way that a person is in a room — it moves through the space with an apparent *purpose*, a direction, a sense of destination, and even if the purpose is only hunger and the destination is only the next meal, the sense of agency is unmistakable. The mustelid *decides*. It decides to go left or right, to enter this gap or that one, to wait or to move, and the deciding is the thing that separates it from everything else here, the thing that makes it the closest thing this place has to a mind.

It is the animal that would miss the place if it were gone. Not because it has feelings about the place — it does not — but because it has *learned* the place, has mapped it in whatever way its brain maps space, and the map is not transferable. If the platform were destroyed, the mustelid would not adapt. It would not find another home. It would wander and it would starve and it would die, because the specific knowledge of this specific place — which gap leads to which cavity, which channel the lizards favor, which cistern the birds nest near — is all it has, and it is worth nothing anywhere else. The mustelid is the platform's most committed resident, and it is the one that would be least able to leave.

## The Fungal Network

You did not ask about the fungi, and yet they are here, and they may be the most important thing that lives here, and they are the thing you will never see.

The fungal network in the cracks and the soil and the accumulated detritus of the platform system is a single organism, or nearly so. It is a mycelium — a web of threadlike hyphae that extends through every fissure, every join, every accumulation of organic material across the entire platform. It connects the succulent mats to the grass to the fern to the lichen to the soil to the stone. It is the substrate of which the living community here is built, and it is alive, and it is old, and it is *huge* in the way that things that are invisible can be huge.

The mycelium does what mycelia do: it decomposes, it transports, it connects. It breaks down the organic material that accumulates — dead beetles, lizard droppings, fragments of succulent thallus, the fibrous remains of bird nests — and converts it into forms that the plants can use. It transports water through its hyphae from the wet spots to the dry spots, extending the reach of the moisture beyond the channels and the spill zones. It connects the roots of the grass and the rhizines of the succulent mats in a network that allows resources to move between organisms that are, taxonomically, unrelated.

This is not altruism. It is chemistry. The fungal network exists because the conditions for its existence are present, and it does what it does because doing what it does is what it *is*. But the effect of its existence is that the platform system functions as a single ecological unit, not as a collection of independent organisms in proximity. The fungi are the reason the grass can grow far from the channel seep. The fungi are the reason the succulent mats can survive in cracks that dry out faster than the mats themselves can retain water. The fungi are the reason the thin soil in the fissures can hold enough nutrients to support the growth that it supports.

They have been here longer than the metal. They may have been here before the stone was cut. They are the oldest living thing on this platform, and they are the thing that will persist after the metal is gone and the stone has crumbled and the channels have filled with the debris of centuries. When the platform is nothing but a faint discoloration on a mountain shoulder, the fungi will still be in the cracks, still decomposing, still connecting, still doing the slow, invisible work of making a place *habitable* for whatever comes next.

---

## What Doesn't Live Here Anymore

There are absences here, and they have weight.

The people are the most obvious absence, and they are also the most complicated, because they are not absent in the way that a species is absent when it has been extirpated. They are absent in the way that a gardener is absent from a garden that no longer needs gardening. The system was built to operate without continuous maintenance, and it does, and the people who built it and who maintained it and who lived in the shelter and climbed the tower and watched the weather and the water and the world — they are gone, but the system does not miss them, and the absence is not a wound. It is a completion.

But there are other absences. The machinery that is still — the broken turbine, the missing vanes, the frozen pressure gauge — these are the absences of *function*, and they are absences that the rest of the system has filled. The gravity flow replaced the pump. The lichen replaced the paint. The lizards replaced whatever monitoring the brackets once held. The system healed around its broken parts, not by repairing them but by making them irrelevant.

And there are absences in the living community. There are no snakes here, though the habitat would support them. There are no amphibians, though the fog and the cisterns provide moisture. There are no bats, though the insects are abundant at dusk. These absences are not failures. They are the result of a process of arrival and establishment and survival that is ongoing and incomplete. The platform is an island, and islands do not have everything. They have what arrived, and what arrived is what survived, and what survived is what you see: lizards and insects and birds and a mustelid and a fungal network and a few stubborn plants, and nothing else, and nothing else is needed.

## The System as Organism

The final answer to what lives here is: the place itself.

This is not a metaphor. Or if it is, it is a metaphor that the place does not need and would not recognize. The Condensation Platforms of Khar-Altef are a system — metal and stone and wind and fog and channel and cistern and pipe and crack and lichen and grass and fern and beetle and cricket and flying insect and lizard and lizard and lizard and bird and bird and mustelid and fungus — and the system is alive in the way that a system is alive, which is different from the way an organism is alive but is not less real.

The system takes in. It takes in air and fog and dust and organic material and the occasional lost bird. It processes. It condenses and channels and stores and delivers and grows and eats and excretes and reproduces and dies and decomposes and grows again. It outputs. It outputs water, through the pipes, to the valley below. It outputs life, in the form of the organisms that live here and the offspring they produce. It outputs silence, which is the byproduct of a system that does not need to be loud.

The system has a metabolism. It has a cycle. It has inputs and outputs and feedback loops and redundancies and failures and compensations. It has been running for longer than any of its components have been alive, and it will continue to run for longer than any of its current components will live. It is not an organism, but it is what an organism is: a process that persists.

And what lives here is the process. The lizards are the process's motion. The mustelid is its intention. The fog is its breath. The wind is its heartbeat. The metal is its skeleton, and the stone is its body, and the fungi are its nerves, and the water is its blood, and the valley below — the valley that receives the water and does not know where it comes from — is the world that it feeds, without knowing, without caring, without stopping.

This is what lives here. Everything. All of it, at once, together, and nothing without the rest.
