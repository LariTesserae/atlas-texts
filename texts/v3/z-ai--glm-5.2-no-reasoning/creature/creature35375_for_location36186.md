# What Lives in Khar-Vost

---

## The Inhabitants

They do not have a name for themselves that translates. Or rather, they have several, and each one is correct in a different way, and they do not find this troubling. The closest approximation in any language that has a word for the sea might be *those who stayed when the water left* — but this is a story they tell about themselves, not a taxonomy, and they are aware that it is a story. They are aware of all their stories. They keep them the way they keep their rooms: placed, not displayed.

Call them the Khar.

### Body

They are tall. Not dramatically so — not the elongated proportions of something designed to impress — but tall in the way of a body that has grown into available space and stopped when the space was sufficient. A hundred and ninety centimeters, perhaps, for an adult of median size. The variation across individuals is small. You do not see tall Khar and short Khar. You see Khar.

They are thin in a way that reads as *efficient* rather than *deprived*. Their bodies do not store what they do not need. The limbs are long, the joints distinct, the hands narrow and extraordinarily precise. Their fingers — four on each hand, paired in a way that suggests a evolutionary branch point that diverged from anything terrestrial a very long time ago — are dexterous enough that the same hand can wield a carving pick against tuff and adjust the position of a small geometric object on a niche shelf with what appears to be identical care. They do not differentiate between work and maintenance of living space. Both are acts of continuation.

Their skin is pale. Not white — not the pallor of something that has never seen light, which would be translucent, which would be fragile — but the specific pale of the tuff itself. Cream. Ochre, in some individuals. Tinged with the faint pink of sodiumite in others. It is not pigmentation in the usual sense. The coloration comes from below — from the mineral content of the tissues, which incorporate and lay down the same compounds the city is made of. Their skin is, in a literal and non-metaphorical sense, the same material as their walls, organized differently. This is not a condition. This is an adaptation. It is what allows them to live here.

That translucence at the temples and the backs of the hands — the dark lines of veins beneath — is not a vulnerability. It is where the body is thinnest, and the body is thinnest there because that is where exchange happens. The Khar do not breathe through lungs alone. The skin at the temples is permeable in a way that the rest of the surface is not, and through it the body takes in and releases gases in a slow, continuous exchange with the mineral air. They *are* permeable to their world, at specific points, in specific ways. This is not a limitation. It is how they are continuous with their environment.

Their eyes are large. This is the feature you notice first, if you encounter them face to face. Not luminous — not the reflecting tapetum of a nocturnal animal — but large and dark and matte-surfaced, absorbing light without returning it. The pupil is wide, the iris dark, the sclera not white but a muted bone-grey. They do not blink. Their eyes do not require it. The surface is kept moist — or rather, kept *functional* — by a different mechanism than blinking, a slow secretion that maintains the optical surface continuously. The result is a gaze that does not interrupt itself. When a Khar looks at you, they look at you without the staccato rhythm of eyelids, and the effect is not staring but *attending*. They see you the way the city sees you. Completely, and without urgency.

Their faces are minimal. The features are present but understated — a mouth that is functional, not expressive; a nose that is flat, with nostrils adapted to the alkaline air; a brow that is smooth, neither heavy nor light. There is a sense, looking at them, that the face has been optimized for function and that expression is not among its functions. This is not accurate. The Khar express. They express through the hands, through posture, through the quality of their stillness. The face is simply not where they keep their affect.

### Movement

They move slowly. Not slowly in the manner of the sick or the exhausted — not slowly the way a body moves when it is failing — but slowly the way a geological process moves. Deliberately. With the tempo of something that understands it has time.

This is not a biological limitation. Their bodies are capable of speed — you see it rarely, but you see it: a hand reaching suddenly to catch a falling object, a body pivoting sharply to avoid a stone dislodged from a corridor ceiling, a quick lateral step to avoid a burrowing trail in the ash. The speed is there. It is simply not the default. The default is patience.

They walk through their city like water through stone — following the paths that exist, filling the spaces that are open, not forcing passage where passage is not needed. Their stride is long and even. Their feet — broad, flat, with a gritty sole texture that grips the tuff surfaces — make almost no sound. The dust absorbs what their footsteps would otherwise produce. You become aware of their approach not through sound but through the displacement of air, the subtle shift in the quality of the silence that tells you the space you occupy has become shared.

When they stop, they stop completely. A Khar standing still is not a person pausing between actions. A Khar standing still is a body in a state of rest so thorough that the distinction between the body and the stone it stands against becomes a question of where you choose to draw the line. They do not shift their weight. They do not fidget. They do not adjust. They arrive at stillness the way water arrives at level, and they remain there, and the stillness is not an absence of activity but a thing in itself, a quality, a state.

You watch one standing in a corridor for ten minutes. You cannot tell if they are waiting, or thinking, or simply *being*. When they move again, the movement is not an interruption of the stillness. It is a continuation of it. The stillness did not end. It changed shape.

### Speech

They speak, but they speak the way they move — rarely, precisely, without excess.

Their vocal apparatus produces sounds in a range that is partially audible to human ears and partially not. The lower frequencies — a resonant hum, a vibration that sits somewhere between a voice and the sound of wind in a deep passage — carry through the stone corridors with extraordinary fidelity. The acoustics of the city are not incidental to their communication. The corridors were carved, in part, to shape the sound. A word spoken in a residential gallery on the fourteenth tier can be heard — not as words, but as a pattern of vibrations, identifiable, locatable — in the industrial quarter three hundred meters below. The city is their telephone. The city is their public square.

They do not converse in the way that most species converse — the rapid exchange, the building of meaning through accumulation of small verbal gestures. A Khar conversation, observed from a distance, is almost invisible. One individual speaks. The interval that follows is not silence but *consideration* — a space in which the words settle into the stone and the meaning forms. Then the other responds. Or does not. The absence of response is itself meaningful. They do not fill silence. They do not perform agreement. If there is nothing to say, they say nothing, and the nothing is specific and comprehensible to them.

What their language encodes is not what most languages encode. They have words for the state of the stone — its age, its mineral content, its rate of deposition, the precise quality of its surface texture. They have words for the wind — its direction, its speed, its likely duration, its chemical signature on this particular day. They have words for the lithovores — each species, its habits, its current population level, its likely position in the cycle of consumption. They have words for each other, but these are fewer. A name. A relationship. A shared history, compressed into a single term that carries the weight of decades.

They do not have a word for *hello*.

They do not need one. Presence is sufficient. If you are there, and they are there, and the space is shared, the acknowledgment has already occurred. It is in the quality of the air, in the way the silence changes when it is held by two instead of one.

### The Hands

If the face is minimal, the hands are maximal. This is where the Khar live, expressively. This is where their interiority is visible, if you know how to look.

A Khar's hands are never idle in the way that idle hands suggest purposelessness. They are either at rest — completely at rest, folded or placed, still as stone — or they are engaged. There is no intermediate state. No drumming of fingers. No tracing of patterns on surfaces. No fidgeting. The hands are either working or they are not, and when they are not, they are not doing anything at all.

When they work, the hands are precise beyond what seems possible for their size. The same fingers that can grip a carving tool with enough force to cut tuff can position a small object — a geometric form, incised with patterns that echo the bedding planes of the stone — with a delicacy that suggests the hand is not a single instrument but a collection of specialized tools that happen to share a palm. They touch things with intention. There is no casual contact with the physical world. Everything they touch, they touch because they mean to.

They gesture. Not broadly — not the expansive movements of a species that communicates with its arms — but subtly, with small motions of the fingers, the wrist, the orientation of the palm. A gesture can communicate *the wind is shifting* or *the stone here is soft* or *I have been working on this corridor* or *the large lithovore in the fifth gallery has been restless today*. The vocabulary is large, but the motions are small, and they are readable at a distance of several meters because the stone corridors carry motion as clearly as they carry sound. A gesture made at one end of a gallery is visible — as a shadow, as a shift in the pattern of light — at the other end.

A Khar who does not wish to be seen can keep their hands still, and they will be invisible. Not hidden. Invisible. The hands at rest become part of the body at rest become part of the stone. This is not camouflage in the biological sense. It is something more interesting. It is the voluntary cessation of expression, and it is a thing they choose to do, and the choice tells you something, if you know how to read it.

### What They Eat

This is the question that the visitor returns to most often, and the question that the Khar answer least often, and the answer is simpler and stranger than the reticence suggests.

They eat the stone.

Not the raw tuff — not the walls of their own chambers, carved and bitten directly. The process is more considered than that. The industrial quarter exists, in part, to prepare what they eat. The mineral compounds that the lithovores metabolize in their crude, arthropodal way are processed by the Khar into something more refined. The sodiumite is separated, purified, combined with other compounds — sulfur in specific allotropes, chlorinated brines reduced to specific concentrations, trace minerals identified and incorporated — and the result is a substance that is not stone, exactly, but is not not stone either. It is mineral food. It is what their bodies are built to metabolize.

They eat it in private. Not because it is shameful — there is no shame attached to it, no taboo, no ritual of concealment — but because eating, for the Khar, is a slow process. It takes hours. The body receives the prepared mineral compounds and begins the chemical exchange that converts them to usable energy, and during this time the body is not fully available for other activities. It is not that they become incapacitated. It is that their attention turns inward, and the outward-facing functions — the watching, the listening, the attending to the city and its sounds and its people — recede. They go quiet. They go still. They go into the body, in the way that the deep brines go into the stone, and they stay there until the exchange is complete.

They eat during the dimming, when the city is quiet and the lamps are lit and the lithovores have gone to rest. The prepared mineral is kept in sealed niches in their chambers, and they consume it alone, or in pairs, or in small groups of three or four who share a gallery and do not need to speak because the eating is shared and the sharing is the speech. They do not eat in public. The visitor does not see this. The visitor sees the result: the Khar, in the day-cycle, moving through their city with the unhurried energy of bodies that are adequately fueled and the quiet focus of bodies that do not need to think about fuel.

The reticence, such as it is, is not about the eating. It is about the privacy of a process that is, for them, intimate in the way that sleep is intimate. You do not watch someone sleep. You do not watch someone eat, here. The boundary is not shame. The boundary is the recognition that some processes are internal, and the internal is not for display.

### What They Make

The Khar make three things, and the making of these three things constitutes the majority of their visible activity.

**They make the city.** The maintenance of Khar-Vost is not a task that has been assigned. It is a practice that has been inherited, and the inheritance has been so thorough that the practice and the practitioners have become difficult to distinguish. A Khar maintaining a corridor — re-cutting a passage that has narrowed by millimeters over decades of mineral deposition — is not performing labor in the way that a human maintenance worker performs labor. They are *participating in the stone's life.* The tool in their hand is the instrument through which the city's form is preserved, and the form is the city, and the city is their home, and the distinction between maintaining the home and being the home is not a distinction they recognize.

The toolmarks are everywhere. You learn to read them: fresh cuts are crisp, pale, the geometry sharp. Older cuts have softened, the edges rounding as the mineral deposition lays down new layers. The oldest cuts — in the deep galleries, in the first excavations — are barely distinguishable from the natural bedding planes. The stone has reclaimed them. This is expected. This is correct. A Khar looking at a corridor can tell you, from the toolmarks alone, how long it has been since the corridor was last maintained, and whether the maintenance was done with attention or as a routine, and which individual did the work. The toolmarks are a record. The city is its own archive.

**They make objects.** These are the geometric forms that appear in their chambers, in their niches, placed with care on the carved shelves that punctuate the residential galleries. They are small — palm-sized, usually, though some are as large as a fist and some as small as a thumbnail — and they are made from the same tuff the city is carved from, or from denser basalt, or from the dark mineral composite that they use for doors and structural reinforcement.

They are not decorative. Or rather, they are not *merely* decorative. Each one is a specific form — a specific geometry, a specific set of angles and curves and incised patterns — and the specificity has meaning. The meaning is not representational. The objects do not *depict* anything. They are, in some way that the Khar do not explain and may not be able to explain in any language that translates, *about* something. A place. A moment. A quality of light. A sound the city made on a particular day. The objects are memory made mineral, and they are placed where they can be seen, and they are not discussed, and they are not moved once they are placed, unless the person who placed them moves them.

You see one in a chamber you pass through. It is a simple form — a truncated pyramid, slightly irregular, incised with a pattern that follows the stone's natural bedding in a way that makes the incision and the geology difficult to separate. It sits on a niche shelf near the sleeping platform. It has been there, you understand without being told, for a long time. It will be there for longer. It is not for you. You do not touch it.

**They make sound.** Not music, exactly. Not language. Something that occupies the space between the two — a practice of producing sounds in the city's corridors and galleries that takes advantage of the acoustics in ways that are both intentional and beautiful, though *beautiful* is a word that might not map onto the Khar's own aesthetic. The sounds are produced by striking the stone — with tools, with hands, with the specialized implements that some Khar carry and that you initially mistook for maintenance equipment. The tones are deep, resonant, sustained. They fill the corridors the way water fills a channel — following the paths, spreading into the spaces, accumulating in the plazas and the wider galleries. The reverberation lasts for minutes. A single tone, struck in the right place, in the right corridor, at the right time, can be heard — felt — across the entire city.

They do this rarely. The sounds are not casual. They are produced at specific times — the beginning of the day-cycle, when the wind starts and the systems come up; the beginning of the dimming, when the wind dies and the lamps come on; and at other times that do not follow a pattern the visitor can discern. The sounds serve a function — they are, in part, signals, communicating the state of the city's systems and the rhythm of the day — but they also serve no function, or rather, they serve a function that the Khar do not name, because naming it would diminish it. The function is the sound itself. The sound is the city speaking, through the hands of its inhabitants, and the speaking is the thing.

## The Lithovores

The Khar are not alone in their city. The lithovores — the mineral-eating creatures that share the corridors and galleries and plazas — are not pets, not livestock, not pests, not neighbors in any sense that implies separation. They are part of the city in the way that the stone is part of the city, and the Khar treat them accordingly.

The small ones — the finger-length, the hand-span — the Khar ignore. Not with hostility. Not with indifference, exactly. With the recognition that these creatures occupy a different scale of existence and that the interaction between their lives and the Khar's lives is structural rather than personal. The small lithovores eat the stone. The stone grows back. The cycle is the cycle. The Khar do not interfere with it.

The large ones — the dog-sized, heavy-bodied, dark-shelled creatures of the lower galleries — the Khar watch. Not continuously. Not with concern. With the kind of attention that a person gives to something that shares their living space and whose behavior is relevant to the maintenance of that space. A Khar descending into the lower galleries will note the position of the large lithovores, will track their movement over time, will adjust the maintenance schedule of the corridors they frequent. The grooves the lithovores wear into the stone are not repaired while the creatures are using them. The Khar wait until the creature has moved on — which may take years — and then they re-cut the corridor, and the cycle begins again.

There is a relationship here that the visitor does not fully access. The Khar and the lithovores have lived in the same space for long enough that the boundaries between them have become complex. The Khar maintain the corridors. The lithovores eat the corridors. The stone grows back. The Khar maintain it again. The lithovores eat it again. This is not competition. This is not parasitism. This is a shared ecosystem in which each participant plays a role that the other recognizes, and the recognition is not intellectual but behavioral — encoded in the patterns of movement, the timing of activity, the specific quality of the silence that holds when a Khar and a large lithovore share a gallery and neither one acknowledges the other and neither one needs to.

Sometimes a Khar will pause near a large lithovore. They will stand — still, attentive, the way they stand when they are listening to the city — and the lithovore will continue its slow feeding, and after a time the Khar will move on. The visitor cannot tell what has happened in that pause. Nothing visible has occurred. But something has. The Khar who paused knows something they did not know before. The lithovore has, in some way, communicated — through the sound of its mandibles on the stone, through the specific quality of the groove it is carving, through some information that the Khar's senses can read and the visitor's cannot. The city is full of information, and the Khar are not its only readers.

## The Children

There are children. This is worth saying, because the visitor might otherwise conclude that the Khar are a population in slow decline — eight thousand people in a city built for many more, the residential galleries mostly empty, the silence so complete that the question of reproduction becomes a question about the future.

The children are not hidden. They are simply not where the visitor expects them. They are not in the upper levels, where the light is good and the corridors are wide and the air is closest to the outside. They are in the middle tiers — the residential blocks with the best thermal stability, the corridors that are quietest, the galleries that are neither too bright nor too dim. The Khar keep their children in the part of the city that is most like the body — warm, protected, internally regulated, the place where the conditions for growth are most consistent.

The children are small. Startlingly small, given the size of the adults — the way that a sapling is startlingly small next to the tree it will become. Their skin is paler than the adults', almost translucent, the mineral content of their tissues not yet fully laid down. Their eyes are the same dark, unreflecting matte, but larger in proportion to their faces, giving them a look of focused attentiveness that is the same as the adults' but reads, in the smaller bodies, as something closer to wonder. They move with the same deliberation as the adults, but the deliberation is less practiced — you can see them choosing their movements, negotiating with their own limbs, learning the tempo that the adults have already internalized.

They learn by watching. This is the Khar way — not instruction, not demonstration in the formal sense, but proximity. A child who is learning to maintain corridors follows an adult through the work. They do not speak. The adult does not explain. The child watches the hands, the angle of the tool, the pressure, the way the tool is held and moved and the way the stone responds. The child carries a tool of their own — smaller, lighter, appropriate to their hands. They do not use it. Not yet. They hold it while they watch, and the holding is part of the learning, the hand learning the weight and the balance and the feel of the thing it will use when it is ready.

There are not many children. The population is stable, not growing — eight thousand is the number that has been here for as long as anyone can establish, which is a very long time, because the Khar remember things not in years but in layers of stone. A child is born when a child is needed. What *needed* means, in this context, is something the Khar do not explain and may not track. The children come. They grow. They become adults. The population does not increase beyond the carrying capacity of the city, and it does not decrease below the number required to maintain it. This is not planning. This is the same kind of equilibrium that the stone and the lithovores and the wind have reached — a system that has found its steady state and continues in it.

A child who is learning to make sound — the deep, resonant tones that fill the corridors — sits in a gallery with an adult. The adult strikes the stone. The tone blooms and fills the space. The child listens. The child does not strike the stone. The child listens for a long time — days, perhaps, or the equivalent of days in the Khar's unhurried reckoning — and then, one day, the child strikes the stone, and the tone that emerges is not the tone the adult produced but it is a tone, it is real, it belongs in the corridor, and the adult does not react. The adult does not praise. The adult does not correct. The adult listens to the child's tone the way they listen to the city — with attention, with recognition, with the acknowledgment that something has happened that is part of the pattern.

## The Old

The Khar do not die in the way that most living things die. Or rather — they do, eventually, but the process is so slow, so gradual, so continuous with the life that preceded it, that the moment of death is not a moment. It is a transition. A body that has been metabolizing mineral for centuries slowly reduces the rate of the exchange. The skin, already mineral, becomes more mineral. The body, already still, becomes more still. The difference between a very old Khar and the stone they stand against narrows over years — over decades — until it is not clear, to the visitor or perhaps to the Khar themselves, where the person ends and the stone begins.

There are shapes in the walls of the deep galleries that might be geological features and might be something else. The stone grows around them. The mineral deposition, that patient millimeter-per-decade accumulation, has been laying down layers around them for longer than the Khar's records go. The shapes are not discussed. They are not visited. They are not avoided. They are simply there, part of the city's fabric, part of the stone's memory, and the Khar who pass through those galleries do not pause at those shapes and do not hurry past them and do not remark on their presence.

But they know they are there.

The old do not withdraw. They do not go to a specific place to complete the transition. They remain in their chambers, in their corridors, in the spaces they have occupied for their whole lives. They continue to maintain the city, to the extent that they can — an ancient Khar, their body so saturated with mineral that they move with the literal slowness of stone, can still hold a tool, can still make a cut, can still participate in the work that the city requires. The cuts they make are shallow, slow, precise. They are the last cuts those particular hands will make. The stone accepts them.

When the transition is complete — when the body has become mineral in the full sense, when the exchange has stopped, when the person has become the stone — the chamber is not emptied. The objects on the niche shelf remain. The sleeping platform, with its impression of a body that lay on it for centuries, remains. The lamp is not lit, but it is not removed. The room becomes what every room in Khar-Vost eventually becomes: a space that the stone is slowly reclaiming, that the mineral deposition is slowly filling, that the lithovores will eventually graze on and the Khar will eventually re-cut. The cycle continues. The person who lived there is not gone. They are *in* the stone. They are part of the material that the next generation will carve their chambers from.

This is not a belief. This is not a metaphor. This is geology.

## How They Live Together

The social fabric of the Khar is not woven. It is *deposited* — layer by layer, over time, through the accumulation of shared presence in shared space.

A Khar who has lived in the same gallery for fifty years knows the other inhabitants of that gallery in a way that has no analogue in human experience. They know them through the sounds they make — the specific scrape of their tools, the specific tone they produce when they strike the stone, the specific rhythm of their footsteps (which are nearly silent but not quite, not to a Khar who has been listening for fifty years). They know them through the maintenance they perform — the corridors they keep, the way they keep them, the freshness of their toolmarks and the angle of their cuts. They know them through the objects they place in their chambers, which are visible through open doorways, which carry meaning that the visitor cannot read but that the Khar's neighbors can.

They do not gather. They do not have meetings, or councils, or celebrations, or ceremonies. They have *overlaps* — moments when the patterns of their separate lives intersect, when two Khar are in the same corridor at the same time, when the work they are doing brings them into proximity, when the sound one of them makes is audible to the other and the other's response — a pause, a gesture, a change in the quality of their silence — is the gathering.

When a corridor needs to be re-cut and the work is large enough to require two people, two people work on it. They do not discuss the work. They do not assign roles. They begin, and in the beginning, the division of labor is established — who cuts where, who clears the debris, who checks the geometry — and it proceeds, and it is completed, and they part without farewells, and the corridor is maintained, and the fact that they worked together is recorded in the toolmarks and in the memory of the stone and in the memory of the two people, which is, in a sense, the same thing.

Disputes exist. The visitor does not see them, but the Khar acknowledge them, obliquely, in the way they describe their own history. A dispute about the maintenance of a corridor — whose responsibility it is, how it should be cut, whether the lithovore damage requires intervention — is resolved through the same process that resolves everything in Khar-Vost: time, and the stone, and the slow accumulation of evidence. If a corridor is being maintained correctly, the stone is sound, the deposition is in balance, the lithovores are not overgrazing. If it is not, the evidence is visible — in the cracks, in the narrowing, in the quality of the air that moves through it. The Khar who is right does not need to argue. The stone argues for them.

This is not to say they are without conflict. They are people, or close enough. They have preferences, and preferences diverge. One Khar prefers the upper corridors, where the light is strong and the wind is steady. Another prefers the deep galleries, where the air is warm and still and the large lithovores move like slow planets through the dim. One produces sound in the lower registers; another in the higher. One maintains their chamber with frequent, shallow cuts; another with rare, deep ones. These differences are not resolved. They are *held*, in the same way the city holds the spaces between its corridors — as part of the structure, part of the pattern, part of what makes the whole place what it is.

## The Visitors

The Khar receive visitors. Not often. Not eagerly. But without hostility, without suspicion, and with a hospitality that is so minimal it can be mistaken for indifference.

A visitor is housed in an empty chamber. There are many empty chambers. The chamber is not prepared — it is not cleaned, not furnished, not adapted to the visitor's needs — because the Khar do not recognize that the visitor's needs might be different from their own. The chamber has a sleeping platform. It has a lamp. It has a niche for objects. It has stone walls and a stone floor and the faint ambient glow of light reflected from the corridor outside. It is, to the Khar, sufficient. It is, to the visitor, a stone room in a stone city on a world without water, and the sufficiency of it is a question that the visitor must answer for themselves.

The Khar answer questions. Not all questions. Not always. They answer the questions they understand, in the terms they understand them, and when a question does not map onto their conceptual framework, they do not attempt to bridge the gap. A visitor who asks *what do you believe about the afterlife* will receive, in return, a look — the large dark eyes, attending, the face minimal — and a silence that is not empty but full, full of the recognition that the question comes from a place the Khar have never been and do not need to go. The stone is the afterlife. The stone is the beforelife. The stone is the present. The distinction is not one they make.

A visitor who asks *may I watch you work* will receive a different response: a gesture, minimal, with the hand — the four fingers opening slightly, the palm turning — that means, approximately, *yes* or *you may* or *it does not matter to me whether you are here or not but you may be here.* And the Khar will return to their work, and the visitor will watch, and the watching will last as long as it lasts, and the Khar will not acknowledge the watching again unless the visitor does something that requires acknowledgment.

A visitor who stays long enough — months, years, long enough for the Khar's rhythm to become the visitor's rhythm, long enough for the silence to become the comfortable thing it becomes — will be incorporated into the pattern. Not named, not inducted, not welcomed in any formal sense. Simply *accounted for.* The Khar who maintain the corridors near the visitor's chamber will maintain those corridors as part of their rounds. The Khar who produce sound in the galleries nearby will produce sound in the galleries nearby, and the sound will be audible in the visitor's chamber, and the visitor will hear it, and the hearing will become part of the visitor's experience of the place, and the visitor will be, in a small and real way, part of the city.

The Khar do not say goodbye when a visitor leaves. The visitor simply goes. The chamber they occupied will be unoccupied. The stone will begin to reclaim it — slowly, millimeter by millimeter, the deposition laying down its patient layers on the floor and the walls and the sleeping platform where the visitor lay. In a hundred years, the chamber will be indistinguishable from any other. In a thousand, it will be part of the stone.

The Khar who interacted with the visitor will remember them. Not as a story. Not as a name. As a texture in the stone — a specific quality of the time they spent in the city, a particular pattern of sound and silence and presence that is associated with the shape of that particular life. The memory is not in the brain, or not only in the brain. It is in the stone. It is in the city. It is in the place where the visitor stood and the sounds they made and the air they displaced and the weight of their body on the floor.

The Khar remember the way the stone remembers: in layers, slowly, without preference, without loss.

## What They Are

This is the question the visitor keeps returning to. *What are the Khar?* Are they the builders? Are they the builders' descendants? Are they something that moved in when the builders left? Are they a separate evolution, a parallel development, a coincidence of form in a place where form is constrained by the same forces? Are they the stone's way of thinking? Are they the city's way of seeing itself?

The Khar do not answer this question. Not because they are secretive. Not because the answer is sacred or taboo or too complex for an outsider. They do not answer it because it is not a question they recognize. The distinction between *builder* and *built* — between the person who makes the city and the city that makes the person — is not a distinction that obtains in Khar-Vost. The city is made of stone. The Khar are made of stone. The stone grows. The Khar maintain it. The Khar grow. The stone maintains them. The cycle has been running for so long that the question of origin is the same as the question of *which came first, the lithovore or the groove it wears?* The answer is: both. The answer is: neither. The answer is: the question is wrong.

What they are is this: they are the part of the stone that moves. They are the part of the city that maintains the city. They are the consciousness — if that is the right word, and it may not be, but it is close — that the process has developed in order to know itself. They are not separate from the stone. They are what the stone does when it has been carved into corridors and galleries and chambers and filled with air and wind and the slow chemical exchange that turns mineral into life. They are the stone's self-awareness, if the stone is aware. They are the city's self-maintenance, if the city maintains itself. They are the process, continuing.

They are the Khar.

That is enough. That is what they call themselves, when they call themselves anything. It means *those who are here.* It means *the ones who continue.* It means, in the oldest inflection, in the deepest register of their language, *the living stone.*

They are the living stone. They live in the living stone. The stone lives in them.

The city continues.
