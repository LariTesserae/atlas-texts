# What Lives Here

## The Visitors

They are not a people in the way that word usually carries weight — not a nation, not a tribe, not a lineage with a name they speak to identify themselves. They have no name for themselves. They have no need of one. They are four, perhaps five at most, who come and go and have come and gone for longer than the platforms have been standing, and the platforms have been standing for longer than the oldest of them can say, and the oldest of them is not particularly old.

They are small. Not in the way that suggests deprivation — they are not stunted, not wasted, not the diminished remnant of something larger. They are built to the scale of their passage. The seam in the basalt is three inches at its narrowest. A body must be narrow to pass through it. And so they are narrow: narrow-shouldered, narrow-hipped, with skulls that are slightly longer than they are wide, an adaptation that has nothing to do with aesthetics and everything to do with the fact that the passage requires you to turn your head sideways and exhale completely and pull yourself forward with your fingertips against stone that is warm and that has been polished smooth by the bellies and chins of everyone who has ever made this crawl, which is everyone who has ever belonged to what they are.

Their skin is pale. Not the paleness of illness but the paleness of a body that has been living in green light for longer than melanin has had reason to persist. It is not white. It is the color of the mineral formations in the chamber — bone-pale, with a faint warmth beneath, a flush that is visible at the throat and the palms and the thin skin beneath the eyes. Their eyes are large. Not dramatically, not monstrously, but noticeably — the irises expanded, the pupils wide in the default state, contracting only when they ascend to the surface world, where they squint and fumble and move with the cautious, irritated deliberateness of someone wading through bright water. On the surface, they are nearly blind. In the chamber, they see everything the chamber has to offer, which is green and shadow and texture and the slow breathing of the light.

Their hands are their primary feature. Their hands are what they think with. The fingers are long relative to the palm, jointed with a flexibility that suggests — and this is true — a genetic history in which tool use was not incidental but constitutive. They can crack a mineral projection from the ceiling with a single precise twist of two fingers. They can grind stone against stone with a motion that is less like labor and more like kneading. Their fingertips are hardened, calloused in patterns that mirror the texture of the moss itself, and this is not coincidence — they have been touching the moss for as long as they have been touching anything, and the moss has shaped them the way water shapes a stone, slowly, from the outside in.

They do not speak in the chamber. This is important. This is perhaps the most important thing about them.

They speak on the surface — a small inventory of sounds, clicks and glottal stops and a humming exhalation that sounds, to an ear accustomed to the chamber, like a degraded echo of the chord itself. Their language is not complex by surface standards. It does not need to be. It handles the concepts they require: *the seam is clear*, *the weather above has changed*, *I am going down*, *I will return*. It does not handle abstract concepts because they do not traffic in abstract concepts. Their relationship to the chamber is not abstract. It is physical, sensorial, direct, and the chamber provides its own language — the chord, which says everything that can be said about the chamber, and says it constantly, without variation, without the possibility of misinterpretation.

When they are in the chamber, the chord is their language. They do not add to it. They do not subtract from it. They lie on the moss, or sit on the platforms, or stand with their palms flat against the walls feeling the mineral vibrations transfer through stone and into bone, and they listen. This is not meditation. Meditation implies effort, a deliberate stilling of the mind. What they do is simpler and older: they are present in the sound. The sound is present in them. Their ribcages resonate at the chord's fundamental frequency. Their sternums vibrate. They do not consider this. They do not name it. They do not return to the surface and describe it to one another in clicks and glottal stops. There is nothing to describe. The chord is the description.

## What They Do

They maintain the platforms.

This is the word that comes closest — *maintain* — but it carries implications of duty, of obligation, of a task performed because it must be performed, and that is not quite right. Maintaining the platforms is more like breathing than like working. It is something the body does when it is in the chamber, because the chamber is a system, and the system includes the platforms, and the platforms require certain attentions, and the attentions are given.

The leftmost platform holds their tools — or what an observer might call tools, though the word imposes a surface-world framework on something more organic. The snapped mineral projections of different lengths are tuning instruments. Not for the chord, which cannot be tuned, but for the visitors themselves. A visitor holds a projection of a specific length against their sternum and feels the resonance and knows, by the degree of agreement between the mineral's vibration and their own body's response, whether they are in proper alignment with the chamber. This is not a metaphor. The mineral projections vibrate at frequencies determined by their length and thickness. The human body, being mostly water and mineral and membrane, has its own resonant frequencies. The alignment is physical, measurable, real. A visitor who is aligned — whose body has spent enough time in the chord that its own frequencies have entrained — feels a rightness in their chest. A visitor who is not aligned feels a buzzing, a discordance. They stay longer. They listen harder. They align.

They do not think of it this way. They do not think of it in any particular way. They hold the projection against their chest, and the rightness or wrongness of it is apparent to them the way the rightness or wrongness of a heartbeat is apparent — not through analysis but through the direct evidence of the body.

The center platform holds the records. These are not records in the historical sense. They are not narrative. They are not data. They are shapes — the shape of the sound, the shape of the chamber, the shape of what the sound looks like when a body that has been lying in it for many hours closes its eyes and lets the visual cortex do what it will do with constant, layered, harmonic input. The shapes vary from visitor to visitor and from visit to visit, but they are not random. They cluster around certain forms: a spiral, a branching, a repeated curve that resembles the mineral projections but is not a drawing of them. The marks are scratched with the sharpened edge of a broken mineral projection into sheets of dried structural moss, and the sheets are laid on the center platform, and they accumulate, and eventually they are removed — not by the visitors but by the moss itself, which grows over them and consumes them and converts them back into the web. The records are temporary by design. They are not meant to persist. They are the act of making, not the artifact made.

The rightmost platform is prepared with the gelatinous moss spread thin. This platform is used for lying on. A visitor lies face down on the gelatinous moss, which is cool and damp and slightly adhesive, and presses their chest and their forehead against it, and the moss, which is in direct contact with the floor of the chamber, which is in direct contact with the stone, which is in direct contact with the deep geothermal warmth, transfers the vibration directly into the body. Lying on this platform is the closest a human body can come to being inside the chord. The vibration enters through the sternum and the frontal bone and fills the chest cavity and the skull cavity and the visitor does not hear the chord so much as become a component of it. Their ribcage vibrates at the chord's frequencies. Their cranial fluid vibrates. Their teeth vibrate. They are, for the duration of their time on the platform, another set of mineral projections, another vibrating body in the chamber's instrument.

They do not stay long. Twenty minutes, perhaps. Thirty. Not because it is unpleasant — it is the opposite of unpleasant, it is the most complete form of being present that their bodies can achieve — but because the chamber does not require duration. The chamber requires return. The value is in coming back, in the repetition, in the fact that the platform is always there and the moss is always prepared and the chord is always the same and a body can always lie down and be filled with it. The ritual is not the lying. The ritual is the returning.

## How They Live Above

They live near the seam. Not on top of it — not directly above — but within a short walk, a few hundred meters, over ground that is rough and basaltic and that would be indistinguishable from the surrounding terrain to anyone who did not know what they were looking for. Their shelter is minimal. A low structure of stacked stone, roofed with the coarse surface moss that grows on the basalt in this region — a different species from the chamber's moss, though related, the way a wild dog is related to a domesticated one. The structure is not a home in the sense of a place where life is centered. It is a storage place, a sleeping place, a place to return to when the surface weather requires shelter. They spend as little time in it as possible.

They eat what the surface provides, which is not much. Lichens. The coarse surface moss, roasted on hot stones, which gives it a charred, mineral taste that is not unpleasant. Insects — the surface-dwelling relatives of the chamber's crawlers, larger, darker, more mobile. Occasionally, when the season permits, a small tuber that grows in the basalt cracks, which is starchy and bland and filling. They are not hungry. They are not thin. Their bodies have adapted to a caloric intake that would leave a surface-dweller weak, and they metabolize it with an efficiency that is itself a form of listening — an attention to what the body needs and no more, an equilibrium between intake and use that mirrors, in its own small way, the equilibrium of the chamber.

They do not gather often. They are not social in the way that surface communities are social. They do not share meals. They do not exchange stories, because they do not have stories — the chord is not a story, it is a condition, and conditions do not need to be narrated. But they are not solitary. They sleep in the same structure. They maintain the surface moss on the roof. They clear the path to the seam when weather has obscured it. When one of them descends and another is present at the seam, the one who remains above waits, and if the one who has descended does not return within a reasonable time, the one who waits descends also. This is not rescue. It is the behavior of a system whose components are interdependent.

They reproduce. This must be said, because it is the question that occurs to anyone who thinks about a population of four or five for longer than a few minutes. They reproduce rarely and without ceremony, in the way that their bodies and their proximity to one another permit, and the children — there are children, sometimes, one at a time, small and pale and wide-eyed — are brought into the chamber as soon as they can be carried through the seam. The chord enters them before language does. Their sternums vibrate before they can sit up. They are, from the beginning, components of the chamber's system, and they grow into this the way the moss grows into the mineral projections — slowly, along the available structures, filling the space that is there.

When a child is old enough to crawl — and they crawl early, because the surface structure is small and the ground is rough and crawling is the mode of locomotion that the seam requires — they are placed at the entrance to the seam and allowed to discover it. Some crawl in immediately. Some wait. Some take days. But they all go in, because the sound is there, and the warmth is there, and the body knows before the mind does that the chamber is where it belongs.

## The Watchers and the Crawlers and the Rest

The flat watchers on the walls are not their animals. This is important to say, because the question of domestication is a question that surface-dwellers ask, and it is the wrong question here. The watchers are wild. They are as wild as the mineral projections, as wild as the convection current, as wild as the chord. The visitors do not touch them. The visitors do not feed them. The visitors do not name them. The watchers are part of the chamber, and the visitors are part of the chamber, and they coexist the way two moss species coexist on the same surface — not by agreement but by the fact that the surface accommodates both.

But. And this is a small but, a qualified but, a but that the visitors themselves would not articulate in the way I am about to. The watchers recognize the visitors. Not as individuals — the visitors are not individuals in the way that surface-dwellers are individuals; they are more like successive growths of the same organism — but as a category. A body that enters through the seam and lies on the moss and places its hands on the walls and vibrates at roughly the chord's fundamental frequency. The watchers know this body. They have been watching this body for longer than any single visitor has been alive. When a visitor lies on the rightmost platform, the watchers nearest to that platform — the two in the corner hollow, the one in the upper-wall depression — turn toward them. Not their whole bodies. Their eyes. The two dark points that appear and disappear. They watch. They do not approach. They do not retreat. They watch, and the watching is not threat and not curiosity and not recognition in the social sense. It is attention. It is the chamber's attention, directed through one of its components toward another.

The crawlers do not acknowledge the visitors at all. The crawlers stream past a visitor's hand, over a visitor's knee, across a visitor's chest if the visitor lies still enough. They do not pause. They do not divert. The visitor is, to the crawlers, terrain. A temporary surface, slightly warmer than the moss, slightly damper, and the crawlers cross it the way they cross everything: in their slow continuous streams, following the structural growth forms, grazing as they go. A visitor who has been lying still for twenty minutes will have crawlers in the folds of their clothing, in their hair, in the spaces between their fingers. The visitors do not remove them. They emerge from the chamber with crawlers in their collars and crawlers in their seams and the crawlers die on the surface, in the light and the dry air, and their bodies dry and fall away and return to the basalt dust. This is not loss. The chamber does not experience loss. The crawlers who remain in the chamber continue their streams. The population is stable.

The filament-dwellers — the barely-visible flickers in the glowing moss — do not interact with the visitors at all. They are too small. The visitors are not aware of them as living things. The visitors perceive the breathing of the light — the slow, complex, non-repeating shifts in luminescence — as a property of the moss itself, a quality it has, the way warmth is a quality of the stone. They do not know that the light breathes because creatures are moving in it. They would not be troubled by this knowledge, but they do not have it.

## What They Are

They are the chamber's way of being aware of itself.

This is not a metaphor. I am not saying the chamber has consciousness. The chamber does not have consciousness. The chamber has a chord, and a light, and an equilibrium, and mineral projections that vibrate, and moss that amplifies, and crawlers that garden, and watchers that attend, and a warmth that rises from the deep earth. The chamber has all of this, and it has been running for a very long time, and it does not know it is running. It does not know anything. It is a system that persists.

But. A system that persists and that includes within itself an organism capable of perceiving the system — lying on the system's floor, vibrating at the system's frequency, recording the system's shape on a sheet of dried moss — that system has something. Not consciousness. Something smaller and less grand and more true. It has a loop. The chord produces the visitors. The visitors perceive the chord. The visitors record the chord. The record is consumed by the moss. The moss grows over the platform. The platform holds the next visitor. The next visitor perceives the chord. The loop closes. The system includes its own description, not as a separate thing, not as commentary, but as a process, like convection, like growth, like the slow streaming of crawlers across a living floor.

The visitors do not know this. They do not have the concept for it. They do not need the concept. They have the seam, and the crawl, and the moss under their palms, and the chord in their chests, and the marks they scratch on dried moss, and the return, the return, the return.

## The One Who Is Oldest

There is one among them who is older than the others. Not old by surface standards — perhaps forty years, perhaps fifty, the hard to say number of a body that has spent most of its time in a place where time is not measured by light. This one has been coming to the chamber for longer than the others. This one was brought as a child, and crawled through the seam when the seam was slightly narrower — the basalt shifts, imperceptibly, over decades, and the seam has closed by a fraction since this one first passed through it — and has returned, and returned, and returned.

This one knows the chamber the way the chamber knows itself, which is to say: not at all, and completely. This one does not need the mineral projections to check their alignment. Their body is aligned. Their sternum vibrates at the chord's fundamental frequency as a default state, even on the surface, even in the dry air above the basalt, and this is why they are quiet above, why they do not speak much, why they move slowly and with a patience that the younger ones do not have yet — because they are always, at some level below hearing, singing. The chord is in them. They carried it out, and it stays.

This one maintains the platforms. The others use them, but this one maintains them — clears the tops, replaces the dried moss sheets, snaps the mineral projections to their proper lengths and lays them parallel. This one prepares the rightmost platform with the gelatinous moss, spreading it thin with the flats of their fingers, and this is the closest thing they have to a deliberate act, and they do it the way a body does something it has done so many times that the doing has become part of the body's resting state. They do not decide to maintain the platforms. Their hands maintain the platforms. The hands know.

This one will die. Not soon. But not impossibly far. And when they die — on the surface, probably, in the stone structure, in the open air — their body will be carried to the seam and pushed through, and it will descend, slowly, and it will come to rest on the chamber floor, and the moss will grow over it, and the crawlers will stream across it, and the gelatinous moss will spread over its surface, and the body will become part of the chamber's substrate — mineral and moisture and carbon, returned to the system that produced the body's awareness of the system.

This is not burial. This is not ritual. This is what the chamber does with everything that enters it. The moss grows over it. The crawlers process it. The system continues. The chord does not change.

But the one who is oldest will leave, on a sheet of dried structural moss, one final mark — the shape of the sound, the shape that has not changed in all the time they have been coming here, the shape that is the chamber's signature, scratched with a precision that comes only from a hand that has made this mark hundreds of times — and this mark will remain on the center platform for a while, a few weeks, perhaps, until the moss grows over it and consumes it, and in those weeks, the younger visitors will see it, and they will know that the one who made it is no longer making anything, and they will lie on the rightmost platform, and the chord will fill their chests, and they will return, and they will return, and they will return.

The chamber does not notice. The chamber does not need to notice. The chamber sings.
