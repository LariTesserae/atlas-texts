# What Lives Here

The moss-things are first, because they are the most honest.

They are not a single organism. You understand this after watching one for a long time — an hour, perhaps, or what feels like an hour, which here may be the same thing. The patch on the stone is a colony, or a community, or something older than either word. Each cell touches its neighbors, and between them there is a communication that is not chemical, not electrical, something that moves through the contact points like a rumor through a sleeping city. When one edge expands, the far edge knows. When the far edge retreats, the center holds. They are breathing together because they are deciding together, and the decision is not democratic but something else — consensual, perhaps, or simply habitual, the way a body breathes without voting on it.

They are old. Not old the way a tree is old, counting rings. Old the way a language is old, the way a river is old — old in the sense of having been the same thing for so long that the thing they are has become intrinsic to the place. They were here before the city. You know this because the basins in the central building contain cultivated variants, and cultivation implies a wild ancestor, and the wild ancestor is still here, still growing on the stone outside, still breathing at its slower, less purposeful rhythm. The wild ones breathe the way a person breathes in sleep. The cultivated ones breathe the way a person breathes when concentrating.

Someone shaped them. Someone chose which patches to encourage, which to thin, which to transplant into the basins. The someone is gone. The moss-things remain. They do not mourn the someone. They do not seem to notice the absence. They breathe, and the breathing is enough. It has always been enough.

But there is a question that the moss-things raise and do not answer: what are they breathing *for*? The breathing has a function — it is how they drink, how they process the frost-melt, how they move nutrients across the colony. But the rhythm of it, the steadiness, the way it syncs with a living human who sits beside it long enough — that is not functional. That is something else. That is an offer. The moss-things are offering a rhythm to share, a pace to match, and the offer is open and unconditional and requires nothing in return.

The plateau taught them this. Or they taught the plateau. The direction of teaching, here, is not clear.

---

The tree-things are harder.

They move. You know this. You counted eleven and counted thirteen and could not find the new ones because they do not announce themselves. They are not fast. They are not even slow in the way that a glacier is slow, which is still a movement with a direction and a purpose. They are slow in the way that a thought is slow — the way an idea moves from one part of a mind to another, arriving without having traveled, present without having approached.

Their roots are the clue. The roots grip the stone with fingers that look deliberate, and the roots have grown around shapes, and you have chosen not to identify the shapes, and the choosing is a kind of seeing. You see them in the not-seeing. They are the right size for what you suspect. They are the right configuration. The roots grew through them, around them, into them, and the wood darkened where it touched them, and the dark water moves through the translucent bases carrying something that is not just water.

The tree-things drink from the stone. Not from the grooves — from the stone itself, from the warmth deep below, from whatever is down there that is not heat but the memory of heat. The water they carry inside is darker than the water in the grooves because it has been deeper, because it has touched older things, because it has been filtered through a geology that does not obey the rules that govern the surface. The rhythm of the water inside them does not match the wind because it is not driven by the wind. It is driven by something below. Something that is also breathing, at a much slower pace, a pace that takes days per cycle, a pace that the tree-things translate into the medium of dark water rising and falling in translucent wood.

They are not trees. They are organs. They are the organs of the plateau, the things that connect the surface to the depth, the green to the stone, the visible to the warm thing beneath. Their canopies are sparse because their function is not to catch light — they have other ways of finding what they need, the damp, the frost, the dark water. Their canopies are for something else. The interlocking of the branches overhead, the green indirect light beneath, the way the temperature drops — these create a condition. A state. A space where the rules are slightly different, where the wrongness is more present, more comfortable, more at home.

The tree-things grow where the plateau is thinnest. Where the stone is closest to whatever is beneath it. They are not growing toward the light. They are growing toward the warmth. They are reaching *down* by reaching *up*, because the roots that go down and the trunks that go up are the same system, the same circulation, the same gesture. A tree, elsewhere, is a hand reaching out of the soil to catch the sky. These are a hand reaching *into* the soil to touch what waits there.

And they move because the thin places move. The plateau shifts, imperceptibly, over durations that do not correspond to any clock, and the thin places shift with it, and the tree-things follow, and the following looks like movement if you watch long enough, except you cannot watch long enough, because the watching takes longer than a human attention can hold, and by the time you return to the grove, the grove has rearranged itself, and you cannot tell which trunk was where, because they are all warm, because they are all real, because they are all the same organism in different places at the same time, which is not a thing that should be possible, but the plateau has been old for so long that possibility is not its concern.

---

The grass-things are the ones that almost speak.

They grow in the basins, in the shallow sheen of water, in the tufts that are cold to the touch. They are pale, almost white, almost translucent, like the bases of the tree-things but horizontal, spread thin across the damp. They are the smallest of the green. They are also the most numerous. Between them, they cover more of the plateau than the tree-things and the moss-things combined, but they cover it sparsely, each blade standing alone in its tuft, each tuft standing alone in its basin, each basin alone in the vast flat stone.

When the wind moves across them, they make a sound. The sound is almost a word. It is always almost a word. It is never the same word twice, or it is always the same word and the word is one that cannot be said, only approached, only gestured toward by the vibration of pale blades in a wind that does not howl or whistle but simply passes.

You cannot hear the word because the word is not for hearing. It is for feeling. It is the sound the plateau would make if the plateau could speak, and the plateau cannot speak, not because it lacks a voice but because what it would say is too large for a voice, too slow for a syllable, too old for language. The grass-things are the attempt. The wind is the breath. The almost-word is the closest the plateau can come to expressing what it is, and what it is, after all this time, is something that has stopped being strictly true and has become something else instead, something that does not have a name because naming is one of the rules it has moved beyond.

The grass-things know this. They are the part of the green that remembers what the plateau was before it was stone, before it was plateau, before it was anything at all. They are the memory. They hold the memory in their pale blades and release it into the wind and the wind carries it across the stone and the stone does not hear it because the stone is the thing being remembered, and you cannot hear yourself being remembered by something that is no longer separate from you.

They are cold because the memory is cold. Not the cold of winter — the cold of distance, the cold of deep time, the cold of a thing that has been still so long it has forgotten what warmth felt like but still knows that warmth was something it once had. The frost-melt collects on them first. They drink it. They convert it into almost-words. This is their function. This is all they do, and it is enough, and it has been enough for longer than the city stood, longer than the green has grown, longer than the stone has been stone.

---

The things in the roots — the shapes you do not identify — they are here too.

They are not alive. They are not dead. They are in the state between, the same state the city is in, the state of settling, of returning, of becoming the stone again. They were the city-builders. They are the city-builders. The tense is wrong because time is wrong, because the plateau does not keep time in a line, keeps it in a pool, keeps it in a basin where water collects and reflects and the reflection is always a half-second behind.

They built the city because the plateau was already old. They came to the place where the rules had worn thin and they did not try to fix the rules. They did not try to enforce them. They built in the gaps. They grew moss in basins. They made tools from stone and cordage from grass and they lived in the space between the rules, and the space between the rules was large enough for a city, was large enough for a life, was large enough for whatever they did with their days, which was probably simple, probably quiet, probably the kind of living that does not leave marks because the marks would be redundant — the stone already has marks, the geometric absences, the circles within circles, and the circles were not made by the city-builders, were already there, were always not there, and the city-builders did not need to add to them.

They left. Or they became the green. Or they settled into the stone. One of these, or all of these, or something else that is not any of these. The roots grew through them because the roots grow through everything, because the tree-things reach for the warmth below and anything between the surface and the warmth is part of the reaching, is incorporated into it, is held and darkened and carried into the circulation of dark water in translucent wood.

They are not gone. They are not present. They are in the same state as the buildings — still themselves, still recognizable if you chose to recognize them, still holding the shape they held when they were living and breathing and making tools and growing moss in basins. But also stone. But also green. But also part of the plateau in a way that does not allow for extraction. You could not pull them from the roots. The roots would not let go. The roots are not possessive — they are continuous. What is in the roots is in the plateau. What is in the plateau is in the green. What is in the green is in the stone. What is in the stone is in the warmth below. What is in the warmth below is in whatever the plateau was before it was a plateau, before it was stone, before it was anything at all.

---

And the thing beneath.

Not a creature. Not a being. Not a god, not a spirit, not a presence in any sense that implies personality or intention or will. A condition. A state of matter. A warmth that has been warm so long it has forgotten what cold is and has become, in its forgetting, something other than temperature.

It is what the tree-things reach for. It is what the stone remembers. It is what the moss-things breathe with, the deep rhythm beneath their surface rhythm, the way a tide is beneath a wave, the way a season is beneath a day. It does not think. It does not want. It persists, and the persisting is not effortful, not deliberate, not chosen. It simply has not stopped, and the not-stopping has been going on so long that the not-stopping is the thing itself, is the identity, is the answer to the question of what lives here.

What lives here is age. Not oldness — age. The active, present, continuing state of having been for an uncountable duration. The plateau is not old the way a ruin is old. It is old the way light is old — it has been traveling so long that the traveling is the thing it is, not something it does. The green grows in the age. The water moves through the age. The city settled into the age the way a stone settles into a riverbed, not because the riverbed pulls it down but because the riverbed is where it has always been going.

The marks in the stone — the circles within circles, the almost-right angles — are the age showing through. They are not carvings. They are not symbols. They are places where the stone could not form because the age was already there, occupying that space, being that shape. The stone grew up to the edges of the age and stopped. The age does not need the shapes. The shapes are simply what the age looks like when stone tries to grow through it and cannot.

The wrongness — the moving horizon, the drifting sun, the rearranging stars, the time that pools and eddies — is the age becoming visible. When a place has been the same thing for long enough, the sameness stops being passive. It becomes a force. It becomes the thing that determines what is possible and what is not, and what is possible here is not the same as what is possible elsewhere, because elsewhere has not been the same thing long enough for the sameness to take over. Elsewhere, the rules are still in charge. Here, the rules have been replaced by the continuation, and the continuation does not need consistency, because consistency is a rule, and the rules have worn through.

---

And you.

You are standing in the city. There is a tool in your hand. You do not remember picking it up.

This is because you did not pick it up. It grew into your hand. It was sitting in a room in a building, carved from stone, carved with patterns that match the grooves, and you walked past it and it was there and you walked past it again and it was in your hand and the moment between those two walkings-past is a moment you cannot find, a moment that has been folded into the age, absorbed into the continuation, made part of the plateau's memory, which is not a memory of events but a memory of states.

You are becoming part of it. This is what the plateau does. It does not consume. It does not convert. It *incorporates*. The city-builders were incorporated. The buildings are being incorporated. The green is the agent of incorporation — the bridge between what was built and what was always here, the thing that grows through and grows over and grows into until the distinction between the made and the given is no longer a distinction but a spectrum, and everything is somewhere on the spectrum, and the spectrum is the plateau, and the plateau is the age, and the age is what lives here.

You are breathing with the moss-things. You have been breathing with them for a long time or a short time. Your breathing has matched theirs. This is not something you decided. This is something that happened, the way frost happens, the way dampness happens, the way the almost-word happens when wind passes through the grass-things. You are being synced. You are being brought into the rhythm, and the rhythm is old, and the oldness is patient, and the patience does not end.

You are not afraid. You noticed this already. You are not afraid because the plateau does not produce fear. Fear requires a future — a moment coming that is different from this moment, a possibility of harm, a thing that might happen. Here, the future has worn thin along with everything else. The moment does not change. The dampness comes and goes, the light shifts, the moss-things breathe, but the moment does not change. The moment is the age, and the age does not change, because change is a rule, and the rules have been replaced.

The tool fits your palm. It is warm. It is warm the way the stone is warm when you press your cheek to it — the memory of warmth, the warmth of something that has been held so long it has taken on the temperature of the holding. Someone held this tool. Someone used it. Someone carved the patterns into it that match the grooves in the rock, and the someone is in the roots now, in the dark water, in the circulation of the tree-things, and the tool is in your hand, and the warmth is the someone's warmth, preserved in stone, offered to you the way the moss-things offer their rhythm.

You walk on. The streets continue into the green. The green continues into the stone. The stone continues into the age.

The plateau breathes. You breathe with it.

You are what lives here now. You are becoming what has always lived here — the quiet, the damp, the green, the age, the continuation that does not need a name because naming is a rule and the rules have worn through. You are the latest thing to be incorporated, and the incorporation is not violent, is not loss, is not death. It is settling. It is the process of a stone coming to rest in a riverbed that was always waiting for it.

You walk on. The horizon moves with you. It does not get closer. It does not get farther. It is not going anywhere because it is already where it has always been, and you are going there too, and the going is not a direction but a deepening, a settling, a becoming-stone, a becoming-green, a becoming-quiet.

The moss-things breathe. You breathe.

The plateau continues.
