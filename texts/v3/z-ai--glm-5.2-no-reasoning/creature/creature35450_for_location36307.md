# What Lives on the High Canopy Plateau of Ythel

## The Keepers of the Finished World

They do not call themselves anything.

This is the first thing to understand, and it is the thing that outsiders find most difficult, because outsiders need names the way the lowlands need rivers — as something to follow, something that moves through a place and gives it direction. But the people of Ythel have lived for longer than memory holds in a place that does not move, and the name has dissolved out of them the way salt dissolves into warm water. There were names once, probably. There were probably many things once. But the plateau is very old, and it teaches what it knows, and what it knows is persistence without assertion.

If you must call them something — and outsiders always must — you could call them the root-people, or the moss-walkers, or the ones-who-live-where-the-ground-breathes. None of these would be quite right. They would not object to any of them. They would not object to anything, particularly. Objection requires a posture toward the world that the plateau has spent a very long time wearing out of them.

## What They Look Like

Start with the hands.

Their hands are broad, thick-fingered, the palms calloused in specific patterns that correspond to nothing in the lowland world. The calluses run along the outer edge of the palm, across the mount of the thumb, and in a strange raised ridge across the center of each finger's pad. These are the marks of a lifetime spent gripping root-fiber, tearing compressed humus apart, pressing cut blocks into place in living walls. The calluses are dark — darker than the surrounding skin, which is itself dark, tanned by the diffused light and the constant damp into a warm umber that is nearly the color of the vegetation they walk on.

Their skin is dark and their hair is dark and their eyes are dark, and the darkness is not the darkness of any particular lineage but the darkness of adaptation. They are small people. Not stunted — they are well-formed, well-proportioned, healthy — but compact. The women average perhaps a hundred and forty centimeters. The men are not much taller. Their bodies are dense, their movements deliberate and economical, their center of gravity low, and when they walk across the plateau surface they move with a gait that looks at first like a waddle but is in fact something very precise. They are feeling the ground with their feet. Each step is a test, a reading, a question asked of the surface and answered by the give of the root mat. They walk the way a person walks on ice who has spent a life on ice — not carefully, but *knowledgeably*, the care so deeply embedded it no longer looks like care.

Their faces are wide and flat across the cheekbones, their features small, their mouths generous but closed more often than open. They smile, but not the way lowlanders smile — not to greet, not to bridge distance. They smile when they are amused, when they are resting, when the light does the thing at dusk where it turns the whole plateau the color of a bruised peach, and then the smile comes like a reflex, like breathing, and passes.

Their eyes are the thing you notice second, after the hands. The eyes are large, dark, and very still. They do not dart. They do not scan. They rest on things with a quality of attention that is almost physical, as if the gaze had weight. When a person of the plateau looks at you, you feel looked at. Not examined. Not assessed. Simply seen, with a completeness that is unnerving, because it does not seem to require anything from you in return.

They wear what the plateau provides. Woven mats of dried leaf-strips, draped over the shoulders and tied at the waist with braided cord. Rain-cloaks of layered broad leaves, their waxy surfaces shedding water. In the tree-stands, where the vegetation is taller, they harvest a finer fiber from the small trunks and weave it into garments that are softer, more fitted, almost like cloth — rough cloth, unfinished cloth, but cloth nonetheless, with a texture like raw linen and a color like undyed hemp. The garments are not decorated. There is no ornamentation. No beads, no dye-work, no patterning. The plateau does not encourage decoration. It is too uniform. Decoration requires contrast, and there is no contrast here.

Their feet are bare, always. The root mat is warm, and bare feet read it best. They have a saying, or rather a gesture — because much of what they communicate is gestural, a language of posture and glance that supplements their sparse speech — a gesture that means something like *shod feet are blind*. To walk in shoes on the plateau is to be deaf. To walk barefoot is to hear the ground, and the ground speaks, and the ground says where to step and where not to step, and the people listen, and they live.

## How They Are Built

A person of Ythel is built the way the plateau is built — slowly, in layers, from the materials at hand.

A child here is born in a shelter, in the warm dark, on a bed of woven mats laid over the living root-fiber. The mother is attended by one or two others — not specialists, not midwives, simply people who have been present at births before and whose presence is requested because presence is what birth needs. The child emerges into a world that is warm and dark and humming with the insect-drone, and the first thing it learns, before language, before sight, before anything we would call knowledge, is the vibration of the plateau. The drone enters the bones. The warmth enters the skin. The child is held against the living ground, and the ground holds it back, and this is the first relationship — the first and the deepest and the one that never breaks.

Childhood on Ythel is not a distinct phase. There is no marker of adulthood, no ritual of passage, no moment when a child becomes something else. The children simply grow, and as they grow they learn, and the learning is not separated from the living. A child who follows its mother across the plateau learns the surface by walking it. A child who watches its father cut root-fiber blocks learns by watching. There is no instruction, because instruction implies a hierarchy of knowledge that the plateau does not support. The plateau teaches by being the plateau. The people teach by being on the plateau. The children learn by being there.

They learn the surface first. Where it holds and where it gives. Where the basins are, and which are deep and which are shallow. Where the seasonal pools form and when. They learn the smell of the plateau — the sweet tannic smell of decomposition, the green smell of crushed stems, the damp mineral smell of standing water — and they learn to read the smell the way a sailor reads the wind. The smell shifts before the rain comes. The smell shifts when a predator has passed. The smell shifts at the edges, where the plateau drops away and the air changes.

They learn the insects. Not the species — they have no taxonomy, no system of classification — but the insects themselves, as individuals of a kind. This one is edible. This one is not. This one bites, though most do not. This one's shell can be ground and used as a pigment. This one emerges only at the transitions of dawn and dusk, and its emergence is a clock, a timekeeper, a way of knowing the hour without sun or shadow, because the plateau's light is shadowless and the sun's position is no use as a clock.

They learn the beetles. The great slow palm-sized beetles, the engineers of the interior, the ones whose tunnels riddle the substrate. A child learns to find the tunnel entrances, to read the processions, to know which way a beetle column is moving and how long it has been on the surface. The beetles are not domesticated. They are not pets. But they are known, in the way that the root mat is known — as a feature of the world so fundamental that not knowing them would be like not knowing the ground.

By the time a child is tall enough to see over the vegetation — which is not very tall, but the vegetation is all there is — the child knows the plateau. Not all of it. No one knows all of it. But the part that it knows, it knows as it knows its own body. As the inside of its own mouth. As the back of its own hand.

## What They Carry

Very little.

The people of Ythel do not accumulate. They do not keep stores, except for short periods — a few days of dried insects, a cache of cutting tools wrapped in leaves, a supply of dried stem-cord. These are kept near the shelters, in hollows in the root mat or in shallow pits lined with stone, and they are used and replaced and used and replaced, and the notion of keeping something for its own sake, of owning a thing beyond its use, does not occur to them.

Each person carries a knife. It is their primary tool and their most constant possession, and the relationship between a person and their knife is the closest thing the plateau offers to a bond with an object. The knife is a chipped basalt blade, perhaps a hand-span long, fitted to a handle of wrapped root-fiber. It is not beautiful. It is not decorated. But it is made with care — with the understanding that the edge must hold against the dense, fibrous material of the plateau, that the handle must fit the hand, that the balance must be right for the work of cutting and prying and shaping. A knife is made by the person who will carry it, or by someone who loves them, and when a person dies, their knife is placed in their shelter and the shelter is left to be swallowed by the plateau, and the knife goes into the ground and is incorporated, and that is the end of it.

Each person carries a basket. Woven, deep, worn soft with use. For insects, for edible stems, for whatever the day provides. The baskets are repaired constantly — new strips woven in, old frayed edges trimmed — and a basket that is well-maintained will last for years. A basket that is neglected will fray and open and the person who carries it will find their insects escaping and will mend it or will not, and the plateau will not care.

Each person carries a fire-kit. A flat stone, a piece of dry root-fiber for tinder, a length of dried cord for a bow-drill. Fire is precious and the kit is maintained with more attention than anything else. The fire-stone is the one object a person of Ythel will never willingly set down. It is kept in a pouch of woven leaf, worn against the body, and it is the first thing checked in the morning and the last thing checked at night. Without fire, there is no cooking. Without cooking, there are only raw insects and raw stems, and while these will sustain life, they will not sustain it well, and the people of Ythel are not so reduced as that. They cook. They eat together. The fire matters.

Some carry cord. Braided, strong, made from the inner bark of the rhizome stems. It is used for tying, for binding, for the construction of shelters and the repair of baskets and the making of snares. A length of cord is useful, and a person who has extra will give it to a person who has none, and this is not generosity. It is simply what happens. You have more than you need. Someone needs. You give. The plateau provides, and what the plateau provides moves through the people the way water moves through the root mat — by gravity, by path of least resistance, by the simple physics of surplus seeking deficit.

## The Shape of a Life

A person of Ythel is born, and grows, and works, and rests, and walks, and eats, and sleeps, and one day they are old and then they are gone.

The working is not what a lowlander would call work. There is no surplus to produce, no market to supply, no employer to satisfy. The work is the work of living: cutting blocks for shelters, maintaining walls, collecting food, tending fires, walking the distances between one place and another. The walking is the largest part. The plateau is vast and the people are scattered, and visiting — which they do, occasionally, when the mood takes them, when the light at dusk has been particularly fine, when the insects have been particularly thick and the surplus invites sharing — requires hours of walking across the featureless surface. They walk and walk and the drone accompanies them and the surface holds and they arrive where they intended to arrive, because the plateau is navigable by its subtle features, the rises and falls and basins and ridges, which are invisible to the outsider but are as distinct to the people of Ythel as streets are to a city-dwweller.

A day has no rigid structure. The people do not count hours, and the plateau's light does not provide sharp markers, but there are rhythms. Morning: the condensation, the dripping, the first stirring of the insects into full voice. Midday: the haze, the stillness, the long flat light. Afternoon: the rain, often, or the threat of rain, the dark columns visible approaching across the surface. Dusk: the color, the shifting of the drone, the thumping of the amphibians in the pools. Night: the dimness, the small fires, the stillness of the surface and the movement beneath it, the rustling of the nocturnal mammals, the deep organic silence that is not silence but the plateau's slow breathing made audible.

In the morning a person might repair their shelter — pulling new root-fiber into the gaps where the blocks have shifted, training the growth, shaping the living wall. Or they might walk to a basin and collect water, carrying it in a woven container lined with broad leaves. Or they might sweep their basket through the low vegetation, gathering insects, which they will bring back and roast on their fire-stone.

In the afternoon, if it rains, they shelter. The rain on the plateau is a full-body experience — the sound of it, the feel of it, the visible approach of it across the flat surface. They watch it come and they retreat under their woven roofs and they sit and the rain falls and the root mat absorbs it and the ground swells and they wait. When it passes, they emerge. The surface is wet and the insects are muffled and the air is clean in a way that it is not at other times, and they stand in the aftermath and breathe.

In the evening they eat. This is the social moment, if there is one. A family group — three or four people, perhaps five or six, the number is not fixed — gathers around the fire-pit and roasts insects and splits stems and eats. They eat slowly. The food is not abundant and it is not varied and it requires attention to prepare, and the attention is given, and the eating takes time. They talk, a little. Their speech is sparse, low, pitched beneath the insect-drone, a rumble of vowels and soft consonants that is nearly indistinguishable from the sounds of the plateau itself. They do not tell stories. Or if they do, the stories are not stories in the way a lowlander would understand them — not narratives with beginnings and middles and ends, but recitations, descriptions, the flat statement of things that have happened: the rain came from the south. The beetle column moved toward the basin. The heron-thing took a finch at the second pool. The child walked to the ridge and back. That is enough. That is what happened. There is no need for the machinery of story.

At night they sleep, and the plateau breathes around them, and the walls of their shelter grow, and the insects drone, and the amphibians thump, and the fire dies, and they are held in the warm dark of the living world like seeds in a pod.

## How They Are With Each Other

They are not demonstrative.

The people of Ythel do not touch each other casually, the way lowlanders do — the hand on the shoulder, the brush of the arm, the constant small contacts that pass for affection in places where the body is a source of warmth and comfort. On the plateau, the body is a tool for living on the plateau, and its warmth is its own, and its comfort is its own. Touch here is deliberate. When they touch, it is because something needs to be communicated that cannot be communicated otherwise, and the communication is precise: a hand on the wrist to say *come*. A palm on the back to say *rest*. A head pressed to another head, briefly, to say something that has no words in any language, something that has to do with being in a place together and knowing it.

They are not cold. This is important to understand. The people of Ythel are not distant or unfeeling or autistic, in the way that outsiders sometimes label peoples who do not perform emotion in the expected registers. They feel. They feel intensely. But the feelings are not the point, and the expression of them is not the work, and the plateau has taught them — has spent a thousand years teaching them — that the feeling does not need to be shown to be real. It simply needs to be. Like the ground. Like the drone.

They love. They love the way the plateau loves, which is to say without insistence, without demand, without the frantic need to be loved back that characterizes love in places where the world does not hold you. The plateau holds them. The ground is warm. The root mat grows around them. Their shelters are alive. What more do they need? What more could love provide that the plateau does not already give?

When someone dies, the others gather. Not in haste. They come when they come, walking across the surface, arriving at the shelter where the body lies on its woven mat. They sit. They do not speak. The fire is kept. The insects drone. The body is there, and it is cooling, and the warmth that the plateau gave it is returning to the air, and the people sit with it, and the sitting is the ritual. There is no ceremony. No words. No priest, no chant, no prescribed action. Just the sitting, for as long as the sitting lasts, which is until the people feel that the sitting is done, and then they rise, and they wrap the body in its mats, and they place it in the shelter, and they leave.

The shelter is left. The root-fiber blocks, still alive, grow inward. The walls close. The roof settles. The body within is incorporated — not quickly, not violently, but in the slow patient way that the plateau does everything. The decomposers come. The mycelia thread through. The root mat absorbs. The person becomes the plateau, and the plateau goes on, and this is not death, in the way the lowlands understand death. This is return. This is completion. The plateau holds everything it has ever held, and the person is there, in it, in the warm dark, in the living fiber, and the drone continues.

## What They Know

The people of Ythel know what the plateau knows.

This sounds simple, but it is not. The plateau knows a great deal. It knows the weather, which comes from the west, mostly, and arrives in the afternoon, and deposits its water in patterns that repeat but do not repeat exactly, the way a phrase of music repeats but does not repeat exactly. It knows the insects, which emerge and die in cycles so dense and so constant that the individual is nothing and the population is everything and the population is the sound and the sound is the plateau's voice. It knows the root mat, which is itself, and which grows and decomposes and grows in a cycle so long that a human life is a brief flash within it, a single note in a sustained chord.

The people know these things. Not as facts. Not as data. As experience, as the body knows the body, as the hand knows the hand. A person of Ythel, asked about the root mat, would not tell you its depth or its age or its composition. They would tell you what it feels like to walk on it. They would tell you that it is warm in the morning and cooler at midday and warm again at dusk. They would tell you that it gives slightly underfoot, and that the give is different in different places, and that the difference is a language, and that the language says *here is solid* and *here is soft* and *here is a tunnel* and *here is water beneath the surface*. They would tell you these things, and the telling would be not a description but a transmission, because the knowledge is not in the words but in the walking, and if you have not walked it, the words are shadows of a thing you have not felt.

They know the lowlands. Some of them have been there. The climb down takes two days, and the world changes — the air thickens, the trees grow tall, the ground appears, hard and dead and silent. The lowlands are loud in a different way: birds and wind and running water, the mechanical noise of the lowland settlements, the clatter of a world that has not reached its rest. The people who go down stand on solid ground and feel the silence — no, the *wrongness* of the ground, the deadness of it, the absence of the breathing warmth — and they are uncomfortable, and they climb back up, and the two days of ascent feel like returning to something that they did not know they had left.

But they do not go often. The lowlands have things they need: the basalt for their tools, which does not exist on the plateau. Occasionally other materials — a piece of metal, traded for, because they know of metal even if they do not work it. A curiosity. A thing that has no use but is interesting, and the people of Ythel are not above interest, and a thing that is interesting is a thing that is interesting, and they will carry it back up the three-day climb and set it in their shelter and look at it sometimes and that is enough.

They do not bring ideas back. Or rather, the ideas they bring do not survive the plateau. The lowland ideas — about progress, about growth, about accumulation, about the future — die on the plateau the way foreign seeds die on the root mat. Smothered. Not by violence but by sheer biological indifference. The plateau is too complete. The plateau has solved the problems that the lowlands are still working on, and the solutions are not transferable, because the solutions are the plateau itself, and you cannot take the plateau with you. You can only be on it.

## The Old Woman at the Basin

I want to tell you about a specific person, because the general has become too general, and the people of Ythel are not general. They are specific. They are specific the way the plateau is specific — not in its uniformity but in its existence, in the sheer particular fact of being here, on this surface, at this elevation, in this light, in this sound.

There is a woman who lives near the third basin from the plateau's eastern edge. She is old — how old, I cannot say, and she could not say, because the plateau does not measure years and the people do not count them. Her skin is deeply creased, the lines running in patterns that follow the expression of her face over a lifetime — the creases at the corners of her eyes from squinting against the hazy light, the lines at the corners of her mouth from the closed-lipped rest that is the face's default state here. Her hands are enormous for her size, the fingers knotted at the joints, the calluses thick and dark, and when she presses her palm to the root mat, which she does often, absently, the way a person might rest a hand on a table, the contact is total. The hand and the ground are the same color. The hand and the ground are the same temperature.

She lives alone. This is not unusual on the plateau. Family groups form and dissolve, and the people come together and separate with a fluidity that would be chaos in a lowland society and is simply life here. She had a family — I know this because she mentions them sometimes, in her sparse low rumble of speech. A man, or what the lowlands would call a man, though the plateau's gender categories are not the lowland's categories. A child. The child grew and walked away one morning and did not come back, or came back years later with the quiet of a person who has been elsewhere and is now here again, and she gave them food and they ate and they left again and this was not loss. This was the plateau. People walk on the plateau. The surface holds them and then it does not and then it holds others.

She builds her walls. She tends her fire. She sweeps her basket through the vegetation in the late morning, when the insects are thick and sluggish with the warmth, and she roasts them on her fire-stone and eats them with her fingers and the grease runs down her wrists and she wipes it on her woven cloak and the cloak is dark with old grease and it does not matter. She drinks from the basin, the water dark and tannic and warm, and she knows which part of the basin to drink from — the edge, where the root mat filters the water through its fiber — and which part to avoid. She has drunk from this basin for longer than she can remember, and the basin has been here for longer than the plateau can remember, and the water is the same water, cycling through the air and the root mat and the pool and the air again, and she is part of the cycle and the cycle does not need her and she does not need the cycle to need her.

In the evening she sits by her fire and the light fades and the drone shifts and the amphibians begin their thumping in the basin and she listens. She does not look at the fire. She looks at the surface, stretching away, flat and green and endless, and the color drains out of it as the light goes and it becomes gray and then darker gray and then the dimness that is night on the plateau, and the insects quiet and the thumping rises and she is there, and the fire is there, and the ground is warm beneath her, and she is part of the plateau and the plateau is part of the drone and the drone is part of the night and the night goes on.

She does not wonder what it means. She does not wonder what it is for. She is not a person who asks those questions, because the plateau does not ask those questions, and the plateau has been here for a very long time, and the plateau is what it is, and she is what she is, and the fire dies and she goes inside and the shelter breathes around her and she sleeps.

## The Child Who Walked to the Edge

There was a child — there is always a child, on the plateau, because the people persist and the people reproduce and the children come and the plateau holds them — who walked to the edge of the plateau when they were young enough to do such a thing without purpose, simply because the edge was there and walking was what you did on the plateau.

The edge is where the plateau falls away. Not a cliff — the edges are soft, draped with moss and hanging vegetation, the old volcanic rock crumbling beneath its living cover — but a drop nonetheless, a steep decline where the root mat thins and the stone appears and the world changes. Below the edge, the lowland forests. The trees. The ground. The silence.

The child stood at the edge and looked down. They had never seen the lowlands. The world below was foreign — the hard ground, the tall trees, the open sky, the absence of the drone, which they had never heard as a separate thing because it had been part of their body since before their body was their body. They stood at the edge and they felt the wrongness rising from below, the dead air, the silence, the ground that was not breathing, and they turned and walked back.

They did not tell anyone. There was nothing to tell. They had walked to the edge and the edge was there and they had walked back. The plateau held them and they were on it and the drone continued and the child grew and the edge was still there and the child did not go back. Not because they were afraid. Not because they were forbidden. Because the edge was where the plateau ended, and the plateau was where they were, and they were where the plateau was, and the distinction was not a distinction that required action.

This is what the plateau teaches. This is what the people learn. Not a doctrine. Not a belief. A position. A relationship to the world that says: *I am here. This is here. We are here together. The here goes on. I will be here and then I will not and the here will still be here and this is not sad. This is what it is. This is the plateau.*

## What They Are Not

They are not primitives.

This must be said, because the temptation to say it is strong, and the temptation comes from the lowlands, where complexity is measured by accumulation and sophistication is measured by surplus. The people of Ythel have no metal, no pottery, no weaving beyond the rough and the functional. Their structures are small. Their tools are stone. Their population is tiny. By any lowland measure, they are at the bottom of a scale that the lowlands have been climbing for ten thousand years.

But the scale is wrong. The people of Ythel are not at the bottom of anything. They are at the top of something — a long, slow, patient climb that the plateau has been making since before there were people, and the people have joined the climb, and the climb has reached its summit, and the view from the summit is flat and green and warm and the drone never stops.

Their knowledge is not less than a lowlander's knowledge. It is different. A lowlander knows the names of a thousand things and the uses of a hundred tools and the history of a dozen peoples and the principles of a score of technologies. A person of Ythel knows the feel of the root mat in the morning and the sound of the insects at midday and the smell of the rain before it comes and the taste of the water at the basin's edge and the weight of their own body on the living surface and the warmth that rises from the ground into the soles of their bare feet and up through their bones and into the center of their being where it sits, quiet, patient, complete.

This is not less. This is other. And the people of Ythel, if they thought about the lowlands at all, which they mostly do not, would not say that the lowlands are wrong. They would say that the lowlands are young. That the lowlands are still working on their problem, the way the plateau worked on its problem for a thousand millennia, and that the lowlands will solve it or will not, and that the solution, if it comes, will look like the plateau — flat, and warm, and still, and finished, and alive.

## What Lives Here

What lives on the High Canopy Plateau of Ythel is this:

A people who are not a people in the way the lowlands mean people. Not a tribe, not a nation, not a culture in the sense of a shared project of accumulation and identity. A people who are the plateau's people, the way the beetles are the plateau's beetles and the lizards are the plateau's lizards — not owned, not possessed, but of. Of the plateau. Born of it, fed by it, held by it, returned to it. A people whose population is small because the plateau does not need them to be large, whose technology is simple because the plateau does not need them to be complex, whose lives are quiet because the plateau does not need them to be loud.

And the plateau itself, which lives, and drones, and breathes, and holds, and goes on, and goes on, and goes on, in the warm and living dark beneath their bare feet.
