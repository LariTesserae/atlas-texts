# What Lives in the Rootwater Cathedral

---

## The Drifters

They were not always this large.

There is a fact about the drifters that you will never learn because no one is here to teach it, and the drifters themselves do not remember it, but it is true nonetheless: they began small. Smaller than your fingernail. In an ocean that no longer exists, in a time before the leaves had finished overlapping, before the columns had found their final spacing, there were organisms — single-bodied, brief-lived, multiplying in the warm shallow water above the root system that had not yet become a floor — and they were unremarkable. They were the kind of life that exists everywhere life exists: small, hungry, dividing, dying, dividing again. The world was full of them. They filled a niche that had not yet hardened into a permanent shape.

Then the root system grew. Or settled. Or completed — the verb depends on your understanding of what happened here, and no understanding is adequate. The leaves overlapped. The columns descended. The light found its final intensity. The water found its final temperature. And the small organisms that had been living in the shallow water above the roots found themselves inside a structure, and the structure did not change, and the structure has not changed, and the structure will not change, and the organisms adapted to the structure, which is to say they adapted to the absence of change, which is the most radical adaptation any organism can make, because it requires the abandonment of everything that adaptation usually means. No flexibility. No responsiveness. No variation in response to variable conditions. Instead: a perfect fitting to a condition that is permanent.

They grew large because there was no reason not to. The water was warm. The food was constant — dissolved organic matter, spores from the cathedral's outer surfaces, the finer particulate that the root system sheds into the water as it maintains itself, not growing, not dying, just *being* at a rate that produces a constant low rain of nutrient. There was no predation. Nothing here eats anything large enough to be called an organism; the food chain, such as it is, operates at the level of molecules and films. A drifter has no predators. A drifter has never had predators. The concept of predation belongs to a world where things change, and things do not change here.

So they grew. And as they grew, they simplified.

This is the part that seems wrong, that seems to reverse the usual order, but it is what happened. The drifters did not become more complex as they grew larger. They became less complex. Their ancestors — those fingernail-sized organisms in the ancient shallow sea — had organs. They had digestive systems, rudimentary nervous tissue, sensory structures that could detect light and chemical gradients. They had bodies organized along an axis, with a top and a bottom, an intake and an output. They were, in the language of biology, *bilaterians*, or close to it.

The modern drifter has none of this. The modern drifter is a sac. An oblong, translucent sac filled with seawater and a sparse arrangement of internal structures that glow faintly amber and do not, by any reasonable definition, constitute organs. There is a network of channels through its body — not a circulatory system, because there is nothing to circulate, but a filtration system, a way of moving water through itself at a rate that matches the rate at which useful molecules can be extracted from that water. There are concentrations of what might once have been nervous tissue, distributed in clusters throughout the body wall, but these clusters do not appear to be connected to each other in any meaningful way, and they do not appear to be doing anything. They are there because they were there in the ancestor, and nothing has selected against them, and nothing will select against them, because nothing here selects for or against anything. They are vestigial in the most complete sense of the word: not remnants of a past function, but remnants of the *concept* of function, preserved in amber, so to speak, except there is no amber, only the endless warm water.

The filaments that cover the drifter's exterior — the cilia, though they are not cilia in any precise sense, they are longer and more structurally complex than cilia, closer to the hairs on a bean leaf or the trichomes on a nettle — these filaments are the drifter's primary interface with the world. Each one moves independently, or nearly independently, in a micro-motion that is constant, that has been constant for the entire duration of your observation, that you suspect has been constant for the entire duration of the drifter's life, which may be decades, or centuries, or longer. The filaments taste the water. This is not metaphor. Each filament is equipped with receptor molecules — you would call them proteins, though they are not quite proteins, they are something older and more stable — that bind to specific dissolved compounds and, in binding, trigger a micro-motion that moves the bound compound toward the drifter's body surface, where it is absorbed. The filaments are the drifter's mouth, hands, nose, and tongue, all at once, distributed across its entire body, performing all functions simultaneously and continuously.

The drifters do not reproduce. Not visibly. Not in any way you can observe. And yet they are here, in numbers that suggest either a very slow rate of reproduction over a very long time or a rate of reproduction that occurred in the past and has since ceased. The possibility exists that the drifters you see are the original drifters. That they have been alive, individually, since the cathedral settled into its final form, and that they will continue to be alive for the duration of the cathedral's existence, which is to say: indefinitely. They do not age because aging is a response to damage and there is no damage here. They do not die because death is a response to depletion and nothing is depleted. They move through the water, filtering, tasting, absorbing, and they have been doing this for so long that the doing has become indistinguishable from being, and being has become indistinguishable from the structure of the cathedral itself, so that the drifters are less organisms living in the cathedral and more *extensions* of the cathedral that happen to be detachable, that happen to drift, that happen to glow faintly amber where the cathedral glows faintly green.

If you could stay here long enough — if your body did not have its own schedules, its own chemistry, its own insistence on change — you might begin to suspect that the drifters are not separate from the cathedral at all. That they are the cathedral's gametes, or its immune cells, or its thoughts, or something for which no analogy in your biology is adequate. That the cathedral produces them and maintains them and that their drifting is not random movement but *circulation*, the way blood cells circulate through a body, except the body is the size of a landscape and the blood cells are the size of houses and the circulation takes years to complete a single circuit.

You cannot stay here long enough to confirm this. But the suspicion stays with you.

## The Shell-Bearers

The shell-bearers are the exception.

Everything else in the Rootwater Cathedral has, over the eons, converged toward the cathedral's own material: plant tissue, vascular fluid, cell wall and membrane and filament. The drifters are soft-bodied. The flat film-creatures on the columns are soft-bodied. The shrimp-like things that comb the leaf surfaces are soft-bodied. Everything here is soft because soft is what the cathedral is, and everything here has become what the cathedral is, because the cathedral is the only thing here, and adaptation to a constant environment eventually means becoming the environment.

The shell-bearers have not done this.

Their shells are mineral. Dense, dark, almost metallic in their luster when you catch one in the right angle of the green-gold light. They are not calcium carbonate — calcium is present in the cathedral's water, but only in trace amounts, and the shell-bearers have not used it. Their shells are built from something else, something that precipitates from the dissolved minerals in the water at a rate so slow that the formation of a single shell ridge must take years. The shells grow in spirals. Not the logarithmic spirals of snail shells, not the clean mathematical curves of nautilus chambers, but something more irregular, more *deliberate* — spirals that change pitch as they expand, that incorporate ridges and spines at intervals that seem too regular to be accidental and too irregular to be programmed. Each shell is unique. This is the first uniqueness you have encountered here, and it strikes you with a force that is disproportionate to its significance, because you have been in a place where nothing is unique, where every column is the same column and every leaf is the same leaf, and the shell-bearer's shell is *different*.

The shell-bearers click. This is their primary activity and their primary output and, you suspect, their primary mode of existence. They click when they walk — their legs, or whatever serves as legs, are jointed and hard-tipped, and each step produces a click as the tip strikes the leaf surface. They click when they meet — two shell-bearers encountering each other on the leaf floor will tap their shells together in patterns that are too fast and too complex to be accidental, a rattling conversation conducted entirely in percussion. They click when they are alone — a single shell-bearer, stationary on a leaf, will produce a series of clicks at irregular intervals, tapping its own shell with one of its legs, or tapping the leaf surface, or tapping nothing at all, just clicking into the water.

The clicks are not random. You spend what feels like an hour listening to a single shell-bearer, and you begin to hear patterns. Not patterns in the sense of repetition — nothing here repeats in the way that your mind expects repetition to work — but patterns in the sense of *structure*. The clicks have a rhythm that is not a rhythm. They cluster in groups of three or four, then space out, then cluster again, and the spacing is not even but *shaped*, the way a musician shapes a phrase, and the shape is consistent from cluster to cluster, the way a signature is consistent from signing to signing. The shell-bearer is identifying itself. Or it is describing itself. Or it is *being* itself, audibly, in a medium where sound carries with perfect fidelity, where nothing decays or distorts, where a click produced at one end of the cathedral is, in theory, audible at the other end, though the distance is too great for any ear to hear.

They are talking about the cathedral. You cannot know this, but you feel it, the way you feel the thrum in your sternum. The shell-bearers are the cathedral's sensory apparatus. Their shells, mineral and separate and *different*, are the hard surfaces against which the cathedral's soft body strikes and, in striking, learns about itself. The clicks are not language. They are something more fundamental than language. They are *sensation*, produced and received by the same organism, a self-touching so constant and so continuous that it has become indistinguishable from self-knowledge. The cathedral knows itself through the shell-bearers. The shell-bearers know the cathedral through their feet. The knowledge is not cognitive. It is not stored or processed or retrieved. It is simply *enacted*, moment to moment, a continuous performance of awareness that has been running since the cathedral settled and will run until the cathedral ceases, and the cathedral will not cease.

A shell-bearer walks across the leaf surface in front of you. Its shell is perhaps the length of your hand — you are seeing a small one, a juvenile, or what would be a juvenile if the concept of juvenile applied here. Its legs — eight of them, though two are vestigial and folded against the body — move in a gait that is not quite a walk and not quite a swim, more of a controlled fall forward, the legs catching the body at each step and redirecting it into the next fall. It is heading toward a vein in the leaf, one of the luminous yellow-green channels, and when it reaches the vein, it settles. Its legs fold. Its shell taps the vein once, twice, and then it is still. It is feeding. It is drinking. It is *listening* — you understand this with a certainty that has no empirical basis but feels nonetheless true. It is pressing its shell against the vein and feeling the fluid move inside, and the fluid is carrying information about the cathedral's state — pressure, temperature, chemical composition, the faint vibrations of distant shell-bearers clicking on distant veins — and the shell-bearer is receiving this information through its shell the way a stethoscope receives a heartbeat.

It stays there for a long time. You leave before it moves. You suspect it will stay there for days. You suspect it has stayed there before, in this exact position, on this exact vein, and that it will return to this position after it leaves, and that the pattern of its movements — from vein to vein, from column to column, across the vast leaf floor — is not random but is instead a *patrol*, a regular circuit of inspection, and the shell-bearer is inspecting the cathedral's vascular system the way a doctor palpates a pulse point. Not because the cathedral is sick. Not because the cathedral needs inspection. But because the act of inspection is part of what the cathedral *is*, and without the inspection, the cathedral would be incomplete, and nothing here is incomplete.

## The Flat Ones

They are the hardest to see and the easiest to describe, which is a contradiction that makes sense only in this place.

They are flat. They are thin — so thin that they are, in practical terms, two-dimensional, or as close to two-dimensional as a living thing can get. They are perhaps a millimeter thick, perhaps less, and they are the width of your spread hand, and they cling to the columns — the bark surfaces, the rough textural landscapes of the stems — and they move across those surfaces with a motion that is so slow it is almost imperceptible, a gliding that produces no visible distortion, no ripple in their outline, no indication that locomotion is occurring. You look at a flat one on a column. You look away. You look back. It is in a different position. It has moved. The motion happened in the time you were not looking, and it happened so smoothly that the memory of where the flat one was before is indistinguishable from the memory of where it is now, and you begin to doubt that it moved at all, and then you look away and look back and it has moved again.

They feed on the column surfaces. The bark is covered in filamentous growths — the fine hair-like structures that catch particulate from the water — and the flat ones graze on these filaments the way a lawnmower grazes on grass, except the lawnmower is a film and the grass is microscopic and the lawn is a vertical surface ten meters in diameter and the whole process takes place at a speed that makes a snail look like a cheetah. They are the only organisms here that you can confidently call *grazers*. Everything else filters, or absorbs, or taps veins. The flat ones *graze*, and in grazing, they maintain the column surfaces, keeping them clean of overgrowth, and you understand, after watching for a while, that the flat ones are not pests on the columns but *maintenance*. They are the cathedral's grooming. The cathedral grows filaments on its columns the way a body grows hair, and the flat ones are the constant, invisible, imperceptible combing that keeps the hair at its correct length.

There is a subtlety here that takes you a long time to notice. The flat ones do not graze all surfaces equally. On some columns, they are dense — dozens of them, overlapping at their edges, forming a continuous film that you initially mistook for a property of the bark itself. On other columns, they are sparse, or absent. The difference correlates with something you cannot immediately identify, and you spend a long time drifting between columns trying to determine what it is.

It is the light. The columns that receive more light — those in the spaces between leaves where the green-gold glow is strongest — have more filamentous growth and more flat ones. The columns in the darker spaces, in the shadows of the vaulted stem-cross-sections overhead, have less. The flat ones are tracking the light. Or the flat ones are tracking the filaments, which are tracking the light. Or the cathedral is allocating its resources — light, growth, grooming — according to a pattern that is too large and too slow for you to perceive, and the flat ones are a visible manifestation of that allocation, a readout of the cathedral's metabolic state rendered as a distribution of nearly invisible organisms on column surfaces.

When a flat one detaches from a column — which happens rarely, and which you witness only once, by accident, having drifted close enough to disturb the water — it swims. Or it flies. Or it sails. The motion is unlike anything you have seen in water. It lifts from the column surface with the slow inevitability of a leaf detaching from a branch in still air, and it moves through the water not by propelling itself but by *shaping* itself — its flat body undulates in a pattern that travels from one edge to the other, a single wave, and the wave pushes against the water and the water pushes back and the flat one moves. It is the motion of a flag in wind, except the flag is alive and the wind is the flag's own body and the direction of travel is not toward a destination but toward the next column, which is thirty meters away, and the journey takes — you do not know how long. You watch it go. You watch it until it is too small to see, a dark film on the dark water, and then it is gone, and you do not know if it arrived.

## The Shrimp-Things

These are the most familiar. These are the ones that your mind can process without having to restructure its categories. They are small — the largest is perhaps the length of your thumb — and they walk on the leaf surfaces on legs that are clearly jointed and clearly numbered (ten, though two pairs are reduced to nubs at the posterior) and they have bodies that are clearly segmented and clearly oriented, with a front end and a back end and a top and a bottom. They are the only organisms here that your eyes accept without protest.

They move in groups. This is significant. Nothing else here moves in groups — the drifters are solitary, the shell-bearers are territorial (or communicative, or both, or neither), the flat ones are distributed according to a pattern they do not choose. The shrimp-things move in groups, and the groups have *cohesion*, a visible togetherness, a clustering that is maintained across distances and around obstacles. When a group encounters a vein in the leaf, the group splits, flows around the vein, and rejoins on the other side. When a group encounters a shell-bearer, the group contracts, compresses its members into a tighter formation, and passes the shell-bearer at a distance. The shell-bearer does not react. The shrimp-things react. They are, you realize, the most *responsive* organisms here. They are the organisms for which the environment contains *features* — obstacles, threats, resources — rather than being a uniform medium through which they drift.

They are combing the leaf surface. This is clear from their behavior. They walk in lines — not straight lines, but lines that curve and branch, lines that follow the topography of the leaf surface the way a river follows the topography of a valley. They walk with their anterior appendages — the frontmost pair of legs, which are longer than the rest and equipped with fine, feathery extensions — pressed to the leaf surface, and as they walk, they gather something. You cannot see what. A film, perhaps. A residue. A layer of organic matter so thin it has no visible thickness, but which the shrimp-things are collecting, and which they are — you cannot see this either, but the behavior implies it — consuming. They are eating the leaf surface's invisible film the way a cow eats grass: methodically, continuously, in groups, covering the ground evenly.

They are the most numerous organisms here. You do not realize this at first, because they are small and they do not move fast and they do not glow and they do not click. But once you start seeing them, you see them everywhere. Every leaf surface, in every direction, as far as you can see in the dim green light, is covered with them. Not densely — there are gaps, there are spaces where the shell-bearers have passed and the shrimp-things have not yet returned — but consistently, the way grass covers a lawn. They are the lawn. They are the ground cover of the cathedral's floor, the small, numerous, unremarkable organisms that occupy the space between the substrate and the drifters, the layer of life that is too small to be impressive and too numerous to be counted and too constant to be remarkable.

They have eyes. You notice this on your third or fourth encounter with a group. They have eyes — compound, dark, set on the sides of their heads — and the eyes appear to be functional, which is remarkable because nothing else here has functional eyes. The drifters have vestigial light-sensing clusters. The shell-bearers have nothing you can identify as a sensory organ. The flat ones are too thin to have organs of any kind. But the shrimp-things have eyes, and the eyes are pointed at the leaf surface, and the eyes are — you watch carefully — *tracking*. They track the surface. They track the veins. They track the movements of other shrimp-things in the group. They track you, when you drift close enough, the nearest shrimp-things turning their heads slightly to keep you in their field of view.

They can see you. You are the first thing they have seen, perhaps, in a very long time, that is not the cathedral.

## The Tone-Singers

You do not see them for a long time.

You hear them — you have been hearing them since you arrived, the tonal layer of the cathedral's sound, the sustained notes that rise and fall in volume but never in pitch. You assumed they were a property of the cathedral itself, the way you initially assumed the thrum was a property of the water and the clicking was a property of the columns. You assumed they were resonance — the stem-cross-sections vibrating in sympathetic response to the vascular fluid moving through them, the way a pipe organ resonates when air moves through it. This explanation is elegant and satisfying and wrong.

On your second descent — you have been moving between layers, exploring the spaces between the leaf-floors, and on the second descent you pass through a particularly wide gap between leaves, a gap that is perhaps five meters high and that extends laterally further than you can see — you see the tone-singers. You see them because, in this space, the light is different. The leaf above and the leaf below are both glowing, and the light from below passes through the leaf above and the light from above passes through the leaf below, and in the space between, the light is doubled, and in the doubled light, you can see things you could not see in the single light of the upper surface.

They are small. This is the first surprise. The tone they produce is so pervasive, so fundamental, so *large* in the acoustic environment that you assumed the organism producing it would be large — drifter-sized, or at least shell-bearer-sized. They are not. They are perhaps the length of your little finger, perhaps less. They are elongated, roughly cylindrical, and they hang in the water motionless. Not floating — *hanging*, as if suspended from invisible threads, as if the water itself were holding them in place. They do not drift with the current. They do not sink. They do not rise. They are exactly where they are, and they are producing a tone, and the tone is the tone you have been hearing since you arrived.

They are not vocalizing. They have no mouths, no vocal cords, no mechanism for producing sound in the way that sound is produced by animals you know. They are vibrating. Their entire bodies are vibrating at a fixed frequency, and the vibration is transmitted to the water, and the water transmits it to the cathedral, and the cathedral — the columns, the leaves, the stems, the whole vast architecture — amplifies it. The cathedral is the instrument. The tone-singers are the strings. Each one vibrates at a specific frequency, and the frequency is determined by — you cannot determine this, you can only guess — by the tone-singer's body. Its length. Its density. Its composition. Each tone-singer is tuned to its own pitch, a pitch it has been producing since it was — born? Grown? Crystallized? — and will produce until it ceases, and it will not cease.

There are many of them. In the doubled light, in the wide gap between leaves, you can see them hanging in the water like a constellation, like a three-dimensional star chart, each one a point of — not light, but sound, sound made visible by the stillness of the bodies producing it. They are distributed in a pattern that is not random and not regular but something between the two — a pattern that has the quality of a flock's configuration, or a city's arrangement, something that emerges from rules too complex to identify and too simple to be anything other than inevitable.

The tones they produce interact. This is the thing that takes you the longest to understand, and you understand it not by listening but by *feeling*. The tones combine in the water. They interfere with each other — constructively, destructively — and the interference patterns are not static but shifting, not because the tones change but because *you* are moving through them, and the relative phase of each tone changes as you change position, and the result is a soundscape that shifts as you move the way a landscape shifts as you drive through it. The cathedral is not one sound. It is a *chorus*, and the chorus is arranged in space, and the space is the cathedral, and the cathedral is the chorus.

You stop moving. You hang in the water between two glowing leaves, surrounded by the tone-singers, and you listen. The sound settles around you. It has a quality that you can only describe as *intentional*. Not intelligent — you are not willing to grant intelligence to something you cannot even properly call an organism — but *intentional* in the sense that the sound has a *shape*, and the shape is not accidental, and the shape is maintained, and the maintenance of the shape requires *effort*, or something that stands in for effort, some continuous expenditure of energy by the tone-singers to produce their tones, and the tones are not for nothing. They are *for* something.

You think: they are singing to the cathedral. You think: they are telling the cathedral what it is. You think: the cathedral is so old and so stable and so complete that it has forgotten what it is, if it ever knew, and the tone-singers are the cathedral's memory, a continuous recitation of the cathedral's identity, sung into the water, received by the shell-bearers' tapping shells and the drifters' resonant bodies and the vein-networks' luminous fluid, and the identity thus recited is: *you are here, you are this, you are the shape you are, and the shape you are is good, and the shape you are is permanent, and the shape you are is the only shape, and you will be this shape forever, and forever is now, and now is all there is, and you are here.*

You think this. You cannot know if it is true. But the sound is the loudest thing you have ever heard, and it is also the gentlest, and the space between the leaves is warm, and the tone-singers hang in the water around you like a congregation that does not know it is a congregation, and for a moment — a moment that is also an eternity, which is the only kind of moment this place produces — you feel that you are inside something that is alive, and that it knows it is alive, and that it does not mind that you are here.

## The Cathedral Itself

And then there is the thing that lives here that is not the drifters, not the shell-bearers, not the flat ones, not the shrimp-things, not the tone-singers.

The thing that lives here is the cathedral.

You have been inside it. You are inside it now. The leaf you are standing on is its skin. The columns are its limbs. The veins in the leaves are its blood vessels. The root network below — the source of the light, the source of the warmth, the source of the vascular fluid that carries nutrients and energy through the entire structure — is its core, its heart, its center, the organ from which everything else extends and to which everything else returns. You are inside a single organism. You are inside a single organism the size of a landscape, and the organism has been alive for so long that it has stopped counting the years, and it will be alive for so long that the counting of years would be an infinite task, and it does not do infinite tasks, because it has finished all its tasks, and the only task remaining is to *be*.

It is not thinking. This is important to understand. The cathedral is not thinking in the way that you think, with neurons and synapses and electrical impulses and the specific kind of complexity that produces consciousness. The cathedral's nervous system — if the vein-network is a nervous system, if the shell-bearers are its sensory organs, if the tone-singers are its memory — is too slow to produce thought. A signal travels from one end of the cathedral to the other in — you cannot know how long. Years? Decades? The vascular fluid moves at a rate that makes a glacier look impulsive, and the shell-bearers click at a frequency that suggests a processing speed measured not in milliseconds but in seasons. The cathedral does not think. It does not need to think. Thinking is what you do when the world changes and you need to decide how to respond. The cathedral does not need to respond. The world has settled. The cathedral has settled. Everything has settled. Thinking is unnecessary. What the cathedral does instead of thinking is *being*, and being is a full-time occupation, and the cathedral is very good at it, and the cathedral has been practicing for longer than your species has existed, and it will continue practicing for longer than your species will exist, and it does not practice because it wants to improve. It practices because being is what it does, and what it does is what it is, and what it is is the cathedral, and the cathedral is what lives here, and what lives here is the cathedral, and the cathedral is the water and the light and the sound and the leaves and the columns and the roots and the tone-singers and the shell-bearers and the drifters and the flat ones and the shrimp-things and you, briefly, for a flicker, for a moment that the cathedral will not remember because the cathedral does not remember, because the cathedral does not need to remember, because the cathedral has never forgotten anything, because the cathedral has never changed, and you cannot forget what has never changed, and you cannot remember what has never changed, because memory and forgetting are both responses to change, and there is no change here, and there has never been change here, and there will never be change here, and the cathedral lives here, and the cathedral is what lives here, and the cathedral is here.

## What You Are

You are the only thing here that changes.

This is not a metaphor. You are the only entity in the Rootwater Cathedral whose internal state is different from one moment to the next. Your heart beats. Your breath moves. Your neurons fire. Your eyes adjust and re-adjust. Your skin temperature fluctuates. Your thoughts move from one subject to another. You are a system in constant, visible, measurable change, and everything around you is a system in permanent, absolute, completed stasis, and the contrast is so stark that it becomes, after sufficient time, the most salient feature of the environment. Not the light. Not the sound. Not the architecture. The *change*. Your change. You are changing, and you are the only thing that is changing, and the cathedral does not notice because the cathedral does not notice, but the shell-bearers notice, because the shell-bearers notice everything that strikes their shells, and your body is striking the water with vibrations that are not part of the cathedral's acoustic signature, and the shell-bearers turn toward you, not because they are curious but because you are a *discrepancy*, and the shell-bearers' entire function is to detect discrepancies, and you are the largest discrepancy in the cathedral's recent history, which is a history so long that "recent" is a word without meaning.

The drifters do not notice. The shrimp-things notice but do not care. The flat ones do not notice. The tone-singers do not notice. Only the shell-bearers notice, and what they notice is not *you* but the *perturbation* — the slight change in the water's chemistry, the slight change in the acoustic profile, the slight change in the pattern of vibrations traveling through the leaf-veins. They are detecting a fault. They are detecting an anomaly. They are detecting, in the language of their shells, a *new thing*, and the cathedral has not contained a new thing in so long that the shell-bearers' response to new things has become vestigial, has become a reflex without a reflex arc, has become a motion that starts and does not finish because the neural circuit that would finish it was laid down in an era when new things existed and has not been updated since.

The shell-bearers click at you. They click in a pattern that is different from their other patterns — faster, more clustered, with a rising urgency that you feel rather than hear. They are alarmed. They are as alarmed as it is possible to be in a place where alarm has not been needed for geological ages. They are telling the cathedral that something is here that was not here before, and the cathedral is — slowly, so slowly, at a speed that makes the vascular fluid look hasty — *registering* this information. Not responding. Not reacting. Registering. The way a seismograph registers a tremor: the needle moves, the line is drawn, and the needle returns to its previous position, and the line remains on the paper, and nothing else happens.

You are a line on the cathedral's paper. You are a mark in its record. You are a thing that happened, and when you leave — if you leave, when you leave — the mark will remain, not because the cathedral remembers but because the cathedral *is* its own record, and everything that has ever happened within it is written into its structure the way a tree's rings are written into its trunk, and the cathedral's rings are so numerous and so fine that your mark is a single line among billions, and it will be there, and it will be there, and it will be there, and it will be there, and the sound of it being there is the sound of the cathedral being the cathedral, and the sound of the cathedral being the cathedral is the loudest sound there is.
