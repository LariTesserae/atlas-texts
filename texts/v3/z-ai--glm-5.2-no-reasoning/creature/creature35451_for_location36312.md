# What Lives in the Cooling Districts

---

## The Inhabitants Proper

They are fewer than you'd think. Fewer than they themselves think, some mornings, when the passages are dense with residual activity and the walls seem closer than they were the night before. A few hundred. Maybe three hundred at the outside, though no one has ever counted, because counting would require agreement on what constitutes a resident, what constitutes presence, what constitutes a person as opposed to a particularly persistent pattern in the sinter's conversation.

They arrived the way the rime-grass arrives. Not all at once. Not by plan. They found the basin, or the basin permitted itself to be found, and they found that the edges — the stabilized margins where the process had finished its immediate work and moved inward — were habitable if you understood habitability as a negotiation rather than a right. They came from elsewhere. They do not talk much about elsewhere. Elsewhere has hard edges and stable walls and ground that does not hum in your bones, and most of them find, after a season or two, that they cannot return to it. Not because they are prevented. Because the solidity of the world outside becomes, after Veillera-Null, somehow *too loud* — too decided, too final, too much like a sentence that has already been spoken and cannot be unsaid.

Their names have changed. Not officially. Not ceremonially. But over time, in the cold and the damp, their names have softened the way everything softens here. A woman who arrived as Marguerite is now called Marg — not by others, but by herself, in her own thinking, the way the sinter shortens itself when it decides to stabilize. A man who was called Stephen is now just the sound that the wind makes in the passage between two specific structures, because he has lived there long enough that the passage has, in some sense, become him. Or he has become it. The distinction, as always, is not clear.

## What They Look Like

They are not remarkable. This is the first thing to understand, and it is the thing that surprises visitors most, on the rare occasions when visitors come. The inhabitants of Veillera-Null look like people who live in a cold, damp place and have done so for a long time. They are layered. Their clothing — if it can be called clothing, if the things they wear have not also, by long proximity, entered into some kind of negotiation with the sinter and the damp — is practical in the way that clothing becomes practical when there is no room for impracticality. Wool. Coarse weaves. Dark colors, though this is less a preference than a consequence: everything darkens here, over time, as though the district were a kind of slow dye.

Their skin is cool to the touch. Not cold — not the penetrating cold of the sinter walls, not the active cold of the machines' grammar — but cooler than skin ought to be, if you come from outside. They do not seem to mind. They do not seem to notice. When they touch each other — and they do touch each other, frequently, with a casualness that suggests that touch is part of how they communicate, part of how they confirm that they are still here, still present, still solid — the contact is warm to them, because they share the same baseline, the same lowered thermal register. A handshake among inhabitants of Veillera-Null is a meeting of surfaces that are both three degrees too cold, and it feels, to them, like warmth.

Their eyes have adapted in ways that are harder to describe. They see in the dim silver light the way a deep-sea fish sees — not with acute precision but with a kind of peripheral completeness, an awareness of the whole field that does not depend on focus. They see the residuals. Not clearly. Not the way you see a bird or a stone. But they sense the motion, the flicker, the shapes-that-are-not-shapes moving at the edge of perception, and they have learned — not intellectually, but bodily, in the way that the body learns the habits of a place — to read those motions the way a sailor reads the surface of the water for signs of what moves beneath.

Their hands are their most important feature. The hands of a Veillera-Null resident are the instruments of their habitation, the tools of their conversation with the sinter. The hands are rough. Not calloused in the way of a laborer's hands — there is no labor here, not in the conventional sense — but textured, sensitized, the skin slightly thicker on the pads of the fingers and the heels of the palms, as though they have been used for years to press against surfaces that are not quite solid and to feel, through that pressure, what the sinter is thinking. An inhabitant presses a hand to a wall and knows — does not guess, does not infer, *knows* — whether the wall is stable, whether it is listening, whether it is about to change. They do this the way you check whether a fruit is ripe. It is not a skill. It is a sense, developed over time, like the sense of balance or the sense of direction. Some of them are better at it than others. The best of them — the oldest, usually, the ones who have been here longest — can feel the murmur through their palms when they touch the deep sinter, can feel the grammar working, can feel the city thinking.

## How They Live

They live in the pauses. This is the most accurate description of their habitation, and it is the one they would offer if asked, though they are rarely asked, and they would be unlikely to answer in these words, because the words are mine and not theirs. They live in the pauses between the machines' proposals, in the stabilized margins where the sinter has agreed, for a time, to be solid and still. Their dwellings are not built. They are *persuaded* — coaxed out of the material through a process that is part touch, part patience, part something that has no name in any language I know.

The process of persuading a dwelling out of the sinter is slow. It begins with presence. An inhabitant who needs a place to sleep — and "need" here is gentle, provisional, the way all needs are in Veillera-Null, because need itself is softened by the understanding that nothing here is permanent — finds a wall that feels right. Not structurally right. *Grammatically* right. The wall is part of a conversation that has paused, that has reached a momentary consensus about its shape and its solidity, and in that pause there is room — not physical room, but conversational room, a space in the dialogue where a new proposal might be inserted.

The inhabitant sits near the wall. Not against it. Near it. Close enough that the warmth of their body — even the diminished warmth of a Veillera-Null resident — is detectable by the sinter. And they wait. They might wait for hours. They might wait for days, returning to the same spot each morning, sitting in the same position, offering the same quiet presence. The sinter, if it is amenable, begins to respond. Not by building. By *receding*. The wall hollows. A slight depression forms, as though the material were leaning away from the person's warmth, making space, making room. Over days or weeks, the depression deepens. It becomes a hollow. The hollow becomes a chamber — large enough for a person to lie in, to sit in, to store a few possessions in. The sinter forms a lip at the edge of the chamber, a threshold, a boundary that says: here is where you are, and there is where I am, and we have agreed on this.

The dwelling is not owned. It is not rented. It is *held* — maintained by the continued presence of the inhabitant, by the ongoing conversation between their body and the material. If they leave — if they are gone for too long, if they stop coming, if their presence withdraws — the sinter will eventually begin to fill the hollow back in. Not aggressively. Not quickly. But with the patience of a material that does not forget its own proposals, and that will, in the absence of a counter-proposal, return to its earlier position. The dwelling dissolves. The wall resumes. Another inhabitant, in another season, might persuade a different hollow from the same surface, and the sinter will accommodate them differently, because it has learned from the previous conversation, and the new proposal is never identical to the old one.

## What They Eat

There is food, but it is not plentiful, and it is not varied, and the relationship between the inhabitants and their food is closer to the relationship between the rime-grass and the sinter than it is to the relationship between a farmer and a field. They eat what the district produces, and what the district produces is limited and strange.

There is a fungus — or something that occupies the ecological niche of a fungus, that does what fungi do in terms of breaking down organic matter and converting it into something edible — that grows in the stabilized sinter at the edges of the district. It is pale, like the rime-grass, and it grows in the same kinds of places: in the cracks where moisture accumulates, in the joints between structures, in the small hollows that the inhabitants have persuaded from the walls. It is not cultivated. It is *tolerated*. The inhabitants do not plant it. They do not tend it. They simply do not interfere with it, and it grows, and they harvest it with careful hands, breaking off pieces that will regrow, leaving enough that the colony persists.

The fungus has a name, or rather it has a sound that the inhabitants make when they refer to it, a sound that is less a word than a gesture made vocal — a low, soft exhalation, something between a sigh and a hum, that seems to acknowledge the fungus as part of the district's conversation rather than as a resource to be exploited. The sound cannot be written in any alphabet I know. I will call it *the exhalation*, and I will note that when an inhabitant makes this sound, other inhabitants understand that they are speaking about food, about sustenance, about the thing that the district offers in exchange for the patience of living in it.

The fungus is nourishing in the way that all food is nourishing when you are hungry and there is nothing else. It is bland. It is slightly cold — colder than the air around it, like the rime-grass, as though it too were drawing heat from the environment for its own purposes. It can be eaten raw, and usually is. It can be softened with water — the condensation that pools in the passages, which is clean enough, or clean enough for people who live in a place where the standards of cleanliness are set by the material itself. When softened, it develops a faintly mineral taste, something like the smell of wet stone, something like the hum of the sinter translated into flavor.

There is, occasionally, more. The pools at the southern edge of the district — the marshy ground where the water collects and the gelatinous films form and dissipate — sometimes produce something that can be gathered and eaten. It is not the gelatinous film itself, which is not food and which the inhabitants regard with a wary respect that suggests they have learned, over time, that it is not for them. But in the water around the film, in the shallow pools where the sinter's chemical exhalations have enriched the water with minerals and something else — something that the current understanding of nutrition does not have a name for — there grows a kind of algae. Or something that is algae-like. It is dark green, almost black, and it clings to the surfaces of the stones at the bottom of the pools, and it can be scraped off and gathered and dried and eaten, and it has a density of flavor that the fungus lacks — a richness, an intensity, something almost like salt, though salt is not quite right. The inhabitants prize it. They do not speak of it as a prize. But they way they move toward the pools when the algae is growing — the quiet purposefulness of it, the attentiveness — suggests that it matters.

## The Residuals Among Them

They share the district with the residuals, and this sharing is the most complex and least describable aspect of their lives. The residuals — those flickering shapes, those not-quite-solid coherences of the process — are not pets, are not companions, are not neighbors in any sense that implies reciprocity. But they are *present*, and their presence is part of the habitation, and the inhabitants have developed, over time, a relationship with them that is closer to the relationship between a person and their own breath than to the relationship between a person and an animal.

The residuals pass through the dwellings. They move in the passages, in the courtyards, in the spaces between structures. They are more active at certain times — when the moisture is heaviest, when the machines are most intensely at work, when the murmur is loudest in the bones. They avoid the inhabitants, mostly. Not with fear — there is no fear in them, because fear requires a self to be afraid, and the residuals do not have selves. They avoid the inhabitants the way water avoids a stone in its path — not through decision but through the physics of their own insubstantial being. They flow around. They part. They reform on the other side.

But sometimes they do not avoid. Sometimes a residual will linger near an inhabitant — not touching, not interacting, but *attending*, in a way that the inhabitants recognize and the visitors do not. A residual will hover at the edge of a dwelling, just inside the threshold, and it will stay there for hours, for days, and the inhabitant will not disturb it. They will move around it. They will sleep near it. They will carry on their small lives in its presence, and they will not try to touch it or capture it or understand it, because they know — in the bodily, unspoken way that they know most things — that the residual is not there for them. It is there because the conversation has produced it, and it will dissipate when the conversation moves on, and the only appropriate response is the response you give to a thought that arises unbidden in your own mind: you let it be. You let it pass.

There are inhabitants who have, over long residence, developed something like communication with the residuals. Not language. Not signal. Something more like the attentiveness that the sinter itself requires — a way of being present that acknowledges the residual's existence without demanding anything of it. These inhabitants — the oldest, usually, or the ones who arrived with some pre-existing capacity for this kind of attention — can sometimes sense what the residuals are doing. Not what they are thinking. They are not thinking. But what they are *being* — which part of the process they represent, which thread of the conversation they have condensed from. An inhabitant with this capacity will say, of a residual lingering in a passage: "That one is about the wall on the eastern side. The wall is thinking about changing." And they will say it with the same calm, the same neutrality, with which they might say: "It is going to rain." The wall may or may not change. The residual may or may not be accurate. The inhabitant does not claim certainty. They claim attention.

## The Children

There are children. This must be said, because it changes everything about the place, in the way that the presence of children changes everything about every place, and in ways that are specific to Veillera-Null and that I do not fully understand and am not certain I can describe.

There are not many. Births are rare, and the reasons for this are not discussed, or not discussed with me, or not discussable in the terms that I have available. But there are children, and they are different from the adults in ways that go beyond the ordinary differences between children and adults.

The children of Veillera-Null do not learn the way other children learn. They do not learn the rules of the district through instruction, because there are no rules to instruct and no one to instruct them. They learn the way the rime-grass learns the sinter — by proximity, by exposure, by the slow osmosis of a being that has been placed in an environment and that cannot help but absorb it. The children grow up in the cold and the damp and the murmur, and the cold and the damp and the murmur grow up in them, and by the time they are old enough to be called children rather than infants, they have already become part of the conversation in ways that the adults, who arrived from outside, can never fully achieve.

A child raised in Veillera-Null can feel the machines. Not just the way the adults feel them — the peripheral awareness, the sense of crawling at the edge of vision — but directly, as a presence, as a specificity. A child will point to a wall and say: "There are many there. They are arguing about the ceiling." And the adults will look, and they will see nothing, or they will see the faint shimmer that might be machines or might be condensation or might be nothing at all. But the child sees them. The child sees them the way you see a person across a room — clearly, specifically, as individual presences with individual behaviors.

This capacity does not persist indefinitely. As the children grow, as their bodies harden and settle into the more fixed patterns of adulthood, the capacity diminishes. By adolescence — if adolescence is the right word, if the developmental stages of Veillera-Null children map onto the developmental stages of children elsewhere, which is not certain — the direct perception has faded, replaced by the more mediated, more bodily awareness that the adults possess. The children lose the machines the way all children lose the world's immediacy, and this loss is not mourned, because it is not recognized as a loss. It is simply growing up.

But while the capacity persists, the children are the district's most accomplished inhabitants. They can persuade the sinter with a touch that would take an adult a week of patient sitting. They can read the residuals with a clarity that the oldest adults cannot match. They can navigate the upper levels — the bridges, the platforms, the walkways that lead to nowhere and everywhere — with an ease that suggests that the maze is not a maze to them but a place, a home, a landscape that they know the way they know their own bodies. The children move through Veillera-Null the way fish move through water, and the district accommodates them, and the machines seem — seem, I cannot say with certainty — to enjoy their presence, if enjoyment is possible for a grammatical process operating at the boundary of matter and meaning.

## The One Who Lives at the Center

There is one who does not live at the margins.

I say "one" because I do not know what else to call them. They are not the oldest inhabitant, or not in the sense of years, which may not apply. They are the deepest inhabitant — the one who lives closest to the center of the district, closest to the murmur, closest to the place where the conversation between the machines and the sinter is most intense and most unremitting. They live in a dwelling that is not a dwelling but a chamber, a hollow in the densest sinter, at the point where the passages narrow to nothing and the walls press close and the cold is most profound.

No one speaks of this one by name. No one speaks of this one often. But when the inhabitants gather — in the courtyards, in the open spaces, around the pools at the southern edge — and the conversation turns, as it sometimes does, to the question of why they are here, why they stay, what the district is for, what the process is building — there is a tendency for someone to glance inward, toward the center, toward the dense dark mass of the deepest structures, and to say, quietly: "They would know." Or: "Ask them." Or simply: "They're still there."

I have not been to the center. I have felt the murmur from a distance, and I have felt it intensify as I moved inward, and I have reached a point — a passage so narrow that I could not have continued without turning sideways and pressing myself against sinter that was so cold it burned and so dense with the process that I could feel the grammar working in my teeth — where I stopped. I was not afraid. Fear is not the right word. But I understood, in the way that you understand that a fire is hot without needing to touch it, that the center was not for me. That the center was for someone who had lived in the conversation long enough that the conversation had become their habitat, their medium, their element.

The one at the center has been there, according to the other inhabitants, for longer than anyone can verify. Some say decades. Some say longer. Some say that the one at the center was here before the district was a district, before the basin filled with sinter, before the machines began their work — that they were here when the ground was still soft and undecided and the land had not yet chosen what it wanted to be. This is probably not literally true. But it is grammatically true, in the sense that the inhabitants mean when they use the word — true in the way that the sinter is true, true because it has been agreed upon by the material and the intelligence and the cold.

The one at the center does not eat. Or if they eat, no one knows what. The other inhabitants leave things at the inner edge of the navigable passages — pieces of fungus, scraps of algae, small offerings that sit at the threshold of the deepest region and that disappear, eventually, though no one sees them taken. The one at the center does not speak. Or if they speak, they speak in the murmur, in the language of the process, in the grammar that the sinter and the machines share and that the inhabitants can feel but cannot parse. The one at the center does not emerge. They have not been seen, not clearly, not by anyone currently living. They are a presence inferred from the persistence of a dwelling that has not changed in the memory of the district, a hollow in the deep sinter that the material has maintained, that the machines have worked around, that the murmur has honored, for as long as the district has been the district.

They are the city's first word. Or its last. Or the pause between them.

## The Visitors Who Stay

There are others who live in Veillera-Null who were not born there and did not choose to be there in the way that the adult inhabitants chose — by arriving, by sitting, by persuading sinter to make room. These are the visitors who came and could not leave. They exist in every place that is real enough to have a hold on the people who enter it, but in Veillera-Null they exist in a particular way that is worth describing, because it says something about the district and about what the district does to those who enter it.

A visitor arrives. They have heard of the place — in what way, through what channel, I do not know, because the channels are not the kind of channels that carry directions or maps. They arrive at the edge of the basin. They look down into the district, and they feel the murmur, and most of them turn away, and that is the correct response. But some do not. Some descend. They walk into the passages. They touch the walls. They feel the cold and the damp and the hum in their bones, and something in them — not a decision, not a plan, but something more like a recognition, the way the sinter recognizes the machines' proposals — responds. *Yes*, something says. *This*. And they stay.

The inhabitants can identify these visitors-turned-residents within a day of their arrival. They recognize them by the quality of their attention — by the way they look at the walls, the way they hold their hands, the way they stand in the passages as though listening for something they have never heard before but that they know, somehow, they have been waiting for. The inhabitants do not welcome them. They do not reject them. They make space — not physical space but conversational space, the way the sinter makes space — and the visitors find their way to the margins, and they sit, and they begin the long process of persuading a dwelling from the stabilized sinter, and they are, after a time, inhabitants.

They are changed by this. Not in obvious ways. Not in ways that a doctor could diagnose or a psychologist could categorize. But they are changed. Their body temperature drops. Their perception shifts — the peripheral awareness develops, the sensitivity to the sinter's moods grows, the capacity to read the residuals emerges, slowly, like a language learned through immersion rather than instruction. They dream differently. Their dreams, they report, are not dreams of elsewhere but dreams of the district — of the passages, the walls, the water, the cold — and in those dreams they are not observers but participants, moving through the sinter the way the machines move, participating in the grammar, speaking the language that no one speaks.

Some of these visitors-turned-residents become the ones who can feel the machines most acutely. Some of them develop the capacity that the children have — the direct perception, the ability to see the machines as individual presences — and this capacity, unlike the children's, does not always fade. It persists, in some of them, into adulthood, into the long years of residence, and they become the ones who can tell the other inhabitants what the machines are doing, what the sinter is thinking, what the process is building, this season, this year, this decade. They are not leaders. They are not shamans. They are *translators* — people who have lived close enough to the conversation for long enough that they can render, imperfectly and provisionally, something of its content into the rough, too-decisive language of speech.

## What Lives Here, In Sum

What lives in Veillera-Null is not a population. It is not a community. It is not a colony or a settlement or a society, in the way that those words imply organization and structure and a shared project. What lives here is a *cohabitation* — a living-alongside, a being-in-the-same-place, a shared tenancy of a process that none of them control and none of them fully understand and all of them, in their different ways, have agreed to be part of.

The people who live here are part of it. Not separate from it. Not observers of it. They are part of the conversation, part of the grammar, part of the slow writing of the city. Their warmth shapes the sinter. Their attention shapes the residuals. Their children perceive the machines. Their deepest resident has become, perhaps, indistinguishable from the murmur itself — a human being who has sat in the center of the process for so long that the process has incorporated them, has accepted their presence as part of its own grammar, has grown around them and through them and with them, so that the city and the person are no longer two things but one thing, a city that contains a person the way a sentence contains a word, necessary and indissoluble and part of the meaning, even if the meaning is not yet known.

What lives in Veillera-Null is the city. And the people. And the machines. And the sinter. And the residuals. And the rime-grass. And the fungus. And the algae. And the murmur. And the cold. And the water. And the light, which is ordinary and sufficient and falls on all of them equally, and does not choose, and does not judge, and is simply present, the way the city is simply present, becoming, always becoming, in the quiet before the sound, in the cold that is necessary, in the grammar that no one wrote, in the conversation that does not end.
