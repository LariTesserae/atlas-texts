# What Lives in the Sump

---

The frogs came first. Or rather — the frogs were first *noticed*, because the frogs announce themselves. But this is not the same as being first, and the distinction matters in a place where sequence has become meaningless.

They are small. Smaller than you expect from the sound they make. A body the length of your thumb from knuckle to tip, dark — not the darkness of shadow but of saturation, as though they had been boiled in the tannin-water long enough to take it into their tissue. They are not uniformly dark. Each one carries a pattern, a mottling along the flanks that is individual, that you could learn to recognize if you had reason to, and if you could see them clearly, which you cannot, because they move. They are always moving. A frog at rest in the sump is a frog that has just landed or a frog that is about to jump. There is no middle state. They launch from reed-stalk to water-surface with a precision that does not look practiced but looks instead *innate*, as though the trajectory from one point to another is not computed but simply *known*, the way the water knows to seek the lowest ground. They spread their webbed feet at the last instant — you can see this, sometimes, if one crosses a patch of open water near you — and the feet strike the surface tension and they do not sink, or they sink only slowly, the surface bowing under them like a membrane, and in that moment they look like something that has been designed for this exact place and no other.

Their eyes are gold. Not the bright gold of jewelry but the dull gold of the haze-filtered light itself, as if they have taken that light in and it has settled behind their pupils. They do not blink. They watch you, if you are near, with an expression that is not fear and is not indifference but is something closer to *punctuation* — a pause between two actions, neither of which involves you.

And they call. The call — you cannot call it a croak. A croak implies throat, implies the vibration of vocal cords, implies the bodily mechanism of sound. This is not that. The sound they make is produced by a sac at the throat, a membrane inflated taut, and the sound is a single note, a percussive *ptt* struck on that membrane, a wet pop that carries further than it should for its size. One frog alone is almost nothing. A click. An irritation. But one frog alone is not how they exist. They exist in *response* to each other. One calls, and the nearest frog calls after a pause — a pause so short it feels involuntary, a reflex — and then the next, and the next, and the sound propagates outward through the reeds in a wave that you can almost see, almost track, if you turn your head in its direction, though it is never quite visible enough to follow. It arrives at the basin wall and reflects, or seems to, and the returning wave meets a new wave going out, and between the two — between the thousands of frogs calling in their overlapping offsets — the silence is filled so completely that silence becomes the wrong word for what was there before. What was there before was the *capacity* for this sound, and the sound is the fulfillment of that capacity.

They breed in the standing water. The eggs are laid in clusters — gelatinous masses the size of a fist, clear but clouded with the dark specks of the embryos — anchored to reed stalks just below the waterline. You see them if you look down. They are everywhere. Each mass contains a hundred embryos, perhaps more, and there are masses on every stalk in every patch of still water across four kilometers of basin floor, and the arithmetic of this is such that you cannot think about it without arriving at a number that ceases to be a number and becomes instead a kind of *weather* — a quantity so vast it describes not a population but a condition of the place.

The tadpoles are darker than the adults. Almost black. They move in the shallows in dense schools that, from above, look like oil on water, like something has spilled and is spreading. They eat the algae film. They eat each other. They eat whatever the warm water brings them, and the warm water brings them a great deal — the mineral silt is dense with organic content, with the decomposing reed-leaves that fall constantly from the stalks above, with the dead insects that drift down through the water column, with the microscopic life that has colonized this warm nutrient bath with the thoroughness of life that has found precisely the conditions it was searching for.

They metamorphose quickly. The warmth accelerates everything. A tadpole in the sump goes from egg to froglet in weeks that would take months in cooler water, and the froglets emerge — impossibly small, translucent at the limbs, still carrying a thread of tail — onto the reed stalks and sit there, drying in the humid air, and within days they are calling. Not the full call. A thin version, a practice call, a rehearsal. But they are calling, and they are adding themselves to the sound, and the sound grows.

---

The insects are harder to describe because they are harder to *see*. Not because they are secretive — they are not — but because they exist in a scale that does not align with the scale of attention the sump demands. You come to the sump aware of the reeds, the water, the rock walls, the structures. These are the things that *register*. The insects are the thing that *fills the gaps*, and the gaps are where they live.

The midges come first. Clouds of them — and *clouds* is the right word, not *swarms*, because they do not move with the purposeful direction of a swarm but with the drifting, dispersing indolence of vapor. They ride the air above the water in columns that shift with the faint breezes that reach down into the basin, and they are so numerous that in certain patches of air the light dims slightly as it passes through them, as though the haze itself were partly composed of their bodies. They do not bite. They have no interest in you. They are feeding — always feeding — on the algae film that coats every surface, and on each other, and on the microscopic detritus that floats in the humid air itself.

Each midge is small enough to vanish at the distance of your arm's length. A body like a dark thread. Wings that catch the dim light and refract it — not with the rainbow iridescence of a dragonfly but with a dull, prismatic shimmer that is visible only when the angle is right, and the angle is never quite right in the sump's flat light, so the shimmer is a rumor, a thing you are never sure you saw.

They mate in the air. You can see this if you watch a column long enough — pairs that have coupled, the female larger, the male attached beneath her, and the paired form drifts in the column with a different weight, a different trajectory, sinking slowly while the single midges around them rise. She will lay her eggs on the water surface, and the eggs will sink, and the larvae will hatch in the warm silt and live there, in the mud, in the dark beneath the reeds, and they will rise again as adults and join the column, and the column will continue, and the column has been continuing for as long as the sump has been the sump, which is long enough that the midges have *become* the column, have *become* the haze, have *become* part of what the air is.

The larger insects — the blue-black ones — are something else. They have a presence the midges lack. A body the length of your little finger, glossy, metallic in a way that reads less like color and more like *machinery*, as though they have been manufactured in a workshop that specializes in small, purposeful, angry objects. They hover. Not drift — *hover*, holding a position in the air with a precision that requires constant micro-adjustment of their wings, and the wings beat so fast that they blur, and the sound they make is a drone that has a specific pitch, a *key*, and each individual is in a slightly different key, and the aggregate is a chord that sits beneath the frog-sound and above the reed-rattle and fills the middle register of the sump's voice.

They are territorial. You can see this. A hovering individual will be approached by a second, and the first will *move* — not flee but intercept, a sudden lateral dash that is over before you have registered it began, and the second will bank away and return to a different hover position, and the first will return to its position, and the territory has been asserted. What the territory *is* — what resource it guards — you cannot determine. It may be a patch of water with desirable oviposition sites. It may be a hunting perch. It may be nothing more than a volume of air that this individual has decided is *its* volume of air, and the decision is sufficient.

They hunt. They take the midges from the air with a precision that looks like violence committed at a molecular level — a sudden contraction, a folding of the body around the prey, and then a return to hovering, and the wings do not stop. The wings never stop. They are the most restless thing in the sump, and the sump is full of restless things, and this is saying something.

---

The birds are the thing you hear and almost never see.

There is a heron. Or there is something that behaves as a heron behaves — long-legged, long-necked, a body built for wading in water that is shallow enough that the birds' legs are a tool for standing *in* a meal rather than swimming through one. It is grey. The same grey as the reeds, which is to say it is not so much a color as an *agreement* with the environment, a concession to the palette of the place. It stands in the water between two clumps of reeds, and it stands without moving, and it stands without moving, and it stands without moving, and you look at it and you are not certain it is alive, and then it strikes — a movement of the neck so fast that the beginning and the end are the same moment, and something that was in the water is now in its throat, and it has resumed standing, and it is standing without moving again.

There is not one heron. There are several. You learn this not by counting but by the distribution of their calls. The sharp interrogative note — a single syllable, almost a bark, almost a question — comes from different parts of the basin in succession, and the pattern of the calls describes territories. Each bird has its patch of reeds, its section of water, its hunting ground, and the calls are the boundaries of those grounds, announced and defended. When one bird calls from the east and another answers from the west, you can map the distance between them, and the distance describes a kind of grid — invisible, acoustic, maintained by sound alone.

They nest in the structures. The rusted beams of the old pumping stations — the steel frames that were meant to hold pipes, to support mechanisms, to do work — these are where the herons build. The nests are platforms of reed stalks, woven with more intention than artistry, lined with the softer material that the reeds produce — a pale fiber at the base of the leaf, almost a down — and on these platforms the birds lay their eggs, and the eggs are pale blue-green, the color of the water in certain light, and the birds sit on them and the rain falls on them and the humidity rises through them and they hatch, and the chicks emerge into the sound, into the warmth, into the fullness, and the first thing they hear is the frogs, and the frogs are the first thing they will always hear, and the sound becomes, for them, the sound of *origin*, the sound of the place where they began.

There are smaller birds too. A species — or several species — that move through the reed tops in flocks, small bodies, quick, restless, always in motion, picking insects from the stalks with a precision that the herons' hunting mirrors at a different scale. They chatter. Their sound is conversational — not the heron's territorial proclamation but a running commentary, a series of short notes exchanged between individuals in a flock that moves as a single body through the reeds, and when they pass overhead you hear them as a wave — approaching, cresting, receding — and then they are gone, and the reeds settle, and the frogs continue.

---

There are things in the water you do not see.

The crustaceans — for they are crustaceans, or near enough — move in the shallows in clouds. You see the clouds before you see the individuals. A patch of water near the basin wall, where the geothermal warmth is strongest, where a faint sulfurous smell marks a vent — in that patch, the water moves. Not with the movement of current or wind, but with the movement of *something living in it*, many things, many small things, in coordination or at least in proximity, and when you kneel and look closely, the water is dense with bodies. Translucent. Smaller than a grain of rice. Legs that paddle continuously, and the paddling is what moves the water, and the movement of the water is what you saw from above. They are feeding. On the algae, on the detritus, on the bacteria that colonize the warm mineral silt, and they are being fed upon — by the frog tadpoles, by the nymphs of the insects that rise from the water as adults, by the herons' strikes that take not just fish but mouthfuls of this concentrated life.

There are no fish. This is important. The sump has no fish. The water may be too warm in places, or too mineral, or too new — the basin is young, geologically, and whatever filled it came from underground springs, from rain, from condensation, and nothing that requires a connection to a river or a lake has found its way here. What lives in the water lives in the water because it can arrive from the air, or because it was already in the ground when the basin opened. The crustaceans may have been in the soil. Their eggs may have been there, dormant, waiting for the specific condition of warmth and standing water that the basin's creation provided. When the sump filled, they hatched. They had been waiting — not consciously, not with intention, but with the patience of organisms that have evolved to wait — and the waiting ended, and they began.

The nematodes are here. Everywhere. You will never see them. They are microscopic, or near enough, and they live in the silt, in the mud, in the substrate, in numbers that make the frogs look scarce. They are the *bottom* — not the bottom of the food chain, which is a human abstraction, but the bottom of the *sump*, the deepest layer of life, the thing that lives in the mud and processes the mud and becomes, in turn, the mud. They eat the bacteria. The bacteria eat the minerals. The minerals are the rock. The rock is the earth. The earth has opened here, and the opening has filled with water, and the water has filled with life, and the life fills the water, and the cycle is not a cycle but a *state* — a sustained, continuous, ongoing state that does not turn but simply *is*, and the nematodes are in it, and they are the smallest visible thing, and they are the largest fact.

---

There is something else in the sump. Not an animal. Not a plant. Something that lives in the structures.

It is — you think, when you first enter the largest building, the one with the generator housing — it is a smell. A smell that is not the sulfurous vent-smell, not the green rot of the reed decomposition, not the mineral tang of the water. A different smell. Organic. Warm. Complex, in the way that a smell is complex when it is produced by *process* rather than by *substance* — when it is the smell of something happening rather than something being.

It is bacterial. It lives in the concrete channels that were meant to carry water to the pumps. The channels are pooled now — the water that seeps in has nowhere to go, and it sits in the channels, and it is warm, warmer than the open water outside, because the concrete holds heat and the structure itself sits over a stronger thermal vent, and the water in the channels has become something that the open water is not. A concentrated broth. The bacteria have colonized it with an enthusiasm that is, in its way, *creative* — they have taken the minerals in the concrete, the iron in the rusted steel, the organic matter that has accumulated from decades of reed-leaf fall and insect death and frog waste, and they have *worked* it. They have processed it. They have produced, in the quiet dark of the concrete channels, a microbial ecosystem that is distinct from the ecosystem outside — a separate metabolism, a different chemistry, a life that is adapted not to the sump but to the *structure*, to the specific conditions that the structure provides.

The film on the channel walls is alive. It is not algae. It is bacterial — a biofilm, thick, viscous, a layer of life that coats the concrete the way the lichen has not yet coated the basin walls, because the bacterial film got there first, because the conditions in the channels were *right*, and when the conditions are right, life does not wait. It arrives. It does not arrive the way a person arrives — with preparation, with intention. It arrives the way water arrives at the lowest point. It flows there. It collects there. And once it has collected, it begins to do what it does, and what it does is *live*.

The biofilm glows, faintly. This is not bioluminescence — not the cold blue light of deep-sea organisms. It is something else. A phosphorescence, perhaps, a chemical process in the bacterial metabolism that produces a faint, almost imperceptible luminescence in the darkest channels, in the deepest pools inside the structure, where the light from outside does not reach. You see it when you enter the building and your eyes adjust. It is not enough to see by. It is enough to *sense*. A presence. A faint warm glow in the interior of the structure, as though the building itself were generating something — not electricity, not power, but the *sign* of life, the visible evidence that something is happening in there, something that the structure enables and that the sump outside does not.

The biofilm has its own fauna. The nematodes are here too, in greater density. There are other things — protozoans, ciliates, organisms that exist at the edge of visibility, that move with the slow, deliberate motion of things that have no muscles, that move by *shape*, by extending and contracting, by *becoming* where they wish to be. They live in the film. They eat the film. They are eaten by the things that come to the film — the frog that has found its way into the channel, sitting in the warm water with an expression that is not contentment but is not anything else either, simply *presence*, simply being a frog in a place where frogs can be.

---

And there is you. Or there has been. The sump does not require you. It does not reject you. It does not notice you in any way that matters. You stand in the water and the water does not part for you. You push through the reeds and the reeds push back, and when you pass they close behind you, and the stalks knock against each other as if you had never been. The frogs near your feet stop calling — for a moment, for the length of a held breath — and then they resume, and the pause is not a recognition of you but a *disruption*, the way a stone thrown into still water is not recognized by the water but merely *displaces* it, and the water closes, and the ripple fades, and the surface is as it was.

You are not what lives here. You are what *visits* here, and the sump makes a clear distinction, and the distinction is this: what lives here does not dry. What lives here has accepted the saturation. What lives here has surrendered the boundary between self and environment that you, standing in your waterproof boots, in your damp jacket, with your notebook softening in your pocket, are still trying to maintain. The frogs have no boundary. They are wet and the wet is them. The reeds have no boundary. They grow from the water and into the water and the water is in their roots and their roots are in the silt and the silt is in the water and there is no point at which one becomes another. The biofilm has no boundary. It is a process, not an object, and it does not end at its surface because it does not have a surface, it has a *gradient*, a slow transition from *more alive* to *less alive* to *not alive* that is so continuous that drawing a line is an act of violence against the truth.

What lives in the sump is the sump. That is the only honest answer. The frogs live here, and the insects live here, and the herons nest here, and the crustaceans cloud the warm water here, and the nematodes process the mud here, and the bacteria glow in the structures here, and the reeds stand and knock and click and grow and fall and rot and are eaten and are rebuilt here, and all of this — all of it together, all of it at once, all of it in the flat light and the warm air and the constant sound — is what lives here. It lives here the way a body lives — not as a collection of parts but as a *whole*, as a single organism that has the basin as its skin and the water as its blood and the reeds as its skeleton and the sound — the endless, layered, percussive, dripping, humming, calling sound — as its breath, its heartbeat, its voice, continuing, continuing, continuing, in the warm dark wet, full, and full, and full.
