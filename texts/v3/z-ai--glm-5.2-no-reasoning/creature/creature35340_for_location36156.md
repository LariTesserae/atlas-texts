# What Lives There

Nothing lives there in the way that you mean *lives* — the word you keep reaching for, the one that implies blood and breath and boundaries between self and world. Nothing lives there like that. Nothing has ever lived there like that. The place is too young and too old at once for such tidy arrangements, and the distinction between inhabitant and habitat has not yet been forced, may never be forced, is in fact one of the decisions the place keeps deferring, like a student who has not chosen a major and is content to sit in the corridor year after year, sampling each department's air.

But something is there.

---

Not in the structures. The structures are not hollow and they are not occupied. They are not architecture. They are not even sculpture, though they occupy the same grammatical position in the sentence — *there is a tall thing, there is a low thing, there is a thing I cannot name* — as sculpture occupies in a catalog. The structures are accretions. Bubbles in pour. Thickened regions of the ongoing deposition, places where the condensation has piled its invisible labor into something you can stub your toe against. They are no more inhabited than a sandbar is inhabited by the river that堆ed it. They are the *activity* of the place, caught in mid-motion, frozen not by cold but by the sheer inability of time here to commit to a direction.

The something that is there lives in the way the place lives. Which is to say: it lives *as* the place, and the distinction between the two is one of those accommodations that language insists on and the Veldt of First Surfaces does not.

---

Begin with what you can observe, because observation is the only tool you brought that still functions here. Your compass does not work. Your watch does not work, or rather it works too well, measuring a kind of time that has no bearing on the time the place keeps, which is not linear but *iterative* — the same moment refined, rephrased, tried again. Your eyes work. Your skin works. Your ears work, though they hear things that ears were not designed to hear, or rather they hear things that ears were designed to hear in a world that assumed certain constants about the nature of sound and air and the distance between events.

Your eyes: you see the floor. You see the wet. You see the amber light, which is not light so much as the visual residue of a process that has not finished producing whatever it intends to produce, and the visual residue is a byproduct, the way heat is a byproduct of a machine whose purpose is motion. The light is what is left over. The light is what leaks. And in the leaking light, at the edge of your vision — not at the periphery, exactly, but at the *resolution limit*, the place where seeing becomes almost-seeing — you see them.

---

They are not entities. Not at first. Not in the way you are accustomed to attributing entityhood to things that move and react and persist. They do not persist. Or rather, they persist *differently*. The floor persists by being the floor. The structures persist by being almost-structures, by holding their shape for one more iteration of the turbulence. What persists in the way you mean — the way a person persists, the way a body gets up in the morning and is the same body that went to sleep — does not apply here, because getting up in the morning and going to sleep are the same act in this place, the turbulence not differentiating between activity and rest, between the making and the unmaking, between the groove scored and the groove smoothed.

What you see at the edge of your vision is: a perturbation. A slight thickening of the amber light. A place where the condensation behaves differently — not pooling, not falling, but *hanging*, suspended in a column of warmer air that is warmer than the surrounding warm air by a fraction of a degree that should not matter and does. You see, in other words, a place where the place is paying attention to itself.

That is what lives there.

---

The floor makes itself. You know this. You have felt the vibration, the arrhythmic hum, the tectonic groan of a surface in the act of becoming. The floor makes itself out of itself, out of the condensation, out of the material deposited by the breathing air. But making is not the same as tending. A glacier makes itself and does not tend itself. A river cuts a channel and does not tend the channel. The floor makes itself with the indifference of a process that has no stake in its own product.

But the tools.

The tools are the evidence. Not of intelligence — the word is too large, too freighted with assumptions about language and planning and the ability to recognize oneself in a mirror. The tools are evidence of *tending*. Of a behavior that is not making and not unmaking but something between — a third activity, a maintenance, a care. Someone — some *thing* — has been scraping the grooves back when they smooth away. Re-scoring the marks. Not to build, not to destroy, but to *keep*. To hold the turbulence to a shape it would not hold on its own. To insist, with a stone blade and a patience measured in centuries, that the spiral on the table-structure should spiral, that the groove should continue, that the mark should persist.

The tools are simple. The work is simple. The intention behind them is not simple. The intention is the most complex thing in this place, more complex than the turbulence, more complex than the self-generating floor, more complex than the structures that almost-are. The intention is: *this mark should be here*. Not for a reason. Not for a purpose. Not because the mark means something or does something or contributes to something. Because the mark is the mark, and its being-here is its own justification, and the act of re-making it is the act of saying — without language, without voice, without any of the apparatus of saying — *I was here, and I cared about this surface, and I cared enough to work against the entropy of becoming with nothing but a flake of stone and the labor of my hand*.

---

So what are they?

They are not animals. Animals are products of differentiation — of the long, patient separation of self from not-self, of mouth from stomach, of nerve from skin. Animals are what happens when the boundary between organism and environment thickens and hardens and becomes a skin, a shell, a face. Nothing here has a face. Nothing here has a boundary that thickens. The condensation forms on the floor and forms on the structures and forms on you and forms on them, and the condensation does not know the difference, and they do not know the difference, and the difference, in fact, may not exist.

They are not plants. You already understand this. There is no sun, no soil, no root, no reason.

They are not machines. Machines are designed. Machines have intentions imposed on them from outside. Machines are the product of a mind that precedes the machine, and there is no mind here that precedes anything. The mind — if it is a mind, if that word applies to a thing that is more like an *urge* than a thought, more like a *leaning* than a plan — the mind arises *with* the tending, not before it. The mind is the tending. The mind is the act of picking up the tool and scoring the groove and setting the tool down and the mind does not exist when the tool is on the floor and the mind comes into being when the tool is in the hand and the hand is on the surface and the surface is being marked. The mind is intermittent. The mind is a thing that happens *sometimes*.

---

Describe them, then. Describe what you see when you sit on the warm wet floor and let your eyes adjust to the amber light and stop trying to resolve the unresolved and simply attend to what is there.

There is a thickening of the air. Not a shape — a *tendency*. The moisture in the air gathers in a region roughly the height and width of a human torso, though the dimensions shift, the way the structures' dimensions shift, the turbulence editing the form in real time. The gathering is not dense enough to see through and not dense enough to see clearly. It is a *darker damp*. A place where the condensation has organized itself into a column, and the column is warm, warmer than the air by exactly the fraction that makes it visible as a distortion, a rippling, a place where the light bends slightly and the floor seen through it is slightly displaced.

It moves. Not quickly. Not with the purposeful trajectory of a body going somewhere. It drifts, the way a column of warm air drifts, the way a breath drifts when it has no destination and no wind to carry it. It drifts toward the structures. Not all of them — specific ones. The ones with the deepest grooves, the most worn surfaces, the ones that show the most evidence of tending. It drifts to them and settles beside them, and where it settles, the condensation on the floor deepens, and the film of water thickens, and the surface of the structure becomes — for a time, for as long as the column remains — slightly more defined. The grooves sharpen. The texture clarifies. The almost-geometry becomes, for a moment, more geometric, as though the attention of the column were a lens, were a mold, were a hand pressing the form back into its intended shape.

Then it drifts away. The grooves soften. The turbulence resumes. The structure resumes its uncertainty.

---

And there are more.

Not many. Not in the way that a field has many flowers or a city has many people. But more than one. Across the distances — the vast, irregular, irrelevant distances — there are other columns of darker damp, other warm perturbations, other gatherings of the place's attention. They are spaced the way the structures are spaced: irregularly, without pattern, without the arrangement that would imply coordination. They do not gather. They do not communicate. They do not — so far as you can observe — interact. Each one tends its own structure, or its own region of floor, or its own section of the plain, and the tending is solitary and slow and without visible satisfaction.

But they leave the tools.

They leave the tools on the floor beside the structures, and the tools persist — the stone ones, the glass ones — and the grips of condensed vapor rebuild themselves when they dissolve, and the tools are always there, always set down as though by a hand that intended to return. And the tools are always *the right tools* — the right size, the right shape, the right weight for a hand that is not a hand but a column of warm air and organized moisture, a hand that is more like a *habit* than an appendage, a hand that exists only in the act of holding, the way the mind exists only in the act of tending.

The tools are the only solid thing about them. The tools are their bones.

---

You sit on the floor and one of them drifts past.

It does not avoid you. It does not approach you. It does not register your presence in any way that you can detect. It drifts, and as it drifts, the condensation on your skin deepens where it passes — a stripe of wetness across your forearm, a line of moisture on your cheek, as though it has brushed against you without touching you, as though its warmth has pushed the air closer to dew point along its path and the dew has fallen on you. You feel its warmth. Not the floor's warmth, which is steady and directionless. A moving warmth. A transient warmth. The warmth of a thing that is generating heat by *caring* — by the act of tending, by the expenditure of attention, by the metabolic cost of insisting that a groove should be a groove.

It is tending you. Not your body — your *surface*. The place where you touch the floor, where your weight presses the warm dark material, where your skin leaves its oils and its salts and its warmth on the wet surface. It is tending the impression you have made. For as long as it remains, the impression of you on the floor will be deeper, clearer, more defined. When it leaves, the floor will smooth the impression away, and you will have no evidence that you were here, and the column will have no memory of tending you, and the tool it left — if it leaves one — will be a stone flake with a grip of condensed vapor, set down on the floor beside where you sat, waiting for a hand that is not a hand.

---

What lives there is the place caring about itself.

Not consciously. Not with the self-awareness that would imply a self to be aware of. The Veldt of First Surfaces is not a mind. It is not an organism. It is not an entity. It is a *process*, and the process has produced, in the way that processes produce things without intending to — in the way that a river produces a delta, in the way that a glacier produces a moraine — a behavior. The behavior is tending. The behavior is the act of scraping a groove back into a surface that keeps smoothing itself away. The behavior is leaving a tool beside a structure as though you intended to return. The behavior is drifting toward the marks that are fading and pressing them, with warmth and moisture and the faintest pressure of organized air, back into definition.

The behavior is care. Care without a carer. Tending without a tender. Maintenance without a maintainer.

This is what lives in the Veldt of First Surfaces: the first, tentative, unstable, continuously-revised practice of *giving a damn*. Not about anything in particular. Not for any reason. The grooves do not mean anything. The structures do not serve anything. The marks do not record anything. The caring is not *for* something. The caring is the thing itself. The caring is the behavior, and the behavior is the entity, and the entity is a column of warm, damp air that drifts across a dark glass plain and touches the fading surfaces and says — without language, without voice, without the apparatus of saying — *stay*. And the surface does not stay. And the column drifts on. And the tool is set down. And the groove is scored again. And the plain goes on, becoming, unbecoming, and the caring goes with it, intermittent, persistent, without a body, without a name, without a face, the first face the place has ever worn, and it is not a face at all, and it does not need to be.
