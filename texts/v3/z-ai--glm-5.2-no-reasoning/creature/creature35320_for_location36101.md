# Who Lives Here

## The People of the Tharrah

### First Encounter

You will not see them first. You will see the evidence — a trimmed edge where the tharrah has been cut, clean and deliberate, the wound sealed with a resin that has dried to a dark, glassy line. A section of secondary growth trained into a shape too regular to be accidental: a windbreak, a water-catch, a wall. A path worn into the living surface by the passage of feet over years, the bark smoothed, the underlying wood slightly depressed, a groove in the continent that can only mean someone walks here repeatedly.

Then you will hear them. Or you will think you hear them. A pattern in the noise — clicks and a sustained tone that rises and falls, interlocking with the insect chorus, fitting into a gap in the sound the way a key fits a lock. You will not be able to separate it from the ambient noise. You will not be able to point to it and say *there, that is a voice.* But something in the pattern will strike you as intentional. A shape in the sound. Meaning, buried in the noise the way their houses are buried in the growth.

And then, if they choose, you will see them.

They will be close. They are always close, by the time you notice them. They do not announce themselves across distance — the sound of this place makes that impossible — and they do not travel in the open when they can travel under cover. They will have been aware of you for some time. They will have been watching. And the decision to reveal themselves is exactly that: a decision, made deliberately, based on a calculation about you that you cannot hear because it was conducted in clicks and tones woven into the biomass-sound around you.

## What They Look Like

They are small. Not remarkably so — not a separate race, not a distinct species, but small by the standards of the dry lands to the south. The men average perhaps a hundred and fifty centimeters. The women slightly less. This is not genetics. This is adaptation across centuries. Large bodies are expensive here. They require more food, more water, more space. They are harder to cool in the wet heat. They are louder on the responsive surface of the biomass, their footfalls deeper, their weight more likely to trigger the slight give-and-take of the tharrah surface that signals presence to anything listening. Small is efficient. Small is quiet. Small survives.

They are dark. Not the brown of sun-darkened skin but something deeper, a darkness that has its own quality, a richness of tone that comes from generations of living under the patchy canopy of the biomass where light is filtered through layers of leaf and the UV that does get through is intense and direct. Their skin is adapted to this specific light: dark enough to protect, efficient at producing vitamin D from the green-filtered wavelengths that dominate. The color has a warmth to it, a quality that the available light brings out — in the greenish-gold of the understory, their skin looks almost to glow.

Their hands are remarkable. This is the feature you notice in detail, once you are close enough to notice detail. Their hands are proportionally larger than you would expect for their size, the fingers longer, the joints more pronounced, the webbing between the fingers — yes, there is webbing, slight but visible, between the second and third fingers of both hands — a genetic marker that has no clear origin but that serves them here. The hands are their primary tools. They live in a world that can be shaped by hand, that rewards manual dexterity, that requires constant tactile engagement. They prune. They carve. They weave. They grip. They hold. The hands are never still.

They wear little. The heat and humidity make clothing a liability rather than a comfort — fabric stays wet, chafes, rots, harbors parasites. What they wear is functional and minimal: cordage belts that hold tools, brief coverings of woven fiber for contexts where the social rules require them, occasionally a cape or wrap of treated leaf-material for the worst of the afternoon rain. Their bodies are marked — scars, deliberate decorative cuts, pigmented patterns applied with resins and plant-dyes, the marks of identity and affiliation that serve the function clothing serves elsewhere. You can read a person's history in their markings: where they are from, who their people are, what they have done.

Their hair is worn in ways that serve function. Tight braids, close to the skull, that do not catch on branches or provide grip for the flying things that might otherwise land on a person's head. The braids are sometimes threaded with fiber or small carved beads, which serve as markers — personal adornment, yes, but also as identity signals in a world where visual recognition at distance is difficult and where the primary identification system is auditory.

## How They Speak

This is the thing you must understand, and it is the thing that outsiders almost never understand, because it requires a rewiring of what you think language is.

Their language is not separate from the environment. It is not a signal laid over the noise, distinct from it, competing with it. It is woven into the noise. It uses the noise. The clicks and tones and whistles of their speech are tuned to interleave with the insect chorus, to occupy the same frequency bands, to match the rhythmic patterns of the biomass-sound so precisely that an untrained ear cannot distinguish the signal from the carrier. Their language is encrypted by the environment. It is hidden in the sound of the world.

This is not metaphor. A person speaking here produces sounds that fit into the gaps in the ambient noise — the brief silences between insect pulses, the spaces between bird calls, the rhythmic pauses in the creaking of the tharrah. They time their utterances to align with the biomass's own rhythms, so that their speech arrives as a variation on what is already happening, a modulation of the existing sound rather than an addition to it. To hear them, you must learn to hear the environment as they hear it: not as background, but as context, as structure, as the medium in which meaning lives.

The clicks are consonants. They are produced in the back of the throat and against the palate, sharp percussive sounds that cut through the ambient noise without adding to its volume. There are dozens of them — the language has a phoneme inventory that dwarfs any dryland tongue, because the sound environment rewards differentiation at the level of timing and pitch rather than volume. A click produced at one pitch means one thing; the same click at a slightly different pitch means another. The tones are vowels — sustained notes that carry through the noise, that resonate with the tharrah itself, that use the living wood as an amplifier. A spoken tone can travel through the biomass surface and be felt as vibration by a listener standing some distance away, their bare feet registering the message through the same medium that carries it.

They can hold conversations at distances that would require shouting in a quieter environment. Two people a hundred meters apart, standing on the tharrah surface, can speak in what an outsider would perceive as near-silence — a few clicks, a few tones, inaudible to anyone not trained to distinguish them from the ambient sound. The conversation is private not because it is quiet but because it is unrecognizable. It is language as camouflage.

Children learn it the way children learn any language: by listening, by imitating, by gradual acquisition of the phonemic distinctions and the timing patterns. But here the learning is environmental. A child must learn the sounds of the biomass — the insect patterns, the bird calls, the water-sounds, the tharrah-creaks — before they can learn to speak within them. Language acquisition and environmental awareness are the same process. A child who cannot hear the environment cannot hear the language. A child who cannot parse the noise cannot parse the speech. The two develop in tandem, and by the time a child is speaking fluently, they are also reading the biomass the way a drylander reads the sky — as a system of signs that carries information about what is happening, what is coming, what to do.

They have words for sounds that have no words in other languages. The specific pitch-shift of the insect chorus when a large predator is moving through the upper growth. The particular gurgling of a water-channel that is partially blocked. The difference between the creak of tharrah growing and the creak of tharrah dying. They have dozens of words for rain — not for rain itself but for the different qualities of rain, the different sounds it makes on different surfaces, the different meanings it carries. They have words for the deep sounds, the low moans from below, and these words are not spoken casually. They are marked words, weighted words, words that carry the freight of caution and respect.

## How They Live

The settlements are small. Three to seven structures, housing perhaps fifteen to forty people. The structures are grown, not built — a practice that produces architecture of a kind found nowhere else.

A house begins with a wound. A cut made in the tharrah surface, a section of bark and outer wood removed to expose the living tissue beneath. The cut is shaped — not arbitrarily, but with a plan, the cut lines determining the eventual walls and partitions of the room. The tharrah responds to the wound by sealing its edges, producing resin, redirecting its growth. The people guide this response. They apply resins to direct the healing. They insert thin wedges of hardened wood to keep certain cuts open while allowing others to close. They train secondary growth — vines, aerial roots, the thinner faster-growing plants that colonize the tharrah's surface — to fill in walls and roofs, weaving them by hand into dense mats that shed rain and provide insulation.

The result is a living room. The walls are alive. The floor is alive. The ceiling, if there is one, is a trained mat of interwoven growth that continues to photosynthesize, producing oxygen, consuming the carbon dioxide of the inhabitants' breath. The house breathes with the people in it. The interior temperature is regulated by the tharrah's own transpiration — slightly cooler than the surface, the living wood pulling heat from the air and releasing it through its own vascular system. The house is not a thing that was made and now exists. It is a process, an ongoing negotiation between the people and the plant, and it requires maintenance the way a garden requires maintenance. Neglect it, and it closes. Tend it, and it persists.

A settlement is a cluster of these living rooms, connected by paths worn into the surface, surrounded by the managed growth that provides the settlement's food and materials. The management is subtle. They do not farm in rows. They do not clear fields. They encourage. Specific plants are pruned to produce more of what is useful — larger fruiting bodies, longer fibers, thicker leaves. Specific animals are trapped and relocated or, more rarely, killed and eaten. The relationship is one of curation rather than cultivation. The biomass provides. The people select.

Their diet is varied and almost entirely local. They eat the fruits and seeds of the secondary growth — dozens of varieties, each with its own season, its own ripening signal that the people have learned to read. They eat the insects — certain species, roasted on small fires, a significant source of protein. They eat the smaller animals: the slow-moving ground-dwellers that can be trapped, the fliers that can be netted in their roosts, the swimmers that can be caught in the water-channels with woven baskets. They eat fungi, which grow in bewildering variety on the decomposing sections of the biomass. They eat the tharrah itself, in small quantities — the young growth, the tender outer rings, harvested sparingly, cooked to break down its fibers, a starchy staple that fills the belly when nothing else does.

They cook with fire, carefully. Fire here is a tool of last resort, a controlled danger, a thing that is never left unattended and never used without water nearby. The fires are small — barely more than embers, contained in shallow pits lined with damp fiber and stone, burning for the minimum time necessary. The people understand fire the way a drylander might understand a venomous snake: useful, potentially, but never to be trusted. A fire that escapes its containment in the biomass is not a campfire that spreads. It is a threat to hectares of living wood, to the structural integrity of the surface, to the ecosystem that keeps everyone alive. The taboo against careless fire is one of their strongest. A child who misuses fire is punished severely. An adult who allows a fire to spread is exiled — and exile, here, is a serious matter, a sentence of solitary travel across a vast and hostile surface.

## The Social World

The basic unit is the household. A household is not a family in the strict sense — it is a group of people who share a structure or cluster of structures, who cook together, who maintain their section of the biomass together. Households are flexible. People move between them. Children are raised by whoever is present, not necessarily by their biological parents, and the concept of biological parentage is less important than the concept of household membership. You belong to the household that feeds you, that houses you, that teaches you. You owe loyalty to that household. You owe labor to that household. When you move — and people do move, joining a new household, forming a new one, relocating to a different settlement — you leave the old loyalty and take up the new.

The settlements are connected. Not by roads — there are no roads, only paths worn into the biomass surface by generations of travel — but by relationships. People travel between settlements, carrying news, carrying trade goods, carrying the social connections that maintain the broader network. The travel is long and sometimes dangerous. The surface of the biomass is not uniform; it has its hazards, its predator-rich zones, its sections of dead and unstable tharrah, its places where the deep sounds are more insistent and the surface-dwellers do not linger. A journey between settlements can take days. It is undertaken when necessary, and it is undertaken with preparation.

They trade in small, valuable things. The dark chert that holds an edge — outcropping somewhere in the basin walls, the location a closely held secret known to a few settlements near the source. Resins with specific properties — sealants, adhesives, medicines, pigments. Pigments themselves, the dyes and markings that signify identity. Finished tools — blades, cordage, containers. Knowledge — songs, stories, techniques, information about the state of the biomass in distant sections, the movement of animal populations, the shifting of water-channels. The trade network is thin and long-range, a web of connections that spans the basin, maintained by the travelers who carry goods and information across distances that would seem enormous for a people with no beasts of burden and no vehicles.

They do not have chiefs. They do not have leaders in the sense that drylanders would recognize. Decision-making is consensual and distributed. A household makes its own decisions about its own section of the biomass. A settlement makes collective decisions through discussion — long, patient discussion, conducted in clicks and tones that weave through the ambient noise, that take the time they take, that do not rush. There is no urgency in the social process that mirrors the urgency of survival. Survival is immediate and individual and requires quick reflexes. Social decisions are slow and collective and require patience. The two modes do not interfere with each other.

Disputes are resolved by separation. This is the simplest and most effective method in a world with vast space and sparse population: if you cannot live with someone, you move. You join another household. You travel to another settlement. You go somewhere else. The biomass is large. There is room. Conflict that cannot be resolved by discussion is resolved by distance, and the distance is usually sufficient.

## The Old Ones

There are people here who are old in a way that drylanders would not recognize. The biomass changes the experience of age. The heat, the moisture, the constant immune challenge of living in and on a living surface — these things wear the body. But the body adapts. The people who survive to old age — and many do not; the mortality rate is high, particularly among children, particularly from the parasites and infections that the warm wet environment encourages — the people who survive are the ones whose bodies have reached an accommodation with the biomass, whose immune systems have learned its particular challenges, whose hands and feet have developed the calluses and sensitivity that the environment demands.

An old person here is not frail. They are dense. Their bodies have been tested and refined by decades of living in a place that does not tolerate weakness. They are smaller than they were in youth — not from shrinkage but from efficiency, the body paring itself down to what is needed, what works, what survives. Their markings are deep and layered, the accumulated record of a lifetime. Their hands are extraordinary — the most capable hands in the community, the most practiced, the most sensitive.

They are the keepers of knowledge. Not in books — they have no books, no writing — but in memory and in practice. An old person knows the biomass the way a drylander knows the seasons: as a pattern, a system, a thing that moves and changes and can be predicted. They know which sections of tharrah are dying. They know where the water-channels are shifting. They know the animal populations — which are increasing, which are declining, which are behaving in unusual ways. They know the deep sounds, and they know what the deep sounds mean, and they know this not from study but from a lifetime of listening, of feeling the tharrah under their feet, of paying attention to the thing they live on.

They are the ones who hear the biomass talking.

## The Listeners

This is the other thing. The thing that separates the people of the biomass from any other people who have ever lived.

Some of them — not all, not many, but some — can hear the deep. Not just the low moaning calls that come up through the tharrah, that everyone hears, that everyone feels in the soles of their feet on quiet nights. Those sounds are ambient. Those sounds are the biomass's surface phenomena, the creaking and groaning of a living structure under tension. What the Listeners hear is different.

A Listener is someone whose auditory sensitivity extends below the range that most people can perceive. They hear — or they feel, and the distinction between hearing and feeling is not clear here, because the tharrah transmits vibration as sound — they perceive patterns in the deep sounds. Not random groaning. Not the stochastic noise of a large structure under stress. Patterns. Rhythms. Repeating sequences that vary in ways that carry — they insist on this word — *meaning.*

The Listeners are not leaders. They are not shamans. They are not priests. They are people with a specific perceptual ability, identified young, trained in its use, given the time and space to pay attention to what they hear. They live in the settlements but they spend significant time alone, standing or sitting on the tharrah surface, their bare feet in contact with the wood, their bodies still, their attention turned downward. They listen.

What they hear is not consistent. The patterns are slow — slower than speech, slower than animal calls, slower than any human communication. A single "phrase" in the deep language, if it is a language, can take hours to complete. A "conversation" — if that is what it is — can span days, weeks. The Listeners do not claim to understand what the biomass is saying. They claim to perceive that it is saying something. They claim that the patterns are not random. They claim that the deep sounds have structure, and that the structure changes, and that the changes correlate with events — with shifts in the water-channels, with the death and regrowth of tharrah sections, with the movements of the deep-dwellers, with the seasons of the rain.

The other people respect this. They do not worship the Listeners. They do not treat them as oracles. But they listen to what the Listeners report, and they incorporate it into their decisions about where to build, where to travel, where to avoid. If a Listener says that the deep sounds are agitated in a particular region, the settlement does not expand in that direction. If a Listener says that the deep sounds are quiet — truly quiet, absent, which is rare and notable — the settlement pays attention, because the absence of the deep sound has historically preceded significant events: a major tharrah death, a shift in the water system, an emergence from the deep.

The Listeners themselves are uncertain about what they perceive. They describe it in terms that are simultaneously precise and vague — precise about the acoustic properties of what they hear, vague about its meaning. They say the deep sounds are like a language you do not speak, heard through a wall. You know it is language. You know it means something. You cannot make out the words.

## How They Die

People die here. The biomass is not gentle to the people who live on it, and the people do not expect gentleness.

The parasites are the most common killer. The warm, wet environment is paradise for organisms that live in and on other organisms. The people carry a load of internal and external parasites that would appall a drylander — worms, flukes, mites, ticks, fungal infections of the skin and nails. Their immune systems are strong, adapted, constantly engaged, but the parasites are also adapted, also evolving, also engaged in the same arms race. Children are particularly vulnerable. A child who survives to adolescence has passed a filter that kills many, and the survivors are the ones whose immune systems are robust enough to handle the ongoing challenge.

The predators take some. Not many — the people are careful, they know which areas to avoid, they know the behavioral signs that indicate a predator is hunting nearby — but some. The large things that move through the upper growth, that drop from above, that emerge from hollows in the tharrah: they are not common, but they are present, and a person in the wrong place at the wrong time is taken. It is not a dramatic event. It is quick. The settlement mourns — quietly, in the clicks and tones of their speech, in the markings they add to their bodies — and they move on.

The deep takes a few. People who go too deep, who explore the hollows out of curiosity or desperation, who descend into the tharrah layers past the point where the light fails and the air thickens. They do not come back. The people have stories about this — accounts, not myths — and the stories are consistent enough and frequent enough that the taboo against deep exploration is absolute. You do not go deep. You do not go deep. You do not go deep. The repetition is deliberate. It is how taboos are reinforced here: by saying the same thing multiple times in different contexts, by embedding the prohibition in the texture of daily life until it becomes automatic.

When a person dies, their body is returned to the biomass. This is not burial. It is placement. The body is wrapped in leaves and placed on a section of tharrah that is dying, that is beginning to decompose, that is being colonized by the fungi and bacteria that will break the dead wood down and return its nutrients to the system. The body becomes part of the decomposition cycle. The biomass takes it. The tharrah grows through it. Within months, there is nothing to find. Within a generation, the section has regrown, new tharrah rings covering the old, the surface continuous again. The dead become the ground. The ground is the dead. This is not disturbing to the people. It is correct.

## What They Are

They are not a lost people. They are not a remnant. They are not a fragment of some larger civilization that collapsed or migrated. They are what they are: a human population that has adapted, over many generations, to a specific environment that no other human population has ever inhabited. They are as much a product of the biomass as the tharrah itself, as the fauna, as the weather system. They have been shaped by the place. They are continuing to be shaped by it.

They do not know their own history in the way that drylanders know history. They have no records. Their oral tradition is deep but not chronological — it does not track years or centuries, does not preserve sequences of events in the linear fashion that written history requires. What it preserves is knowledge: how to read the biomass, how to hear the language, how to build a living house, how to avoid the deep-dwellers, how to survive the afternoon rain, how to find food, how to navigate a continent that moves under your feet. The knowledge is the history. The accumulated practice of a people who have lived here long enough to become part of the system.

They do not think of themselves as separate from the biomass. This is the thing that is hardest for an outsider to understand. The dryland mind sees the biomass as environment — as a place, a thing, a setting for human activity. The people of the tharrah do not share this view. They are in the biomass the way blood is in a body: integral, circulating, part of the whole. They do not stand on the biomass. They are held by it. They do not use the biomass. They participate in it. The distinction is not semantic. It is the foundation of their world.

When a Listener stands on the tharrah and hears the deep sounds, they are not listening to something external. They are listening to the thing they are part of. When a person dies and their body is placed on the dying tharrah, they are not being discarded. They are being returned. When a child is born and the tharrah surface twitches under the new weight, the child is not arriving in the world. The child is being added to the world. The biomass knows. The biomass adjusts. The people and the place are the same thing, seen from different angles.

This is who lives here: a people who have become the place they live in, who are shaped by it and who shape it, who hear it and are heard by it, who are held by it and who hold it, who will be taken by it and who will become it. They are few and they are small and they are quiet, and they are the human element of a continent-sized living thing, and they know this, and they do not want to be anything else.
