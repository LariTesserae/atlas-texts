# What Lives Here

The first thing to understand is that nothing here considers itself a resident.

That word — *resident* — implies a kind of choosing. A settling. An arrival at a place and a decision to remain. Nothing in the Drowned Cascades arrived. Everything here was already somewhere when the water came, and the water came to them, and they either persisted or they did not, and the ones that persisted did not choose to persist — they simply lacked the capacity to do otherwise. What lives here lives here because it cannot leave, or because leaving was never a concept that applied to it, or because the thing it was changed into the thing that could be here, and the changing was so slow it happened without a moment of transition.

This is a crucial distinction. It changes the character of everything.

## The Kelp

Begin with the kelp, because the kelp is what you see first, and because the kelp is the closest thing this place has to a citizen — a organism that belongs here in the full, committed sense, that has staked its identity on the permanence of this configuration and has built its body around that assumption.

The *Stericola maxima* — that is not its name, nothing here has been formally named, but it serves — is not the kelp you know from coastal waters. Its shallow-water ancestors held to rocks in the intertidal zone and grew toward the sun and lived fast and tore loose in storms and regrew. The *Stericola* does something different. It has colonized the dead trunks of the drowned forest — the pines, the hardwoods, whatever they were, their wood long since softened and hollowed and colonized — and it uses them as scaffolding. Its holdfasts are not the simple disc-clamps of its ancestors. They are complex, probing, root-like structures that penetrate the dead wood, that spread through the softened fibers, that anchor not to a single point but to a network, a lattice of dead organic material that extends across meters of substrate. A single *Stericola* individual — and there is reason to think some of the larger ones are single individuals, connected by rhizome-like holdfast networks, covering tens of meters of substrate — is not so much an organism as a system, a distributed body that has woven itself into the corpse of a forest and now wears that forest like a skeleton.

The fronds are the visible part. They rise from the holdfast network in columns — three, five, eight meters of tough, leathery tissue, broad-bladed, dark brown-green, streaming in the current like banners. In the strong flow between the peaks, the fronds of adjacent colonies overlap and interweave, creating a continuous canopy layer at fifteen to twenty meters depth — a false forest, a forest of algae wearing the architecture of a forest of trees, and beneath the canopy the light is greened and broken and the water moves in slowed, redirected patterns, eddying between the trunks, and smaller organisms shelter in the turbulence, and the whole system sways, and the swaying is not random — it is synchronized, the fronds moving together in the current's pulse, and from a distance the canopy looks like a single organism breathing.

The *Stericola* has no brain. It has no nervous system. It has no capacity for decision. But it is, in a meaningful sense, aware of its environment. Its holdfast network contains chemical signaling pathways — crude, slow, nothing like a neural system, but functional. When a section of the colony is damaged — by a predator, by a falling rock, by the current tearing a frond loose — chemical signals propagate through the network, and adjacent sections alter their growth patterns, redirecting resources, thickening holdfasts, producing defensive compounds. The colony responds. Not quickly. Not intelligently. But it responds, and the response is appropriate to the stimulus, and over time the colony learns its environment — not in the cognitive sense, but in the developmental sense, its growth form reflecting the specific pattern of current and substrate and competition it has experienced, so that no two colonies look the same, each one shaped by its particular history, each one a record of where it is and what has happened to it.

The oldest colonies are old. The holdfast networks penetrate wood that has been dead for thousands of years, and the growth rings — if you can call them that, the layered accretions of the holdfast tissue — suggest ages in the centuries. Some of the colonies have been growing in the same spot, on the same dead trunk, for longer than the current mills have been dead. They have outlasted the engineers. They will outlast the remaining structures. They are not old by geological standards, but they are old by the standards of living things, and they carry their age in their bodies — the thickened, gnarled holdfasts, the scarred frond bases, the layers of growth and damage and growth again, the biological record of a thousand seasons of current.

What the kelp *does* — its function, its ecological role — is provide structure. The canopy layer is a habitat, a refuge, a nursery. The smaller fish live in it. The invertebrates cling to its fronds. The current slows in its interior, creating pockets of calmer water where larvae can settle and spores can germinate. The kelp is the architecture of the shallow zone, the way the sponge-mounds are the architecture of the mid-zone, and without it the community would collapse into a simpler, sparser thing. The kelp knows this in the same way a wall knows it is holding up a roof. It does not know. It holds.

## The Sessile Community

Below the kelp, the substrate is owned.

The word is deliberate. The sponge-mounds — the colonial organisms that crust the rock surfaces in the mid-depths — do not merely inhabit this zone. They own it. They have claimed it, defended it, and held it, and they have been holding it for so long that the claim is no longer contested in any meaningful way.

They are not sponges. That needs to be said. They are colonial tunicates, or something descended from colonial tunicates, though the taxonomy is — complicated. The situation in the Drowned Cascades is that the slow rising of the water created an environmental gradient that moved over centuries, and the organisms that lived in each zone had time to adapt, and the adapters in the mid-zone were tunicate colonies that discovered a niche in accreting mineral substrate and never left it. Over thousands of years, they have diverged from anything you would recognize as a tunicate, and the word *sponge* persists as a convenience, a label for the thing that covers the rocks in thick, living crust, but it is not accurate.

The colonies grow by accretion. Each individual organism in the colony is small — a few millimeters, a filter-feeding body enclosed in a cellulose-like tunic — but the colony as a whole builds structure. Calcium carbonate and silica, drawn from the water and deposited in layers, create the mound-like forms that cover the rock. The colonies grow outward and upward simultaneously, the living tissue on the surface, the mineral accretion beneath, and the forms they create are directed by the current — in strong flow, they grow as fingers and ridges, oriented into the current, their shapes minimizing drag while maximizing the surface area available for filter feeding. In calmer areas, they grow as mounds, convoluted, brain-like, the surface folded to increase area. The growth form is a direct record of the local current regime, and you can read the flow patterns of the water in the shapes of the colonies the way you can read wind direction in the growth of a tree.

The oldest colonies are the largest structures in the Drowned Cascades that were not built by people. Some of the mounds are four meters across, two meters high, and their ages — estimated by growth rate and accretion layering — are in the range of three to five thousand years. They have been growing since before the current mills were built. They have been growing since the water was still rising. They were here when the engineers dove into these currents and set their stones and built their machines, and they are here now, and the machines are dead, and the colonies are not.

The colonies compete. This is the thing that gives the mid-zone its character — the constant, slow, invisible war between adjacent colonies for substrate. When two colonies meet, when their growing edges contact, there is a confrontation. The growth slows. Chemical compounds are exchanged — toxins, inhibitors, signaling molecules — and one colony retreats, or the other retreats, or a boundary forms, a narrow strip of bare rock between them, a no-colony's-land that neither can cross. The boundaries are visible on the rock surfaces — lines where one color gives way to another, where rust meets cream, where purple meets pale orange, and these lines are stable, they persist for decades, for centuries, the borders of territories held by organisms that have no concept of territory but hold it anyway.

The competition is not violent. It is chemical, architectural, a matter of growth rate and allelopathy and the ability to overgrow or undermine or poison your neighbor. It is a war fought in millimeters per year. But it is a war, and it has been going on for a very long time, and the pattern of territories on any given rock face is the result of thousands of years of this slow, silent conflict, and the pattern is stable — the boundaries do not move, the territories do not change hands, because the colonies are evenly matched, because the conditions that favor one over the other have not changed, because the system is in equilibrium.

This is what the colonies are: a landscape of very slow wars, fought by immobile combatants, on terrain that does not move, for stakes that are measured in square centimeters of rock. They are the most patient things in the Drowned Cascades. They are more patient than the mountains.

## The Fish

The fish are the obvious life. They are the movement, the color, the thing that catches the eye. They are also the thing that is hardest to write about, because fish are difficult — they are present and then gone, they are individual and then collective, they are instinct and then something that is not instinct but is also not thought, and the language for what they are does not quite exist.

The *Pelagic Schoolers* — the silver fish in the upper water, the fast ones, the ones that move in thousands — are the visitors. They come through the Drowned Cascades on the current, riding the flow, and they pass through, and they are gone. They do not live here. They live in the open water above and around the submerged range, and the Cascades are a feature of their territory — a place where the current accelerates, where food is concentrated, where the upwellings bring nutrients from the deep — but it is not their home. They are the commuters. They are the people who pass through the neighborhood but do not live there.

The *Residents* are something else. These are the fish that live in the kelp and on the sponge-mounds, the territorial species, the ones that stay. They are not numerous in species — perhaps a dozen species make up the bulk of the resident community — but they are numerous in individuals, and they are the life of the place, the thing that gives the Cascades its particular character.

The most visible is the dominant resident species — a fish that has no common name, because no human has ever needed to name it. It is a moderately-sized fish, fifteen to twenty centimeters, laterally compressed, with a continuous dorsal fin and a small mouth designed for picking. Its color shifts with its environment — in the kelp zone, it is green-brown, mottled, the color of fronds and rock. On the sponge-mounds, it is rust and cream, the color of the colonies. In the deeper zones, it darkens to slate. The color change is not rapid — not the chromatophore flicker of an octopus — but it is real, and it occurs over days and weeks, and it reflects the fish's relocation to a new substrate, a new territory, a new visual context.

These fish are territorial. Each individual holds a patch of substrate — a section of kelp trunk, a face of sponge-mound, a stretch of cliff — and defends it against conspecifics. The territories are small — a few square meters — and they are held for life, which is to say for several years, perhaps a decade. The fish knows its territory. It knows the current patterns, the hiding places, the feeding stations, the escape routes. It knows where the prey organisms settle, where the current deposits the edible particulate, where the cracks in the rock provide shelter from the larger predators. This knowledge is not abstract — it is spatial, physical, encoded in the fish's body and behavior, and it is precise, and it is necessary, because a fish that does not know its territory is a fish that does not survive.

When a territorial fish dies — and they do die, regularly, to predation, to age, to disease — its territory becomes available, and it is claimed, usually within hours, by a younger fish, a fish that has been waiting, holding a smaller, inferior territory nearby, and that moves in immediately and takes over and learns the new space and defends it and lives there, perhaps for years, perhaps for the rest of its life.

This is the rhythm of the resident community: territories held, territories lost, territories claimed, the fish replacing each other in a pattern that is as old as the kelp and as stable as the sponge boundaries. The individual fish are temporary. The territories are permanent. The system persists.

The *Predators* are the ones that make the residents' lives conditional. They are larger — half a meter, a meter, one species up to two meters — and they are built for the current, their bodies streamlined, their fins adapted for station-keeping in strong flow. They do not chase. They do not need to. They position themselves in the current, upstream of a kelp stand or a sponge-mound, and they hold position — effortlessly, the current flowing past them, their bodies adjusted to the flow — and they wait. The current brings the prey. The prey — plankton, larvae, the smaller resident fish — drift or swim past, and the predator strikes, a lateral lunge, a flash of mouth, a contraction of the body that is over in less than a second, and then the current carries the prey — or what remains of it — away, and the predator settles back into its station and waits again.

The predators are not many. A given stretch of cliff might support three or four individuals, each holding its own station, its own stretch of current. They are spaced by aggression — they do not tolerate conspecifics in their feeding zone — and the spacing is regular, almost mathematical, a function of prey availability and current speed and the energy budget of holding position in the flow. They are the regulators. They are the reason the resident fish are cautious, the reason the territories are defended, the reason the smaller fish hide when the current shifts and a predator adjusts its station. The predators are not malevolent — they are not anything — but they are the condition under which the community exists, the pressure that shapes it, the fact that every other fish in the Cascades is responding to, constantly, in every decision it makes about where to be and when to move and how to behave.

There is a fourth category — the *Transients*. These are the fish that come through the Cascades irregularly, that do not live here and do not commute here but appear, sometimes, in the water column or on the substrate, and then disappear. Some of these are deep-water species, drawn up the slopes by the upwellings, disoriented by the light and the current and the unfamiliar topography. Some are open-ocean wanderers, carried by the large-scale currents into the Cascades and then out again. Some are — oddities. A fish that appears in the kelp canopy and stays for three days and is never seen again. A species that appears once in a decade, in the deep channel between two peaks, and is recorded by no one and remembered by nothing except the colony it briefly grazed on. The Transients are the reminder that the Drowned Cascades is not a closed system, that the water connects it to the larger ocean, that things can arrive from outside and leave to outside, and that the completeness of the place is not absolute.

## The Things That Are Not Fish

The fish are the visible life. The visible life is not most of the life.

The invertebrate community of the Drowned Cascades is enormous, and enormously varied, and it occupies every niche the fish do not — and several the fish do, as well, because the fish are latecomers here, evolutionarily, and the invertebrates were established long before the first fish adapted to the submerged range.

The crabs are the most visible invertebrate. They are not the crabs you know — not the broad, flat, scuttling crabs of tide pools and dinner plates. They are something else. Their bodies are narrow, elevated, and their legs are long — disproportionately long, stilts that lift the body clear of the substrate, that allow them to walk over the surface of the sponge-mounds without contacting them, that give them a gait that is more walking than scuttling, more spider than crab. They pick their way across the colonies, their legs finding the ridges and folds, their claws reaching into the gaps and channels to extract the smaller organisms that live there — worms, copepods, the juveniles of other species. They are the walkers of the mid-zone. They are everywhere, and they are constantly moving, and their movement is the small-scale movement of the Cascades — the movement that happens in the centimeters, the movement of things that live in the texture of the substrate rather than in the water column.

The crabs are territorial in a different way than the fish. They do not hold fixed territories — they hold routes. Each individual has a circuit, a regular path across the substrate that it walks, daily, visiting the same feeding stations, the same sheltering crevices, the same stretches of sponge-mound. The circuits are stable — the same crab walks the same route for years — and they are defended, not by direct aggression but by a system of spacing and timing that ensures no two crabs are at the same station at the same time. This is not cooperation. It is the result of a simple rule — if another crab is at your station, wait — and the rule produces, at the population level, a pattern of coordinated movement that looks, from outside, like a schedule, like a plan, like something that was organized. It was not organized. It emerged.

There are worms. There are always worms. The polychaetes of the Drowned Cascades are not dramatic — they are small, mostly, a few centimeters, and they live in the substrate, in the sediment that accumulates in the lee of the mounds, in the cracks and crevices of the rock, in the dead wood of the drowned forest. They are the decomposers, the recyclers, the things that eat the dead and return the nutrients to the living. They are essential. They are not interesting, in the way that plumbing is not interesting — they are infrastructure, they are the system beneath the system, and without them nothing else works.

Except that some of them are interesting.

There is a species — one species, in the mid-zone, living in the walls of the current mills — that is interesting. It is a polychaete, a bristle worm, five to eight centimeters long, iridescent, a faint blue-green sheen on its body that catches the light when the current shifts. It lives only in the worked stone of the structures. Not on natural rock — only on the worked stone, the cut stone, the stone that was shaped by the engineers and placed in the cliff recesses. It colonizes the mortar, the gaps between the stones, the spaces where the masonry has shifted and the original seal has failed. It is found nowhere else in the Drowned Cascades. It is found nowhere else anywhere, as far as anyone knows.

The worm does not know it lives in a ruin. It does not know what the structures were. It does not know that the stone it lives in was placed there by hands, that the gaps it occupies were created by the failure of an engineering project, that the mortar it eats was mixed by someone for a purpose. It knows nothing. It is a worm. It lives in the stone because the stone is there and the gaps are the right size and the chemistry of the mortar is compatible with its physiology, and that is all. But the worm is, in a real sense, a consequence of the engineers. It exists because the structures exist. It is — possibly — the last living consequence of the people who built the current mills. Not their descendants. Not their legacy. But a fact about the world that is true only because they were true first.

When the remaining structures fall, the worm will lose its habitat. It will not go extinct — it will retreat to the rubble of the fallen structures, the debris fields on the ledges below, and it will persist there, in the gaps of the broken stone, for as long as the stone persists. But the population will shrink. The range will contract. The worm will become rarer, and then rare, and then — eventually, when the last worked stone has been reduced to sand — the worm will be gone.

The worm does not know this. The worm will never know anything.

## The Things That Are Between

There is a category of life in the Drowned Cascades that does not fit. It is the category of organisms that are not quite plant, not quite animal, not quite colony, not quite individual — the things that exist in the模糊 space between the categories that humans use to sort the living world.

The *Bacterial Mats* of the deep zone are the clearest example. They are not bacterial, not entirely — they are complex communities of bacteria, archaea, and something else, something that has not been classified and may not be classifiable, organisms that live at the boundary of the known. They form pale patches on the dark stone of the deepest slopes, thin, almost translucent, growing in the dim emerald wash where the last light reaches. They are chemosynthetic — they draw energy from the mineral-rich water that seeps from the rock, converting chemical bonds to biomass without the mediation of light. They are slow. They grow at rates measured in millimeters per year. They have been growing, in the same patches, in the same places, for as long as the sponge-mounds have been growing in the mid-zone, and they are the same age, and they will be here when other things are not.

But the mats are not just bacterial. They contain — or they are associated with, or they are hosts to — something else. Eukaryotic organisms, simple ones, that live within the mat structure and interact with the bacterial community in ways that are not parasitic and not symbiotic and not commensal but something else, something that the vocabulary of ecology does not have a word for. These organisms — they have no name, they have no description, they are known only from a few samples and a lot of uncertainty — appear to be involved in the cycling of nutrients within the mat, breaking down the bacterial biomass and returning the components to the community, but they also appear to carry genetic material between patches, to facilitate some kind of exchange, some kind of communication, between mats that are separated by meters of bare stone. Whether this is gene transfer, or signaling, or something more complex — it is not known. It is not understood. It is, at the moment, a question.

The mats glow. This is the thing that you notice, if you descend to the deep zone at night. The bioluminescence is not the bright, deliberate flashing of the fish or the plankton — it is a constant, low-level emission, a faint blue-green light that comes from the surface of the mat and illuminates the stone for a few centimeters in every direction. The light is produced by the bacteria, or by the associated organisms, or by the community as a whole, and its function — if it has a function — is not clear. It may be a byproduct of metabolism. It may be a signal. It may be a trap, a lure for the tiny organisms that drift past and settle on the mat and are consumed. It may be nothing. It may simply be what happens when certain chemicals are processed in certain ways by certain organisms in the dark.

At night, in the deep zone, the mats glow, and the stone is covered in faint light, and the light is the light of something that has been alive for a very long time doing what it does, and the doing produces light, and the light is visible, and that is all.

## The Ghosts of the Engineers

The people who built the current mills are gone. They are not here. There are no remains — no bones, no artifacts except the structures themselves, no trace of the builders in the biological community except the worm that lives in their stone. They left nothing of themselves except their work.

But there is a question that persists, and the question is this: were they people?

The structures are sophisticated. The placement is precise. The engineering — the choice of location, the orientation to the current, the design of the mechanisms — implies a level of understanding that is difficult to attribute to anything other than a technological civilization. But there are no other artifacts. No tools, no waste, no settlement sites, no evidence of a population large enough to support the engineering. The structures exist in isolation, seven of them on the steepest cliffs, and they are the only evidence that anyone was ever here.

Possibilities: the engineers were visitors. They came from somewhere else, built the structures, used them for a time, and left. They did not live here. They worked here. This would explain the absence of domestic artifacts. It would not explain why they left.

Or: the engineers were not human. The structures are old — the corrosion, the growth, the sedimentation suggest ages in the thousands of years, and the design does not match any known human engineering tradition. This does not mean they were non-human. It means they were not any human tradition we recognize. They could have been a human culture that is lost to the record. They could have been something else.

Or: the engineers were the kelp.

This is not a serious hypothesis. It is, however, a thought that occurs to you, in the water, in the current, looking at the structures. The *Stericola* is old enough, complex enough, distributed enough, that the question of its cognitive capacity — not intelligence, not consciousness, but some form of environmental processing that could, in a generous reading, produce deliberate modification of the substrate — is not entirely absurd. The kelp's holdfast network processes chemical signals. It responds to damage. It redirects growth. It learns its environment, in the developmental sense. Could it — over thousands of years, over the slow rise of the water, over the long adaptation from terrestrial forest to submerged forest — could it have developed the capacity to build?

No. Probably not. The structures are stone and metal. The kelp works in organic tissue. The gap between the two is the gap between biology and engineering, and it is a wide gap.

But the thought persists. The structures face the current. The kelp faces the current. The structures are placed where the flow is strongest. The kelp grows where the flow is strongest. The structures harness energy. The kelp harnesses energy. The correspondence is — it is probably coincidence. It is probably the simple fact that the same physical principles apply to both, and the same locations are optimal for both, and the overlap is a function of physics, not of intention.

Probably.

## What It Is Like to Live Here

Nothing here experiences the Drowned Cascades as a place. The kelp experiences the current as a force, the substrate as an anchor, the light as a gradient. The fish experience the kelp as shelter, the predators as risk, the territory as a set of locations and movements. The crabs experience the sponge-mounds as a walking surface and a food source. The colonies experience the rock as a claim and the adjacent colonies as rivals and the current as a source of food and a sculptor of form. The bacterial mats experience the mineral seeps as energy and the dark as a condition and the faint glow they produce as a consequence.

None of this is experience in the human sense. There is no self here. There is no perspective. There is no observer standing in the kelp forest and feeling the current and looking at the structures and wondering. There are processes. There are responses. There are patterns of behavior and growth and consumption that have been stable for a very long time and that constitute, in their aggregate, the life of this place.

And yet.

The kelp knows the current. It knows it in the way its fronds bend, in the way its holdfasts thicken on the upstream side, in the way its growth form reflects the specific pattern of flow it has experienced. The fish know their territories. They know them in the paths they swim, in the crevices they use, in the distances they maintain from each other and from the predators. The colonies know the rock. They know it in the shapes they take, in the boundaries they hold, in the accretions they build. The knowledge is not cognitive. It is embodied. It is in the structure of the organism, in the pattern of its growth, in the record of its responses to its environment, written in tissue and mineral and behavior.

This is what lives in the Drowned Cascades: a community of organisms that know where they are, in the only way they can know, and that have been knowing it for a very long time. The knowing is not awareness. The knowing is persistence. The knowing is the fact of having been here, in this specific configuration of rock and water and light, for long enough that the being here and the knowing here have become the same thing.

The current moves through the kelp and the kelp bends. The current moves through the structures and the structures stand. The current moves through everything, and everything responds, and the responses have been happening for so long that they are not responses anymore — they are the shape of the place. They are what the place is. The mountains are under the sea, and the sea is on the mountains, and the things that live here live in the fact of that, in the specific condition of water moving over stone, and they have been living in that condition for long enough that the condition is not a condition. It is a world. It is the only world. It is the world that is here, and the things that are in it are the things that are in it, and there is no other arrangement, and there has been no other arrangement, and there will be no other arrangement, and the current keeps moving, and the kelp keeps bending, and the colonies keep growing, and the fish keep holding their territories, and the crabs keep walking their routes, and the worms keep living in the stone, and the mats keep glowing in the dark, and the structures keep facing the current, and the sound keeps going, and the sound is the sound of all of it, all at once, all the time, and it does not stop.
