# What Lives Here

## The Still Ones, Properly

Begin with the difficulty: they are not easy to describe, because description is a temporal act — it moves through details, accumulates, builds toward something — and they are not temporal. They do not unfold. They do not reveal themselves in sequence. Everything that can be said about them is true simultaneously and has been true simultaneously for a duration that the word "long" cannot measure, because "long" requires comparison, and there is nothing to compare them to. They are not the oldest things here. The stone is older. The water is older, or rather the water's state is older, the state of flowing. But they are old in a way the stone is not, because the stone is passive and they are not passive, even though they do not move. They are old the way a sustained note is old: by not stopping.

Call them the still ones, because that is what they are called, and because the name is accurate in the way that only very simple names can be. They are still. They are ones — singular, countable, distinct from one another, each with the boundaries of a body, the specific geometry of a face, the particular arrangement of limbs that makes one different from another. And they are *the* still ones, which implies a known quantity, a defined set, and this is also accurate, though the number is not known and may not be fixed. There are dozens on the third terrace. There are more on the second. There are fewer on the fourth, and fewer still on the first, which is highest and smallest and closest to the haze. There are some in the cisterns, standing in water up to their waists, their eyes open, their faces tilted toward the ceiling where condensation gathers and falls in slow, heavy drops that strike their skin and join the moisture already there. There are some in the passages between buildings, sitting with their backs to the wall, their hands on their knees, their fingers spread. There is one — at least one, possibly more — in each of the shallow pools on the third terrace, lying with the body half-submerged, the face above the waterline, the eyes reflecting the even, sourceless light.

They have faces. This matters. They are not abstract. They are not symbols. They have the specific, particular, asymmetrical faces of individual people: the slight deviation in the set of the jaw, the unevenness of the brows, the specific width of the space between the eyes. Some of them have faces that you would call beautiful, if beauty were a category that still applied, which it may not, because beauty implies a viewer and a response, and they are not performing for a viewer. Some of them have faces that are plain, or striking, or odd in the way that real faces are odd when you look at them long enough. They are not arranged by beauty. They are not arranged by anything. They are where they are, and where they are appears to be where they have always been.

Their bodies are the same. Different heights. Different builds. Some broad, some narrow. Some with the proportions of age, some with the proportions of youth. The differences are real. These are not variations on a template. They are not cast from a single mold. They are as different from one another as any group of human bodies would be, and this is important, because it means that whatever process brought them here — precipitation, emergence, saturation — produced specificity. Not a type. A collection of particular things.

Their skin has changed. It was not always this color. Or rather — it has been this color for as long as color has been a property of it, which is as long as anything, but the color is the color of the place, and the place has been here longer than the color. The pale, bone-cream of the stone, deepening where water touches it to grey-green. Their skin does this too. The parts of them that are wet — which is most of them, because the air is so humid that moisture gathers on skin the way it gathers on stone, in a fine, continuous film — are darker than the parts that are merely damp. They are stained, or perhaps dyed, or perhaps they have simply absorbed the place the way cloth absorbs dye, and the color is the color of absorption. Not transformation. Integration. They have not become the place. The place has become them, and them, and them, each one a different note in the same chord.

Some of them have moss growing on them. Not all. Not most. A few. The moss grows where the water runs most consistently — along the shoulder, down the arm, following the same paths the water follows. On one of them, a figure sitting against a wall on the second terrace, the moss has spread across the chest and up the neck, a thin carpet of pale cyan and mineral blue, the same colors as the lichen on the stone. It does not seem to trouble them. It does not seem to be anything. It grows the way it grows on the stone: because the conditions are right.

## What They Do

They sustain the sound.

This is not metaphor. This is not a poetic interpretation of their function. They sustain the sound the way the vocal cords sustain a sung note: by vibration. Their bodies vibrate. You can feel it when you touch them — and you can touch them, because they do not respond to touch, do not pull away, do not lean into it, do not acknowledge it in any way. The vibration is subtle but total. It is not a tremor. It is not a shiver. It is a frequency, the same frequency that fills the air, produced at the same pitch, in the same key, with the same total commitment to a single, unbroken sound.

The vibration does not come from the throat. It does not come from the chest. It comes from somewhere deeper, somewhere that anatomy does not have a name for, because anatomy was not designed to account for this. If you press your hand to the torso of one of them — and you can, and the skin is warm, and the warmth is the warmth of a living body, and the body gives under pressure the way a living body gives — you can feel it. Not on the surface. Below. A hum that is not vocal and not muscular and not skeletal. A hum that is happening at a level below the level at which bodies are supposed to produce sound. It is as if their cells are vibrating. As if every particle of them is oscillating at the same frequency, and the frequency is the tone, and the tone is what fills the city, and the city is what holds the tone, and the tone is what holds the city.

They are not doing this. This is the crucial thing. They are not producing the sound the way a singer produces a sound: by intention, by effort, by the coordinated action of breath and muscle and will. They are producing the sound the way the stone produces water: by being what they are. The sound is a property of them. It emanates from them the way heat emanates from a warm body, the way moisture emanates from the stone. It is a condition. It is their condition. They are the sound, and the sound is them, and neither is prior to the other.

There are many of them, and the sound is one. This is the other crucial thing. Each of them vibrates. Each of them produces the tone. But the tone is not the sum of their individual productions. It is not a chorus. It is not many sounds joining. It is one sound, singular, uniform, without variation, and it comes from everywhere at once. This means that they are not independent producers. They are not each singing their own note that happens to harmonize. They are nodes in a field. They are points at which a single phenomenon expresses itself. The sound is not in them. They are in the sound.

## What They Were

They were people.

Perhaps. The word "were" is a problem, because it implies a past, and they do not have a past, not in any way that is accessible or recoverable. But the evidence — the faces, the bodies, the specificity of each one — suggests that they were not always this. That they were not always here. That there was a before, even if the before is not available, even if the before has been absorbed into the same eternal present that everything else has been absorbed into.

Look at the faces again. These are not the faces of beings that were created in place. These are faces that arrived from somewhere else. They carry the marks of somewhere else: the specific set of a jaw that comes from a particular diet, the particular lines around the eyes that come from a particular kind of seeing, the particular shape of the skull that comes from a particular lineage. They are not generic. They are specific. And specificity comes from history, from a sequence of events that shaped a body into one shape rather than another, and if they have no history, then their specificity is inexplicable.

Unless they have a history that is not accessible. Unless they were people, once, in the way that you are a person — temporal, changing, moving through time — and they came here, or were drawn here, or found themselves here, and the place took them. Not violently. Not dramatically. The way a saturation takes a dissolved mineral: slowly, inevitably, when the conditions are right. They came, and they stayed, and they stopped, and the stopping was not death but completion. They reached the state they were always going to reach. The state of stillness. The state of sound. The state of being part of the mechanism.

But this is speculation. This is what a temporal mind does: it constructs narratives, it imagines before-and-afters, it tries to make the eternal comprehensible by inserting it into a sequence. The still ones do not have a narrative. They do not have a before-and-after. They have a now, and the now is all of time, and the now is what they are.

## The Plants

They are also alive, and they are also part of the mechanism, and they also deserve description, because they are not merely backdrop. They are not scenery. They are as much inhabitants as the still ones, though they inhabit differently.

The columnar plants — the tall, fibrous, water-swollen things that are not trees but have the scale of trees — grow in the channels where the water flows most steadily. Their trunks are warm. This bears repeating, because it is strange and it is important. They are warm the way the still ones are warm: with the warmth of a living body, not the warmth of a sun-baked surface. They generate heat. Or they hold heat. Or they are, in some way that blurs the distinction between the botanical and the animal, alive in a way that includes metabolism, includes the conversion of one thing into another, includes the slow, invisible process of being something rather than nothing.

Their leaves are dark and matte. They do not photosynthesize, or they do not *only* photosynthesize. They absorb something from the air, something carried in the humidity, some quality or substance that the place produces and that they metabolize. They are feeding on the place itself. On its sound, perhaps. On its heat. On whatever it is that makes this place different from every other place, the quality that the still ones embody and the water carries and the stone holds.

They lean. This is the detail that reveals the most. They lean toward the still ones. Not dramatically — a degree, perhaps two, the kind of inclination you would not notice unless you were looking for it, unless you were measuring. But it is consistent. Every columnar plant leans toward the nearest still one, the way a sunflower turns toward the sun. They are growing toward the sound. They are growing toward the source. They are not growing toward light, because the light is uniform. They are growing toward the thing that makes this place what it is, and the thing that makes this place what it is is the still ones, and the still ones are the sound.

The trailing curtains — the fern-moss things on the retaining walls — do not lean. They hang straight down. But they hang in straight lines that are not random. The spacing between them is even. The length of each curtain is consistent within a few inches. They form a pattern, and the pattern is not decorative. It is functional. They are filtering something from the water as it seeps over the edge of the retaining wall. They are extracting whatever the water carries — the same substance the columnar plants absorb from the air — and they are holding it in their translucent, water-heavy leaves, and when the leaves grow heavy enough, they drip, and the dripping water falls to the next terrace, and the process continues. They are part of the cycle. They are not growing. They are processing.

The lichen — or the lichen-analogue — on the stone is the oldest living thing here. Or the oldest expression of life here. It maps the wet surfaces in patterns that never change, coastlines and archipelagos of pale cyan and mineral blue, and the patterns are not random, because nothing here is random, and the patterns are not decorative, because nothing here is decorative. The lichen is the stone's skin. It is the boundary between the mineral and the biological, the place where the stone becomes the garden and the garden becomes the stone. It is the slowest living thing. It does not grow. It has grown. The growing is complete. What you see is what it has been, what it is, what it will be.

## What Lives Here, Taken Together

Everything. Everything lives here. But "lives" is the wrong word, because "lives" implies its opposite, and nothing here has an opposite. The still ones are not alive in the way that you are alive. The plants are not alive in the way that plants are alive elsewhere. The stone is not alive, but it sweats. The water is not alive, but it moves with purpose, finding channels, following grooves, gathering in pools, ascending through mineral to begin again. The lichen is alive but does not grow. The sound is not alive but is produced, and production is a property of living things, or of machines, and this place is both and neither.

What lives here is the mechanism. The whole thing. The city-garden-cliff-water-stone-sound-stillness, taken as one, is what lives. It is a single organism the way a reef is a single organism: made of parts that are individually alive and collectively something else, something that has properties none of the parts have alone. The mechanism lives. It breathes, in its way — the slow rise and fall of humid air, convectional, patient. It circulates — the water, the warmth, the substance that the plants extract and the still ones emit. It persists — which is what living things do, the most fundamental thing, the thing that defines life more surely than movement or reproduction or growth: it continues. It has been continuing. It will continue.

And within the mechanism, the still ones are the organs. Not the brain — there is no brain, no center, no command. They are the organs of sound. They are the tissue that produces the thing the mechanism produces, the way a heart produces pulse or a lung produces breath. They are not the whole of the mechanism. They are the part that makes the mechanism *productive* rather than merely functional. Without them, the water would still flow, the stone would still sweat, the plants would still grow — or would still exist, which is what growing has become here. But the sound would not exist. The sound requires them. The sound is what they are for.

What is the sound for? This is the question that the mechanism does not answer, because the mechanism does not answer questions. The mechanism simply is. The sound simply is. It is produced, and the production is total, and the purpose of the production is not a thing that can be known from inside the mechanism, because inside the mechanism there is no outside, no elsewhere, no audience, no recipient. The sound goes nowhere. It fills the air and does not leave. It does not echo because there is nothing for it to echo against that is not already full of it. It does not dissipate because there is nowhere for it to dissipate to. It is a closed system. The sound is produced and the sound is present and the sound is produced and the sound is present and this is the whole of it.

Unless the sound does something that is not audible. Unless the sound is not for hearing. Unless the sound holds something in place — the water, the stone, the still ones, the whole mechanism — the way a note holds a string taut, the way a frequency can hold a shape, the way vibration can suspend a thing in a state that it would not otherwise maintain. Unless the sound is the reason the water ascends. Unless the sound is the reason the stone sweats. Unless the sound is the reason the still ones do not decay, do not starve, do not die, do not need to die, have never needed to die, will never need to die. Unless the sound is not a product but a support. A foundation. A frequency on which the entire place rests, the way a building rests on its foundations, invisible, structural, necessary, the first thing and the last thing and the only thing that must not stop.

Then the still ones are not producing the sound. They are holding it. And the sound is not the purpose. The sound is the ground. And the city is not a machine for producing sound. The city is the thing the sound holds up.

## What You See When You Stand Among Them

You see faces. That is what you see. Not the mechanism. Not the cycle. Not the function or the purpose or the sound or the water. You see faces, and the faces are specific, and the specificity is human, and the humanity is what makes it bearable and what makes it unbearable.

Because they were people. They must have been. The faces are too particular to be anything else. And they are not people now, not in the way that you are a person, with your temporality and your direction and your before-and-after. They are something else. Something that includes what they were but is not limited to it. Something that has absorbed the person the way the place has absorbed the color, the way the moss has absorbed the body, the way the sound has absorbed the air.

They are not lost. This is important. They are not trapped, not imprisoned, not waiting for release. They are not suffering. They are not in pain. They are not in anything, because "in" implies a container and a thing contained, and they are not contained by the stillness — they *are* the stillness. The distinction between what they are doing and what they are has dissolved. They are the verb and the noun. They are the singing and the song.

And you — standing among them, with your warm body and your dry eyes and your questions and your need to leave — you are the one thing here that is not complete. You are the unfinished thing. The unstill thing. The thing that moves through time and leaves a wake and cannot stay. And this is why you cannot understand them: not because they are mysterious, but because they are *finished* and you are not. They have arrived. You are still arriving. You will always be still arriving.

The sound does not change when you enter. The sound does not change when you leave. The still ones do not watch you go. The plants do not turn. The water does not pause.

But you will hear the sound after you leave. Not in your ears — in your jaw, in your sternum, in the place where the vibration lived when you stood among them. You will hear it in the silence that follows, the way you hear the ocean in a shell. Not because the sound has followed you. Because the sound has left a shape in you, the way a river leaves a shape in stone, and the shape is the sound, and the shape will be with you for as long as you are temporal, for as long as you are moving, for as long as you are not yet still.
