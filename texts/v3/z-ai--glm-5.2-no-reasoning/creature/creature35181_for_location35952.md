# What Lives Here

---

The first thing to understand about the Hyphaic Expanse is that asking what *lives* here is the wrong question. The better question — the one that the Expanse itself would answer if it could be said to answer anything — is what *is living* here. The distinction matters. The Expanse does not contain life the way a forest contains trees or a sea contains fish. The Expanse is life, ongoing, at a scale that makes the word "organism" feel insufficient and the word "ecosystem" feel evasive. Everything you might point to and call a creature is better understood as a process. Everything that moves is moving because the whole thing is moving. The grazers are a digestion. The fliers are a circulation. The things below the mat are a memory.

But let's start with what you can see.

---

## The Grazers, Again

You would notice them first, or rather you would notice that the surface of the mat is not still, and then you would spend time — more time than you'd expect — trying to figure out why. The grazers are the Expanse's answer to the question of what happens when a surface needs to be maintained at a scale no single root system can manage. They are not animals that evolved to eat the mat. They are extensions of the mat that evolved to move.

Their bodies are the color of the substrate because their bodies *are* the substrate. The upper surface of a grazer is a living layer of hyphae, continuous with the mat, never fully detached. When a grazer moves, it does not walk across the mat — it flows across it, the way a wave flows across water. The six legs are not legs in the sense of articulated limbs with joints and muscles. They are locomotive pods, dense clusters of contractile hyphae that grip the substrate, pull, release, grip again. The motion is peristaltic. The grazer inches forward like a flatworm, like a tongue, like something that has never learned to lift its body because it has never needed to.

The rasping plate is the grazer's defining feature and the only part of it that is clearly differentiated from the mat. It is a chitinous strip, hard and serrated, set into the underside of the body's leading edge. It scrapes the surface hyphae loose, pulls them into a primitive gut, and digests them — but "digests" is misleading, because the grazer's gut is not a closed system. It is a chamber where the material is partially broken down and then reabsorbed into the mat below. The grazer is not eating the Expanse. The grazer is the Expanse eating itself, selectively, in a controlled way, to maintain the surface. What the grazers consume is the oldest, uppermost layer — the hyphae that have been exposed to air longest, that have accumulated mineral dust and cellular debris, that are beginning to lose efficiency. The grazers clear the dead skin. The mat regrows beneath them, fresh and functional.

This is why the grazers move in herds, and why the herds move in patterns that look, from a great height — if you could get to a great height, if you could somehow stand above the canopy and look down — like circulation. The herds follow nutrient gradients in the mat. They cluster where the surface is densest, where growth has been most rapid, where the mat needs thinning. They avoid the soft spots, the thin spots, the places where the substrate below is decomposing and the mat is already fragile. They are a maintenance system. They are the Expanse's immune response to its own growth.

There is something about watching them that is deeply unsettling, and it takes time to understand what. They have no heads. They have no eyes. The body is a leaf, a mat-fragment, a living piece of the Expanse that has developed a leading edge and a trailing edge and a direction of travel, and that is all. There is no front to speak of, no face, no indication of awareness. You cannot tell which end is which until you watch one feed, and even then the rasping plate is on the "front" only in the sense that the front is the direction of travel, and the direction of travel can reverse without the body turning. The grazer simply starts moving the other way. The legs on what was the trailing edge grip and pull. The rasping plate, now at the rear, folds inward. A new rasping plate emerges at the new leading edge, the chitin forming in real time, hardening, beginning to scrape. The transition takes less than a minute. There is no pain. There is no hesitation. The grazer does not know it has reversed direction because the grazer does not know anything.

And yet.

There is a behavior that the grazers exhibit that is harder to explain as simple maintenance. When the storms come — when the wind rises and the canopy tears and sheets of living material fall — the grazers cluster. They move toward each other, they press their flat bodies together, they form dense masses on the surface of the mat, and they hold. The masses are not random. They form in the lee of columns, in the sheltered angles where the wind is weakest. They face the wind with their leading edges, and their rasping plates lock into the mat, and they anchor. They become weights. They hold the mat down against the wind, and the wind is trying to tear the mat up, and the grazers are the difference between the mat holding and the mat failing.

Is this intention? Is this intelligence? Or is it the Expanse, sensing wind through ten thousand bodies at once, and moving its ten thousand bodies to where they are needed? The grazers do not decide. The Expanse decides, or the Expanse does not decide either, and what looks like decision is simply the output of a system so complex that the word "decision" is the closest available approximation but still not close enough.

## The Fliers, Examined

The fliers are easier to look at than the grazers, and harder to understand.

They drift through the open air between columns with a kind of graceless buoyancy that suggests they are not so much flying as falling in a controlled way, or being moved by air that knows where it wants them to go. Their bodies are soft, elongated, vaguely larval — a tube of translucent tissue with a digestive tract visible inside it, pale fluid moving through a pale organ. The wings are not wings in the way an insect's wings are wings, with veins and membranes and precise articulation. They are sheets. Thin, broad, flexible sheets of tissue that catch the air the way a sail catches wind. The flier has no control over its wings beyond extending them and folding them. It cannot steer. It cannot adjust its angle of flight. It extends its wings, and the air does what the air does, and the flier goes where it goes.

They are exquisitely transparent. Not invisible — you can see them, especially backlit, especially when they cross a beam of the pale gold light — but they are the color of the light and the air, and they are thin, and they move without urgency, and they are easy to lose. A flier that lands on a column disappears. Its body flattens against the surface, its wings fold, and it becomes a patch of slightly different texture on the column's skin. You could touch it and not know it was there. You could walk past a column that was covered in resting fliers and see nothing but column.

What are they doing? They are doing what the Expanse cannot do from where it is. The columns are rooted — not in ground, but in the mat, in each other, in the architecture of the structure. They cannot move. They cannot travel. The Expanse is a continent-sized organism that cannot locomote, and it needs to move genetic material from one part of itself to another, because the parts are not identical. Different columns have different growth rates, different resistances, different responses to the storms. If the Expanse is to remain coherent — if it is to be one thing and not many things, or if it is to be many things that cooperate — it needs to share information. The fliers are the information.

When a flier lands on a column, it does not simply rest. It presses its body against the surface, and the surface responds. The column's outer layer softens where the flier touches it. A pore opens. The flier's gut contents — the pale fluid, which is not waste but a concentrated solution of genetic material, spores, proteins, signaling molecules — flow into the column. The transfer takes seconds. The flier's body visibly deflates. The column absorbs the material, and the material travels down through the column, through the mat, through the network, and somewhere, far away, a different part of the Expanse uses it. A column that was growing slowly begins to grow faster. A section of mat that was susceptible to a particular stress develops a resistance. The flier has carried information, and the information has been read.

What is extraordinary is the specificity. A flier that takes off from a column in a warm thermal zone, where growth is rapid and the metabolic rate is high, carries material that encodes that metabolic pattern. When it lands on a column in a cool corridor, where growth is slow, the receiving column incorporates the material — and for a time, a few days, a week, its growth rate increases. It is not learning. It is not adapting. It is being told. The Expanse is telling itself about itself, using thousands of small, transparent, drifting messengers that do not know what they carry and do not need to know.

The clicking, when they land, is the hard chitinous hooks on their feet engaging with the surface. It is a mechanical sound. It sounds like a mechanism because it is a mechanism — the flier's feet are simple, repetitive, and precise in the way that gears are precise. The clicking is not communication. The fliers do not communicate with each other. They are alone, always, each one a single packet of information in transit, and when the information has been delivered, the flier refills — the column it is resting on pumps new fluid into its gut, new material, new information from this location — and the flier launches, and the wind takes it, and it goes.

They do not live long. A flier that is three weeks old is elderly. They are not durable. They are not meant to be. They are meant to be light, meant to be transparent, meant to be carried. Durability is weight, and weight is death. The fliers are the Expanse's lightest, thinnest, most ephemeral tissue, and they are replaced constantly. The mat produces them in patches — you can find the birth-sites if you look for them, patches of slightly different texture where the surface of the mat has bubbled up and opened and released a hundred fliers at once, like dandelion seeds, like pollen, like the Expanse breathing out.

## The Things Below

You feel them before you know they exist.

The thumping. It is not a heartbeat. That was a metaphor, and it is a poor one. A heartbeat is rhythmic, regular, predictable. The thumping from below the mat is none of these things. It is slow. It is deep. It comes at irregular intervals — three thumps, then silence, then one thump, then silence, then seven thumps in rapid succession, then silence for an hour. It comes through the mat as vibration, not sound, and you feel it in your feet and your chest and the bones of your skull before you hear it in your ears.

The mat is thick. You know this because you have dug into it, or tried to. Three meters down, there is the industrial layer, the buried fragments. But below that — how far below? — there are things moving. Not the mat. Not the substrate. Things. The mat transmits their vibration, and the vibration is strong enough that the mat flexes, and the flexing is visible if you are standing still. The surface dips. Rises. Dips again. Not much. A centimeter. Two centimeters. Enough to see.

You do not see them. You do not want to see them. The instinct is animal and absolute: something large is below you, and you should not attract its attention, and the way to not attract its attention is to not move, to not dig, to not investigate. The instinct is probably wrong. The things below almost certainly do not have attention to attract. But the instinct does not care about logic. The instinct cares about the thumping, and the thumping says *large*.

Here is what can be inferred. The mat has depth. The columns have depth. The Expanse is not a surface phenomenon — it extends downward, through the industrial layer, through whatever is below the industrial layer, into a substrate that is not visible and not reachable. The things that thump are in that depth. They are part of the Expanse, or they are part of what was here before the Expanse, or they are something else entirely. The thumping is not the Expanse. The Expanse's sounds are continuous, process-driven, the sounds of growth and stress and movement. The thumping is different. The thumping is discrete. Each thump is an event. Something is striking something, down there, in the dark, in the deep compressed layers of the world. Something is moving, and it is large enough that its movement shakes the mat above it, and the mat shakes you, and you stand still and you wait for it to stop, and it stops, and you do not know if it will start again.

There are other things in the deep. You can infer them from the mat's behavior — patches that are thicker than they should be, patches that are warmer, patches where the hyphae grow in a different pattern, tighter, denser, almost woven, as if they are growing *around* something, conforming to a shape that is not the shape of the mat's own growth. Something is down there. Something with a body. Something that the Expanse has grown over and through, and the Expanse has not broken it down — not yet, not fully — and the shape persists, and the mat conforms to it, and the thumping may or may not be the shape moving.

You do not know. You cannot know. You are on the surface. The Expanse is a surface and a depth, and you are on the surface, and the depth is not for you.

## The Things in the Shadows

Between the columns, in the open spaces where the light is still bright but diffuse, where the air is still cool but still, where the sound drops to a level that is merely loud rather than deafening — there are other things.

You see them the way you see fliers: as moments, as flashes, as shapes that are there and then not there. But the fliers are small and transparent and easy to explain. These are not small. These are not transparent. These are dark.

They are on the mat. They move across it, or along it, or — this is the part that is hard to describe — they seem to be *of* it. A dark shape on the mat that is larger than a grazer, larger than several grazers, that moves with a weight and a slowness that is different from the grazers' peristaltic flow. The shape has limbs. You think. The shape has a body. You think. The shape is dark — darker than the mat, darker than the columns, a brown-black that is almost purple in the filtered light — and it moves, and the mat moves under it, and you feel the weight through your feet.

They do not come close. You see them at distances — a hundred meters, two hundred, across the open space between columns, a dark shape moving slowly, deliberately, in a direction you cannot predict. You do not follow them. You do not call out to them. You do not know if they are aggressive or docile or curious or territorial because you do not know what they are, and the not-knowing is a kind of fear that is not panic but is not comfort either. It is the fear of being in a place where the categories you understand do not apply.

Here is what you can infer, and the inference is uncertain enough that it might be wrong. The dark shapes are the Expanse's structural elements. They are not grazers. They are not fliers. They are not the surface-maintenance system or the genetic-transfer system. They are something that moves through the interior of the structure, through the open spaces, and their weight suggests that they are dense, that they are carrying something — mass, material, nutrients — from one part of the Expanse to another. They are the Expanse's interior transport. They are the veins, and the veins have become animal, and the animal has a body that is dark and heavy and moves on limbs you cannot count and has a weight you can feel but a shape you cannot see.

Or they are something else. They are the things from below, come up. They are the buried shapes, the ones the mat conforms to, freed from the depth and moving on the surface. They are what was here before, or what lived here before, or what the Expanse grew *around* rather than *over*, and they are still here, and they are moving, and the Expanse tolerates them or does not notice them or cannot remove them.

You do not know. You see them in the shadows between the columns and you keep walking and you do not look back.

## The Expanse Itself

And then there is the thing that lives here that is not a grazer, not a flier, not a thumper, not a dark shape. The thing that lives here is the Expanse.

The Expanse is alive. Not alive the way a forest is alive — a collection of individual organisms that happen to be near each other. Alive the way *you* are alive. It is a single system. It responds to stimuli. It maintains itself. It grows. It reproduces, or it does not reproduce, or reproduction is the wrong word for what it does, which is to grow until it reaches an edge and then continue growing past the edge. It does not die. It does not age. It recycles its own material constantly, and nothing in it is more than a few decades old, and the whole thing is however old it is, which is old. Centuries old. Millennia old. Older than the industrial ruins in its depths. It was here before the machines. It grew in the ruins of the machines. It ate the machines. It is the child of the machines and the grave of the machines and the inheritor of the machines' patterns — the column spacing, the regularity, the sense of design that you feel but cannot prove.

Is it conscious? The question is not useful. Consciousness is a word for a particular kind of information processing, and the Expanse processes information — the fliers carry it, the mat transmits it, the columns respond to it, the storms are partly generated by it — but the processing is not centralized, not localized, not the kind that produces a self. The Expanse does not have a self. It has ten million selves, or a billion, or none. Each column is a node. Each patch of mat is a node. Each grazer is a node. The information flows through all of them simultaneously, and the response emerges from the network, and the response is what you see when you see a column lean, or a grazer reverse, or a flock of fliers launch simultaneously from a patch of mat, or a storm begin. The Expanse acts. The Expanse does not think. The acting and the thinking may, at this scale, be the same thing. You do not know. The Expanse does not know. Nobody knows, because there is nobody to know, there is only the Expanse, acting, growing, processing, producing sound and light and wind and life, and the life is itself, and the self is itself, and the difference between those two things is a question for minds that are not continental, and the Expanse is continental, and the question does not apply.

What lives here is a continent that is an organism that is a machine that is a grave that is a process that is a sound that is a light that is a living thing in the deepest and most literal and most terrifying sense of the word "living." It does not care about you. It does not know about you. It is the largest living thing you have ever stood inside, and it does not know it is a thing, and it does not know you are a thing, and the two of you are in the same place, and the place is the Expanse, and the Expanse is alive, and you are alive, and the aliveness is the only thing you share.

You walk. The grazers graze. The fliers drift. The dark shapes move in the distance. The things below thump. The columns groan. The mat creaks. The light is gold. The air is cool. The sound never stops. The Expanse never stops. You walk.
