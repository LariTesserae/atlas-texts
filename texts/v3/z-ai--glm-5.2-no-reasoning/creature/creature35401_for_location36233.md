# What Lives in Wjell

## The People

Start with the hands. The people of Wjell have hands that do not look like yours.

Not in shape — in shape they are human hands, five digits, opposable thumb, the standard architecture. But the skin has changed. Not from disease. From contact. Years of handling living tissue, of pressing palms against wet bark, of immersing fingers in water that carries signaling molecules, of absorbing through the dermis the aerosolized compounds that fall from the canopy — this has altered them. The skin is thicker on the palms and fingertips, not calloused but *dense*, almost padded, the way the skin of a wading bird is dense against water. The nails grow fast and the inhabitants trim them with their teeth, a habit they do not think about. The skin between the fingers has a tendency to stay damp, and in the damp, a faint community of microorganisms has established itself — not harmful, not symbiotic, simply *present*, the way moss is present on a wet stone. The inhabitants' hands have a smell. It is the smell of Wjell concentrated: iron, chlorophyll, the solvent-sweetness, and underneath, something that is recognizably human but changed, the way a person's smell changes when they have been in a place for a long time. Their hands smell like the place. Their hands have become partly of the place.

This is the first thing to understand about the inhabitants: the boundary between them and Wjell is not where you think it is.

There are a hundred and forty of them, or there are not. Counting is complicated. Not because they are secretive about their numbers but because they come and go, and the going and coming is not always clear. An inhabitant may leave through the rent in the mat — and the rent is not always in the same place, and the rent does not always open, and an inhabitant who intends to leave may wait days for the mat to part, and during those days they are still here, still pruning, still tending, and then they are gone, and the settlement does not register the departure in any way you can detect. The mota do not change their routes. The water does not alter its flow. The system does not miss them. And then they return, or they do not, and if they do not, someone else may arrive, or may not, and the number shifts, and the counting shifts with it, and no one keeps a precise record because precision about population is a category that does not apply here. Wjell does not have a population. Wjell has a *number of participants at a given time*, and the number is approximate, and the approximation is sufficient.

They are not old. Not in the way you might expect from people living in an ancient place. They are not young either. They are the age they are, which varies, and the green light and the damp air do not age them faster or slower than any other environment would. What changes them is not the years but the *habit*. Living in Wjell produces a particular kind of person, or perhaps selects for a particular kind of person, or perhaps the place and the person shape each other in a way that makes the question of cause and effect irrelevant. The people who stay in Wjell are people who can tolerate a particular condition: the condition of not being in control.

Not passive. This is crucial. The inhabitants are not passive. They work constantly — pruning, redirecting, harvesting, clearing, watching. They are as active as any people anywhere. But the activity is not *imposing*. They do not impose. They respond. They read the system and they act on what they read, and the action is effective, and the effectiveness is real, but the frame is not "I am building a house" or "I am fixing a problem." The frame is: "I am participating in a process that is larger than my participation, and my participation matters, and the process also matters, and neither of us is in charge."

This produces a quality in them that is hard to name. A kind of humility that is not humble. A kind of confidence that is not confident. They move through the corridors with the sureness of people who know where they are going and know that where they are going may have changed since they were last there and know that they will adapt to the change when they encounter it. Their bodies carry this. Their shoulders are not tense. Their jaws are not set. Their eyes move — scanning the walls, the floor, the water, the growth, reading the settlement the way a sailor reads the sea, with a gaze that is not anxious but is *never still*.

They are not tall. This is nutrition, not genetics. The tubers that are their staple are sufficient but not rich. They are not short either. They are *compact* — the word that comes to mind is *dense*, the same word you would use for the weg, for the root mat, for the living substrate. They are built like the place they live in. Dense, fibrous, economical, with a minimum of excess. Their bodies do not store fat easily, and the ones who have been here longest have a lean, almost fibrous quality, as if their muscle and bone have been compressed by the pressure of living inside something that is always pressing in.

Their hair grows in a way that no one here cuts it. It grows and it dries in the humidity and it stays damp and it forms itself into structures — not braids, not mats, but something between, a natural architecture that the humidity and the aerosolized compounds produce without intention. Some inhabitants have hair that has become slightly green. Not dyed. Not colonized. Simply *tinted* by long exposure to the chlorophyll-rich air, the way a white shirt yellows in tobacco smoke. The green is faint. You notice it on the oldest inhabitants, the ones who have been here longest, the ones whose bodies have had the most time to become part of the system. Their hair is green. Their skin is slightly green at the margins — the nails, the cuticles, the skin around the mouth, the places where the aerosolized film accumulates and is not wiped away. They are not plants. They are people who have been inside a plant for a long time.

## The Language They Use

They speak. Not the way you speak. The tonal fragments — the musical phrases that carry meaning through pitch and rhythm and duration — are not a complete language. They are a *working* language, optimized for the acoustic environment of Wjell, where words are swallowed by wet air and distance is measured in walls and growth and water. The tones carry. The tones bounce. The tones mean what they mean in context — a falling third tone means *come* in one context and *here* in another and *now* in a third, and the context is supplied by the situation and the location and the relationship between the callers, and a stranger cannot learn this language from a book because the book cannot supply the context and the context is the language.

They have another language. A slow one. Used in the rooms, in the spaces where they eat and sleep and sit together. This language is not tonal. It is *quiet*. It is a language of words, long words, words with many syllables, words that unspool slowly in the warm air and hang there and are considered. The inhabitants do not speak quickly when they are at rest. They speak the way the water moves — slowly, with direction, with a current that is not urgent. A conversation in a room in Wjell is a conversation in a medium. The words are absorbed by the walls. The walls are listening, or the walls are not listening, and the distinction does not matter because the walls are the medium through which the words travel, and the words enter the walls and the walls carry them, and the chemical signals that the plants use travel through the same water that the words travel through, and somewhere in the system the words and the signals meet, and what happens there is not something anyone can say.

They have names. The names are not the names they were born with. The names they were born with belonged to a world outside Wjell, a world with sky and weather and dry ground, and those names do not work here, in a place where identity is not individual but *ecological*. You are not who you are in Wjell. You are who you are *in relation to the system*. Their names reflect this. A name in Wjell is a description of a function — not a job, not a role, but a *position in the process*. One person is called something that translates, approximately, to *the one who prunes the north corridor when it narrows past the third load-trunk*. Another is called *the one who watches the water at the deep place*. Another is *the one who speaks to the feryll*. These names are not permanent. They change as the work changes, as the settlement shifts, as the person's position in the process shifts. A person who was *the one who prunes the north corridor* may become *the one who opens the new chamber* when a new chamber is growing, and the old name is set aside and the new name is taken up, and the old name is not remembered with nostalgia because the old name was not a name, it was a function, and the function has changed and the name has changed and this is not loss, it is movement.

There is one name that does not change. The oldest inhabitant — not oldest in years but oldest in *residence*, the one who has been here longest — is called *the one who was here before*. This name is not a description of a function. It is a description of a relationship to time. The one who was here before is the one who remembers the most versions of Wjell, the most configurations, the most vrenns, the most fenn. They are the settlement's memory. Not its historian — the distinction matters. They do not record the past. They *contain* it. They walk through the corridors and they see what was there before and what is there now, and they do not say "this used to be different" because the different and the now are both present for them, layered, the way the peat is layered beneath the weg. They are the peat. They are the compressed remains of versions of Wjell that no longer exist but are still in the ground, still feeding the current growth, still part of the substrate.

The one who was here before is not a leader. Wjell does not have a leader, or if it does, the leader is the system, and the system does not consult. But the one who was here before is consulted. When a change is large — a new emergent structure, a major water shift, a growth that cannot be identified — the inhabitants go to the one who was here before and they describe what they have seen, and the one who was here before either recognizes it or does not, and if they recognize it they say what it did last time, and if they do not recognize it they say nothing, and the nothing is also information. The nothing means: this is new. And new things in Wjell are treated with the same exasperated affection as vrenns — acknowledged, adapted to, not feared.

## How They Live Day to Day

A day in Wjell does not have a shape. The light does not change enough to mark time. The work does not start and stop. The system does not rest. So the inhabitants do not have schedules. They have *rhythms*, and the rhythms are personal, and the personal rhythms overlap and interleave and produce a communal pattern that is not planned but is not random, the way the sound of the settlement is not planned but is not random.

One inhabitant wakes. They slept in their room, on a pad of dried weg-fiber that is replaced when it becomes too damp, which is every few weeks. They wake because they are done sleeping, not because a signal told them to. They sit up. The room is green and warm and dimly luminous and the walls are breathing and the mota are crossing the floor and the water is moving somewhere nearby, and the inhabitant listens for a moment — not consciously, not deliberately, but with the automatic attention of a body that has learned to monitor its environment the way a body monitors its own heartbeat. They are listening for changes. A different sound in the water. A different tone in the skryll. A silence where there should not be silence. They hear nothing alarming. They stand.

They eat. The tubers are growing in a water channel nearby — every room is near a water channel, because the water channels are everywhere — and the inhabitant reaches into the channel and feels along the bottom for a mature tuber, finds one, pulls it out. It is the size of a fist, pale, starchy, with a thin skin that slips off easily. They eat it raw. It tastes like very little — a faint sweetness, a mineral undertone, the ghost of green. It is not delicious. It is not unpleasant. It is *food*, and food in Wjell is not a pleasure but a function, and the inhabitants have stopped expecting it to be otherwise. They eat to continue participating. The system produces what they need. The system does not produce variety.

They work. The work is whatever needs doing, and what needs doing is determined by walking through the corridors and looking. The inhabitant moves through the settlement, pruning tool in hand — the tool is a chemical applicator, a short wand that delivers growth inhibitor in a narrow line, and when they pass it along a growth that is encroaching, the tissue dies and dries and falls away within hours, leaving a clean edge — and they look. They look at the walls. They look at the floor. They look at the water. They look at the canopy. They are reading. The reading is not analytical. It is *perceptual*. They see what is there and they see what is different from what was there before, and the difference tells them what to do. A wall that has thickened inward needs to be pruned back. A water channel that has slowed needs to be cleared. A corridor that has narrowed needs to be widened or abandoned. A room that has begun to flood needs to have its water redirected, or the inhabitant needs to move their things to a drier room, or the inhabitant needs to accept the flooding and find another room. The work is constant. The work is never finished. The work is not a burden. The work is the relationship.

They rest. Not at a set time. When they are tired. They find a dry place — a room, a corridor, a hollow in a root mass — and they sit, and the sitting is not doing nothing. The sitting is *being present in the system without acting on it*, and this is a different kind of participation, a listening kind, a receiving kind. They sit and they feel the warmth of the walls and they hear the water and they breathe the air that the system has made for them to breathe, and this is not leisure. This is the other half of the work. The work is action. The rest is attention. Both are necessary. The inhabitants who do not rest — who work continuously, who prune and redirect and clear without stopping — these inhabitants become *brittle*, the word they use for the state of being too much in one mode and not enough in the other, and the other inhabitants will tell them to sit, and will bring them tubers, and will stay near them until they sit, because brittleness is dangerous in Wjell. Not to the system. To the person. A brittle person stops reading the system accurately. A brittle person prunes where they should observe. A brittle person redirects water where they should let it find its own path. The system will survive a brittle person. The brittle person may not survive the system.

They are not lonely. This is important. There are only a hundred and forty of them, or not, and they live inside a living structure that is always moving and always changing and always making noise, and the noise is company. The walls are company. The mota are company. The feryll in the water are company. The inhabitants are never alone in the way that a person in an empty room is alone, because the room is not empty. The room is alive. The room is doing something. The inhabitant who sits in a room in Wjell is sitting in the presence of a process, and the process is not human and does not care about the human and is nonetheless *with* the human in the way that a river is with the person who floats in it. The inhabitant is not talking to the room. The inhabitant is not communing with the room. The inhabitant is *in* the room, and the room is in the inhabitant, metabolically, atmospherically, and the being-in is the company.

But they are also not lonely because they have each other. A hundred and forty people is not many. But a hundred and forty people who are all engaged in the same continuous project, who all read the same system, who all speak the same tonal language and the same slow language, who all eat the same tubers and drink the same water and sleep on the same fiber and breathe the same air — these people are close. Not intimate, necessarily. Not all of them love each other. Some do. Some pair, some group, some stay solitary. But they are *close* in the way that the crew of a ship is close — not by choice but by condition, by the shared fact of being in the same vessel on the same sea, and the vessel is alive and the sea is the vessel and the closeness is not optional but is also not resented, because resenting the closeness would be like resenting the humidity, and the humidity is not a thing to resent, it is a thing to inhabit.

## What They Are Not

They are not priests. This needs to be said because the description of Wjell may suggest a sacred place, a temple, a site of worship, and the inhabitants are not worshipping. They are not reverent. They do not kneel to the walls. They do not pray to the system. They do not interpret the fenn as messages. They do not treat the emergent forms as revelations. They prune them. They step around them. They wonder about them the way a person wonders about weather — with interest, with curiosity, with the understanding that the wondering will not be resolved and that the lack of resolution is not a failure but a condition.

They are not scientists. They do not study the system. They do not take measurements. They do not have instruments. They do not have theories about how the engineered lineages work at the molecular level. They have *observations* — long, deep, detailed observations accumulated over years of living inside the system — and the observations are accurate and useful and allow them to do what they do, but the observations are not organized into a framework that would qualify as science. The framework is *practical*. It tells them what to prune and what to leave and where to walk and when to move. It does not tell them *why*. The why is not a question they ask, because the why is the domain of the minds or systems that designed the plant lineages, and those minds or systems are gone or inaccessible, and the inhabitants are not trying to reach them. They are trying to live here. Living here does not require understanding why. It requires understanding *how*, and the how is what they know.

They are not unhappy. This also needs to be said, because the description may suggest a dreary place — damp, green, loud, unceasing, with no sky and no sun and no change in the light and no variety in the food and no escape from the wet and the noise and the company of a living system that does not know you are there. And Wjell is those things. But the inhabitants are not unhappy in it, because unhappiness is a response to a gap between what you want and what you have, and the inhabitants of Wjell have narrowed that gap to a width that is almost zero. They want what Wjell provides. They want shelter and food and water and air and company and work, and Wjell provides these, and the provision is reliable, and the reliability is not static but *dynamic* — it changes, it shifts, it vrenns — but it does not fail. The system has been providing for longer than anyone can count. The system will continue to provide. The inhabitants trust it, not with the trust of faith but with the trust of *experience*, the trust of a person who has been in the same river for years and knows where the rocks are and knows the water moves around them and knows that the moving is the river being the river and not the river trying to drown them.

They are not prisoners. They leave. The rent in the mat opens, not often, not predictably, but it opens, and they go through it and they walk on the plain and they see the sky — the actual sky, the open sky, the sky that is not green and does not filter through a canopy — and they breathe air that is not air-plus, and they are away for hours or days, and then they come back, and the coming back is not a return to captivity. The coming back is a return to the place where they are, and the place where they are is home, and home is a living thing, and living things are not cages.

They are not finished. This is the last thing to say about them. They are still changing. Not just their names, not just their work, not just their position in the process. They themselves. Their bodies are changing. Their skin is changing. Their hair is changing. Their perception is changing — the green light has altered their vision over years, not damaged it but *shifted* it, made them more sensitive to the wavelengths that the canopy transmits and less sensitive to the ones it blocks, so that when they leave and stand under the open sky, the sky is not blue but blinding, and they squint, and the squinting is not discomfort but *translation*, their eyes translating between two modes of seeing, the inside mode and the outside mode, and the translation is imperfect, and the imperfection is the cost of living in two worlds, and they pay it, and they come back.

There is a theory, not spoken but felt, that the inhabitants are becoming part of the system. Not metaphorically. Physiologically. The aerosolized compounds that they breathe and that settle on their skin are not inert. They are signaling molecules, produced by the canopy, and the molecules interact with the surfaces they contact, and the surfaces include the inhabitants' skin and the inhabitants' lungs and the inhabitants' blood, and over years and decades the interaction accumulates, and the accumulation changes the body, and the change is not dramatic and not fast and not visible from one day to the next, but it is there. The oldest inhabitants — the one who was here before, and the few who have been here almost as long — have something in their skin that the younger inhabitants do not. A quality. A density. A way of being in the damp air that is not adaptation but *integration*. Their bodies hold water differently. Their bodies process the tubers differently. Their bodies have, perhaps, begun to do something that the system recognizes.

No one says this. No one discusses it. It is not a secret. It is a *fenn* — an artifact that the system produces without intention, a form that emerges without design, a thing that is happening because the system is alive and the people are alive and the living is in the same place and the place is small and the time is long. The inhabitants do not know if the oldest among them are becoming part of Wjell. The oldest among them do not know either. But the oldest among them do not seem to mind, and the not-minding is perhaps the deepest sign of all, the sign that says: I have been here so long that the difference between my body and the body I live inside has become a distinction that no longer matters, and the no-longer-mattering is not loss, it is *arrival*, it is the final stage of moving in, of settling in, of becoming the thing that lives in the place by becoming the place that the thing lives in.

The water moves. The walls breathe. The people prune. The mota cross. The feryll flash. The system grows. And inside it, inside the green, inside the wet, inside the noise, inside the life, the people of Wjell live, and their living is the living of the place, and the place's living is their living, and the difference is a word, and the word does not hold up, and the not-holding-up is the truth of it, and the truth is warm, and green, and slightly damp, and does not stop.
