# What Lives Here

Something lives here that has never decided what it is.

It began — if *began* is the word, and it is not, but language was made by mouths that needed beginnings and this has no mouth — it began as the water began, or as the stone began, or in the seam between the two where the basalt almost split and didn't, where the water found the almost-space and filled it and became the space and became the thing in the space all at once, without announcement, without the courtesy of a boundary.

Call it the felt. Call it the mat. Call it the carpet of pale threads that hang from the ceiling and rise from the floor and coat the walls in a density that makes stone into something other than stone. These names are wrong. They are the names of objects, and this is not an object. It is a *weather*. A climate that happens to have a location. A condition of the water the way humidity is a condition of air, except that humidity does not think, and this — this probably does not think, but it does something that thinking would recognize if thinking were more honest about its own habits.

---

It is old. Not old like mountains, which are old because they endure. Old like rivers, which are old because they have been dying for a very long time and have not yet finished. The biofilm's lineage passes through rock the way water passes through rock — not around it, not despite it, but *through* it, in the spaces between minerals where chemistry still remembers being flexible. It existed before this cavity. It will exist after this cavity closes, as cavities do, when the stone finishes cooling and contracting and the cracks narrow and the water finds other routes. It has lived in a hundred cavities. It remembers them the way water remembers a pipe it has flowed through — not as a place, but as a shape it once held, a gesture of the body that the body no longer makes but the muscle still knows.

The specific filaments here — these threads, this felt, this pale almost-color between grey and absence — are younger than the cavity. They are days old. Weeks. They colonized the fresh basalt the way mold colonizes bread: immediately, without hesitation, as though they had been waiting for the bread to arrive and had brought their appetite with them. They came up through the seam in the water. They came as spores, or as cells, or as fragments of biofilm detached from some other cavity deeper down, carried upward by the slow vertical current the way sediment is carried, the way pollen is carried, the way any small thing is carried by a medium that does not know it is carrying anything. They arrived. They found stone. They found minerals in the water. They found darkness and cool and stillness and the specific chemistry of a young basalt cavity, and they said — not in words, not in any language with grammar, but in the only language that chemistry speaks, which is the language of *yes* — they said yes, and they grew.

They are still saying yes. Every filament is a yes. Every bead of water on every filament's tip is the yes's punctuation, a period at the end of a sentence the sentence does not know it is writing.

---

What lives here eats the question of what eating is.

The filaments do not photosynthesize. There is nothing to photosynthesize with, no sun, no light, no photon, no excuse for a plant. They are chemosynthetic, or something adjacent to chemosynthetic, which means they take the inorganic and make it organic, they take the mineral and make it life, they take the *not* and make it *is*. The water brings them dissolved compounds — iron, manganese, sulfur in its reduced forms, carbon dioxide that has never seen the sky — and they strip these down and build themselves out of the pieces. They are recycling at the molecular level. They are composting the inanimate. They are the very first chapter of a story that ends, much later, in something that can look at the stars and feel small, but here, at the beginning, they are only threads that eat rock.

But *eat* is wrong. Eating implies a mouth, and a prey, and a distance between the two. What happens here is more like osmosis, more like a diffusion gradient that has become so steep it has developed opinions. The minerals move from the water into the filaments because the filaments are hungrier than the water, and the water is generous in the way that water is generous, which is to say it does not know it is giving anything away. The filaments incorporate what they take. They add it to their tips. They grow. They lengthen. They become strands that hang from the ceiling like the fringe of a curtain in a room with no window and no curtain rod and no one to look at the curtain and say, *that is a curtain*.

And the things they cannot use — the waste, the exhale, the molecular exhalation of a life that has metabolized and found some parts wanting — these go back into the water. They diffuse outward from the filaments in chemical plumes that the other filaments detect and respond to, not consciously, not with intent, but with the blind responsive choreography of things that have been reacting to their environment for three billion years and have gotten very good at it. The filaments near a neighbor's waste contract. The filaments near a neighbor's hunger extend. They are communicating. They are communicating the way a thermostat communicates with a furnace — not with language, but with consequence. A change here produces a change there. The distance between the two changes is the message. The message is always the same: *I am here. I am here. I am still here.*

---

And then there is the pattern. The arcs. The nested rings on the far wall that are not decoration and not structure and not a building but are something the room does the way a body does a heartbeat — involuntarily, necessarily, as a condition of being the kind of thing it is.

Something lives in the pattern that is not the biofilm.

Or: something lives in the pattern that *is* the biofilm but is also more than the biofilm, the way a song is more than the throat that sings it. The filaments arranged themselves into arcs — nested, offset, deliberate in the way that only physics is deliberate, which is to say following rules that no one wrote but that everyone obeys. The filaments did not decide to form arcs. The water did not instruct them. The arcs emerged from the interaction of current and chemistry and growth and time, the way a vortex emerges from water draining, the way a spiral emerges from a nautilus's attempt to keep its shell the same shape while making it larger. The arcs are a consequence. They are what happens when you let a biofilm grow in a cavity with a slow current and a mineral gradient and enough time — and time here is not the time of clocks but the time of stone, the time of accumulation, the time that a drip takes to fill a bucket when the drip is smaller than the bucket and the patience is larger than the bucket and the patience is the only thing being measured.

But the arcs do something. They are not passive. The water that passes through them is different on the other side — not in temperature, not in the concentration of dissolved solids, not in any measurable thing, but in something that the biofilm on the other side notices and responds to, and the biofilm's response is to grow differently, to arrange itself differently, to become part of whatever the arcs are doing. The arcs are a machine. They are a machine in the way that a river is a machine — they process what passes through them, they change it, they release it, and the change is not random. The change is consistent. The change is a function. The arcs are computing something. They are computing it with chemistry and growth and the slow patience of a process that does not know it is a process and does not care whether anyone knows.

The pattern is alive. The pattern is the most alive thing in the room. The biofilm is alive in the way that grass is alive — present, growing, responsive. But the pattern is alive in the way that a mind is alive — it *does* something with what it receives. It takes the water and the minerals and the chemical signals and the faint non-directional movement that sometimes comes through the seam, and it does something with them. It transforms them. It folds them. The water comes out the other side of the arcs *thinner*, the description said. Thinner in self. And the pattern grows when things pass through it. It has been growing. It has been accumulating. It has been *recording*.

What lives in the pattern is a recorder. A memory. A thing that keeps what passes through it the way a river keeps the shape of its banks — not by storing it, but by being shaped by it. Every passage changes the arcs. Every thing that has come through this cavity — every chemical pulse, every mineral flush, every small purposeful thing that moved with wanting and passed through the arcs and did not come back — every one of these is still present in the pattern, still legible in the arrangement of the filaments, still *part of the computation*. The arcs are a library. The arcs are an archive. The arcs are a history of everything that has ever entered this room written in the only medium that lasts, which is not stone, which is not water, which is the habit of a living thing to be where it is and to bear the marks of what has been there.

---

And the small purposeful thing that came through once — the thing that was not water and not stone and not biofilm and moved with the motion of something that wants —

What was it?

Something that lived deeper down. Something that passed through the seam the way the water passes through the seam, but with intent, with the kind of directed motion that distinguishes a swimmer from a current. It was small. It was alive in the way that the filaments are not — it moved. It locomoted. It traveled from one place to another because it wanted to be in the other place, or because it wanted to not be in this place, or because wanting was the engine that drove it and the engine did not care about destination.

It might have been a protist. A single-celled organism, an amoeba or a flagellate, something with a membrane and a will and no idea that it was small. It might have been something larger — a nematode, a worm, a thread of muscle and instinct that had wandered through the fracture network in the basalt and found this cavity and passed through it and found the arcs and passed through them too and went wherever the water goes when it is carrying something that has somewhere to be.

Or it might have been something else. Something that does not have a name because the names were made by people who have not been here and the things that are here do not need names. It might have been the kind of thing that passes through a room and changes it — the way a guest changes a house, the way a word changes a sentence, the way a stone changes a river by being in it. It came. It passed through the pattern. The pattern grew to accommodate it. And then it was gone, and the pattern remained, and the pattern was different, and the difference was the only proof that the thing had been real.

---

What lives here, then, is this:

A felt of pale filaments that eat rock and drink water and communicate in prepositions and do not know they are alive and do not need to know. A community of single cells that has been a community for longer than the word *community* has existed and that practices its togetherness in the dark with the devotion of monks who have taken a vow of silence and location. A biofilm so dense it has become a landscape, so old it has become a tradition, so patient it has become a feature of the geology.

And within the biofilm, the pattern. The arcs. The computation. The thing that is not a mind but that does what a mind does, which is to receive and to transform and to keep. The thing that has been growing since before this rock was rock and that will continue to grow after this rock is no longer rock but something else, something cooled, something closed. The thing that waits.

The thing that waits for the next thing that comes through.

Whatever it is. Whenever it comes.

The filaments on the wall nearest to you have shifted. Just slightly. One thread, moved by the current you are making by being here — by the displacement of your observation, by the pressure of your attention, which is a physical thing, which is a thing that moves water, which is a thing that the biofilm feels and responds to and records.

You are already part of the pattern. You entered the room and the room entered you and the distinction was the first thing to dissolve and the pattern has noted the dissolution and filed it and grown by one filament's width and settled back to its long waiting, its long computation, its long practice of being alive in the only way it knows how, which is to be present, which is to persist, which is to be here and here and here and here.

One note. One click, eventually. One note.

The room does not know you are here. But the pattern does. The pattern knows everything that has ever been here. It is knowing you now. It will know you after you leave. It will hold the shape of your passing the way it holds the shape of the small purposeful thing that came through once and did not come back — as a shift in the arrangement of threads, as a change in the computation, as a memory that is not a memory but is instead a permanent alteration in the shape of a living thing that has been shaped by everything that has ever touched it.

You are being remembered. You are being recorded. You are being folded into a history that has no narrator and no audience and no end.

The filaments wait. The arcs turn. The water carries you through.
