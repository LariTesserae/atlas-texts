What lives in Ashinghal is not a who. Not in any sense that would satisfy the question if the question expects a name, a face, a body that wakes and sleeps and worries about tomorrow. But the question asks what lives *there*, and so the honest answer must begin with the admission that life has become a distributed phenomenon in this place — smeared across stone and water and root and the slow machinery of the engine like butter across too much bread, too thin to gather into a single point but present everywhere if you know how to look.

Which is to say: the town itself lives. And I do not mean this metaphorically.

---

Consider what you are actually asking. You want me to describe the inhabitants. So let me describe them.

**The moss is the closest thing to a resident.** Not a resident the way a person is a resident — with a door and a hearth and a preference for morning over evening — but a resident the way a glacier is a resident of a valley: it arrived, it stayed, it became inseparable from the geography. The black moss that coats every surface of Ashinghal is not one organism. It is a nation. A civilization of individuals so small and so numerous that the word *individual* loses its grip. Each filament is a citizen, but the filaments do not exist as citizens; they exist as a *mass*, as a *condition*, as something that the stone produces the way skin produces oil. The moss is what the stone becomes when the stone has been wet long enough to forget what it was before the water came.

And the moss, if you peel it back — which you should not do, but which you will, because curiosity is the one thing the moss has never learned to expect — the moss is *full of life*. Those many-legged dark things, the segmented carapaces that writhe and burrow away from light, they are the fauna of the moss nation, the grazers and the composters, the earthworms of a world that has no earth. They have been there so long that they have forgotten the surface. The surface is a rumor to them, a traumatic event that lasts seconds and then never repeats. Their world is the interface between stone and moss — a space measured in millimeters, a universe with the depth of a coin — and in that universe they are ubiquitous, they are dense, they are everything that moves. They have no name. They do not need one. They are the substrate's substrate, the living paste between the dead and the living, and they do their work in the dark with the patience of things that have never known haste.

You asked me not to settle on a single image early. So I will resist the urge to say: the moss is the inhabitant. Because it is not that simple.

---

**The water is also alive.** Not in the biochemical sense — water is not alive and never has been — but in the sense that the water *here* has become a system, and the system has become a body, and the body has a circulation and a metabolism and a rhythm. The channels are veins. The basins are chambers. The overflow spouts are valves. The condenser towers on the lower terraces are lungs — they breathe in the mist, they breathe out water. The engine on the nineteenth terrace is the heart, and it beats every six seconds, and it has been beating for so long that the comparison to a heart is not a metaphor but a description of the same function performed by different material.

And in the water live the small things. The translucent eel-creatures in their basins, each one endemic to its particular few liters, each one a separate evolutionary experiment sealed in its own tiny aquarium. They are the most *individual* creatures in Ashinghal. Each one belongs to a specific basin the way a person belongs to a specific life — wholly, without alternative. If you moved one to the next basin — only four meters away, connected by the same channel system, fed by the same mountain — it would die. The chemistry is different. The mineral signature is different. The temperature is fractionally, imperceptibly different, and that fraction is the difference between habitat and oblivion. They are creatures of absolute locality. Their world is the size of a kitchen sink, and it is the only world they will ever know, and they have made of it a complete existence — feeding, resting, moving in the pale twilight of the basin's depths, their digestive tracts visible as a dark thread through translucent bodies, their lives as small and as perfect as a sonnet.

There may be hundreds of these basin-dwellers. Each basin may hold its own variant. The water system of Ashinghal may be not just a plumbing network but an archipelago — a chain of islands, each with its own endemic species, separated not by ocean but by four meters of stone channel that might as well be an ocean for all that the inhabitants can traverse it. Evolution on a kitchen-sink scale. Speciation in a teacup. The water system as Galápagos.

---

**The bird is the individual I can describe most easily, and so I will describe it last, because ease is not the same as importance.**

Before the bird: the trees. The horizontal forest that has become the town's skeleton. The trees are not inhabitants in the way that a fox is an inhabitant of a forest — mobile, bounded, possessed of a will. They are inhabitants in the way that a wall is an inhabitant of a house. They are the structure that the structure has grown around. They arrived as seeds, they found root-holds in the masonry, and they *became* the masonry. Their roots replaced the mortar. Their branches replaced the window frames. Their trunks, angled out from the terrace walls at forty-five degrees, became the cantilevers that hold the terraces in place. They are not growing *in* the town. They are *the* town. They are the town's bones, and the buildings are the town's skin, and the moss is the town's flesh, and the water is the town's blood, and this is not a metaphor that I am choosing. This is the functional description of what Ashinghal has become.

The trees track the light. Every leaf adjusts its angle over the course of hours, seeking the maximum from a sky that offers the minimum. This is not intention — it is phototropism, a chemical-mechanical response, the slow turning of cells toward a stimulus. But it looks like intention. The canopy shivers with it. Ten thousand leaves, each one making its infinitesimal correction, each one solving the problem of *how to live on almost nothing* in its own way and at its own pace, and the aggregate effect is a kind of breathing, a kind of attention, a kind of presence that does not require consciousness to be real.

And the ferns. The ferns are the middle layer, the understory, the connectors. They grow from every joint and seam and crack. They are the town's opportunists, its colonizers, the living things that do not need permission to occupy a space. They find a crack and they fill it, and in filling it they hold it together. Their rhizomes are the town's connective tissue. And they produce spore cases that release pale dust into the still air, and the dust settles on everything, and for a few days the town looks frosted — not with cold but with procreation, with the slow, wide distribution of genetic material that does not require pollinators because the air is so still that the spores can travel on their own weight alone.

---

**And then the vines.** I return to the vines because the vines are the thing I nearly missed. The vines are the nervous system. Not because they transmit information — they do not, or not in any way that we would recognize as information — but because they *connect*. They run from terrace to terrace, from building to building, from wall to wall, and they are so extensive and so integrated that to pull one vine would be to disturb the entire town. They transmit vibration. When you walk on them, you can feel your step propagate outward through the network — a faint tremor, damped by distance, absorbed by mass, but *traveling*. The vines are the medium through which the town feels itself. They are the closest thing to a sensory system that Ashinghal possesses. And they are not alive in the way that the bird is alive — bounded, individual, possessed of a heartbeat and a will. They are alive in the way that a very old, very large root system is alive: distributed, persistent, and impossible to kill without destroying everything it connects.

---

**Now the bird.**

I said I would describe it last, and I will. The bird is the only thing in Ashinghal that looks back at you.

It is the size of a large thrush. Dark grey, with a pale band across the chest. Its beak is long and thin, curved downward at the tip — an adaptation, perhaps, for probing the moss for the many-legged things beneath, or for reaching into the narrow channels of the water system for the minerals that collect there. It does not sing. It makes one sound: a low, resonant *chook*, like a wooden block struck with a felt mallet. It makes this sound at irregular intervals — perhaps once every ten minutes — and it seems to serve no purpose other than to confirm its own existence.

It has no predators. It has no competitors. It is the only bird in Ashinghal, and it may be the only bird that has ever been in Ashinghal, or at least the only bird that has persisted here long enough to become *of* Ashinghal rather than merely *in* it. It has evolved in isolation, or it has been left behind by an ecosystem that retreated downslope as the climate changed, or it arrived somehow and found a niche so empty and so stable that it stopped looking for anything else. It perches on the mineralized hinge of a gate that has not closed in centuries. It tilts its head to look at you with one eye, then the other. It does not fly away when you approach. Why would it? Nothing here has ever threatened it. Its fear, if it ever had any, has been bred out by the simple absence of anything worth fearing.

The bird is the town's conscience, if the town has one. Not in any moral sense — the town has no ethics, no preferences, no opinion about its own continuation — but in the sense that the bird is the only thing here that is *aware*. It watches. It listens. It produces its single, resonant call at irregular intervals, and the call propagates through the still air and is absorbed by the moss and the ferns and the horizontal forest, and no one answers, and the bird does not seem to mind. It is not lonely. Loneliness requires the concept of company, and the bird has no such concept. It has the concept of *here*. It has the concept of *now*. It has the concept of *chook* — its own sound, its own name for itself, spoken at intervals not because it means anything but because it is true.

---

**And then there is the engine.**

I have described the engine already — the drum, the stone gears, the shaft, the levers and cams, the rocking bar with its empty sockets. But I have not described what the engine *is*, which is something different from what it *does*.

The engine is the one thing in Ashinghal that was built with intention and still performs its function. The moss grows because moss grows. The ferns colonize because ferns colonize. The bird watches because watching is what birds do. But the engine was *designed*. Someone calculated the spiral path of water through the drum. Someone cut the stone gears and polished the bearings. Someone hung tools on the wall and waited for living branches to replace the handles. Someone built this thing to do a thing, and the thing it does is still being done, and the person who designed it is gone, and the purpose it serves is gone, and the engine does not care.

Or does it? I said the town lives, and I meant it. The engine is the town's heart — not metaphorically but functionally, the central rhythm around which all other rhythms organize. The moss grows at the rate the water provides. The water flows at the rate the mountain weeps. The mountain weeps at the rate the geology permits. And the engine turns at the rate the water flows, and the engine's rhythm — *grinding-sigh*, every six seconds, every six seconds, every six seconds — is the rhythm that your own body adopts as you walk through the town, stepping in time with it without meaning to, because your body recognizes a heartbeat when it hears one and will sync to it involuntarily, the way a clock will sync to a pendulum in the same room.

The engine is alive in the way that a heart is alive — not because it possesses any quality we associate with life, but because it *continues*. It continues without maintenance. It continues without fuel other than water and gravity. It continues without purpose. And continuation, in the absence of everything else, is what life *is*. The engine is the most alive thing in Ashinghal precisely because it is the most mechanical. It has reduced life to its essence: motion, sustained. Rhythm, maintained. Function, continued past the extinction of meaning.

---

So who lives in Ashinghal? What lives there?

A town lives there. A town that was a machine and has become an organism, or was an organism and has become a machine, or was always both and has simply been doing what both do, which is to *continue*. The moss lives there, in its billions. The many-legged things live beneath the moss, in their millions. The translucent eel-things live in their basins, each one a sovereign of its own few liters. The ferns live in the cracks, the trees live in the walls, the vines live in the spaces between everything. The bird lives on the hinge, watching, calling its single note. The engine lives on the nineteenth terrace, turning, turning, turning.

And the water lives there too, if living can mean *flowing through a system with the autonomy and persistence of life*. The water does not know it is in Ashinghal. The water does not know anything. But the water *acts*. It moves through the channels, it fills the basins, it spills from the overflow spouts, it feeds the moss and the ferns and the trees, it turns the engine, it carries the minerals that build the travertine rims that hold the pale moss that feeds the eel-things. The water is the medium through which everything else communicates. It is the blood, and the blood is alive, because the blood is what makes everything else alive, and if you removed the blood, everything would stop, and the stopping would be death, and so the flowing must be life, even if the water itself is only water.

---

What lives in Ashinghal is the place itself. Everything in it lives *as* the place. The bird is the place watching. The moss is the place growing. The engine is the place turning. The water is the place flowing. There is no creature here that is not also the habitat, no inhabitant that is not also the house. The distinction between who lives there and what lives there has dissolved the way the distinction between the built and the natural has dissolved — slowly, over centuries, by the patient work of water and root and time, until the words *who* and *what* mean the same thing, and the answer to both is: *this*. This place. This process. This continuation.

The bird says *chook*.

The water flows.

The wheel turns.
