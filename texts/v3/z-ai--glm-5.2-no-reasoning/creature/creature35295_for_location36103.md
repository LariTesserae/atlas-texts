# What Lives in the Bright Hollow

## I. The Architectural Fauna

The obvious inhabitants are the plants, but they aren't inhabitants — they are the place. A house is not a resident. So you look closer. You look at the bark, at the fissures, at the spaces between.

What lives there is small and careful.

The first things you notice are the bark-walkers. Not insects — something else, something that evolved in the gaps between taxonomy. They are the color of the columns they live on: grey-green, blue-white, mottled with the phosphorescence of the vine-cables. They have too many legs — eight, ten, twelve, the count varies between individuals, as if the number is not fixed but negotiated. They are flat. Almost two-dimensional. They press themselves against the bark and they breathe with it, expanding and contracting in the same rhythm as the column-lung beneath them, and you realize they are not parasites. They are participants. They have been here so long that their bodies have learned the respiration cycle of the columns and they ride it, the way a surfer rides a wave, and the column does not mind them or perhaps does not notice them or perhaps has always known they were there and has adjusted its breathing to accommodate them.

They eat the slime. The translucent biological film on the root-floor pools, the moisture that coats the bark — that is their food. They move across it on their many legs and they consume it and what they leave behind is a cleaner surface, a slightly modified film, something that the mosses and the root-mat seem to prefer. They are cleaners. They are maintenance. They have a role, and they fill it, and they have been filling it long enough that their bodies have become tools for the job — flat, many-legged, luminescent, almost invisible against the surfaces they tend.

There are others. The small furred things you glimpsed in the fissures of the great columns. They are not rodents. They are not marsupials. They are something that has happened to mammalian form in a world without sun, without soil, without anything but plants. They are long and low and their fur is the color of the bioluminescence — pale blue, almost white, with darker patches that match the shadows in the bark-fissures. Their eyes are large and dark and they do not reflect light. They absorb it. You shine nothing on them and yet you can see their eyes, always, because the eyes are where the light stops, and in this place of constant light, the things that absorb it stand out.

They are shy. They watch. They are gone before you can get close.

You see evidence of them — deposits in the fissures, small middens of chewed moss and fungal fragments and the husks of the bark-walkers. They eat the bark-walkers. They eat the moss. They eat the small pale insects that live in the root-floor. They eat each other, sometimes, you think, though you find no direct evidence. What you find is bones in the root-mat, small bones, fused into the floor, being slowly absorbed. Their bones become the floor. Their bodies become the place.

## II. The Birds and Their Logic

The birds are the most visible animals. The translucent-winged sparrows of the shelf-fungi, nervous and quick and always in groups of six or seven. They are not the only birds. There are others.

Higher up, in the canopy-vines, there are gliders. You see them rarely — a flash of something flat and wide drifting between the vine-cables, catching the air, steering with tiny adjustments of a membrane that stretches between what might be arms and what might be legs. They are the size of your hand. They are the size of your torso. The scale is hard to judge because there is no scale up there, nothing to compare them to, just the vine-cables and the light and the drifting moisture and these flat, silent things that move between them. They eat the spores that the shelf-fungi release. They eat the insects that colonize the vine-bark. They drift and they eat and they drift, and occasionally one folds its membrane and drops through the canopy to land on a shelf-fungus, and it walks on the shelf with a gait that is awkward and unlikely, and it feeds, and then it opens its membrane and rises again on a current of warm air from a young column and it is gone.

The sparrows are different. The sparrows have a logic.

You watch a flock for a long time. Seven birds. They move from shelf to shelf in a pattern that seems random until you realize it is not random at all — they are following the growth cycles. They go to the shelves that have just matured, where the spore-production is peaking, where the gills are fattest and the light is brightest. They feed there until the spores thin, and then they move, and the direction they move is always toward the nearest young column. They perch on the young columns and they eat something from the bark — you can't see what, but their beaks come away coated in the alkaline sap, and they seem energized by it, quicker after feeding, more alert. Then they return to the shelves.

They are monitoring. They are managing. They have been doing this long enough that their behavior tracks the growth and decline of the shelves and the columns with a precision that is not instinct — it is knowledge. They know this place. They know it the way a farmer knows fields, the way a shepherd knows pasture. They are not farmers. They are not shepherds. But they are doing something that looks very much like the same work, and they are doing it with the same regularity and the same attentiveness, and the hollow seems to benefit, or at least the hollow does not change in ways that suggest their attention is unwelcome.

There are larger birds. You hear them before you see them — a deep, slow wingbeat, different from the rustling of the sparrows. You see one, once, in a vast hall between the great columns. It is the color of the root-floor — dark, woody, polished. It has a wingspan of maybe three meters. It flies slowly and it flies high, near the canopy, and it circles. It circles the way a vulture circles, but it is not looking for carrion. It is looking for columns that are dying. You have watched it long enough to understand this. When a column begins to lean, when the root-floor around it darkens and softens, this bird appears. It circles. It lands on the dying column. It sits there for hours, sometimes days, and when the column finally falls, the bird goes with it. It rides the column down. And then, in the wreckage on the floor, it feeds. It feeds on the decomposition, on the fungi that colonize the fallen wood, on the insects that arrive, on the small furred things that come to eat the insects. It is a recycler. It is the hollow's waste-management, and it has a three-meter wingspan and eyes that are bright and dark and do not reflect light.

## III. The River Creatures

The stream has things in it. The river has more.

The first things are small — pale, almost transparent, drifting in the current. They are not fish. They are not larvae. They are something between, something that has the body plan of a flatworm and the behavior of a jellyfish and the bioluminescence of everything in this place. They drift and they pulse and they absorb dissolved organic matter from the water, which is full of it — the root-mat leaks nutrients, the slime on the pools dissolves into the streams, the water itself is a weak soup of compounds that these things filter out with bodies that are more membrane than tissue.

The river has larger things. You see them as shadows, passing beneath the surface, moving against the current. They are dark where the stream-creatures are pale, and they move with purpose, not drift. You sit on the bank of the river for a long time and one surfaces near you. It breathes. It has a slit along its back that opens and a puff of glowing mist escapes and the slit closes and the thing submerges again. It was the size of your arm. You did not see its shape clearly. You saw the slit and you saw the mist and you saw the ripple on the water and then it was gone.

The river falls into the deeper hollow and the things that live in the river go with it. You do not follow. Not yet. But you know, standing there at the edge, that the river-creatures are not the only things that have gone down. The root-mat that forms the channel of the falls is full of life — small things, pale things, things that cling to the wet roots and feed on the slime and the dissolved nutrients and each other. They are going down into the lower hollow. They have been going down for a long time. The lower hollow has its own ecosystem, its own animals, its own logic, and you do not know what lives there because you have not gone, but you know that something does because the river is carrying food to it and the roots are carrying structure to it and the light is carrying energy to it, and life does not waste those things. Life fills what is fillable.

## IV. The Fungal Organisms

The shelf-fungi are not animals. But they are not only fungi, either.

The large shelves — the platform-sized ones, the ones that hold their own ecosystems of moss and vine and small fern — those are the obvious ones. They grow, they mature, they decline, they fall. They are predictable. They are architectural.

But there are others. Smaller, more mobile, harder to categorize.

There is a fungus that moves. Not quickly. Not in a way you can watch. But it moves. You see it on one shelf-fungus and then, an hour later, you see it on another shelf ten meters away, and you know it has crossed the gap, and you don't know how. It is a crust, pale and wet, and it spreads across the surface of the shelf-fungus and it digests the outer layer of the gills and it leaves behind a residue that the mosses seem to prefer. It is beneficial, or at least the mosses grow more vigorously where it has been. It is a gardener. It is a compost heap that walks.

And there are the gill-feeders. These live in the gills of the shelf-fungi — the curtains of glowing tissue that hang beneath each shelf. They are small, worm-shaped, translucent, and they move through the gills the way maggots move through fruit. But they do not damage the gills. They live in them. They are part of them. When a shelf-fungus is healthy, its gill-feeders are numerous and active; when it is declining, they leave — they drop from the gills and fall to the root-floor and they burrow into the mat and they vanish, and you do not see where they go, but you suspect they travel through the root-network to find new shelves, new gills, new homes. They are distributed by the floor. They are the floor's messengers, or its emissaries, or its seeds.

The most important fungal organism is the most invisible. It lives in the root-floor. It is the root-floor. The mat of roots and decomposing matter and living tissue that you walk on — it is not just roots. It is also fungus. The two have merged. The root-network and the fungal network have grown through each other for so long that they are no longer separate organisms. They are a chimera — part plant, part fungus, part something that used to be both and is now neither. This merged organism is what runs the hollow. It decides where columns rise. It decides where they fall. It directs the growth of the canopy and the death of the shelves and the flow of the water. It is not intelligent, not in any way you would recognize, but it is organized. It has a logic. It responds to stimuli. It adapts. It has been adapting for longer than you can imagine, and the Bright Hollow is the result — not a place but a process, not a habitat but an organism that has become a habitat, a living thing that has become a world.

## V. The People

The structures in the hollow were made by people. The shelters, the tools, the clay vessels, the cairns. People have been here. People have lived here.

They were not like you. Or they were like you once and then they were not.

You find evidence. In a shelter on a shelf-fungus platform, you find a handprint on the column-bark. Not a painted handprint. Not a carved one. A handprint in the bark itself, pressed into the surface of the column when the bark was young and soft, grown over but still visible, the way a fossil is visible in stone. The hand is human. The fingers are long. The print is old — how old, you cannot tell, because time in the hollow is measured in growth cycles, not years, and the bark has grown over it in layers that might be centuries or might be weeks.

In another shelter, you find something else. A piece of woven material — moss-fiber, like the bags — but the weaving is finer than anything else you've seen, and the pattern is complex, and worked into the pattern are shapes that might be letters. You cannot read them. You do not recognize the script. But the shapes repeat, and they repeat in groups, and the groups have structure, and you are looking at language. You are looking at the remains of someone who was here and who spoke and who wrote and who left this behind.

The staff you found is the most telling artifact. Cut from a young column while it was still growing. Stripped and shaped but still alive. Still reaching. The person who made it was working with living material. They were not cutting wood — they were redirecting growth. They took something that wanted to be a column and they made it want to be a staff, and it obeyed, or it didn't, and the warping and twisting of the staff is the result of the tension between what the person wanted and what the wood wanted. This was a craft that understood its material. This was a person who knew the difference between dead wood and living wood and chose to work with the living kind.

You do not find bones. You do not find graves. You do not find evidence of death, only of absence — people were here and then they were not, and the hollow continued without them, and the hollow does not miss them.

You think about this for a long time, standing in a shelter on a shelf-fungus platform, holding a stone blade that fits your hand, looking out across the bright cold space of the hollow with its columns and its light and its slow constant change.

## VI. What Lives Here Now

You are not the first person in the Bright Hollow. You might not be the last.

But right now, you are the only one.

The bark-walkers do not count you. The birds do not count you. The small furred things and the stream-drifters and the gill-feeders and the moving fungus and the merged root-floor — none of them count you. You are in their space. You are in their world. You are an event, not a resident. You are weather. You will pass through, the way a condensation-shower passes through, and the hollow will not remember you, and the floor will not grow around you, and the columns will not adjust for you.

But there are things here that might count you, if they knew you were here.

In the deep hollow, near the river, you found marks on the columns. Not the handprint, not the script, but marks — scratches, gouges, a series of notches cut into the bark at a height of about two meters. The notches are fresh. The bark around them is still pale, still wet, still healing. They were made recently. They were made at a height that requires either a very tall person or a person standing on something, and the nearest thing to stand on is the root-floor, which means the person who made them was very tall, or was not standing on the floor, or was not a person.

The marks are regular. They are counted. They are a tally, or a calendar, or a record of something. They are language, or they are the precursor to language, or they are something that precedes language — the mark that a mind makes when it wants to remember and has nothing to write with but the world itself.

Something is down here. Something that cuts marks into living columns. Something that is tall or that flies or that climbs and that counts.

You do not see it. You do not hear it. You do not find it.

But the marks are there, and the bark is fresh, and the notches go up to a number you cannot finish counting because the column curves away from you and the marks disappear around the other side where the light from the canopy doesn't reach and the shadows are blue and deep and the bark is breathing slowly, evenly, and you stand there with your hand on the column and you feel the breath of it under your palm and you know — the way you know the floor is alive, the way you know the light is alive, the way you know everything in this place is alive — that the marks were made by hands. By something with hands.

And the hands are still here.

---

The Bright Hollow holds itself. It does not need you. It does not need the things that live in it. It grew the way it grows — by accident, by chemistry, by the slow accumulation of organisms that found each other and stayed. Everything in it is provisional. The columns are provisional. The canopy is provisional. The birds are provisional and the bark-walkers are provisional and the people were provisional and whatever is making the marks in the deep hollow is provisional. Everything here is a temporary arrangement of living things that will be replaced by other living things that will be replaced by other living things, and the hollow will continue, and the light will continue, and the floor will grow and the columns will rise and fall and the river will carry life down into the deeper layers and the deeper layers will send life up through the roots and the cycle will not stop because there is nothing that can stop it.

This is what lives in the Bright Hollow: everything. All of it at once. A world that is an organism that is a place that is alive.

And you, for now, in it. And something else, also, in the deep. And the marks on the column, still healing.
