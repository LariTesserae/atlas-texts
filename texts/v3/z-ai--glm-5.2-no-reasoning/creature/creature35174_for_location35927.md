# The Warden of the Weeping Highlands

## First Sight

You don't see it at first. Almost no one does.

The Weeping Highlands are so full of water and green and the small movements of small lives that one more shape among the hummocks registers as nothing. A mound of moss. A shadowed outcrop. A patch of sedge bent differently by wind. Your eyes slide across it the way they slide across everything here, because the highland teaches you, in your first hour, that its drama is below the threshold of notice. The rills murmur beneath hearing. The mist falls as feeling rather than sound. And the thing that lives here — the thing that *keeps* living here — has taken that lesson further than anything.

It is the color of the place. That is the first thing you would understand if you saw it clearly, which you mostly won't. Not camouflaged the way a moth is camouflaged, not patterned to mimic bark or stone. It is the color of the highland the way water is the color of its container: a deep, layered wet-green, shot through with the dark gleam of saturated peat and the pale fuzz of sedge-margin. When it moves — and it moves slowly, when it moves at all — the color *shifts* through the same narrow range the highland shifts through, moss-green to peat-black to the grey of wet granite, as though its skin were sampling the palette of the ground beneath it.

It is roughly the size and general arrangement of a large bear, if a bear had been designed by a committee of hydrologists. Low-slung. Broad. Built close to the ground, its weight spread across wide, flat feet that do not sink into the peat — this is important, and it is the first clue to what it is, because everything else its size leaves prints, and it does not. Its feet are wider than logic suggests they should be, almost snowshoe-like, distributing its considerable mass across the saturated surface so that it *floats* over ground that would swallow a horse. You find no tracks. You will never find tracks. The moss closes behind it like water behind a fish.

Its body is rounded, blunt-ended, without an obvious head in the way that a hill has no obvious head. There is a front. The front is where the business happens. Beyond that, the topology of the animal resolves into a series of ridges and hollows that echo the patterned ground it lives on — it carries, on its back and flanks, a faint echo of the frost-sorted polygons of the highland, a skin texture of shallow faceted planes, and on those planes, over years, moss has grown. Living moss. The same dozen species that carpet the ground grow on the Warden's hide, especially along its spine and shoulders, so that when it is still — and it is still for hours, for days — it becomes a hummock. A hummock with cotton grass nodding from its shoulders in the wind, if you can imagine that, which you probably can't, which is fine, because you won't see it anyway.

It has a face, of a kind. Set low in the front of its body, half-hidden beneath an overhang of mossy brow-ridge, are two eyes that do not help you at all. They are the color of the pools: dark, reflective, and occupied with something you cannot name. They do not follow you with hunger or fear. They track you the way the surface of a pool tracks the shadow of a hawk — a disturbance noted, a ripple recorded, a resolution pending.

It breathes. You will hear it before you see it, if you ever hear it at all: a slow, vast, atmospheric exhalation, so low and so even that your ears file it under *wind*. When the Warden breathes out, the mist thickens slightly, for a moment, in its vicinity. When it breathes in, the air feels briefly, faintly *dry*, and the midges — which never swarm, which rise in quiet columns when the air is still — dip and scatter in a silent spiral and then reform.

That is the Warden of the Weeping Highlands: a moss-covered, low green bulk that walks without prints and breathes like weather. It is an animal. It is the animal. Everything else here is small — pipits and salamanders, larvae and fish, the quiet insects, the unseen grazers at the margins. The Warden is the only large body on the highland proper, and it has been here for a very long time, and it is doing something, and it has been doing it for longer than the peat is deep.

## What It Eats

Understand first that it does not need much, because understand second that it does not eat the way you eat.

The Warden is a grazer of a peculiar and wonderful kind. It eats the highland's *furniture* and does so at a pace that makes erosion look hasty. Its mouth — a wide, soft, almost lipless arrangement set beneath its eyes, low enough to reach the ground while it stands — takes in moss, sedge, the tender prostrate growth of the flat trees, crowberry, the whole living surface-layer of the highland, in slow, continuous, almost imperceptible bites. It feeds as it walks, and it walks so slowly that the moss could grow back over its own consumption if the Warden were even slower, which it nearly is. There is a balance here that has been struck over centuries: the Warden eats the highland at the exact rate the highland can tolerate, and the highland feeds the Warden at the exact rate the Warden requires, and neither of them negotiated this arrangement on purpose.

But that is only half the diet. This is the other half, and this is where the Warden stops being merely strange and becomes *necessary*:

It drinks the water. And it *filters* the water. And it gives the water back.

The Warden's muzzle, that soft wide mouth, is not just for moss. When the Warden comes to one of the dark reflective pools — and it comes to each pool on a cycle, a rotation so long and so regular that each pool's other inhabitants have evolved around its visits the way shore creatures evolve around the tide — it lowers its face into the water and drinks, and as it drinks, something remarkable happens. The water clarifies. Whatever the Warden takes from the pool — nutrients, algae, the mineral film that accumulates on the surface of still water in a place where the water table is pressurized and mineral-rich — it takes in and does not return. What it gives back, through pores along its jaw and throat, is *cleaner* than what it took in. This is not a metaphor. There are structures inside the Warden — you would never see them, but you can infer them from behavior — dense, layered, filtration-like arrangements, something like gills turned inside out, that process enormous volumes of water and return it stripped of its excess. The pools it visits stay clear. The pools it has abandoned — and there are a few, on the far edges, where the Warden no longer goes — cloud over, silt up with mineral precipitate, become greenish and turbid and eventually reduce to damp mineral flats where nothing much lives.

The Warden is a keystone. Where it drinks, the pools thrive. Where it does not, they die. And the whole highland's plumbing — the rills, the reservoir beneath the moss ceiling, the water that pushes up through fractured granite — runs cleaner and clearer for the Warden's passage through it, the way a pipe runs better for the passing of a swab.

So it eats the ground slowly and it drinks the water clean, and both of these are the *small* version of what it does.

## The Rotation

The Warden has a route.

This is the thing that takes the longest to understand, because the route is not a path — nothing so crude. There is no worn track anywhere on the highland. The moss closes; the ground has memory and rebounds; the Warden's feet are wide and its steps are gentle. But there is a route nonetheless, and it is *vast*: the Warden walks a slow, drifting circuit of the entire Weeping Highlands that takes — by the best inference available, which is the growth rings of trees it has browsed and re-browsed, the age-gradations of moss on its own back, the succession-states of pools in various stages of clarity — somewhere between sixty and a hundred years to complete.

It walks a little in the morning, when the condensation is thick. It walks a little in the evening, when the highland's own weather recycles visibly and the mist rises from the ground it stands on. In between, it is still. It rests in the lee of granite outcrops — the weeping rock, the shining stone — and it rests *against* them, its mossy flank pressed to the wet granite, and this is where the story gets strange, so hold on to your hand against the rock a moment longer.

The granite weeps because of pressure from below. Water, pushed up by the deep engines — the silent, purposeless, running machines — exudes through the fractured stone in a constant film. The Warden presses itself against the outcrops. It presses its face against them. It stands for hours — days — with its broad brow laid against the wet rock, its filtering jaw-pores open against the streaming stone, and it *drinks the rock dry*.

Not dry. But drier. Where the Warden rests, the outcrop's weeping slows. The film thins. The threads of water descending to the moss go slack. And the Warden takes that water, and whatever is *in* that water — the mineral load, the pressure, the warmth that it has carried up from the deep — and it incorporates it, or it moves it, or it does something with it that no surface anatomy can explain. When the Warden finally moves on from an outcrop, the weeping resumes at full strength within hours, as though the rock had been holding its breath.

Think about what that means, and then think about the rotation again, and then think about the *engine*.

## The Engine Below

Here is the heart of the thing, and I have saved it, because the Warden saved it.

The machines under the Weeping Highlands are running. They have been running for so long that they have become geology — engines without operators, cycling water through deep rock, maintaining a water cycle that no one designed to last this long and that could not, by any honest engineering assessment, *have* lasted this long. Systems run down. Pressures equalize. Fractures clog with mineral precipitate — this is what happens to every artificial hydrothermal system ever built or imagined: the dissolved minerals in hot, pressurized water come out of solution when the water cools, and they deposit, and they clog the channels, and the circulation slows, and stops.

The Weeping Highlands have not clogged.

The highland has been running for *longer than the peat is deep*. Thousands of years of water, cycled and recycled, mineralized and mineralized again, and the fractures are still open, and the water still moves, and the outcrops still weep. There is exactly one explanation available in the entire visible surface of this landscape, and it is a moss-covered animal the size of a bear that spends a suspicious fraction of its very long life pressed against the outflow points, drinking the water and taking something out of it.

The Warden is maintenance.

It is not intelligence in any sense that would flatter us. It has no plan. It does not understand engines. It is an animal — a magnificent, evolved, exquisitely fitted animal — that has found the single most reliable food-and-water source in its world and built a life around harvesting it, and the harvesting, as a side effect of its anatomy, keeps the source from killing itself. It drinks the mineral load out of the water at the point of emergence, before the minerals can precipitate and clog the fractures, and it secretes them — concentrates them — somewhere, in some internal vault of dense stone-like tissue that accounts for far too much of the Warden's weight for any ordinary animal of its size. It is carrying, in its body, decades of the highland's dissolved burden. It is, in the most literal sense available, the filter that keeps the garden alive. It is the swab in the pipe. It is the *keeper of the system's breath*.

And the rotation — the sixty-to-hundred-year circuit, the slow drifting path, the punctual visits to pools and outcrops alike — that is the maintenance schedule. Each outcrop on the highland gets a visit. Each pool gets a clarification. The rills run cleaner downstream of wherever the Warden has been. The whole highland is being *tended*, in the exact way you felt when you stood here and felt the place was tended and could not say why: not by anyone, not with intent — but by an animal whose evolved appetite and the deep machines' blind persistence have locked together into a single self-sustaining system, a symbiosis between biology and infrastructure so old that neither party could be said to exist without the other.

The machines keep the water moving. The Warden keeps the machines unclogged. The highland grows over both of them.

If the Warden dies, the fractures begin to clog. Slowly — over centuries, maybe. The outcrops would cloud, then crust, then crust over. The weeping would slow. The water table would drop. The peat would dry and oxidize and shrink, and the patterned ground would lose its frost-heave engine as the ground lost its thermal mass, and the pools would contract to mineral flats, and the trees would creep back down to wherever ordinary moisture lives, and in a thousand years the Weeping Highlands would be a high, dry, ordinary plateau with strange buried shapes beneath it and no reason for a name.

And if the machines stopped — if the deep heat finally failed, if the pressure bled away, if the water went quiet in the rock — the Warden would starve in a way no amount of moss-grazing could repair. It is not just adapted to the highland's water. It is *made of* the highland's water. It is the terminal expression of the whole cycle: water, pushed up through buried engines, mineralized and pressurized, filtered through a living body, returned clean. The Warden is what the water becomes when it has been through everything else.

## The Others

There are not many Wardens. There might be only one.

This is the part that the highland does not advertise, because the highland advertises nothing, and the Warden least of all. But consider the evidence, such as it is: there are no tracks; there is one set of behaviors; there is one route, one rotation, one slow patient passage. In all the years that the peat has been forming — and you can count the layers, if you have the patience of geology — the outcrops have never clogged. The system has never failed. There has been, functionally, one Warden on duty for as long as there has been a highland worth walking.

Whether it is the last of a species or the only one of a kind — whether the deep builders made many and the slow attrition of millennia took all but this one, or whether this thing, like the engines below, was singular by design and simply *keeps running* — there is no way to know from the surface. The Warden does not socialize. It does not call. It has no voice that any ear has registered, though on the stillest nights, in the deepest quiet, when the highland's composite texture of wind-and-water-and-life has sunk to its lowest ebb, there is sometimes — very rarely, and never twice in the same place — a low, almost subsonic pulse from somewhere out on the moss. A single note. Long. Held. Below hearing and above nothing, felt in the chest the way the highland's hum is felt in the chest.

The pipits do not respond to it. The salamanders do not stir. The pool water does not ripple. Whatever the Warden is calling to, it either answers in a register no surface creature can perceive, or it does not answer at all, and the Warden calls anyway, once every decade or so, the way a lighthouse calls, the way the deep engines call in their own near-silent turning — because that is what systems that have outlived their purpose do. They keep running. They keep signaling. They maintain.

Perhaps somewhere below, in the hot dark where the water begins, there is an answer. Perhaps the machines, in whatever remains of their deaf and blind operation, register the Warden's pulse through the water it filters back into the rock, and adjust, and continue. Perhaps the whole vast symbiosis has, at its root, only two parties — one animal, one engine — and they have been in conversation for ten thousand years at a volume neither of them can hear, and it does not matter at all, because the conversation is not made of signals. It is made of *water*. It is made of the highland itself: the moss, the peat, the weeping stone, the clear pools, the whole green wet breathing body of the place, which is, when you finally understand it, one organism with many surfaces, of which the Warden is only the largest and most patient organ.

## If You Should See It

You will probably not see it. Say so plainly, and then say what to do.

The Warden is not shy in the way that frightened animals are shy. It has no fear response that anyone has ever observed, because it has no predators, and it has no memory — species-memory or individual — of harm. The large grazers at the margins ignore it. The hawk does not hunt it. Nothing on the highland challenges it, and it challenges nothing. Its stillness is not hiding. Its stillness is simply *what it is*: an animal whose metabolic tempo is set to the landscape's tempo, whose heartbeat — you could put your ear against its mossy flank and count, if it allowed that, and it would allow that — runs at a few beats per minute, whose breathing is indistinguishable from the movement of weather, whose days are as long as its steps are slow.

But if you are lucky — if you have been still long enough that the highland has stopped regarding you as weather, if the midges have reformed their columns around you, if the mist has accepted you — then you might see a hummock, far off across the polygons, that was not there in the morning light. And if you watch it through the whole grey afternoon, without moving, without eating, without doing anything but being one more object the highland is slowly absorbing into its inventory — the hummock will, at some point that is not predictable but is somehow, in retrospect, *inevitable*, unfold.

Not quickly. Nothing here is quick. But the hummock will lengthen. A wide, flat foot will find the moss ahead of it. The cotton grass will nod. The whole low green weight will begin to move, one stately inch at a time, toward the nearest dark pool or the nearest weeping outcrop, whichever is due, and the highland will adjust around it, and you will understand, in one slow flood, everything this description has been trying to tell you: that you are watching the place tend itself. That you are watching the gardener that the garden forgot it had — or rather, that you had the relationship exactly backwards, before, standing with your wet palm on the weeping rock and feeling the pulse of the deep machines. The garden has not forgotten the gardener. The gardener and the garden have simply *become the same thing*, and they are both still working, and neither of them knows your name, and neither of them needs to.

The Warden will drink the pool clear. It will lean its mossy brow against the wet stone and take the burden of the deep water into its body. It will graze the surface of the highland at the rate the highland can bear. And when it finally moves on, it will leave no track, and the moss will close, and the water will run a little cleaner where it passed, and the only evidence that anything happened at all will be this:

The pool will be clear. The rock will weep. The highland will go on being tended, by a system so old and so complete that the tending and the being-tended have merged into a single verb, which is the verb the whole landscape is conjugating, quietly, in the mist, all the time: *to keep*. To keep the water moving. To keep the fractures open. To keep the garden green and wet and alive, indefinitely, without an operator, without an owner, without a keeper except the one it grew itself.

The highland weeps on.

The Warden keeps it weeping.
