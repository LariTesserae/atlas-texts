# What Lives Here

Begin with the stone.

Not the creatures — the stone. Because the stone is not where they live; the stone is what they are. The distinction between inhabitant and habitat has never been solid here, and what time has done is dissolve it further, until the boundary is a matter of opinion held by no one qualified to hold one. The dark fractured polygons, the skin-surface, the moisture-collecting joints — this is not a floor they walk on. This is the medium they exist in, the way a fish exists in water, the way a thought exists in a mind. When they settle over a crack in the stone and press their broad lower surfaces against the damp, they are not feeding from the stone. They are communing with it. Or digesting it. Or being digested by it. The stone gives something up to them — some ion, some compound, some trace element carried in the film of moisture — and they give something back, a faint darkening, a chemical signature left where they sat, and this exchange has been happening for so long that the stone and the fauna have become metabolically entangled. The stone feeds them. They weather the stone. The cycle does not have a name because there is nothing here capable of naming and nothing elsewhere that has observed it long enough to describe.

Now. The bodies.

---

Consider an individual. Not a species — an individual. Pick one. It does not matter which because they are all, in some sense, the same, and they are all, in some sense, entirely alone in their specificity. Choose a large one. A building-sized one, low and wide and hunched against the dark stone, its upper surface a shallow concavity that holds a thin film of the plateau's omnipresent moisture. Its color is the stone's color, or slightly darker — a dense, weathered charcoal that seems to absorb the dim amber light rather than reflect it. Its surface is not skin. It is not shell. It is not carapace. It is something that has no analogue because it has never needed one — a material that is flexible and impermeable and slow, that responds to pressure over hours the way skin responds over seconds, that can sit against wet stone for weeks without absorbing water and without drying out. It has a grain to it, a subtle directional texture, like wood grain or the striations in muscle, but running in patterns that do not correspond to any underlying musculature because there is no underlying musculature. The body is not built in layers. It is not built at all. It has the quality of something that has *accreted* — that has accumulated itself, particle by particle, over a duration that makes the word *growth* feel rushed.

Its edges are where it is most itself. The boundary of its body is not sharp. It gradients. The outermost margin of the creature is a zone of ambiguity where the body becomes thinner, more permeable, more like a film than a structure, and where it meets the stone, there is no seam. There is no gap. The body simply thins until it is indistinguishable from the moisture film on the stone, and then it is the stone. This edge shifts. Over the course of hours, the body's margin will extend in one direction and retract in another, a slow tidal movement that is not locomotion and not growth and not breathing but something that serves a function no outside observer has terminology for. It is probing. It is tasting. It is reading the stone the way a blind hand reads a surface, and the hand is part of the stone, and the surface is part of the hand.

Inside — if there is an inside, if the word *inside* means anything when applied to a body with no clear boundary — there is density. The creature is heavy. Not heavy in the way of an animal, with the concentrated weight of bone and organ and gut contents, but heavy in the way of a saturation, a body that is thoroughly and uniformly itself, with no voids, no cavities, no air spaces. If you were to cut it open — and there is no blade here, and no hand to hold one, and no reason to — you would not find organs. You would not find a digestive tract. You would find more of the same: the same dense, dark material, the same grain, the same slow responsiveness, all the way through, from the upper surface to the lower, from one edge to the other. It is not layered. It is not segmented. It is *continuous*. It is a body that has no architecture because it does not need architecture, because its functions are not localized. Whatever it does — processing the moisture, converting the stone's minerals into the material of its own body, maintaining the strange slow integrity of its form — it does everywhere, at once, at the same pace, without concentration or delegation.

It does not have a head. It does not have a front or back. It does not have a mouth. It does not have eyes. The absence of these things is not a deformation or a regression. It is a design — or an arrival, a form that has reached the point where the features that animals use to interface with the world are no longer necessary because the interface has become total. The entire lower surface of the body is the mouth. The entire upper surface is the skin. Every point on the body's margin is a sensory edge, a tongue, a fingertip. It does not need a head because it does not face a direction. It does not face a direction because it does not travel. It moves, but it does not travel. It redistributes, and where it redistributes to is determined by gradients in the stone — moisture, mineral content, temperature, the faint vibrations of the engines — that it reads with its entire body, the way a root system reads the soil, the way a coastline reads the tide.

Now watch it move.

---

The motion begins as a thickening. One edge of the body — call it the leading edge, though this is a convenience, not a truth — becomes slightly denser, slightly more present, as though the body were pushing material toward that margin the way a slow wave pushes water ahead of itself. The trailing edge, opposite, thins. Not dramatically. Not visibly, unless you are watching with the patience of stone. But the body's center of mass shifts — that word again, *mass*, which here means something more like *commitment*, the degree to which the body has invested itself in one location — and the shift propagates through the body at a speed that makes the propagation itself invisible. You do not see movement. You see that the body is here, and then, hours later, you see that the body is there, and between *here* and *there* was a process so gradual that no single moment of it could be identified as the moment of change.

This is how all of them move. This is the only way anything happens on the plateau. A redistribution. A shift in where the body has chosen to be, expressed not as locomotion but as a slow migration of presence. They do not walk. They do not crawl. They do not slide. They *extend* — in one direction, out of themselves, into the space they will next occupy — and they *retract* — from the direction they are leaving, pulling themselves back into themselves — and the net effect, over hours or days, is a change in position that would be imperceptible to any observer operating on a human timescale but that is, to the plateau, the equivalent of a stampede.

An individual can cross ten meters in a day. This is fast. This is, by the standards of the plateau, very fast. Most of them move much slower. Most of them move a meter a day, or less, or they do not move at all for weeks at a time, holding their position against the stone with a commitment that looks like death but is the opposite of death. Death is the cessation of process. This is process so thorough and so complete that it has become indistinguishable from stillness.

---

And the small ones.

The small ones — torso-sized, relative term, relative to what — are not young. This is important. This is the thing you must understand about them, because without this understanding, the relationship between the large and the small becomes a story about parenthood or hierarchy, and it is neither.

The small ones are not children. The small ones are not a different species. The small ones are *the same*, at a different scale, and the scale is not a stage of development. It is a state. It is a choice, if choice is a word that can be applied to a body with no brain and no endocrine system and no mechanism of decision more complex than chemical gradient — a choice to be less, to carry less mass, to require less from the stone, to move more freely. The small ones are the same organism expressing itself at a smaller amplitude.

They sit on the large ones. This is the fact. They settle into the concavity of the broad upper surface and they stay there, their own bodies pressed against the body beneath them, and something passes between them. Not warmth — the plateau does not deal in warmth. Not food — the large ones do not produce food. Something else. Something chemical. Something that involves the transfer of compounds through the surfaces in contact, a slow exchange that serves a function neither one can perform alone. The large one provides a surface, a stability, a particular concentration of the stone's minerals that its larger body has processed and made available. The small one provides... what? Mobility? Variation? The small one is a probe, an experiment, a way for the large body to extend its sensory capacity without extending its mass. The small one moves from one large body to another, crossing the open stone over days, carrying with it whatever it has accumulated from its previous host, and depositing it — through contact, through the slow exchange of surfaces — on its next host. It is a messenger. It is a gamete. It is a thought passing between neurons that are the size of buildings.

This is how they reproduce. Or rather, this is how they maintain themselves, because *reproduction* implies the creation of new individuals, and what happens here is more subtle than that. The small ones carry material between the large ones, and the material is not genetic in any familiar sense — it is not DNA, it is not a code — but it is *information*. It is the chemical record of one large body's interaction with one part of the stone, transferred to another large body that has been interacting with a different part of the stone, and the combination of these two records, meeting in the body of the small one or on the surface of the next host, produces something. A change. A slight adjustment in the chemical processes of the receiving body, a new pattern of mineral exchange, a slightly different texture on its lower surface, a slight shift in the way it reads the stone. This is their evolution. This is their sex. This is their conversation. It happens in silence, on a timescale that makes geological time feel hasty, and it has been happening for as long as there have been bodies on the stone, and it will continue for as long as the stone holds moisture in its joints.

Sometimes a small one stays on a large one for a very long time. Months. Years. And sometimes, after a long residency, the small one is no longer small. It has grown — or accumulated, or been added to — and its body has become denser and wider, and it has sunk lower into the concavity of the large one, and the boundary between the two bodies has begun to blur. The small one is becoming part of the large one. It is being absorbed, or it is merging, or it is returning to a state from which it came. And the large one, in turn, is slightly larger now, slightly more complex, carrying within its body the chemical record of the small one's travels, the information gathered from other hosts, the accumulated experience of a life spent crossing the open stone. This is how they grow. This is how they learn. Not as individuals. As a network. As a population that is also a single organism that is also a landscape.

And sometimes, the opposite happens. A large one produces a small one. Not from an orifice, not from a bud, not from any identifiable point of origin. The large one's body, in a particular region — usually near the edge, usually where the body is thinnest and most permeable — develops a density. A thickening that becomes a distinctness. A region of the body that begins to have its own grain, its own direction, its own chemical identity, separate from the parent body's. Over weeks, this region separates — not cleanly, not with a break, but with a slow, spreading thinning of the connective material between itself and the parent, until one morning, if mornings existed here, it would be accurate to say that there are two where there was one. The small one sits in the concavity of the large one for a time, then begins to move. It has places to go. It has information to carry. It has a function, and the function is to be elsewhere, and it goes.

---

Now the violet mats.

The filamentous patches on the raised stone — the bruised-purple radial growths, the stains that are not stains — are related. Not closely. Not in any way that a biology built on Earth's templates would recognize as relatedness. But the chemical signature of the mats is present, in trace amounts, in the bodies of the fauna. The mats are not plants. They are not fungi. They are not lichens. They are a fourth thing, a thing that has no name because the category they belong to has no representatives anywhere else. They are what happens when stone, under specific conditions of weathering and moisture and mineral concentration, begins to organize. Not to live — or not to live in any way that involves cells or membranes or genetic material — but to *pattern*. The mineral content of the stone, in these particular spots, has reached a state where the crystalline structure of the rock begins to produce complex, repeating arrangements that have the quality of metabolism without the machinery of metabolism. They take in. They put out. They maintain a boundary between themselves and the stone around them. They grow, at a pace that makes the fauna look fleet. They are the stone thinking. They are the stone's slow first draft of life.

The fauna eat them. Not exclusively, not primarily, but the fauna's lower surfaces, pressed against the stone, will absorb the mineral content of a violet mat over a period of weeks, and the mat will shrink, and the stone beneath it will be slightly different — slightly more weathered, slightly more chemically complex — than it was before. The mats are, in this sense, a delicacy. A supplement. A concentrated form of the stone's self-organization that the fauna can process more efficiently than raw rock. The mats exist because the stone has reached a certain age, and the fauna exist because the mats exist, and the engines exist because... because.

---

The engines. Now the engines.

The fauna are aware of the engines. This must be said, because the description of their indifference might suggest otherwise, and indifference is not the right word. They are not indifferent. They are *familiar*. The engines have been here as long as they have, or longer, and the vibrations the engines produce — the sub-audible tremor in the stone, the constant low frequency that micro-fractures the rock — is part of the texture of the plateau the way the moisture is part of the texture, the way the dim light is part of the texture. The fauna read the vibrations. Their lower surfaces, pressed against the stone, can feel the engine-hum the way a hand pressed to a wall can feel the plumbing running behind it. They know where the engines are. They know this the way they know where the moisture collects — not by seeing, not by hearing, but by the quality of the stone beneath them, the way it feels, the way it vibrates, the particular pattern of micro-fracture that radiates from each dark circle.

They do not avoid the engines. They do not seek them. But there is a behavior — a subtle, glacial behavior, visible only over very long observation — that might be called *acknowledgment*. When a large individual moves across the plateau, its path is not random. It follows gradients — moisture, mineral, the chemical signals left by other individuals in the faint dark marks where they sat — and one of the gradients it follows is the engine-hum. Not toward. Not away. But *along*. The paths of the large ones, traced over months or years, curve gently around the engine circles, not in avoidance but in the way a river curves around a boulder. The boulder is not an obstacle. The boulder is a feature of the riverbed that the river accommodates. The engines are features of the plateau's bed that the fauna accommodate. Their paths flow around them. Their bodies settle over them and lift away from them with no change in behavior, no acknowledgment, no special treatment, and yet — and yet — the paths curve. The bodies pause, very slightly, at the point of closest approach. There is something there. Something that registers. Something that is not food and not information and not threat but is *present*, and the presence is noted, in the way that a body notes the position of its own limbs, without thought, without attention, without any of the machinery of awareness. The engines are part of what the fauna know about their world, and what the fauna know about their world is written in the positions of their bodies, in the paths they have traced across the stone, in the slow, curving records of their movement that will be legible in the chemical traces they leave behind long after they have moved on.

What the engines do, the fauna do not know. What the engines are for, the fauna do not contemplate. The engines are. The fauna are. The stone is. These are three facts about the plateau that are all the same fact, stated in different ways, and the fauna have been living inside this fact for longer than the word *fact* has had meaning.

---

Consider the silence one more time.

The silence is not empty. The silence is full. It is full of the engines' vibration, which is below the threshold of hearing. It is full of the wind, which is so slow and so wide that it does not rustle or whisper but simply *is*, a pressure, a presence, a thing that moves against the skin without entering the ear. It is full of the sounds that the fauna do not make — the sounds of bodies shifting against stone that are so low and so slow that they do not register as sound but as a change in the quality of the silence, a modulation, a note so deep it has become a texture.

If you were very still — still enough, for long enough — you would begin to hear the plateau. Not the fauna, not the wind, not the engines. The plateau. The stone itself, vibrating at frequencies that are below any instrument's range, producing a sound that is not a sound but a feeling, a deep, continuous, bone-level hum that is the sum of every process happening on the plateau — every micro-fracture, every moisture-seep, every body-shift, every engine-pulse, every slow chemical exchange in the joints of the rock — all of it adding up to a single, continuous, directionless vibration that is the plateau's voice, and the voice is saying nothing, and the nothing it is saying is the most articulate thing about this place.

The fauna hear this. They have always heard this. They are made of this. Their bodies are tuned to this frequency the way a tuning fork is tuned to its note, and they vibrate with it, very slightly, at all times, a micro-tremor in their dense dark bodies that is not visible but that is present, a constant resonance with the stone they sit on, the air that moves over them, the moisture that seeps beneath them, the engines that hum below. They are instruments. They are the plateau's instruments, played by the plateau, producing no sound, making no music, but resonating, always resonating, with the one frequency that this place has, the one note, the one tone that everything here shares.

---

And the small ones on the backs of the large ones, still now, resting in the concavity of bodies that have not moved in weeks, their own bodies trembling at the same frequency as the bodies beneath them, the same frequency as the stone, the same frequency as the engines, the same frequency as the wind — the small ones are not resting. They are listening. They are carrying the plateau's frequency from one body to another, from one place on the stone to another, and the frequency is all they carry, and it is enough, and they will carry it for as long as the plateau produces it, which is as long as the plateau exists, which is as long as anything exists, which is a duration that has no end because it has no beginning, because the plateau did not start, the plateau simply *is*, and what lives here is what has always lived here, which is the plateau itself, expressed as stone and water and body and machine, all vibrating together, all silent together, all here together, in the dim amber light, under the pewter sky, on the high, flat, ancient ground.

This is what lives here. Not creatures. Not organisms. Not fauna. Not life, if life means what it means in places that have names and histories and changes rapid enough to record. Something else. Something that does not have a word because the word would have to describe a thing that is its own habitat, its own food source, its own reproductive system, its own sensory apparatus, its own history, its own future — a thing that is complete, that is whole, that does not need anything from outside itself because there is nothing outside itself, because the plateau is all there is, and what lives here is the plateau, and the plateau is what lives, and the distinction between the two was always an illusion brought by eyes that move too fast.
