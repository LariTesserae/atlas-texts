# What Lives Here

The first answer is: everything, and nothing, and the question itself is slightly wrong.

---

**The lizards** are the ones you meet first, because they are the ones who meet you. They are small — finger-length, hand-length, none much larger — and dark-backed, the color of wet basalt, which is not a coincidence but a strategy worn so long it has become a description. Their bellies flush pink with warmth, a slow blush that spreads from the throat downward, and you realize after a while that this is not display but thermometry: they are showing you their temperature. A warm lizard is a pink-bellied lizard. A cold one is pale. You can read their comfort at a distance.

They live in the crevices. Every gap in the basalt is a home to something, and the lizards have claimed the medium-sized ones — too narrow for birds to reach into, too shallow for crabs to bother with, too dry for the tadpoles. They emerge in the morning onto the rounded backs of the stone humps and they do not bask so much as *resume* — resuming a stillness that is their primary activity, their chief occupation, the thing they are best at. They sit. They watch. Their golden eyes are flat disks that catch the diffuse light and give nothing back, and they track your approach with a patience that does not look like patience but like assessment. They are not deciding whether to flee. They are calculating the distance at which fleeing becomes more efficient than staying, and they are in no hurry to complete the calculation, because they have been running this math their entire lives and the answer is almost always the same.

What startles you, after a time, is that some of them do not flee at all. They sidestep. They shift one body-length to the left or right and resume. You are not a predator to them. You are a weather event — large, slow, predictable, and of no more concern than a change in humidity. They have lived on these rocks for ten thousand generations. They have watched the ground subside by the width of a fingernail. You are a brief, warm disruption in a very long afternoon, and they will be here after you leave, sitting on the same stone, watching the same flat light, their bellies the same slow pink.

They drink from the rock faces where water trickles, lapping with pale tongues in quick, mechanical motions that seem too rapid for the slow setting. The motion is reptilian in the way that word is usually used — dry, efficient, emotionless — but here, in this place where everything else is so slow, the quickness of their drinking has a quality of urgency that feels almost misplaced, like someone rushing through a prayer.

They eat the insects. They are eaten, occasionally, by the birds, though this seems to happen more by opportunity than design — a lizard in the wrong place, a bird in a hungry moment, the boundary between territory and meal briefly and brutally crossed. Mostly the lizards die of cold, or of age, or of the slow failure of some small organ in some small body, and they are found by the water and carried to the low places and they become sediment, and the sediment becomes substrate, and the substrate becomes home to the bacterial mats, and the cycle compresses them without ceremony into the next version of the place.

---

**The birds** are the ones you hear first, and the ones you never stop hearing.

They are rails. Something like a rail. Long-legged, sharp-billed, rust-and-grey, with voices that seem too large for their bodies, as if the sound is being produced by a mechanism that does not fit comfortably inside the animal making it. The call is croaking, guttural, low — a series of notes that sound like they are being pulled from the bottom of a throat rather than the top. The males call. The females call. The juveniles call in voices that are thinner, less certain, cracking occasionally into higher registers as if the mechanism is still being calibrated.

They are territorial in a way that looks, from the outside, like conversation.

Each pair holds a few hundred square meters. The boundaries are invisible to you but clearly legible to them, marked not by scent or sign but by the acoustic edge — the point at which one bird's call is answered by another's, and the exchange that follows establishes, reaffirms, negotiates the line between *here* and *there*. The calling is constant. It rises. It falls. It does not stop. When two birds dispute a boundary — and they dispute them constantly, with the weary persistence of neighbors who have been arguing about the same fence line for years — the calling intensifies into overlapping volleys, a rapid back-and-forth that sounds less like aggression and more like bickering. Old acquaintances. Familiar grievances. The same points made the same way, day after day, year after year, until the argument is no longer an argument but a ritual, and the ritual is no longer a ritual but a texture, and the texture is the sound of the Flats themselves.

There are perhaps two thousand of them. The number feels both precise and irrelevant — they are not counted, and they do not count themselves. They simply exist at the density the place supports, each pair in its small plot, each plot centered on a cluster of weepers, each weeper-cluster producing insects and water and the thin conditions of survival that are enough, that have always been enough, that will probably continue to be enough for as long as the water rises through the stone.

They nest in the sparse vegetation — the tussocks of salt-tolerant grass, the low mats of fleshy succulent. The nests are minimal. Not lazy — *efficient*. A scrape in the soft substrate, lined with whatever dry material can be found, which is not much. The eggs are speckled and warm-colored and they hatch into chicks that are immediately mobile, immediately loud, immediately part of the argument. The young learn their territory's boundaries by calling across them and being answered. They learn the weeper-rhythms of their plot — which tubes pulse at dawn, which sigh at midday, which fall silent at dusk — and this knowledge becomes the architecture of their lives, the map they carry that tells them where water will be and when, where insects will collect and when, where the stone is warmest and when.

They do not migrate. They do not wander. A bird born on a particular plot will live its entire life on that plot, and when it dies, its mate will call from the same stones for a season or two before finding a new partner, and the new partner will learn the territory from the surviving bird, and the territory will continue as if the bird, not the ground, is the thing that persists.

They are not shy, but they are not bold. They watch you with the same flat, practical assessment the lizards use, but they have more options — they can call, they can retreat, they can threaten, they can leave. They usually call. A short, sharp series of notes that is clearly addressed to you, clearly means something specific, and is delivered with a conviction that makes you feel that *you* are the one who does not understand the situation, not them. You are standing in someone's living room. You have not been invited. The host is too polite to remove you but would like you to know that your presence has been noted.

---

**The crabs** are the ones you almost never see.

They live inside the rock.

Not metaphorically. The wider weeper channels — the ones you could fit a hand into, if you were foolish enough to try — extend back into the basalt for distances no one has measured. The crabs live there, in the dark wet interior of the stone, in the interconnected pockets and tubes and caverns that the water has carved over geological time. They are small. Translucent. Long-armed. Their bodies are the color of the water they live in, which is the color of stone that has been wet for forty million years — a pale, brownish grey that shifts with the light. You see them only when they emerge, cautiously, at the mouths of the wider weepers, reaching out with those long, thin arms to feel for whatever the water has pushed out of the stone's interior. Detritus. Insect larvae. The occasional dead thing. They are scavengers, and they are patient, and they live in a darkness so total that eyes would be useless, and so their eyes are reduced to small, dark, non-functional smudges on their pale heads, and their arms are long and sensitive and they read the world through touch the way you read it through sight.

They are the thing the rock has produced. Not lava, not stone, not water — life, from the interior, life that belongs to the dark and the wet and the ancient, patient geology of the basalt. They come out of the stone and they go back into the stone, and if you sit beside a wide weeper long enough — an hour, two, the time it takes for the bird-calls to become background and the weeper-rhythms to become familiar — you will see one emerge. One arm first. Feeling. Testing. Then the body, pale and slow, then the other arm. It will feel along the rim of the opening, collecting whatever film of organic matter has accumulated there, and it will eat what it finds, and it will retreat, and the weeper will sigh as the water adjusts to fill the space the crab's body displaced, and that is all. That is the whole encounter. The interior of the world came out for a moment, touched the surface, and went back in.

You do not know how many there are. No one does. The interior of the basalt is unmapped, unmeasured, unknowable, and the crabs are distributed through it the way the water is distributed through it — everywhere, unseen, in numbers that could be in the thousands or the millions, depending on how much of the rock is habitable, which no one has determined, because the question requires entering the stone, and the stone does not invite entry.

---

**The tadpoles** are the ones that make you reconsider what a life is.

There are several species. Some mature quickly — a season, a year — and become small, drab frogs that live in the thin films of water and the wet crevices and eat the insects and are eaten, in turn, by the birds. These are the ordinary ones. They are interesting only in that they are here at all, in a place with so little vertical space, so little shelter, so little of what frogs usually need.

The others are different.

Some tadpoles live as tadpoles for years. Some live as tadpoles for decades. They delay their metamorphosis — hold it, postpone it, refuse it — in a strategy that makes sense only in a place where conditions are stable enough that the ordinary pressures of amphibian life do not apply. Why become a frog if the water is always here? Why leave the tadpole's world — the warm, shallow, liquid world of the film — for the dry, dangerous, exposed world of air? They stay. They grow slowly. They eat algae and detritus and the thinner bacterial mats. They are dark, elongated, ghostly things, some of them as long as your finger, moving in the centimeter-deep water with a slow, undulating swim that is more drift than motion. They have been tadpoles longer than you have been alive. Some of them have been tadpoles longer than you have been a person. They will eventually become frogs — or they will die as tadpoles, and their bodies will join the sediment, and the sediment will feed the mats, and the mats will feed the next generation, and the next generation will also wait, will also delay, will also choose the water over the air, the stable over the urgent, the long, slow, warm, submerged life over the brief, dangerous, exposed one.

You find yourself thinking about them more than the others. The lizards are comprehensible — small, quick, familiar enough in their reptilian economy. The birds are comprehensible — territorial, vocal, organized around boundaries and resources in a way that maps onto human social logic. The crabs are alien but distant — they come from the interior and return to it, and their lives are hidden. But the tadpoles are here, in the open, in the shallow water you can see, and they are choosing not to grow up. Not from trauma. Not from deficiency. From a patient, strategic, evolutionary wisdom that says: *this is good enough. This has been good enough for a very long time. Why change?*

And you realize, standing on the warm stone, that this is not stasis. This is not failure to develop. This is a decision made at the level of the species, over thousands of generations, in response to a question the Flats have been asking for millions of years: *why hurry?* The tadpoles have answered. The answer is: *we don't.*

---

**The insects** are the ones you forget to notice, and the ones everything else depends on.

Water striders. Backswimmers. Diving beetles. They live in the thin water — the centimeter, the two centimeters, the film that is neither pond nor puddle but something else, something flatter, more distributed, more like a living membrane than a body of water. The water striders move across the surface on legs that dimple but do not break the meniscus, and their motion is quick and directional and purposeful in a way that nothing else here is. They have somewhere to be. They are hunting.

The backswimmers hang below the surface, inverted, their legs spread, their bodies catching the diffuse light from above. They are also hunting. The diving beetles move between the surface and the shallow substrate, carrying small silvery bubbles of air trapped against their bodies, and they are hunting too. Everyone is hunting. The water is thin, the space is compressed, the prey is concentrated, and the hunting is constant and small-scale and invisible unless you kneel down and look closely, and when you do, you see that the centimeter of warm water is not calm. It is a battlefield. It is an economy. It is the place where energy moves from the sun to the bacteria to the grazers to the predators to the scavengers and back to the sediment and back to the bacteria, and it all happens in twenty vertical centimeters, and it never stops.

There are midges. Small ones, pale ones, emerging from the wet substrate in small clouds that hang in the warm still air above the stone. They are the reason the birds can afford to be territorial — the insects are the resource, the insects are the economy, and the birds are simply the largest and loudest expressions of a system that runs on things too small to see from standing height.

The insects live short lives. Weeks. Days. They hatch, they hunt, they mate, they die, they become sediment, they become substrate, they become the next generation. They are the fast layer — the metabolism of the Flats, the quick pulse beneath the slow pulse, the rapid cycling that feeds the slower cycles above and below. Without them, nothing else works. With them, everything works. They are not impressive. They are not beautiful in any obvious way. They are the infrastructure, and infrastructure is always most visible when it fails, and it does not fail here, because the conditions that support it have not changed in a very, very long time.

---

**The bacteria** are the ones that are hardest to see and most important to understand.

They are the rust-colored mats that spread across the wet stone. They darken to near-black where the water runs heaviest, which is where the nutrients are richest, which is where the organic sediment collects, which is where the dead things come to rest. They are cyanobacteria — ancient, photosynthetic, among the oldest forms of life on the planet — and they have been doing what they do for three billion years, and they are doing it here, now, on the surface of the basalt, in the warm, wet, flat light of the Flats.

They are the foundation. The sun feeds them. They feed everything else. The mats are not continuous — they are patchy, discontinuous, spreading where the conditions are right and retreating where they are not, and the pattern of their spread is the pattern of the water's movement across the stone, and the pattern of the water's movement is the pattern of the weepers' pulsing, and the pattern of the weepers' pulsing is the pattern of rainfall in distant highlands months ago. The bacteria are, in a real sense, a map of last year's weather. A living record of history that has not yet finished arriving.

They are also the color of the place. Not green — not the lush, assertive green of a landscape that is trying. Rust. Dark orange. Near-black. The colors of oxidation, of slow process, of things happening at a rate that makes the word "process" feel too active. They are not growing. They are *being*. They are the surface of the stone and the film of the water and the boundary between the two, and they have been that boundary for longer than anything else here, and they will be that boundary after the lizards and the birds and the crabs and the tadpoles and the insects have cycled through their ten-thousandth generation, because they are the thing that the cycling is made of.

---

There is a temptation, when describing what lives in a place, to name the inhabitants and stop. To list: lizards, birds, crabs, tadpoles, insects, bacteria. To make a catalogue and consider the work done.

But what lives here is not a list. What lives here is a *layer*.

Everything here — every species, every individual, every life — exists in a band perhaps twenty centimeters deep, measured from the surface of the stone to the top of the thin water that covers it. Twenty centimeters. The height of a book. The width of a hand and a half. In that twenty centimeters, the entire ecosystem operates: the sun enters, the bacteria grow, the insects feed, the tadpoles drift, the lizards bask, the birds hunt, the crabs scavenge, the sediment collects, the bacteria grow again. In that twenty centimeters, everything is born and everything dies and everything is recycled and everything begins again. In that twenty centimeters, two thousand birds argue and uncountable insects hunt and translucent crabs emerge from the interior of the stone and tadpoles postpone their own adulthood and lizards calculate the precise distance at which a walking human becomes worth avoiding.

Twenty centimeters of life. Stretched across three days of walking. Laid over a formation of basalt that extends further than anyone has mapped. Sustained by water that fell as rain in distant highlands months ago and is only now arriving, through the stone, at the lowest point of the basin.

What lives here is a thin film of organization on the surface of an immense patience. The patience is the rock. The patience is the water. The patience is the forty million years of slow cooling and slow erosion and slow settling that made the Flats what they are. The organization is everything else — the territorial calls, the quick tongues, the translucent arms reaching from dark tubes, the dark tadpoles choosing not to change, the rust-colored mats quietly converting sunlight into the stuff of everything.

The Flats do not notice you. This is not indifference — it is scale. You are a event of seconds. The Flats are a duration of eons. The lizards will watch you pass and resume their stillness. The birds will call at you and resume their arguments. The crabs will wait until you are gone and emerge. The tadpoles will not change their course. The insects will not change theirs. The bacteria will not change at all.

You walk across the Vargan Flats and you are, for the duration of your crossing, a small perturbation in a thin layer of life on the surface of an ancient, patient, slowly breathing stone. The perturbation passes. The layer persists. The stone exhales. The birds argue. The lizards watch.

The world goes on being exactly itself, and you are not part of it, and you are not separate from it, and the distinction does not matter to anything here, and that — that specific, radical, unhurried indifference — is the thing that stays with you after you leave, after the sound of the weepers fades, after the ground firms up and the hills rise and the landscape becomes ordinary again. The Flats did not need you. The Flats did not reject you. The Flats simply *were*, and you were simply *there*, and now you are simply not, and they still are.

That is what lives in the Vargan Flats. Not creatures. Not species. Not individuals. A *persistence*. A continuation so steady and so complete that the word "life" feels too active for it. What lives here is the fact of life itself, in its most reduced, most patient, most essential form: something continuing, in a thin warm layer, on a flat old stone, for a very long time.
