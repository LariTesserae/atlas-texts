# What Lives in the Seep

---

It is tempting to begin with the birds, because the birds are what you notice first — or rather, the birds are what notice *you* first, and the weight of that attention is the thing that settles into your chest and stays. But the birds are not the beginning. Nothing here is the beginning. The basin has the character of a middle paragraph, a passage from something already underway to something not yet arrived, and the things that live here live in that passage the way fish live in a current — oriented always toward the flow, never toward the source.

Begin, then, with what the flow carries.

---

## The Microscopic Tenants

The water that seeps through the limestone is not empty. This is the first fact that the few systematic studies of the basin have established, and it is the fact that raises the most questions it cannot answer. The water — warm, mineral-heavy, carrying dissolved calcium carbonate and iron and traces of compounds that do not appear in any standard mineralogical reference — is dense with life at the scale that requires microscopes to see.

They are not bacteria, exactly. They are not archaea. They are not any of the categories that microbiologists use to sort the invisible world into manageable tiers. They are something older, or something that has arrived at a similar solution by a different route — the way a bat and a bird both fly but arrived at flight through separate histories. These organisms are small, single-celled, and they are everywhere in the water. Every drop. Every film of moisture on every wet stone. Every thread of seepage that traces its way down a fracture face. The water is not water with organisms *in* it. The water is organisms, suspended in a medium of themselves, and the liquid you see is the aggregate expression of their living.

They do something to the water. Or the water does something with them. The eleven-minute delay — the phenomenon by which the pools reflect a sky eleven minutes ahead of the sky overhead — has been hypothesized to originate at this scale. Not because the microorganisms *see* the sky and project it. But because they process time at a different rate, and the water that passes through them carries the imprint of that processing, and the pools, which are the gathering points of that water, display the result. If this is true, the microorganisms are not predicting the future. They are simply *arriving there* before the rest of the basin, in the same way that a deep current reaches a destination before the surface water above it.

They are the most numerous living things in the basin by many orders of magnitude. They are the least seen. And they are, in a sense that no one has articulated but that visitors report feeling with an insistence that borders on consensus, the most *present.* The water is aware of you because the water is them, and they are aware of everything, in the way that a substance is aware of the shape of the container it fills.

## The Salamanders

The large amphibians that gather in the shallow pools are, in the assessment of the herpetologist who spent three days in the basin in 2019 and left with notes she has not published and a breathing pattern she has not been able to normalize, "not salamanders, but the idea of salamanders, executed in a medium that is not quite the one salamanders are usually executed in."

She did not mean this metaphorically. She meant that the creatures have the form of salamanders — the long body, the four legs, the tail, the wide mouth, the smooth skin — but the form has been arrived at through a process that does not match the one recorded in the fossil record or the genome. Their skin is translucent, and beneath it the veins are visible, and the substance that moves through those veins is dark — darker than blood, darker than hemolymph, a fluid that appears, under the limited examination the herpetologist was able to conduct before she felt she should stop, to carry dissolved minerals rather than gases. They do not breathe through lungs. They do not have lungs. The dark fluid circulates and something is exchanged with the water around them directly through the skin, and the water that leaves their bodies is different from the water that enters — cooler, slightly, and carrying a faint luminescence that fades within seconds.

They gather in clusters. This is the behavior that draws attention: a dozen or more pressed together at the edge of a pool, barely moving, their bodies overlapping, limbs draped across each other with the casual intimacy of sleeping children. They are not sleeping. Their eyes — small, dark, lidless — are open. They are aware of each other and of their surroundings. But the gathering itself appears to be the *purpose,* not a means to a purpose. They are together because being together is what they do. The clusters form and persist and occasionally one salamander will leave one cluster and move with that fluid, boneless locomotion to another and join it, and the cluster it left will close the gap without reaction, and the cluster it joins will make room without acknowledgment.

They do not eat. They have been observed for periods extending to a week. They do not eat. They do not excrete. They do not reproduce, or at least they have not been observed reproducing, and there are no juvenile salamanders in the basin — all of them are the same size, the same proportion, the same degree of physical maturity. They are not immortal. There are fewer of them near the structures where the engines hum, as if the hum displaces them, and there are more of them near the largest pools, as if the pools' connection to the ahead-sky draws them. But the population is stable. Nothing is born. Nothing dies. They are here at a count of perhaps forty, and they move between pools in a slow rotation that has no schedule but does have a pattern, if you watch long enough — a circuit, touching each pool in sequence, as if checking something, as if confirming something, as if the pools need to be visited and the salamanders have decided, or been assigned, to visit them.

The herpetologist described holding one. She did not recommend this. The salamander did not resist. It was warm, she said — the same warmth as everything in the basin, that common low heat — and its body yielded to her grip without fighting, the way a saturated sponge yields. Her hand came away wet. The fluid on her skin was not the fluid in the pool. It was the dark fluid from the veins, and where it touched her skin she felt a sensation she described, after a long silence, as "remembering something I have never experienced." She washed her hands immediately. She did not elaborate.

## The Beetles

There are more beetles than there are anything else in the basin that can be seen without magnification. This is an estimate — no one has counted them — but it is an estimate that the landscape itself supports: in the fractures where the mat-organisms grow thickest, the stone is *covered* in beetles, layer upon layer, a living crust of black shells that shifts and clicks and flows.

They are beetles in the way the salamanders are salamanders. The form is right — the domed carapace, the six legs, the segmented antennae, the mandibles — but the form has been *simplified.* They have no eyes. The head is smooth where the compound eyes should be, a continuous curve of black chitin unbroken by any feature. They do not need to see. They navigate by something else — humidity, perhaps, or the gradient of the organisms' slow breathing, or the faint electromagnetic signature of the engines' hum, which they can sense through the substrate of the stone itself. They move toward the organisms and away from the dry stone and they aggregate where the feeding is good, and they feed, and the feeding is the only clearly legible behavior they exhibit.

They eat the mat-organisms. This has been observed directly. A beetle will position itself on a frond — one of the larger, shelf-like growths that pulse on the two-breaths-per-minute cycle — and press its mandibles to the surface, and scrape, and ingest. The scraping is audible. It is one of the small sounds of the basin, and when you hear it in the dark — the tiny, methodical rasping of a thousand mandibles on a thousand fronds — it has the character of a sound that has been happening for longer than you have been alive and will continue after you leave.

The organisms do not appear to suffer from this. The mats and fronds that are most heavily grazed are also the thickest, the most robust, the ones that pulse most strongly. There is a relationship here that is not predation and not symbiosis but something else — a maintenance, a pruning, a conversation conducted in the medium of consumption. The beetles eat the organisms and the organisms grow where the beetles have eaten them, and the growing is shaped by the eating, and the eating is shaped by the growing, and the result is a landscape of forms that are neither natural nor designed but *negotiated.*

They flow around obstacles. This is the behavior that visitors describe with the most discomfort. When you walk through a column of beetles — and you will, because the columns cross the open ground between fractures, moving in lines that are straight at the scale of meters but curve at the scale of the basin — they do not scatter. They do not react. They flow around your feet and re-form on the other side as if you were a stone in a current, as if you were not there, as if the thing that is there is not you but an obstruction, a temporary shape in a medium they are moving through. They do not acknowledge you. You are not the kind of thing they acknowledge.

There is one behavior that is harder to categorize. At the bases of the structures — the concrete structures that house the engines — the beetles gather in dense, quiet masses. They do not feed there. The organisms do not grow on the concrete. The beetles simply gather, pressing themselves against the base of the walls, covering the first few centimeters of the concrete surface in a layer of black shells, and they are still. They do not click. They do not move. They are listening, or they are feeling, or they are doing something that has no analog in behavior as we understand it. They stay there for hours. Then they leave. They return. They leave. No pattern.

An entomologist who visited the basin for a single overnight observation described the beetles at the structure bases as "congregants." He did not mean this as metaphor. He meant that the behavior was, in its structure and its regularity, indistinguishable from devotional practice. He did not return.

## The Things in the Distance

The large shapes that move across the basin at the edge of visibility are the most difficult to describe because they are the most difficult to *see.* Not because they are far away, though they are. Not because the darkness is total, though it is. But because they exist at a level of visual resolution that the human eye is not designed to process.

They are large. This is known from the parallax — when you move across the basin, the shapes shift against the distant stone at a rate that indicates they are not small things seen close up but large things seen from far away. Estimates based on the parallax and the faint bioluminescent backlighting of the organisms suggest they are roughly the size of cattle. Perhaps larger. Their gait is quadrupedal, or appears to be — four supports, moving in a slow, deliberate sequence, covering ground at a pace that makes glaciers look hasty.

Their edges are the problem. The boundary between the shape and the air around it is not a line but a gradient, a region where the thing and the not-thing shade into each other by degrees. This is not blur — the center of each shape is dense, solid, dark in a way that suggests mass and presence. But the periphery *thins.* It becomes less certain. It is there and it is not there, and the transition is not a mistake of vision but a property of the shapes themselves. They are not shapeshifting. They are not fading. They are *partial.* They exist in full at their centers and in part at their edges, as if they were never quite finished being solid, as if the process that made them ran out of material or conviction at the margins.

They do not eat. They are not seen near water. They do not interact with the salamanders or the beetles or the birds. They move through the open ground between the structures, and their movement has a quality that the few people who have watched them long enough have described as *patrolling.* Not searching. Not hunting. Patrolling — walking a circuit that has been walked before, that will be walked again, that exists as a route the way a riverbed exists as a route: not because someone decided but because the water has always gone that way.

There are four. Or five. The count is uncertain because the edges are uncertain, and at the limits of visibility it is not possible to determine whether a shape is one animal or two animals close together. But there are at least four. They have been in the basin for as long as anyone has been visiting. They are always in the same places — not the same coordinates, but the same positions relative to each other, maintaining a spacing that is roughly equal, as if they are the corners of something. A perimeter. A boundary. A shape that is defined not by walls but by the animals that walk its edges.

A physicist who spent a week in the basin and who had a background in fluid dynamics proposed that the shapes were not animals at all but *standing waves* — patterns in the medium of the basin's darkness, the way a wave is a pattern in water. The darkness, she said, is a substance. Substances support patterns. The shapes are patterns. They move because the patterns propagate. They have edges that are indeterminate because waves have edges that are indeterminate. She was asked whether this meant the shapes were alive. She thought about this for a long time. She said: "A wave is not alive. A wave is not dead. A wave is what the medium does when the medium is disturbed. I do not think the question of alive or dead is the right question. I think the question is: what is disturbing the medium, and where is it coming from?"

She did not answer her own question. She packed her equipment and left the basin and did not return, and in her notes — which she did publish, in a paper that was read by eleven people and cited by none — she wrote, in the final paragraph, that the shapes had moved closer to her on the third night, and that she had felt, for the first time in her life, the sensation of being *evaluated* by something that was not using any sense she could identify.

## The Birds

The birds are the ones that stay with you.

They stand in the shallow water between the structures. There are six of them. Six. The count is precise because they do not move, and when something does not move, you can count it. They are large — standing, they reach roughly a meter and a half, the height of a tall heron, though their proportions are wrong for herons. The legs are too long relative to the body. The neck is too thick. The beak is heavy, curved slightly downward, and it has the character not of a bird's beak but of a tool — a shape designed for a function that is not feeding.

They are flightless. Their wings are present but reduced — small, folded tight against the body, visible only as a ridge of darker feathers along the flank. They do not use them. They do not need them. Nothing in the basin requires flight. The air is too heavy and the ground is too wet and the distances are too vast and too small, simultaneously, for flight to be useful.

They do not eat. They have been watched for a week. They do not eat. They do not drink. They stand in the water, and the water moves around their legs, and they breathe.

Five seconds in. Seven seconds out.

The breathing is the sound of the basin. Not the only sound — there is the hum, and the water, and the clicking of the beetles, and the faint scraping of mandibles on fronds — but the breathing is the sound that fills the space the way the darkness fills the space. It is not loud. It is present. It is the baseline against which all the other sounds are measured, and when the engines shift their chord — when the hum changes quality, moving from warm to anxious over the course of hours — the breathing does not change. It is the constant. It is the thing the basin always does.

They notice. This is the behavior that defines them, that separates them from everything else that lives here. The salamanders do not notice you. The beetles do not notice you. The shapes in the distance might notice you — the physicist believed they did — but their noticing is distant, ambiguous, filtered through gradient edges and uncertain forms. The birds notice you with a directness that is total.

When you approach a bird — and you will, because they are between the structures, and the structures are what you came to see — its head turns. It turns slowly. It turns with a speed that is mechanical, not organic — a constant rate of rotation, no acceleration, no deceleration, no settling. It turns until the heavy beak is pointed at you and the head is angled slightly upward, and then it stops. And it is looking at you.

It does not have eyes. This is the thing that you will not believe until you see it. The head is smooth. There are no orbits, no sockets, no depressions, no features. The surface of the head is a continuous covering of dark, slightly rough feathers that flow without interruption from the crown to the base of the beak, and where the eyes should be there is only more of this surface. It is looking at you without eyes. It is seeing you without seeing.

The sensation of being seen by something that has no eyes is specific and unforgettable. It is not the sensation of being watched, which implies a surveillance, a tracking, a gathering of information. It is the sensation of being *known.* The bird does not gather information about you. It does not need to. It sees you the way a stone sees the ground — completely, without effort, as a condition of its existence. You are there. It is there. The seeing is the relationship between those two facts. Nothing more is required.

The birds do not do anything with this seeing. They do not approach. They do not flee. They do not call or display or react. They see you, and they continue to breathe, and you stand in the shallow water with the warmth around your ankles and the darkness around your body and the hum in your sternum, and you are seen, and the seeing does not end, and you leave, and the seeing does not end. You walk back up the ramp and the stars rearrange and the air thins and the world resumes its speed and you are still being seen. You are being seen now. The birds saw you, and the seeing did not stop when you left, because the seeing was not an event but a state — a condition established by the encounter, permanent, a small permanent weight carried in the body like a stone in a pocket.

A behaviorist who spent two weeks in the basin — the longest documented stay — reported that the birds occasionally moved. Not walking. *Repositioning.* Once every several days, a bird would lift one leg from the water and place it in a different location — a displacement of perhaps half a meter — and settle again. The movement was slow. The movement was deliberate. The movement had the quality of a correction, a recalibration, as if the bird were a needle on a dial adjusting to a new value. After the movement, the breathing would resume. Five seconds in. Seven seconds out. The same. Always the same.

The behaviorist was asked, in a later interview, what she believed the birds were doing in the basin. She paused for a long time. She said: "I think they are holding the basin in place. I think without them, the basin would not be here. I think they are the anchor. I think the engines do something and the water carries something and the organisms process something and the beetles maintain something and the shapes in the distance patrol something, and the birds — the birds are the reason all of it stays. They stand in the water and they breathe and they see, and the seeing is the anchoring. The seeing is what holds it."

She was asked whether she believed the birds were conscious. She paused again. She said: "I think the birds are the most conscious things I have ever encountered. I think they are more conscious than I am. I think they are conscious the way the ocean is deep — not as a quality they possess but as a fact about their nature. I think if the birds stopped breathing, the basin would unseal. I think if the birds stopped seeing, the stars would correct. I think the birds are the only reason the copy of the sky holds its errors. And I think they know this. I think they have always known this. I think that is what the breathing is. It is not respiration. It is *attention.* Five seconds of attention in, seven seconds of attention out. Maintained. Unbroken. For as long as the basin has been here, which is longer than the word *here* has had meaning."

## What Else Lives Here

There is something else. It is not an animal. It is not a plant. It is not a microorganism or a shape in the distance or a bird. It is the thing that the basin *is,* apart from the things *in* it.

The people who have visited the basin and who have been asked the direct question — "Is the basin itself alive?" — do not answer quickly. They pause. They look at something that is not in the room. They say variations of the same thing.

One said: "The basin is not alive. The basin is the *condition* for life. The basin is what life looks like when life is a place rather than a thing in a place."

Another said: "I do not think the basin is alive. I think the basin is *awake.* I think there is a difference. I think life is what happens inside an awake thing. I think we are inside an awake thing. I think it knows we are here. I do not think it cares. I think caring is what life does, and the basin is not life. The basin is the *space* in which life does what it does, and the space is awake, and the waking is the hum."

A third said: "The basin is an organ. I do not know whose. I do not know where. I know that the water is the blood and the engines are the nerves and the organisms are the tissue and the birds are the — I don't know. The birds are the thing that keeps the organ from dissolving. The birds are the membrane. The birds are the skin. And the shapes in the distance are the immune system, or the memory, or the dream. I do not know. I know it is an organ. I know it is doing something for something. And I know that the something it is doing for is not us."

The basin holds this. The basin holds the water and the stone and the organisms and the beetles and the salamanders and the shapes and the birds and the structures and the engines and the hum and the darkness and the wrong sky and the eleven-minute delay and the warmth that accumulates like something poured and the time that dilates like something dissolved and the breathing, the breathing, the breathing that goes on and on in the dark below the copy of a sky, five seconds in and seven seconds out, the attention of a place that is not a place but is, undeniably, *here* — and what lives here is everything that the here-ness contains, and everything that the here-ness contains is what the here-ness *is,* and the distinction between what lives in a place and what a place is has, in the basin, dissolved the way everything dissolves: slowly, in warm water, in the dark.
