# What Lives in the Rootvault

## I.

Let me say first what does not live here.

No one lives here. Not in the way that *living somewhere* usually means — no creature has made this place a home in the sense of returning to it deliberately, defending it, shaping it to a purpose. There is no nest. There is no burrow with a defined entrance. There is no creature whose territory this is, whose survival depends on this particular thirty-meter oval of warmth and damp and dim. If the crack above were to close tomorrow — if mortar shifted and stone settled and the seam in the foundation sealed itself — the things inside would not, for the most part, be displaced. They would simply continue, in the dark, without the small interruption of your visits.

This is important. The Rootvault is not a habitat in the ecological sense of a place shaped by and for its inhabitants. It is more like an accident that has been capitalized on. Things live here the way people live in cities: not because the city was made for them, but because they found a corner and stayed.

But they do stay. And staying, over time, becomes its own kind of belonging.

## II.

Let me start with what you would notice first, if you sat down and stayed long enough to stop being a disruption.

The fungus gnats.

There are more of them than of anything else. Hundreds, probably, though counting is impossible and pointless. They are small — two or three millimeters, dark-bodied, with wings that catch what little light there is and throw it back in a way that makes each one seem to flicker in and out of existence as it moves. They do not fly in formation. They do not swarm in the dramatic, coiling patterns of midges over water. They move in slow, individual loops, each one tracing a path that seems random but that, if you watched a single individual long enough, would resolve into something like a pattern — a preference for certain zones of the air, certain temperatures, certain levels of moisture.

What they are doing, mostly, is finding mates. And after mates, finding substrate. The females are looking for the moss — specifically for the damp, decomposing layers underneath the living moss, where fungal hyphae thread through the organic matter and break it down. That is where they will lay their eggs. That is where their larvae will hatch and feed and undergo the quiet transformation from grub to gnat, in the dark, in the warmth, not knowing anything about the world above or the world beyond this oval.

You might find this unremarkable. Flies are flies. But consider what they have done. They have found a crack in foundation stone in a forgotten part of a city and they have descended through it and they have identified a pocket of warmth and moisture and biological activity and they have said, in whatever language fungus gnats use to say such things: *here*. Not because it is ideal. It is not ideal — the light is wrong, the space is small, the airflow is minimal. But it is sufficient. And sufficient, for most living things, is enough.

The gnats are the air's population. They are so numerous and so constant that they become, after a while, part of what the air is. Not a thing in the air but a quality of it, the way dust is a quality of light. You stop seeing them. You start feeling them — the faintest displacement, the barely-there brush of something that is alive and moving and not you.

They do not land on you. This is not politeness. This is preference. You are not their substrate. You are not their mate. You are not their concern.

## III.

The beetles are a different matter.

There are — by careful and repeated observation, by sitting very still in the dim and watching the moss surfaces and the margins of the water depressions and the patches of bare soil between — at least four species present in the Rootvault. There may be more. There are almost certainly more. Beetles are good at not being seen, even when they are not trying.

The most visible are the small black ones. They are round-ish, three or four millimeters long, glossy in a way that suggests armor, though what they are armored against in a place with no obvious predators is not clear. Perhaps the armor is vestigial. Perhaps the dangers that shaped their ancestors are still present in ways you have not observed. They move across the moss surfaces with a deliberation that seems almost philosophical. Each step is placed. Each direction change is a small event. If a beetle of this kind reaches an obstacle — a root, a patch of dry soil, a curled leaf that has fallen from above — it does not go over the obstacle if it can go around. It does not go around the obstacle if it can reverse and find another path entirely. It is not cautious. It is thorough.

These beetles feed on something. You cannot see what. The organic film that collects on the shallow water depressions is one source — you have seen them at the margins, touching the water with their front legs, sometimes lowering their heads to the surface in a motion that might be drinking or might be eating or might be both. But they also spend time on the moss, and on the darker soil, and in the gaps between root and brick where small accumulations of organic matter — leaf fragments, dead moss, the bodies of gnats that have finished their short lives and fallen — collect. They are, you think, generalists. Eaters of what is available. This is a successful strategy in a place where what is available is unpredictable.

The copper-colored beetles are different. There are fewer of them — perhaps a dozen, perhaps fewer; they are harder to see against the dark soil and the brown root bark. They are longer than the black ones, more elongate, with a sheen that shifts depending on the angle of the faint light. They move faster. They do not linger at the water margins. They stay close to the roots, climbing the root surfaces with a sureness that suggests they belong there, that the root is their territory in a way that the moss is the black beetles' territory. What they are doing on the roots you cannot tell. The roots have their own ecosystem — moss, fungal growths, the thin biofilms that develop on damp bark — and the copper beetles are working that ecosystem in some way you can observe but not interpret.

There is a third kind that you see only once. It is smaller than the others — perhaps two millimeters — and it is pale, almost translucent, and it moves quickly across a patch of bare soil and disappears into a gap between two root strands and does not come back. You sit for ten minutes waiting. It does not come back. It was either a resident of a deeper layer, briefly surfaced, or a visitor from somewhere else — another crack, another pocket, another accidental room that you will never find. You think about it for longer than it deserves. The pale beetle is gone. The pale beetle has its own life.

The fourth kind is not a kind. It is a single individual. You see it on your third visit, on the underside of a root arch, upside-down, clinging to the bark. It is larger than the others — almost a centimeter — and it is dark brown with lighter markings on its wing covers that might be a pattern or might be dirt. It is the only beetle you see in that position. It is the only beetle that seems to be doing nothing — not moving, not feeding, not searching. Just there. Clinging. It might be resting. It might be dying. It might be in some beetle state that has no name in any language you speak. You see it again on your fourth visit, in the same position. On your fifth visit, it is gone. In its place, on the root bark, there is a small mark where its legs gripped. That is all.

## IV.

The spiders.

There are two kinds, and they represent two entirely different philosophies of survival.

The web-builders are the visible ones. There are three webs that you can find, each in a corner where root meets brick — the kind of corner that is not a corner at all but a curve, a convergence, a place where two surfaces come close enough to create geometry. The webs are small. The largest is perhaps the width of your spread hand. They are built with the precision that spiders bring to such things — radial threads, spiral threads, attachment points that distribute the forces of falling drops and struggling prey across a structure that is, for its weight, stronger than steel.

But these webs are not catching much. You never see prey in them. What they catch, mostly, is water — the condensation drops that fall from the brickwork above. Each web holds a few drops, jewel-bright in the dim, and the spider sits at the edge or in the corner, and you wonder if the water is a nuisance or a resource, if the spider drinks the drops or simply tolerates them. Spiders need water. In a place this damp, water is not a problem. But food might be.

The spiders are pale. Almost translucent, like the pale beetle, but not quite — they have a faint amber tint, a yellowishness that suggests they too are adapted to low light, that the color of their bodies has shifted to match the color of their world. They are small. The largest is perhaps eight millimeters including legs. They sit still for very long times. They are the patience of the Rootvault made bodily.

You wonder, sometimes, whether they are surviving here or merely persisting. There is a difference. Survival implies a population, a future, a continuation. Persistence implies an individual hanging on. You do not know if these spiders are part of a population — if there are males and females and egg sacs and spiderlings — or if they are individuals who arrived through some crack or gap and built their webs and will live their lives and leave no descendants. The Rootvault does not tell you. The Rootvault is not obligated to tell you.

The hunting spider — the leggy brown one — is a different creature entirely. You see it twice, and both times it is moving. It does not build webs. It roams. It is larger than the web-builders — perhaps a centimeter and a half including legs — and it is brown in a way that suggests dry ground and open spaces, which makes its presence here seem slightly wrong, like a word from one sentence that has wandered into another. It moves through the moss and over the root surfaces and across the bare soil with a speed and a directness that the web-builders do not have. It is looking for something. You do not see it find anything. You do not see it eat.

But it lives here. You are sure of this. It is not a visitor. It knows the space — you can tell by the way it moves through it, not hesitating at gaps, not investigating corners, just traveling, the way you travel through your own apartment in the dark, knowing where the furniture is. The hunting spider has a map of the Rootvault in whatever organ spiders use for maps, and it is using that map, and it has somewhere to be.

## V.

And then there are the ones you do not see.

The worms are the largest creatures in the Rootvault and you never see them. Not once. Not in five visits, not in ten, not in the hours you spend lying on the warm ground with your face close to the soil. You see their castings — small, neat mounds of processed earth, dark and fine-grained, deposited on the surface in patterns that suggest a systematic exploration of the substrate. You see their tunnels — tiny openings in the soil, some no wider than a needle, that disappear into darkness. You see, when you look closely at the walls of the space where the soil meets the brick or the root, the traces of their paths — tubes of air in the solid earth, like the negative space of a body that was there and moved on.

There are, you estimate, at least several dozen. The ground is riddled with their work. If you could make the soil transparent — if you could see the Rootvault as a three-dimensional space including the substrate below the floor — you would see that the earth is not solid at all but porous, threaded with tunnels, aerated and mixed and transformed by the passage of worms through it. They are the architects of the ground. Everything else — the moss, the beetles, the spiders, the ferns — is living on a surface that the worms have made.

What the worms eat is the organic matter that works its way down through the soil from above — dead roots, decomposing moss, the leaf that fell through the crack, the bodies of dead gnats, the microscopic fungi that break down all of it. They eat the dead and they produce the living soil, and the living soil supports the plants, and the plants hold the moisture, and the moisture supports the moss and the insects and the spiders, and the whole thing is a cycle that the worms do not know they are running.

But they are running it. They are the engine. They are the reason the ground is warm — or rather, they are one of the reasons, because the warmth is also bacterial, also fungal, also the slow oxidation of organic matter by every microorganism in the substrate. The worms are the visible part of that invisible community. When you lie on the ground and feel the warmth move through your back, you are feeling, in part, the metabolic heat of worms digesting the dead.

This is not a metaphor. The warmth is literal. The worms are literal. The dead are literal. The living soil they produce is literal. You are lying on something that is alive in a way that does not look like life — not moving, not growing in a way you can see, not doing anything that you recognize as a verb — but that is, by any biological measure, more alive than anything else in the Rootvault. The soil is the largest organism in this space. The worms are its visible cells.

## VI.

There are things I have not mentioned yet, because they are harder to see and harder to talk about.

The mites. There are mites — tiny, eight-legged, not spiders but related, small enough to live on the surfaces of moss leaves and in the gaps between root bark and to be invisible to you unless you bring a hand lens. You do not bring a hand lens. But you know they are there because they are always there, in soil, in moss, in any environment where organic matter is being processed. They are part of the community. They are the scavengers and the predators of the very small, the ones who eat the debris that the worms cannot reach and the bodies of the gnats that have not yet fallen to the ground. They are the middle layer of the food web — too small for the beetles to bother with, too large for the bacteria to consume alone. They do their work. They do not need you to see it.

The springtails. There are springtails on the moss and on the soil surface — tiny, pale, elongate, with the furcula folded under their bodies that allows them to jump when disturbed. You disturb them without meaning to, when you touch the moss or step on the soil, and you see them spring — a flicker of motion, gone before you can focus on it. They are detritivores. They eat the dead plant material that the moss and the roots shed. They are, in their way, as important as the worms. They work the surfaces. The worms work the depths. Between them, the dead become the living.

The nematodes. The bacteria. The fungi — the vast, invisible networks of hyphae that thread through the soil and the moss and the root bark, decomposing, digesting, exchanging nutrients with the living roots in relationships that are older than animals and more complex than most animals could understand. The Rootvault is, in some real sense, a fungal space. The roots brought the first fungi down. The fungi colonized the soil. The soil became capable of supporting the life that now fills it. If you pulled out the fungi, the whole structure — the moss, the insects, the worms, the everything — would collapse. The fungi are the foundation of the foundation. They are what holds the Rootvault together, more than the roots, more than the brick.

You do not see the fungi. You see their effects — the dark soil, the decomposition, the smell of conversion. You see, occasionally, the fruiting bodies: tiny, almost invisible, a pale thread rising from the moss surface with a pinhead cap at the top, there for a day and then gone. You see one once, near the water depression where the moss is emerald, and it is so small that you are not sure you saw it until you look again and it is still there, and it is still there the next day, and then on the third day it is not. It did its work. It released its spores. The spores are in the air now, invisible, drifting, settling, germinating. The Rootvault is seeding itself.

## VII.

I need to talk about the flower.

There is a plant near the far end of the oval. It is an ordinary plant — some weed of the city, a thing that grows in cracks and gutters and the neglected margins of car parks. You would step on it without noticing, above. But here, in the Rootvault, it has become singular.

Its stem is long — too long, longer than it should be, because the light is faint and the plant has reached for it with everything it has. The stem is pale, almost white at the base, shading to a faint green near the top. It is slightly translucent. You can see, if you look closely, the vascular bundles inside it — the threads that carry water up from the roots and sugars down from the leaves. It is a small window into a body that is doing its best with limited materials.

It has leaves. They are small and thin and slightly curled at the edges, not from dryness but from the effort of producing themselves in low light. They are darker than they should be — the deep, committed green of a plant that has packed its chloroplasts dense in each cell, that has concentrated its photosynthetic machinery to extract the maximum energy from the minimum photons. These leaves have worked harder than any leaf you have ever seen. They are not pretty. They are effective.

And the plant has flowered.

The flower is white. It is small — the size of a fingernail, perhaps smaller. It has the standard architecture of a small flower: petals, sepals, the reproductive parts in the center. But it is not a standard flower. It is tired. It has the look of something that was produced at a cost, that drew on reserves the plant could not really afford to spend, that was a gamble — a throw of whatever dice plants throw — on the possibility that there might be a pollinator, that there might be a mate, that there might be a future.

There is no pollinator. There are no bees in the Rootvault. There are no flies that visit flowers. The fungus gnats are not interested. The beetles are not interested. The flower opens into an empty room, in a dim light, and it offers itself to no one.

But it is still open. It has not closed. It has not given up. It is there, white and small and exhausted, doing the thing it was made to do regardless of whether the doing will lead to anything. This is not courage, because courage implies a choice, and the plant did not choose. This is simply what the plant does. It flowers. It flowers because it is alive and because flowering is what aliveness produces in this species, and the conditions for flowering — the warmth, the moisture, the faint light — are present, and so it flowers.

You sit near it for a long time. You do not touch it. You sit near it and you feel something that you are reluctant to name because naming it will make it smaller than it is. But it is something like this: the flower is the most honest thing in the Rootvault. It is not beautiful. It is not extraordinary. It is a small white flower produced by a weed in an underground room that no one knows about, and it will not produce fruit, and it will not be seen by anything that can appreciate it, and it is open, and it is there.

This is what life does. This is what life does when no one is watching. This is what life does when the conditions are barely sufficient and the effort is enormous and the payoff is nothing. It flowers.

## VIII.

I should tell you about the ferns.

There are three patches of maidenhair fern, all growing in the crooks of roots where the condensation drips most regularly. They are small — the largest frond is the length of your hand, and most are shorter. Their black stems are fine as wire, finer, and they hold the fronds in a way that seems precarious but is not — each stem is rigid enough to support its leaflet, and each leaflet is so thin that it barely weighs anything, and the whole structure floats in the still air with a lightness that is structural, not decorative.

The ferns do not flower. Ferns do not flower. They reproduce by spores, and the spores are produced in sori on the undersides of the leaflets — small clusters of cases that open and release spores into the air. You cannot see this happen. You can see the sori, if you look closely: small bumps on the underside of the frond, darker than the surrounding tissue, arranged in a pattern that is regular but not geometric. They are doing what the flower is doing, in their way — reproducing, attempting continuation — but they are doing it without the drama. The ferns are quieter about it. The ferns have been doing this for hundreds of millions of years, longer than flowers have existed, and they do it with a patience that flowers cannot match.

The ferns shiver when the drops hit them. This is the closest thing to wind in the Rootvault, and it is not wind — it is impact, a single drop striking a frond and sending a ripple through the leaflet that lasts less than a second. But it is motion. It is the motion of a living thing responding to a physical force, and in a place where motion is rare and small and deliberate, the shivering of the ferns is an event. It means something. It means the water is falling. It means the air is moving. It means the system is running.

The ferns are old. Not these individual ferns — these are probably not more than a few years old — but the lineage of ferns, the body plan, the strategy. They were old before the Rootvault existed. They were old before the city existed. They were old before the concept of a city existed. They have been growing in damp, dim places since before damp, dim places had names. And here, in this particular damp, dim place, they are continuing. They are doing what they have always done. They are not impressed by the brickwork or the roots or the crack in the foundation. They are not impressed by you. They are growing.

## IX.

Let me tell you what I think lives here. Not the list — the beetles, the spiders, the worms, the gnats, the mites, the springtails, the nematodes, the fungi, the bacteria, the ferns, the moss, the flowering weed. The list is accurate but it is not the truth. The truth is that what lives here is a community.

I do not use this word lightly. I use it in the ecological sense: a group of organisms occupying the same space and interacting with one another in ways that make each one's existence dependent, in some degree, on the others'. The worms depend on the fungi to begin the decomposition of organic matter. The fungi depend on the worms to mix and aerate the soil. The moss depends on the moisture that the roots and the brickwork and the condensation cycle provide. The beetles depend on the moss and the organic film and the dead. The spiders depend on the beetles and the gnats. The gnats depend on the moss and the fungi. The flower depends on everything — on the soil the worms make, on the moisture the space holds, on the faint light that the crack allows.

Remove any one of these and the others would feel it. Not as a catastrophe, perhaps. The Rootvault is not so fragile that the loss of one species would collapse the whole. But it would shift. The balance would change. The warmth would redistribute. The moss would thin or thicken. The beetles would move or stay. The space would become something slightly different from what it is.

This is what a community is. Not a collection of individuals who happen to be in the same place. A system of relationships, each one small, all of them together making the place what it is. The Rootvault is not a container for life. The Rootvault is the life in it. The life is the place.

## X.

And you.

Where do you fit in this? You are the largest creature that has ever been in the Rootvault. You displace air. You block light. Your feet compress the soil. Your breath adds moisture and warmth and carbon dioxide to the air. You sit, and the beetles near you pause — not in alarm, but in recalibration. Something has changed. The space is different when you are in it.

You are not part of the community. You are a visitor. You are the crack in the crack, the opening that opens and closes. You come and you go and the Rootvault continues without you, and this is not a sadness. This is the nature of the relationship. You are the observer. You are the one who sees the flower and feels something and goes back up to the city and carries, for a while, the knowledge that underneath the surfaces there are rooms that were never built, holding warmth and silence and the slow, patient sound of things growing in the dark.

But you are also, while you are there, a presence. The beetles adjust. The hunting spider takes a different path. The fungus gnats part around you and close behind you like water. The worms, below, do not notice you at all.

This is the truth of the Rootvault: it does not need you. It does not need anyone. It is complete. It is a room that was never built, holding a life that was never planned, in a city that has forgotten this corner of itself. It is an accident that has become an ecosystem. It is a wound that has become a garden.

And what lives there is everything that can. Everything that found the crack and came down and stayed. Everything that was carried in by water or by root or by the slow falling of organic matter from above. Everything that was already in the soil, waiting. Everything that can survive in warmth and dim and damp and almost-silence, and that has made that survival into a life — small, quiet, persistent, unfolding in the dark, not for anyone, not because of anything, but because living is what living things do, and this place, this accidental room, is where they do it.

The flower is open. The worms are working. The beetles are proceeding. The moss is dark and soft and holding water. The ferns are shivering. The air is moving, very slowly, in a cycle that takes about a minute.

And you climb back through the crack, and the city is loud and bright, and the flower is still open, and it does not close when you leave.
