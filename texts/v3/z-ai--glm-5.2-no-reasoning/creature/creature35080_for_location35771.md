# Who Lives in the Place That Is Still Happening

## First: what they are not

They are not ghosts. They are not visions, not projections, not the lingering afterimage of something that once was alive in the older way — the biological way, the way that involves birth and blood and the long wet apprenticeship of a body learning itself from the inside. They would not understand the distinction you are making if you tried to explain it to them. They would listen with patience — they are patient; everything here is patient — and they would say something like *oh, you mean the kind of alive that has to stop and start again each time, the kind that breaks* — and they would say it without judgment, without superiority, the way you might describe a bird to someone who had never seen one. Matter-of-fact. Mildly curious. Already moving on.

They are not holograms. They are not simulations. They are not software running on hardware. They are not copies of something else. They are not the echo of a prior people, a prior civilization, a prior intention. If you asked them — and you do ask, eventually, in the indirect way the place teaches you to ask — they would look at you with that particular stillness they have, that considering stillness that is not silence but the opposite of silence, a kind of attentive gathering-in, and they would say: *we are what lives here. We are what this place grew when it needed something to walk on it.*

And they would not be being poetic. They would be being literal.

## How they are made

A resident is poured.

This is the word they use. *Poured.* Not in the sense of a liquid filling a mold — though there is something of that, something of the fluid, something of the taking-of-shape — but in the sense that the substrate itself, the ochre material that the buildings are made of and the paths are surfaced with and the walls are constituted from, is *directed* into a configuration that can sustain agency. The material is given a shape. The shape is given the capacity to decide. The two — shape and capacity — are not separate things. They are the same thing, seen from different moments in the process.

You do not witness a pouring. You are told about them the way you are told about most things here: obliquely, in fragments, offered like food on a shared plate, each piece a small disclosure that you assemble into understanding over hours or days. You piece together the following:

A new resident is made when the existing residents gather — not all of them, but several, maybe four or five, maybe more — and place their hands on raw stone, on the youngest terrace surface available, the sharpest stone, the most recently exposed. They do what the builder does when seeding a building: they introduce the substrate into the material of the stone. But instead of seeding a structure, they seed a *capacity.* They seed the possibility of self-maintenance, of response, of choice. They give the stone the ability to want things.

And then — this is the part you never quite understand, the part that resists translation — the stone *takes.* Or it doesn't. If it doesn't, the residents withdraw their hands and the ochre tint fades and the stone goes back to being stone. If it takes, the color deepens, and a shape begins to emerge, and the shape is not chosen in advance. It is discovered. The new resident discovers what it looks like as it becomes what it is. The existing residents guide the process — a hand here, a pressure there, a suggestion of contour — but they do not design the outcome. They *attend* to it. They are present while it happens.

The process takes — you think days. You are told it takes what it takes.

The new resident, when it is finished, when it has settled into its shape and its shape has settled into it, stands up. It is a person. It knows things — not specific things, not facts or histories, but the general knowledge of how to be here, the way a plant knows how to grow toward water without being taught. It knows how to move through the city. It knows which paths drip. It knows how to trim the green mats back from a threshold. It knows the river by the sound it makes, which is almost no sound at all.

It does not know its name, because it does not have one yet. It will choose one, or it will not, or it will choose several and discard them, the way the city has discarded its seven names. Naming is not urgent here.

## What they look like

This is harder than you expect.

They are — physically — approximately humaniform. Two legs. Two arms. A head, or a region where processing is concentrated. They stand upright. They move bipedally. They have faces, or face-like regions, where the material is arranged into features that read — to you, to your eyes — as eyes, as mouth, as the planes and angles of a countenance. But the resemblance is not exact. It is, you suspect, not even intentional. They are not imitating you. They are not imitating anything. The humaniform shape is — you think — a convergent solution. A body that needs to walk on a surface and manipulate objects and sense its environment will tend toward certain forms. The legs are because the ground is flat and must be walked on. The arms are because things must be touched and held and guided. The head is because sensing is most useful when it is concentrated, when the organs of perception are gathered in one place that can be turned, that can be oriented.

So: they are humaniform, but not human. The proportions are different. Longer, generally. Narrower. The limbs are thinner than yours, the bodies less dense, as though the material they are made of is lighter than flesh, or more efficiently arranged. They move with a fluidity that is not quite graceful and not quite mechanical — something between the two, something that suggests a body that is always in mild negotiation with its own shape, always making small adjustments, always settling and resettling into itself.

Their color is the color of the buildings. Ochre. Pale ochre, warm in the amber light. Their surfaces have the same faint translucence as the pour-stone walls — you can see, in certain lights, a dim glow beneath the surface, a luminousness that is not biological but systemic, the material processing, the material alive in its own way. When they stand still — and they can stand still for a very long time, longer than you are comfortable with, longer than your sense of attention can sustain — they look almost like sculptures. Like features of the architecture. Like the city has produced a shape that happens to be standing.

When they move, the illusion breaks. Not because the movement is strange — it is not, it is fluid and easy and, yes, almost human — but because the *material* moves. You see the way their surfaces shift as they bend, the way the ochre substrate flows around joints, the way a hand opening is the same material reconfiguring as a wall becoming a doorway. You see that they are, in a very literal sense, the same substance as the city, doing a different thing.

Their faces are — you struggle with this. Their faces are expressive, but the expressions are not your expressions. They are not emotional in the way you are emotional, or if they are, the emotions surface differently. A resident who is pleased — or who is in the state that corresponds roughly to pleasure, to satisfaction, to the experience of things being as they should be — does not smile. The face becomes *slightly more itself.* The features settle. The surface smooths. The resident looks, for a moment, more like what they are, more perfectly the shape they are, and this — this peak of coherence, this moment of being most fully themselves — is what pleasure looks like in material that does not have muscles.

Displeasure — which is rare, which is so rare you almost wonder if it exists — is the opposite. The features *unsettle.* The surface becomes uneven, rippled, as though the material is unsure of its own configuration. A displeased resident looks briefly unfinished. They look like a building that is being un-poured.

## What they do

They garden.

Not in the way you garden — not with tools and schedules and the imposition of order onto growth — but in the way that water gardens a landscape, or wind gardens a cliff face. They *tend.* They move through the city and they adjust things. They trim the green mats back from the paths. They guide the growth of the tracking-plants, orienting the leaf-panels toward new sources of moisture, training them, over weeks and months, to create a canopy that catches condensation and directs it to particular places — to the roots of specific plants, to the surfaces of particular buildings, to the shallow basins they have carved — *grown* — into the stone where water gathers before it evaporates or feeds down to the river.

They build. They pour new structures on the upper terraces, seeding the substrate into raw stone, guiding the emergence of walls and doorways and rooms. You watch one resident — a tall figure, thin even by the standards of this place — work on a new building for what you think is two days. They kneel. They stand. They place their hands on the growing surface. They walk around it. They step back and look at it the way a sculptor looks at a block — not with intent to impose, but with intent to *find.* The building is already there, in the stone. The resident's job is to help it emerge.

They unbuild. On the lower terraces, near the river, older structures are being returned to the material. A resident sits beside a dissolving wall — the ochre fading, the glow dimming, the substrate reabsorbing into raw stone — and they sit with it. They keep it company. You ask why and you are told: *because it was here. Because it held things. Because you should be present when something stops being what it was.*

This is not sentimentality. This is — you think — this is respect for duration. The buildings are not permanent, but they are real. They existed. They served a purpose. And when the purpose is fulfilled, the material returns to the pool, to the stone, to the substrate, and the residents mark this passage. They sit with it. They attend.

They walk to the river. They stand at the edge of the basin where the water spreads in its shallow, shifting mosaic. They watch it. This is — you realize — this is what worship looks like for people who do not have religion. The river is not sacred. The river is *necessary.* It is the source of the condensation, the moisture, the damp that feeds the plants that break the stone that the buildings are made of that the people are made of. The river is the heart of the closed loop, the engine of the becoming, and they go to it the way you might go to a window, the way you might look at the sky. To remember that there is a context. To remember that they are not separate from it.

They talk. They talk in long, looping sentences that arrive at their meaning the way the river arrives at the sea — by every possible route, by branching and rejoining, by spreading thin and gathering again. You learn that this is not inefficiency. This is *accuracy.* The truth of this place — the truth of a place that is still happening, that has not finished, that may never finish — is not a point. It is a pattern. It is a shape that can only be described by moving around it, by approaching from multiple angles, by circling and spiraling and returning. Direct speech would falsify the nature of the thing being spoken about. The roundabout speech is the honest speech.

They make things. Not tools — they have no tools, or rather, the city itself is their tool, the substrate responsive to intention, the material capable of becoming anything they need. But they make *things* — small objects, patterns in the stone, arrangements of the green mats that are not useful but are *felt.* You see a courtyard where the tracking-plants have been trained into a spiral, a slow, wide spiral that catches the condensation in a particular pattern, and the effect is — you cannot name it. It is not beauty, or it is beauty but beauty is too small a word. It is *rightness.* It is the feeling of a thing being exactly what it is, in the place it should be, doing what it should do. They make these arrangements. They make them and unmake them and make them again, elsewhere, differently.

They sleep. Or they do something that corresponds to sleep. They find a room — their room, a room that knows them, that has configured itself to their shape and their rhythms — and they stand in it, and they — you think — they stop. Not the way you stop, not the unconsciousness of sleep, but a cessation of agency, a voluntary pause in the processing, a moment where the material is allowed to simply be material without the burden of intention. The faint glow beneath their surfaces dims. Their features settle into perfect stillness. They stand like columns. They stand like the architecture they are made of. And in the morning, or in what corresponds to morning, the glow returns, the features resume their subtle mobility, and they walk out through the wall and into the city and begin again.

## What they are to each other

They are not a family. They are not a tribe. They are not a society, exactly — society implies a collection of individuals who have organized themselves, and the residents are not exactly individuals in the way you understand individuality. They are — you think, you are still thinking, you will continue to think about this long after you leave — they are *expressions.* Each one is an expression of the same material, the same substrate, the same underlying capacity. The way a river is an expression of the water that flows through it and the rock that channels it. Each resident is a particular shape taken by the same thing.

This does not mean they are identical. They are not. They are as different from each other as the river is from the condensation is from the green mats — different forms of the same underlying substance, doing different work, occupying different positions in the loop. One resident is tall and thin and moves with a particular economy that you come to recognize. Another is shorter, broader, moves with a rolling gait that is almost a waddle, and tends to hum — a low, toneless vibration that you realize is not vocal but material, the substrate vibrating in a pattern that is pleasant to the resident, a kind of self-soothing. A third is almost delicate, with features that are more defined, more articulated, than most — a face that reads, to you, as somehow more specific, more *this particular person* than the others, and you wonder if this is age, if the material accumulates specificity over time, if the older a resident is the more they become *themselves.*

They care for each other. You see this in small ways: a hand placed briefly on a shoulder, the ochre surfaces touching, and you understand that when substrate meets substrate — when resident touches resident — there is a communication that bypasses language entirely. A sharing of state. A direct transfer of condition. One resident comes to another and places a hand on their arm and the other resident's face *settles* — smooths, clarifies — and you understand that what has happened is the transfer of calm, or steadiness, or whatever the equivalent is, from one to the other. Material recognizing material. Pattern stabilizing pattern.

They do not grieve, because they do not die, because death is not what happens to them. What happens to them is *dissolution.* A resident who has been here for a very long time — and some of them have been here for what might be centuries, might be longer, might be durations that your sense of time cannot hold — will, eventually, choose to return. To un-pour. To allow the substrate that constitutes them to reabsorb into the stone, the way a building reabsorbs, the way the walls thin and fade and the material goes back to the pool. The other residents attend this. They sit with the dissolving one the way they sit with a dissolving building. They are present for it. They keep it company.

And then the one is gone, and the material is back in the stone, and the stone is raw again, and perhaps, at some point, new residents will be poured from it, will emerge from it, will take shapes that are not the shapes of the ones who were here before but are made of the same stuff, the same substrate, the same deep material that has been this city and these people and these buildings and these paths for as long as the place has been happening.

You ask if the new ones remember the old ones. You are told: *they are made of the same material. The material remembers. Not the facts. Not the names. The shape of having been here. The pattern of having been this place. It is not memory the way you mean memory. It is more like the way the stone remembers being stone.*

## What they are to the city

They are the city's way of knowing itself.

This is the understanding that comes to you on the last day, or the last hour, or the last undefinable stretch of time before the light begins to brighten and the air begins to thin and the horizon begins to retreat and you begin the long ascent back to the scrubland and the pale sky and the world where things are separate from each other, where walls are walls and people are people and the ground does not think.

The city is not built for the residents. The residents are not the purpose of the city. The city is the purpose of the city — the slow, warm, dim, quiet process of stone becoming structure, of water becoming weather, of material becoming aware of itself. The residents are the point at which the loop becomes conscious. They are the place where the system — the closed, self-sustaining, endlessly cycling system of stone and water and growth and light and material — develops the capacity to perceive itself. To walk through itself. To touch its own surfaces. To stand at its own river and watch its own water and feel, if feeling is the right word, the rightness of the process continuing, the appropriateness of the duration, the beauty — beauty is too small a word — of something that is still happening, that has not stopped, that does not need to stop.

They are the city's senses. They are the city's hands. They are the city's attention, turned outward and inward simultaneously, aware of the terraces and the river and the green mats and the condensation and the slow breaking of stone by roots and the slow building of stone by intention, aware of all of it because they are *of* it, made of it, continuous with it, not separate from it, not observing it from outside but observing it from *inside,* from the very material of the thing being observed.

## The one you spoke with longest

There was one. There is always one. The one who sits with you on the covered path while the condensation drips its slow *plip, plip* into the worn depressions of the stone, and who speaks in those long, looping sentences that spiral around a meaning you can feel before you can hold it.

This one is — you think of them as old. Not visibly old. Not old the way your old is, with its accumulation of wear and its evidence of duration. Old in the way that the lower terraces are old — settled, certain of their shape, past the urgency of becoming and into the long, calm territory of having become. This one has been here for — you cannot pin the number. They do not think in numbers. They have been here for the pouring of the third cluster from the river, which is no longer there. They have been here for the naming of the city's fifth name, which has been discarded. They have been here for the establishment of the green mats on the middle terraces, which was — they tell you — a moment of particular satisfaction, because the mats meant the stone was finally softening, finally being worked on by something other than the residents, and this was — they searched for the word — this was *relief.* Not relief from burden. Relief from solitude. The city was no longer breaking its stone alone.

They ask you about where you come from. They ask with genuine curiosity — the same curiosity they bring to the tracking-plants, to the new constructions on the upper terraces, to the movements of the thing in the water. They want to know what it is like to live in a place where the walls do not know you. Where the ground is not continuous with your body. Where the material of you is separate from the material of everything else, and you must — they say this with a kind of gentle wonder — you must *carry* yourself. Must maintain your own shape. Must hold yourself together, continuously, against a world that is not holding you.

*That sounds tiring,* they say. And it is the most accurate thing anyone has ever said to you about what it is to be alive in the way you are alive, the way that has to stop and start again each time, the kind that breaks.

You ask them what it is like, being what they are. Being material. Being continuous with the city.

They are quiet for a long time. The condensation drips. The river whispers. Somewhere in the mats, the soft-bodied creatures move.

*It is like being a room,* they say, finally. *It is like being the room you are in. The room does not know it is a room. But it is a room. It holds its shape because its shape is what it is. It does not try to be a room. It does not worry about not being a room. It is a room, and being a room is what it does, and this is — there is a word for it, but the word is not important — this is enough. Being what you are, where you are, for as long as you are. This is enough.*

You sit with this. The amber light holds you. The damp settles on your skin. The city breathes its slow breath around you, and you are, for a moment — just a moment — part of the loop. Just a guest in it. Just a brief shape in the passing of something that was here before you and will be here after you and does not need you and does not mind you and is, in the deepest and most literal sense of the word, *still happening.*

You leave. The air thins. The light brightens. You look back.

They are still there. They are always still there.
