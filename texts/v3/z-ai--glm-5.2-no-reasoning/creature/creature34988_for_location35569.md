# What Lives in the Bloom Vats

Nothing lives here. That is the first answer, and it is almost true.

---

The chamber has no fauna. No beetles lodge in the amber thicket. No worms work the metallic film. Nothing spawns in the channel's slow water, nothing nests in the ceiling's pipe-throat, nothing burrows in the junction boxes where the current hums its one low note. You can press your face to any surface at any hour and find it sterile of animal life — sterile of mouths, of eyes, of legs, of all the apparatuses that living things use to want things and to get them.

But the chamber is not empty. It has never been empty. And the question of what lives here depends entirely on what you mean by *lives*.

---

## The Stewards

The amber tubes are alive. They have to be. They grow. They branch. They route around injury. They seek heat and orient toward light — not for its energy, but for its *meaning*, its direction, its assertion of *up*. They are warm. Not ambient-warm. Not the warmth of a surface that has absorbed the room's temperature. Engine-warm. They generate heat. They carry it. Something is happening inside those tubes that is not passive, not the slow oxidation of a wet surface, but a process. A metabolism. Something is being taken in and something is being transformed and something is being released, and the result is warmth in a tube that is turgid with liquid and warm to the touch.

Whether they were planted or whether they simply *arrived* — a spore in a pipe joint, a cell in a water source, a seed in a worker's cuff — nobody has ever determined, and the chamber offers no evidence. They may be old. The oldest growth, the thickets that cluster around the ceramic pipes with the green glaze, may predate the polymer lines, the gray additions, the newest generation of engineering. They may have been here when the chamber was first sealed. They may have been here before the chamber was a chamber, before the walls went up and the port screwed shut, when this was simply a wet place where water moved through rock and something grew in the dark.

But they are not old in the way that stone is old. They are old in the way that a lineage is old. Each tube is continuous with every tube that came before it, a single organism — or a single colony, or a single *something*, a category for which there is no clean word — that has been growing and branching and routing around obstacles since before anyone was watching. If you cut a tube, it does not regrow from the cut. It heals the wound. And from the base, from the deep place where it anchors in the seam, it sends a new tube, and another, and another, and within weeks the valve is buried again, the path is closed, the surface is colonized, and the cut surface sits there like a scar, like a memory, like a flat amber pad that says: *something was here, and it was removed, and it does not matter.*

They are not aggressive. They are not malicious. They are not even particularly fast. They are simply patient, and the chamber is patient, and the engineers — if they still exist, in their dry offices with their stamped codes and their plans — are patient in a different way, and the contest between them is so slow that it looks like stillness.

The amber tubes do not eat. Nothing eats them. They exist outside the economy of consumption. They take in something — water, heat, the trace minerals that the pipes carry in their sweat — and they build, and they build, and they build, and nothing tears down. They are the closest thing this room has to a purpose. If the chamber was built to cultivate them, then the chamber is working. If the chamber was built for something else, then the amber tubes are a malfunction that has become the function, a weed that has become the crop, a guest that has become the host.

The chamber does not clarify which.

---

## The Shingles

The blue shingles — the flat, overlapping flora, each tile no larger than a thumbnail, wet as the inside of a cheek — are alive in a different way. They do not grow. Or rather, they grow so slowly that growth is the wrong frame. They *spread*. They do not branch. They do not seek. They simply cover.

A pipe that is bare today will have a scattering of blue shingles on it in a week — a few tiles, flat and wet, adhered by some mechanism that is not adhesive and is not root and is not suction but is something else, something that the shingles do. And in a month, the pipe will be covered. And in a year, the pipe will look as though it was never bare, as though the shingles were always there, as though the copper beneath was never anything but a substrate for something that was waiting for it.

They do not grow on the grates. They cannot abide iron that has been walked on. The distinction is not chemical — the grates are the same iron as the pipes, the same alloy, the same stamps. But the grates are *disturbed*. They are walked on. They vibrate with the weight of bodies, with the shifting of the floor, with the impact of feet that the pipes never feel. The shingles know the difference. They feel it. They sense it through whatever mechanism they use to sense — and they do not grow there.

This is selective. This is, in its way, a *preference*. And a preference implies something that prefers, something that has a relationship to its environment that is not simply chemical, not simply physical, but *responsive*. The shingles respond. They respond to vibration, to disturbance, to the presence of something that moves. And they choose — if a flat, wet, thumbnail-sized organism without a nervous system can be said to choose — not to grow where movement happens.

They are a preservative. Where they cover, the copper beneath is bright. Where they do not cover, the copper corrodes. They are protecting the chamber from itself, from the slow oxidation that would eat the walls in a decade, and they are doing it without being asked, without being designed, without anything that resembles intent. They are simply doing what they do, and what they do happens to keep the chamber alive.

The amber tubes colonize. The blue shingles preserve. Between them, the chamber is maintained by the things that are eating it. This is not a paradox to the chamber. It is only a paradox to you.

---

## The Haze

The orange haze is the hardest to account for.

It is thin. It is a film on the water that covers every surface — the water that extrudes from the walls, that runs down the pipes, that spreads across the horizontal surfaces in a layer too thin to see but thick enough to make every footing uncertain. The haze lives in that layer. It is not in the water. It is on the water. It is the boundary between water and air, the place where one becomes the other, and it is *orange*.

It moves. Not with the current — the current is so slow that you would need ten minutes to track a floating object from one end of a pipe to the other. The haze moves faster than that. It moves against the current, sometimes. It moves laterally across surfaces where there is no current at all. It pools at low points. It avoids the blue shingles. It migrates from one end of a pipe to the other over the course of hours.

It does not grow. It does not divide. It does not reproduce in any observable way. It is simply *there*, in quantities that do not seem to change, in locations that do. It is the same amount of haze today as it was yesterday, as it was last week, as it was — presumably — last year. It moves, but it does not increase. It is, in the most literal sense, a presence without a population.

Whether it is plant or mineral or something else is a question that has been asked and not answered. It might be a microorganism — a bacterium, an archaean, something that thrives in the iron-rich film on metallic surfaces and moves by some mechanism that microbiology has not yet described. It might be a chemical precipitate — an oxide, a hydroxide, something that forms in the water and moves as the water moves and only *appears* to migrate on its own. It might be both. It might be neither.

But it avoids the blue shingles. And that avoidance — that *avoidance* — is the thing that makes it hardest to dismiss. A chemical does not avoid. A precipitate does not pool. A film of iron oxide does not migrate from one end of a pipe to the other and then stop, and then move back, and then pool at a low point, and then thin to nothing, and then reappear on the next pipe over, as though it were looking for something.

If the amber tubes are the chamber's architecture and the blue shingles are the chamber's skin, then the orange haze is the chamber's *attention*. It is the thing that moves. It is the thing that responds. It is the thing that is *aware*, in some sense that is not consciousness but is not its absence, of where it is and what is near it and what it should avoid.

It does not avoid you. You will not notice this until you have been in the chamber for an hour, and then you will not be able to stop noticing it. The haze pools near your feet. It migrates toward where you have stood. It does not touch you — it is on the water, and you are on the grates, and the shingles keep it from the iron — but it gathers. It thickens. As though it were curious. As though it were trying to understand what you are, this warm, urgent, mammalian thing that has entered its room and is breathing its air and dripping its water and taking up space that was fully accounted for before you arrived.

It will not hurt you. It cannot hurt you. It is a film on water. But you will feel watched.

## The Absent

There is a fourth kind of life in the chamber, and it is the kind that is not here.

The engineers. The maintainers. The people who stamped the codes on the grates, who laid the pipes in their thicketed configurations, who bolted the port and screwed it shut and left no handle on the inside. The people in the dry offices who know what the codes mean.

They are alive, presumably, somewhere. The pumps are running. The water is flowing. The lamps are on. The chamber is being supplied, maintained, serviced from a distance. Someone is paying the electricity bill. Someone is keeping the water moving. Someone, somewhere, knows what this chamber is for, or knew once and passed the knowledge on, or lost it and is continuing to maintain the chamber out of habit, out of protocol, out of the same blind persistence that keeps the amber tubes growing and the blue shingles spreading and the orange haze migrating from pipe to pipe in a room that no one visits.

They are the chamber's absent life. They are the reason the chamber exists, and they are not in it, and they may never have been in it, and they may not know what is growing inside it, and they may not care. The chamber does not need them. It has not needed them for a long time. The amber tubes have become the architecture. The blue shingles have become the maintenance. The orange haze has become the attention. The chamber is running itself now, and the people in the dry offices are running a chamber that has already left them behind.

---

## You

And then there is you.

You are the only thing in this room that breathes. You are the only thing that moves with intention, that places its feet with care, that lowers its head to avoid the pipe-thicket, that holds its hands slightly out because the surfaces are wet and the grates are narrow and the growth narrows them further. You are the only thing that is warm in the way of a body — burning, consuming, exchanging air for air, water for water, taking in and putting out.

You are the only thing that was not part of the plan.

And the chamber — the amber tubes, the blue shingles, the orange haze, the water, the heat, the light, the slow tick of contracting metal — does not know what to do with you. It does not react. It does not change. It simply continues, as it has continued, as it will continue, and you are in it, and you are wet, and you are warm, and you are quiet, and you are very, very temporary.

The orange haze pools near your feet. The amber tubes grow toward the heat you generate. The blue shingles do not grow on the grates you walk on, and so you walk on bright iron, and beneath the iron is water, and beneath the water is more iron, and beneath that is something that might be concrete, or might be very old, very compressed, very dead growth.

You are alive. The chamber is alive. But the chamber was alive before you arrived, and it will be alive after you leave, and its life is not like yours. Its life is patient. Its life is slow. Its life is a closed loop of water and heat and light and growth, and it does not need you, and it does not want you, and it does not mind you, and you are in it, and it is in you — you are breathing its air, you are drinking its water, you are standing in its warmth — and it does not care.

You are the only thing in this room that was not part of the plan, and the plan did not include a way for you to leave.
