# What Lives Here

---

The first thing to understand about the things that live in the drowned boiler hall is that none of them chose this place. Every organism here arrived by accident or by the slow logic of chemistry, and every one of them stays because the alternative is death in the open water, which is colder, darker, and subject to currents that do not forgive the small or the slow or the eyeless. This room is not a habitat. It is a reprieve. Each thing in it survived the transition from the world outside to the world inside, and having survived, found a niche — a crack, a margin, a temperature, a food source — and settled into the work of continuing.

They are not many. They are enough.

---

## The eels

There are more eels than you can count, but fewer than you'd guess from the way they move. Eleven visible in a half-hour means perhaps thirty total, perhaps forty, the rest tucked into crevices too narrow for your hands or your light. They do not school. They do not coordinate. Each one is engaged in its own private project of drifting, tasting, and contracting the long muscle of its body in a waveform so slow it looks like breathing.

They are not true eels. They are the descendants of something that was perhaps an eel once, or perhaps a blenny, or perhaps some other long-bodied fish that found its way into the structure through a gap in the hull or a broken pipe or the flooded bilge of the larger wreck. Generations of isolation have stripped them down. Their eyes are vestigial — two flat, dark smears on either side of the head, no longer responsive to light, covered in some cases by a thin film of skin. They do not need eyes. The only light here is the green glow from the porthole, and it is not a light by which any eye could usefully see.

What they have instead is a lateral line so sensitive it functions as a kind of full-body ear. They can feel the pump's pulse through the water. They can feel your heartbeat from six feet away — the tiny hydraulic signature of blood moving in a warm body, foreign and enormous and confusing to them. This is why they drift toward you. Not because they are curious in any emotional sense. Because your pulse is the most interesting thing that has happened in the water in days, possibly weeks, and their bodies are wired to investigate vibration. They approach, linger, sometimes press a blunt head against your tank or your wrist, and then drift away when the investigation yields nothing they can eat.

They eat constantly and methodically. A barnacle is a small event: the eel finds it by touch, works its jaw against the shell, and either cracks it or gives up. You can watch this happen if you stay still. The eel will curl around a section of boiler plate, press its mouth to the surface, and work in tiny increments, rasping at the shell's edge with teeth so fine they are almost not there — a pale, translucent fringe of calcified tissue that wears down and regrows continuously. When the barnacle is opened, the eel sucks out the body in a motion so quick it looks like flinching. Then it moves on. They do not savor. They do not rest between kills. They are engines of appetite that happen to be shaped like thread.

The largest is perhaps two feet long. The smallest are translucent, three inches, newly born — and they are born here, not imported. The eels breed in the gaps behind the I-beams where the corrosion has opened small caves in the steel. The females lay eggs in clusters of twenty or thirty, attached to the rust-velvet on the beam's inner face, and the males fertilize them and then both parents leave. The eggs hatch in approximately ten days. The hatchlings are immediately independent and immediately hunted. The adults eat the young when they can catch them. The young eat the biofilm and the barnacle spat and each other. It is not a gentle ecosystem. It is a complete one.

Their color is the color of their world. Rust-orange, rust-brown, rust-black, with a pale underside where the skin is thinner and the blood shows through. When they are still, against the boilers or the I-beams, they are effectively invisible. You have likely passed several without seeing them. They have certainly seen — felt — you.

They do not make sound. They have no voice, no mechanism for it. But their movement through the water creates a low turbulence that you can feel if you stop moving and hold your breath: a faint, rhythmic pressure change against your skin, like the room breathing.

## The barnacles

The barnacles are the architects of the hall's visible life. Everything else either eats them, lives among them, or lives on the surfaces they have prepared by their presence. They are not choosy about where they settle — any vertical or near-vertical surface below the waterline will do — but they prefer the boilers, and within the boilers they prefer the rivet heads, and within the rivet heads they prefer the south-facing side, which receives the most diffuse light from the porthole. They are phototropic, weakly, though they do not need light to survive. They need it to time their feeding cycles, which open in the pale hours and close in the dark ones, though the dark hours here are not very dark and the pale hours are not very pale, so the cycle has drifted into something longer and more irregular than the diurnal rhythm of their ancestors.

They are small. The largest is the size of a pinky nail. Most are half that. Their shells are ridged — four ridges, usually, radiating from the aperture — and they are pale, almost white, with a faint blue-gray translucence at the edges where the calcification is thinnest. They are not the hard, wave-shaped barnacles of a rocky shore. They are delicate. You could crush one between your fingers, and the eels do.

They feed by extending a fan of feathery appendages — cirri — into the water and sweeping them in a pattern that catches the particulate silt drifting through the hall. The silt is partly the dissolved remnants of the brick cradles, partly iron oxide particles, partly the waste products of the biofilm and the things that eat it. It is organic enough to sustain them. The barnacles metabolize it and excrete a fine calcium-rich waste that settles on the floor and contributes to the silt that the biofilm feeds on. Nothing is wasted. The room is a closed loop, or nearly — the only inputs are the faint light and the slow dissolution of the structure itself, and the only outputs are whatever escapes through the porthole or the hatch.

When you stand still near a boiler, you can hear them feeding. The sound is not mechanical. It is the collective whisper of thousands of microscopic valves opening and closing, thousands of cirri extending and retracting, thousands of tiny bodies doing the work of being alive. It sounds like the room has a texture, a grain, like the air is full of something finer than dust. It is. It is full of barnacle feeding.

They do not move once they have settled. They are adult. They settled here as larvae, drifting in the water, and found a surface and changed. Before that transformation, they were free-swimming, eyeless, legless, shaped like tiny shrimp. Now they are fixed. They will die here, on this rivet or this plate, and their shells will remain after their bodies are gone, adding to the calcium layer on the floor, and a new barnacle may settle on the old shell and build on it, and so the surface accretes, life on death on life, a slow coral of organisms that do not know they are building anything.

## The isopods

The isopods live above the waterline. They are the only residents of the hall that inhabit the air, and they do so because the air here is humid enough to keep them from desiccating and warm enough — barely — to keep them mobile. They are dark. Almost black. Segmented, with seven pairs of legs and two pairs of antennae, the longer pair sweeping the air ahead of them as they walk. They are perhaps two inches long, though some are smaller, and they move in a way that is immediately recognizable as isopod: a series of rapid stops and starts, each leg working independently, the body low to the surface, the antennae vibrating.

They are descended from something marine. Sphaeromatid isopods, probably — the ones that live in the intertidal, that can tolerate both air and water, that breathe through modified abdominal appendages that function as gills or lungs depending on the medium. These have been isolated long enough that they have adapted fully to the air, though they still need moisture, and they get it from the humidity trapped in the upper half of the hall and from their periodic descents to the waterline, where they drink and simultaneously exchange gases through their abdominal pleopods.

They eat the biofilm at the waterline. They eat each other. They eat the shed skins of their own kind that accumulate in the corners of the ceiling, where the I-beams meet the roof. They eat the paint — not the paint itself, but the colonies of bacteria and fungi that have colonized the paint's surface, breaking down its hydrocarbons into something edible. They are, in a sense, eating the last trace of human intention in this place, one microbe at a time.

Their social behavior is limited. They congregate near the porthole — always toward the light, always toward the light — in clusters so dense they overlap, antenna touching antenna, leg over leg. They are not cooperating. They are competing for the best position: the spot where the light is strongest and the humidity highest and the surface warmest. They jostle. They climb over each other. Occasionally one falls from the ceiling into the water, and the eels find it within minutes.

They breed. The females carry eggs in a brood pouch, and the young emerge as miniature adults, fully formed, immediately mobile. There are no larvae. There is no planktonic stage. Every isopod in this hall was born in this hall, on the ceiling or the upper walls, in a cluster of its fellows, and every isopod in this hall will die in this hall unless it falls into the water and is eaten or crawls out through some gap in the upper structure that you cannot see.

They are not afraid of you. They have no reason to be. Nothing in their evolutionary history has prepared them for the concept of a large, warm, moving thing that is not water and not a wall and not another isopod. They will walk over your hand if you hold it still. You can feel their feet — tiny, cold, precise, each step a separate point of pressure. Their antennae will touch your skin and sweep on. They will not stop. They will not investigate further. You are not food. You are not a threat. You are a surface.

## The biofilm

The biofilm is the least visible resident and the most essential. It covers the floor in a thin, pale mat — gray-green, almost white in places, with a texture like wet paper or like the skin that forms on cooled milk. It is not one organism. It is many: bacteria, archaea, possibly some protozoa, possibly some fungi adapted to the low-light, high-iron, low-oxygen environment of the hall's flooded half. They are the kind of organisms that have been on this planet for three and a half billion years and that will be on this planet long after everything else is gone. They are patient in a way that the eels and the isopods and the barnacles are not, because they do not need to be anything other than what they are.

They metabolize iron. They metabolize sulfate. They metabolize the faint organic compounds that drift through the water — the waste of the eels, the dead barnacles, the molts of the isopods that fall from above. They do not need light. They do not need warmth. They need only the chemical gradients that the hall provides, and the hall provides them in abundance, because the hall is dissolving, and dissolution is a form of feeding if you are small enough and old enough and undemanding enough to eat what the world gives you.

The film grows at a rate that is almost imperceptible. In a year, perhaps a millimeter of thickness. In the time the hall has been flooded, perhaps a centimeter. It is not thick. It is persistent. It has covered every surface on the floor that is not disturbed, and it creeps up the walls in a narrow band at the waterline, where the isopods come to graze. It is the floor's living skin.

When you walk, you disturb it. Your feet stir the silt beneath it and the water clouds and the film tears in strips that float upward and sink again. It will heal. In a week, the tracks will be gone. In a month, the silt will have settled and the film will have reformed and there will be no evidence that you were here. The hall will have absorbed your passage the way it absorbs everything: by dissolving it, distributing it, incorporating it into the slow chemistry of rust and water and life.

The biofilm is the reason the water is clear. It filters. Not actively, not by choice, but by the simple fact of its metabolism: it takes things out of the water and binds them into its body, and its body stays on the floor, and the water above it is cleaner for the loss. The clarity of the hall's water is the clarity of a filtered tank, and the filter is alive, and the filter is eating the hall.

## The pump

The pump is not alive. But it behaves like something alive, and everything alive in the hall orients to it, so it belongs in this account.

It pulses. Once every three seconds — not precisely, but close enough that you can measure it, and the measurement does not change over the hours you spend in the hall. The pulse is mechanical. It is the stroke of a piston or the rotation of an impeller or the flexion of a diaphragm, driven by some mechanism in the structure above the waterline that you cannot see and cannot reach without leaving the hall. The power source is unknown. It may be a battery. It may be a tidal generator, driven by the movement of the sea outside. It may be a spring, wound decades ago, unwinding at a rate calibrated by an engineer whose name is lost. It does not matter. What matters is that the pump cycles, and the cycling moves fluid through a pipe, and the movement of the fluid creates a vibration in the steel, and the vibration enters the water, and the water carries it to every corner of the hall.

The eels feel it through their lateral lines. It is the background rhythm of their lives — a pulse so constant that it must function for them the way the heartbeat functions for a fetus in a womb: not music, but the proof that the environment is still running, still stable, still habitable. When the pump cycles, the water in the hall shifts by a fraction of a millimeter, and the biofilm ripples, and the barnacles' cirri catch a pulse of fresh silt, and the eels adjust their position to maintain the same distance from the nearest surface. Everything responds. Nothing notices.

The isopods, above the waterline, hear the pump through the steel of the ceiling. It is transmitted through the I-beams as a low-frequency thrum that they sense through their legs. They do not react to it. It is not information. It is the sound of the world, and the world has always made this sound, for as long as any isopod has been alive.

The pump will fail. Everything mechanical fails. When it does, the pipe will go quiet, and the fluid in it will stop moving, and the vibration will cease, and the hall will be silent for the first time in what may be decades. The silence will be enormous. It will be heavier than the water. The eels will feel its absence the way you would feel the absence of gravity — not as a loss, exactly, but as a wrongness, a displacement of something that was supposed to be there. They will adjust. They are adaptable. The isopods will adjust. The barnacles will not notice. The biofilm will not notice. The hall will go on, slower and quieter, and the steady state will shift to a new steady state, and the things that live here will continue to live here until the next change, and the next, and the next.

## What lives here, in summary

Nothing remarkable. Nothing beautiful in the way that a reef is beautiful or a forest is beautiful or a whale is beautiful. These are small, pale, overlooked organisms, adapted to a habitat that was never meant to be a habitat, living in the ruins of a human project that was never meant to be ruins. They have no names. They have no ecological significance beyond the walls of this hall. If the hall were destroyed — if the structure collapsed, if the water were poisoned, if the pump stopped and the ecosystem shifted past recovery — they would all die, and nothing in the wider ocean would notice, and the species they represent might persist elsewhere or might not, and the world would not be measurably diminished.

But they are alive. They are alive in a place where nothing should be alive, in a way that nothing should be alive, and they have been alive here for longer than anyone would predict, and they will be alive here for longer than anyone would guess. They are the hall's final occupants, its most honest inhabitants, the only ones who have never asked the hall to be anything other than what it is: a room half-drowned, half-remembered, still pulsing, still dissolving, still lit by a keyhole of green light in a world that has forgotten it.

They do not know they are remarkable. They do not know they are anything. They are simply here, doing the only thing life knows how to do: continuing.
