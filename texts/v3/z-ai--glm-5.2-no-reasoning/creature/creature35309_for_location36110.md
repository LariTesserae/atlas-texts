# What Lives Here

## The Singers

Begin with the domes, because the domes are where the place begins. They are not the oldest things here — the mineral substrate is older, the water is older in a sense that depends on how you define age — but the domes are the oldest *living* things, and they are the source of the sound, and the sound is what makes the place itself, so the domes come first.

Each dome is a single organism. This is important to establish because the initial impression is otherwise. The first reading is that the flats are covered in a continuous living mat, a moss-equivalent, a lichen-analog, a single biological surface that happens to be shaped into mounds. This is not the case. Each dome is an individual, discrete, separated from its neighbors by a meter or more of bare mineral substrate, and while they communicate acoustically — their chords responding to and adjusting to the chords of neighboring domes in a slow, constant negotiation that plays out across timescales too long for a human walk to perceive — each one is making its own sound. Its own decision, if that word applies. Each dome is a throat, and each throat is singing a note it chose, or was chosen for, or was shaped into by the chemistry of where it sits, and the sum of those notes is the sound of the place, but the sum is not the intention. The intention, if there is one, is singular. The dome sings for itself.

The body of the dome is layered. This is visible only where one has been damaged — and damage is rare, because there is no wind to drive abrasion and no freezing to crack stone, because the climate here is so stable it has ceased to be weather in any meaningful sense. But in one or two places, a dome has been split, perhaps by the weight of a crawler settling too heavily on a weakened edge, and the interior is exposed. It is concentric, like a cross-section of a tree, but the layers are not annual, because there are no years. They are growth layers — each one representing an expansion of the organism's body, a new ring of filament-producing tissue laid down over the old, the oldest material at the center compressed to a dense, dark core, the newest at the surface where the cilia wave in the grey air. The material is not mineral, though it has a mineral quality — it has the look of dark chert, or of compacted keratin, and it rings when struck. It is biological. It is produced by the organism from the dissolved compounds in the water that seeps around its base. It is, if you must have a word, a shell. An exoskeleton. But it is not shed. It is added to. The domes grow the way coral grows, accreting themselves from the outside in, and the oldest material at the center is the densest and the hardest and the most resonant, which means that as a dome ages, its note gets lower, and its shell gets harder, and the sound it produces gets more complex, because the new outer layers vibrate at their own frequency while the old inner core maintains its bass undertone, and the dome becomes a chord rather than a note, a harmony of its own history.

The filaments — the cilia — are the instruments. Each one is a single fiber, tapering, dark, roughly the length of a human eyelash but far more numerous. A dome the size of a spread hand has perhaps ten thousand of them. They are not hair, though they look like hair. They are not setae, though they function like setae. They are specialized structures, grown from pores in the shell, each one connected to a fine fluid channel that runs into the body of the organism, and each one is capable of independent motion — slow, deliberate swaying, or rapid, fine shivering — which produces sound by displacing air at a specific frequency. The filaments are tuned by their length and their thickness and the tension the organism applies through the fluid channels. A dome can adjust its own chord. It does so constantly, in small ways, in response to the vibrations of its neighbors — a slow, continuous tuning that keeps the overall sound of the flats in a state of dynamic equilibrium, each dome adjusting to fill gaps in the frequency spectrum, each one pulling slightly away from notes that are already well-covered by nearby domes. The result is a sound that is not random but is not composed. It is emergent. It is the sound of ten thousand independent musicians who have never met each other but who have been listening to each other for so long that they have, without intention or agreement, found a chord together.

The domes do not reproduce in any way that a walk across the flats will reveal. There are no young domes. There are no half-grown domes. Every dome you encounter is mature, fully formed, its filament coat complete and its shell thick and its note established. This does not mean they do not reproduce. It means that if they do, it happens rarely, or it happens somewhere you have not walked, or it happens on a timescale that makes the word *reproduction* feel like the wrong word entirely — too fast, too individual, too animal. Perhaps the domes are clonal. Perhaps each one is a fragment of a single organism that has been slowly separating for a million years, each fragment developing its own note as it drifts acoustically from its origin. Perhaps the dense clusters — the ones on the dark volcanic stone, the ones that produce the massive bass tones — are the parent colonies, and the scattered domes on the pale chalk are the offspring, acoustic pioneers spreading out across the flats, each one finding a frequency of its own. Or perhaps the domes are not organisms at all in the sense that requires reproduction. Perhaps they are a process. A mineral-biological crystallization that occurs wherever the water chemistry and the substrate and the acoustic environment are correct, the way a crystal forms in a supersaturated solution, not because it was born but because the conditions for its formation were met. In this reading, the domes did not come from anywhere. They emerged. They are the sound the flats make when the flats are left alone long enough.

This is the thing about the domes that is hardest to hold: they may not be alive. They may be a phenomenon. They may be the place itself, expressed at a specific scale, the way a mineral expresses its chemistry by forming a crystal. The filaments move. The notes adjust. The organism — if it is an organism — responds to its neighbors. But a crystal adjusts to its neighbors too, growing into available space, its lattice orienting to the existing matrix. The domes behave, but behavior is not proof of life, and in a place this old, where the distinction between the biological and the geological has been dissolved the way everything here has been dissolved, the question may not have an answer. The domes sing. Whether they sing because they are alive or because the place is alive and they are its voice is a question the place does not care about.

## The Crawlers

The crawlers are unambiguously alive. This is the first thing that becomes clear when you spend time watching them, because the domes have introduced doubt, and the doubt makes the certainty of the crawlers feel like relief.

There is no central body plan. This is the second thing. A crawler is not a creature with a head and a tail and a front and a back. It is an oval, flat, and when you see it from above — which is how you always see it, because the crawlers never rise above the waterline and never leave the flats — it appears symmetrical along its long axis, a shape that has no obvious direction of travel until it moves, and then it moves in the direction it moves, and the shape that seemed symmetrical turns out to be slightly longer at the leading edge, the legs slightly more numerous there, the dark branching pattern slightly more concentrated. When it changes direction, the leading edge shifts. The body reorganizes. The legs that were numerous become fewer, and the legs that were fewer become numerous, and the crawler moves the other way. It does not turn. It re-fronts. The direction of travel is a decision the body makes, and the body can make it at any point, and the shape that seemed so simple is actually a fluid distribution of resources — legs, sensory surface, pigment — along an axis that has no preferred orientation.

The legs are the third thing. They are too small to see. You know they are there because the crawler moves, and the movement is smooth and steady and not consistent with rolling or sliding or jetting. It is walking. But the legs that produce the walk are below the resolution of human vision in the grey half-light. They are numerous — dozens, perhaps hundreds — and they are the same pale color as the mineral substrate, and they are moving in a coordinated pattern that is invisible because each individual leg is invisible. You can feel them, though. If you stand very still in the shallow water and a crawler passes near your ankle, you feel them — a fine, tickling vibration against your skin, a sensation like static electricity, like being brushed by something too small to see. The legs are not gripping the substrate. They are feeling it. The crawler is reading the surface as it moves, the way a blind person reads with their fingertips, gathering information about mineral composition, moisture, the presence or absence of dome-excreted compounds in the water. The crawler navigates by taste and by sound and by the chemical gradient of the water, and its legs are its tongue and its ears and its nose, and its body is its brain, distributed across the entire oval, every square centimeter of surface equally capable of processing the information the legs collect.

The dark branching pattern on the crawler's back is the fourth thing, and it is the thing that makes you understand that the crawlers and the domes are not separate. The pattern is iron. Not a mimicking of the iron stains on the mineral substrate, not an evolutionary convergence toward a visual camouflage, but the same compound, deposited by the same process, in the same dendritic pattern that water follows when it carries dissolved iron across a flat surface. The crawler's body is absorbing iron from the water — from the same water that stains the mineral flats, from the same water that the domes draw their shell-compounds from — and the iron is being deposited in the crawler's dorsal tissue in patterns that follow the same physical laws that govern the stains on the rock. The crawler is not trying to look like the rock. The crawler and the rock are both expressions of the same chemistry, and they look alike because they are made by the same process, and the process is the water, and the water is the place.

The crawlers feed on the domes. This is visible if you watch long enough. A crawler climbs onto a dome — a slow, deliberate process, the invisible legs finding purchase on the shell surface, the body settling onto the filaments — and the dome's note changes. The change is not random. The dome's frequency drops, or it becomes more complex, or it shifts in a way that suggests the crawler is altering the tension of the filaments, pressing them, dampening some and freeing others. The crawler stays on the dome for a long time — an hour, more — and when it leaves, the dome's note returns to its previous state, but not immediately. It adjusts back slowly, over the course of minutes, as though recovering from something. The crawler, during its stay, is feeding. What it takes is not visible. It is not eating the filaments — they are intact when it leaves. It is not eating the shell. It is absorbing something through its ventral surface, something the dome produces, something that is present in higher concentration on the dome's surface than in the surrounding water. A compound. A metabolite. A byproduct of the dome's acoustic process, perhaps — the way a hive produces heat as a byproduct of bees, the way a fire produces ash as a byproduct of burning. The dome sings, and the singing produces a substance, and the crawler eats the substance, and the substance is what the crawler needs to live.

There are not many crawlers. Across a hundred meters of flats, there might be ten. They are not dense. They do not need to be dense. Each one is efficient, systematic, moving from dome to dome in a nearest-neighbor traversal that covers ground steadily and without repetition, and each one seems to know which domes it has visited recently and which ones are ready to be visited again. The clicking — the subsonic pulse that two crawlers produce when they meet on the same dome — is a negotiation. Not a conflict. An exchange of information about which domes are depleted and which are producing, and one crawler leaves because the information has been shared and there is no reason for both of them to be at the same dome. They are not territorial. They are cooperative. They are managing the flats, in a way — not consciously, not with intention, but with the same kind of emergent, distributed optimization that a slime mold uses to find the shortest path through a maze. The crawlers are the flats' digestive system, and the domes are the flats' voice, and together they are a single organism that is also a landscape, and the distinction has been dissolved.

The crawlers do not avoid the water channels — the sudden deep places, the waist-deep drops in an otherwise ankle-deep flat. They enter them and continue along the bottom, their legs still finding purchase on the submerged mineral surface, their bodies unaffected by the change in depth. They can go where the domes cannot, because the domes are fixed, and the crawlers are the mobile component of the system, the part that moves, the part that connects the fixed singers to each other through their own bodies, carrying chemical information from one cluster of domes to another in the same way that insects carry pollen from one flower to another, and the flats are pollinated by the crawlers, and the crawlers are fed by the flats, and the system is closed and stable and has been running for longer than anything alive can remember, because nothing alive here remembers anything.

## The Haze-Dwellers

The things in the air are the hardest to describe because they are the hardest to see.

They are pale. They are translucent. They are roughly the size of a human hand, though their edges are so poorly defined that *roughly* is doing significant work in that sentence. They drift in the slow currents of the saturated atmosphere, and they are made, as far as you can tell, of the same substance as the haze itself — a suspension of fine mineral particles in water vapor, a colloid, a thing that is neither solid nor gas but something between. When one settles on a dome, it flattens, and it becomes nearly invisible, a slight thickening of the moisture on the dome's surface, and the dome's note does not change, or changes so slightly that the change is within the constant micro-adjustment of the dome's own tuning and cannot be distinguished from it. When one settles on the water, it becomes a faint opacity, a pale spot in the tea-colored surface, and then it dissolves, or appears to dissolve, its particles dispersing into the water, and it is gone. Then, some time later, it reassembles, or a new one assembles, from the haze, and the cycle continues.

Whether they are alive is a question that has two answers, and both answers are correct, and neither answer matters.

If they are alive, they are alive in the way a cloud is alive — transient, responsive, shaped by their environment, capable of existing and ceasing to exist and existing again without the continuity that a body provides. They are not organisms. They are events. They are the thing that happens when the mineral content of the air reaches a certain concentration and the humidity reaches a certain level and the acoustic vibration of the domes provides a specific frequency that nucleates the particles into a coherent shape. They form because the conditions for their formation are met, and they dissipate when the conditions change, and they form again when the conditions return. They are not born. They are not dying. They are precipitating.

If they are not alive, they are not alive in the way that a crystal is not alive — which is to say, they exhibit behavior so complex and so responsive that the word *not alive* becomes a technical distinction rather than a meaningful one. They approach domes. They adjust their drift. They settle on specific domes and not on others, and the domes they settle on are the ones that are producing the highest-frequency notes, the keenest sounds, and the settling is not random. It is directional. It is intentional, if intention can be applied to a thing that is a mineral colloid. They move toward the highest frequencies the way a plant moves toward light, not because it wants to but because the frequency creates a gradient — acoustic, thermodynamic, chemical — and the gradient organizes the particles, and the organization is indistinguishable from behavior, and behavior is indistinguishable from life, and the haze-dwellers live at the exact point where these words stop meaning different things.

They do not interact with the crawlers. The crawlers do not interact with them. They occupy different media — water and air — and they are, perhaps, different expressions of the same underlying process, the way the domes and the mineral stains on the substrate are different expressions of the same chemistry. The haze-dwellers are the air's version of the crawlers. The crawlers are the water's version of the haze-dwellers. The domes are the solid's version of both. And the place is the thing that contains all three and does not distinguish between them.

## The Absent

There were builders. There are no builders.

The structures are the evidence, and the evidence is complete, and the evidence tells you nothing except that hands made these things and the hands are gone.

The hands were not large. The finger marks in the clay are narrow — narrower than a human hand, narrower than a child's hand. Three fingers and a thumb, or four fingers and no thumb, the impression is ambiguous because the clay has been worked and reworked and the original marks have been smoothed and revised. The hands that made these structures were skilled. Not the skill of an engineer — there is no engineering here, no load-bearing calculation, no structural innovation. The skill of a potter. A maker. Someone who understood material by working it, who knew how wet clay behaves when it is pressed against itself, who knew how long to let it dry before shaping it further, who knew that the chalk-rich clay from the pale substrate would hold its shape better than the iron-rich clay from the dark patches, and who used this knowledge not to build shelters or walls or monuments but to build acoustic spaces.

The builders were the audience. This is the thing that the structures make clear, the thing that the structures *are*: they are the physical record of a species that heard the domes and wanted to hear them better. The curved walls are ears. The openings are ear canals. The shelves and ridges and carefully placed lumps of clay are the components of a hearing aid, a device for focusing and refining and concentrating sound, and the builders made these devices because the sound of the domes was important to them — so important that they settled on a flat at the end of the world where nothing grew and nothing changed and the only resource was clay and water and the constant, deafening song, and they built their listening rooms, and they listened.

What they heard is not recoverable. The sound has not changed. The domes sing the same chords now that they sang when the builders were here, because the domes do not change — they accrete, they adjust, but they do not change in the way that would make the song different from one century to the next. So you are hearing what the builders heard. The same frequencies, the same harmonics, the same bass tones from the dark-stone clusters, the same keening high notes from the small domes on the pale chalk. You are hearing it through the same structures, standing in the same listening rooms, your skeleton vibrating in the same way that their skeletons vibrated. The experience is available to you. The *meaning* of the experience is not.

Because the builders are gone. Not dead — gone. There are no bones. There are no remains. There is nothing in the structures or on the flats that suggests the builders died here or were buried here or left any trace of their bodies behind. They were here, and then they were not, and the not-ness is so complete that it feels deliberate, a departure so thorough it was an erasure, and the only things they left behind were the listening rooms, which they could not take with them, and which were made from the place itself, clay from the substrate, water from the flats, shaped by hands that are no longer anywhere.

Where did they go? The question is wrong. The question assumes a somewhere to go to, and the flats extend in every direction, and the flats are the same everywhere, and there is no edge to walk to and no beyond to walk into. If the builders left, they left the flats entirely, which means they were capable of leaving the flats entirely, which means they came from somewhere else, which means the flats were not their home but a destination — a pilgrimage site, perhaps, a place they traveled to in order to hear the sound and to build rooms that would help them hear it better, and when the pilgrimage was over, or when the reason for the pilgrimage ended, they went back to wherever they came from, and they did not come back, and the listening rooms have been empty ever since.

Or they did not leave. Or they left by not being, which is different from leaving, and the structures are not ruins but shells, the way the domes are shells, the outer expression of something that was once alive inside and is now not, and the structures persist because the wind does not erode and the water does not wear and the clay holds its shape because there is nothing to make it lose it, and the finger marks remain because there is nothing to smooth them, and the listening rooms stand open and empty because the builders stood in them and listened and then the builders stopped, and the stopping was so gentle and so total that the rooms did not notice.

The domes did not notice either. The domes never noticed. The domes have been singing since before the builders came and they are singing now and between those two facts there is no gap, no change, no acknowledgment that something was here and is not. The domes are the most indifferent thing in a place defined by indifference, and their indifference is not cold, it is not cruel, it is not the indifference of a god or a machine. It is the indifference of a process. The way water is indifferent to the shape of the channel it flows through. The water does not care about the channel. The water flows. The domes do not care about the listeners. The domes sing.

## What Remains

What lives here is the sound, and the sound is not alive, and the question was never the right one.

The domes persist. They accrete their shells, layer over layer, and their notes drop, and their chords grow more complex, and the oldest among them — the ones at the center of the dense clusters, the ones on the dark volcanic stone — are so old that their shells are thick as a hand is wide and their notes are so low they are felt only in the bones and the teeth and the fluid of the eyes, and they have been singing for longer than the word *longer* can meaningfully describe. They are not the first things to live here. They may not be the last. But they are the things that live here now, and they are singing, and the crawlers move between them, and the haze-dwellers drift above them, and the water carries the dissolved minerals that feed them, and the air carries the sound that they make, and the listening rooms stand open and empty and the finger marks in the clay are still visible and the sound that the rooms were built to focus is the same sound that the rooms are focusing now, and there is no one to hear it, and there has been no one to hear it for a very long time, and the sound does not require hearing. The sound requires only the singing, and the singing requires only the domes, and the domes require only the water and the minerals and the flat, ancient, eroded end of the world, and the world provides these things, and will provide these things, and the song will continue, steady and deafening and absolutely without need, until the water dissolves the last dome and the last filament stops vibrating and the last note fades into the silence that has been waiting, patient and equal, since before the sound began.
