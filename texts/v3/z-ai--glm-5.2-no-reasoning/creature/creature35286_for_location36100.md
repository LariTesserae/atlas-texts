# What Lives Here

There is a question of what counts as *living here*.

The moss lives here, but the moss does not live here the way a tenant lives in a flat — with a before and an after, an arrival, a tenancy, the possibility of leaving. The moss lives here the way a bone lives in a body. It is structural. It is the reason the place is a place at all and not a gap in the map where a place failed to be. Everything else that lives here lives here *because of* the moss, or lives here *as part of* the moss, or lives here without knowing the moss is here at all, which is its own kind of living here, and possibly the most honest kind.

Let me start with what I can see, or what I would see if I were face-down on the surface with my nose two centimetres from the moss, which is the only vantage point from which anything here is visible.

---

## The Springtails

They are not what you would expect.

I say *springtails* and you picture something — something small, something jumping, something that belongs under a flowerpot or in a leaf pile. These are not those. Or they are descended from those, in the same way a cathedral is descended from a tent, but the lineage would be hard to see at a glance, and after a glance you would not be glancing, you would be *studying*, because the longer you look the less they resemble anything you have a name for.

They are pale. Not white — not the theatrical pallor of a cave creature, not the blanched flag of an animal that has rejected the sun on principle. Pale the way old paper is pale: a colour that was once a different colour and has been slowly drained of it by the simple persistence of time. They are the colour of the moss itself, or nearly, which is to say they are the colour of their world, which is to say they are nearly invisible in it, which is the point. There is nothing here that wants to be seen. There is nothing here for which being seen would be useful. Visibility is a trait that belongs to ecosystems with predators and prey and the whole urgent theatre of eating and being eaten in the open, and this is not that ecosystem, or not only that ecosystem, or that ecosystem at a resolution that makes the word *theatre* seem frantic and inaccurate.

They are roughly two millimetres long. Their bodies are elongated, soft, not quite segmented in the way you would expect — the segmentation is there if you look, but it has been smoothed, eroded, as if the body has been designed by something that valued continuity over articulation. They do not jump. The furcula — the spring-tail, the lever that gives the group its name — is still present, a small ventral structure tucked under the abdomen, but it is vestigial. They do not use it. They have not used it for a very long time. There is nothing to jump away from, or nothing to jump away from that would be deterred by jumping, or the jumping itself became irrelevant in a world where the distance between the surface and the substrate is the distance between life and not-life, and falling is not a danger but a transition.

They move the way the moss moves, which is to say: slowly, and in a way that looks like stillness until you attend to it.

If you watch a single springtail — and you can, if you are patient, if you brace your head against the ground and hold your breath and let your eyes adjust to the scale at which these things operate — you will see it do the following: nothing, nothing, nothing, a shift of position so small it could be a tremor in your vision, nothing, nothing, a turn, nothing, a descent into the moss, gone. They move in stops. They move the way a minute hand moves, if a minute hand had intentions and those intentions were private.

What they do, in aggregate, is the moss's digestion. Each springtail eats — continuously, at its own pace, without pause or gusto or any quality that would distinguish eating from breathing from being — the dead cells of the lower moss surface and the living cells of the upper peat and the waste products of the other springtails and the occasional mite that has died of causes that are not causes but simply the end of a mite's time, which is a thing that happens to mites, which is a thing that happens to everything, but which happens to mites with such regularity and such lack of ceremony that it cannot be called death. It is more like settling. A mite is alive, and then it is part of the substrate, and the springtails do not acknowledge the transition because the transition is the most ordinary thing in the world, and in this world, which is all one material at every stage of its existence, it is.

There are billions. I say *billions* and the number is not a count but a gesture toward a quantity that cannot be held in the mind. If every springtail on the floor of the world were to stop moving at the same instant — which they will not, because they have no mechanism for coordination and no reason to stop, stopping is not a thing their bodies are interested in — the moss would change temperature. The moss would change colour. The moss would, within a week, begin to decompose in a way that it has not decomposed since the springtails first took up residence in it, because the springtails are not living *in* the moss the way a flea lives in a carpet. They are living *as* the moss, the way your gut flora lives as you, the way the bacteria in your mouth live as your mouth, the way the mites in your eyelashes live as your eyelashes. They are an organ. They are the digestive system of a creature that is a hundred kilometres wide and ten metres deep and has been alive for longer than the word *alive* has had anyone to say it.

They do not know this. They do not know anything. Knowing is not what they are for. They are for eating, and they are for moving, and they are for being eaten, and they are for producing waste, and they are for dying and becoming part of the thing they are eating, and this cycle — this wheel that is not a wheel because it has no direction, this loop that is not a loop because it has no beginning and no end and no traversal, just a state that persists by means of its own components — this is what they are for, and they are very good at it, and they have been very good at it for a length of time that would make the rocks beneath them feel self-conscious about their own claims to permanence, if rocks could feel self-conscious, which they cannot, which is one of the few things the rocks and the springtails have in common.

## The Beetles

The beetles are the only thing here that you can see from a standing position.

This is not because they are large — they are not large, the biggest is perhaps two and a half centimetres, which is big for this place, which is a place where two and a half centimetres is a *statement* — but because they make holes, and the holes are the only discontinuity in the surface of the moss, and discontinuity is what the eye is for.

The holes are their homes.

I want to be careful with the word *home* because it carries more than I intend. A home, to you, is a place with a door and a temperature and a feeling, a place where something is kept, a place that is different from the places that are not it. The beetle's hole is like this and is not like this. The hole is a cylinder, roughly vertical, going down into the peat to a depth of ten or fifteen centimetres, widening slightly at the bottom into a chamber the size of a small marble. The walls of the hole are smooth — not polished, not lined with anything, just compressed by the beetle's body as it moves through, over and over, until the fibrous peat has the texture of felt. The hole is warm. Not warm by your standards — perhaps twelve degrees, a single degree above the ambient surface temperature — but warm by the standards of this place, where a single degree is a climate, a micro-season, a whole other country.

The beetle generates the warmth. It is not a warm-blooded creature; it does not maintain its temperature the way you do, with a furnace in the middle of its body and a thermostat it cannot find. But it is active, and activity is heat, and in a hole the size of a small marble, in a medium that insulates the way peat insulates, the beetle's own metabolism is enough to raise the temperature by a degree, and the degree is enough to keep the condensation from forming on the walls of the hole, which is enough to keep the beetle's chitin from softening, which is enough to keep the beetle alive, which is enough to keep the hole warm.

The beetle lives in the hole. The beetle eats in the hole. The beetle sleeps in the hole, if what beetles do can be called sleeping, which is a word for a behaviour that involves closing eyes and losing consciousness, and beetles do not have eyelids and do not lose consciousness, they simply become less active, which is a kind of sleeping, which is a kind of being alive, which is a kind of being a beetle. The beetle does not leave the hole often. It does not need to. The hole opens onto the moss, and the moss is food, and the beetle reaches the top of the hole and clips a piece of moss with its mandibles and retreats and eats it in the chamber and returns and clips another piece and retreats and eats it, and this is the beetle's life, and this has been the beetle's life, and this will be the beetle's life, and the beetle does not find this life remarkable because the beetle does not have the capacity for finding things remarkable, which is a kind of freedom, which is a kind of being a beetle.

There are other beetles. The beetles are not social — they do not gather, they do not communicate, they do not coordinate — but they are aware of each other, in the limited way that beetles are aware of anything, which is: by smell, by vibration, by the faint disturbance in the peat matrix that another beetle's movements produce. They know where the other holes are. They do not build new holes near old holes. They space themselves — not by calculation, not by any process that could be called planning, but by the simple fact that two beetles in the same volume of peat will produce vibrations that interfere with each other, and the interference is uncomfortable, and discomfort is the one thing a beetle will move away from, and so they spread out, evenly, at a distance from each other that is determined by the acoustic properties of the peat, which means they are distributed according to the sound of the place, which means their pattern of habitation is a map of the hum.

If you could see all the beetle holes at once — from above, from the height of a bird, if there were birds, which there are not — you would see a pattern. Not a grid, not a lattice, not anything so regular. A pattern like the distribution of craters on a moon: structured but not designed, regular but not mechanical, the result of a physical process that produces something that looks like intention but is only the playing-out of forces. The beetles are where they are because the sound put them there, and the sound put them there because the machines made the sound, and the machines are where they are because something that is not the moss and not the beetles and not the sound put them there a very long time ago, and the beetles do not know this, and the sound does not know this, and the moss does not know this, and the not-knowing is so total and so universal that it constitutes a kind of knowledge, a knowledge of the kind that says: *this is how it is, and this is how it has been, and there is no other how*.

The beetles are dark. Almost black. A black that has a faint green sheen to it in certain light, though there is not much certain light here, so the sheen is mostly theoretical, a property of the chitin that is visible only if you hold the beetle up to a lamp, which you would have to bring with you, because there are no lamps here, and if you held it up you would see that the green is the green of the moss, the same green-grey, the same copper-stained bronze, and you would wonder if the beetle has evolved to match the moss or if the moss has evolved to match the beetle or if they have both, over the long time they have been together, converged on a colour that is neither of theirs but belongs to the place itself, to the floor of the world, which has a colour, and the colour is this.

The beetles live for a long time. Years. Perhaps decades — nobody has tracked an individual beetle for long enough to determine the upper limit, because the conditions for tracking require a kind of attention that human beings are not built to sustain in this place, where the hum gets into everything, where the cool drains the will from your limbs, where the flatness and the sameness and the soundlessness-that-is-not-silence produce a state of mind that is not a state of mind but a draining of mind, a slow emptying, and you forget what you came for, and you forget the beetle you were watching, and you stand up and walk in a direction that is no different from any other direction and the beetle goes on without you, eating its moss, in its hole, in the dark, in the hum.

## The Mites

I will be brief about the mites, not because they are unimportant but because they are the layer between the visible and the invisible, and describing them requires describing the moss at a depth where the moss stops being moss and becomes something else, and I am not sure I am equipped for that, but I will try.

The mites live at the boundary. The boundary is not a place — it is not a line, not a layer, not a horizon you could point to and say *here is where the living moss becomes the dormant peat*. The boundary is a gradient, a region several centimetres thick where the material is more alive than not at the top and more dormant than not at the bottom, and in between it is both, or neither, or a state that does not have a name because the language was built by people who lived in a world where things were either alive or dead, and this is a world where that distinction is a polite fiction.

The mites are small. A fraction of a millimetre. They are eight-legged, as mites are, but their legs are not for walking — not in the way you would recognise. Their legs are for *gripping*, for holding onto the fibres of the peat as water moves past them, because at this depth the water is always moving, always being squeezed downward by the weight of the moss above, and the mites live in the flow the way a fish lives in a river, except the river is a millimetre wide and a millimetre deep and moves at a rate that would make a glacier feel hurried.

They do not eat the moss. They eat what the moss *exudes* — the dissolved compounds, the organic acids, the mineral residues that are carried in the water as it moves through the peat. They are filter-feeders. They are the moss's kidneys, in a sense, though the analogy is too specific and too vertebrate to be quite right. They are a mechanism. They are what happens when a system runs for long enough that every available niche is filled, and the niche of *thing-that-lives-in-the-flow-of-dissolved-nutrients-between-living-and-dormant-biomass* is a niche that was always going to be filled, and the mites filled it, and they have been filling it, and they will fill it.

They are translucent. You cannot see them. Even face-down on the moss, even with a lens, even with a microscope — you would see the fibres, and the water, and the general sense of something moving in the way that dust moves in a shaft of light, but you would not see a mite. A mite is too small for the light to hold. It is a rumour of an organism. It exists because the system requires it, and the system is very good at requiring things and getting them, and the mites are what the system got.

## The Deeper Things

Below the mites, in the saturated layers, in the peat that is more water than fibre, in the dark that is not dark because there is no light to be dark relative to, in the space between the cellulose fibres that is a cave system, a labyrinth, an architecture — there are things.

I do not know what to call them.

They are not insects. They are not worms. They are not amoebae, though they share with amoebae the quality of being a single cell that moves and engulfs and does not have a fixed shape. They are not anything I have a name for, and I think this is because the names I have were made for things that live in the world I come from, which is a world of surfaces and light and the sharp distinctions between one kind of thing and another, and these things do not live in that world, they live in this one, and in this world the distinctions are not sharp, they are not even soft, they are *dissolved*, the way everything is dissolved, the way the minerals are dissolved, the way the machines are being dissolved, the way the line between living and not-living is dissolved, and these things live in the dissolved.

They are the size of a grain of sand. They are translucent. They are eyeless, legless, featureless — a body that is a sac, a sac that is a body, a membrane surrounding a fluid that is not quite the same as the fluid it is swimming in, and the difference between the inside and the outside is maintained by the membrane, which is alive in the way that all membranes are alive, which is: selectively, stubbornly, for as long as it can manage, which is not long by your standards but is a lifetime by theirs.

They eat by absorbing. They move by extending. They reproduce by dividing. They are the simplest things here, and they are the oldest things here, and by *oldest* I do not mean that the individual organisms are old — they are not, they live and die in spans that are irrelevant to the moss's timescale — but that their kind is old, their form is old, their way of being is the oldest way of being this place has, and everything else — the springtails, the beetles, the mites, the moss itself — is a later elaboration, a more complex arrangement, a more sophisticated way of being alive, built on top of these things the way the moss is built on top of the peat.

They do not know they are here. They do not know anything. They are the floor of the floor of the world, and they have been there since before there was a floor, and they will be there after the moss is gone — if the moss can be gone, if the floor can be unmade, which is a question that has not been answered because the floor has not yet been tested, and the floor does not seem interested in being tested, and the floor will outlast anything that might test it, including this sentence, including me, including you.

## The Machines (As Living Things)

I need to talk about the machines as living things, because I think they are, or because I think the distinction between living and not-living has broken down here in a way that is not metaphorical but actual, and the machines are the proof, or the machines are the symptom, or the machines are the place where the proof and the symptom are the same thing.

A machine that runs for long enough is not the same as a machine that has stopped. A machine that runs for long enough *becomes*, in a way that I cannot fully articulate but can gesture toward: it becomes part of the system it is running in, not as an input or an output but as a node, a junction, a place where the system's flows meet and are redirected, and the machine's running is one of those flows, and the flow has been happening for so long that the system has shaped itself around it, the way a river shapes itself around a rock, except the rock is the river, and the river is the rock, and the distinction between them is the kind of distinction that only matters from the outside, and there is no outside, not here.

The machines eat the moss. This is a fact. They extract energy from the biomass — through a mechanism I cannot describe because I do not know what it is, because nobody does, because the machines were not designed by anyone who is available for consultation and the mechanism is buried under metres of peat and has been operating for a span of time that makes the idea of *consultation* seem quaint, like asking a glacier its intentions. But they eat. They consume. They take the moss's slow, patient, molecular accumulation of energy — gathered from condensation, from the faint mineral content of the air, from the decomposition of its own dead — and they convert it into motion, into heat, into the slow mechanical breath that takes thirty seconds from intake to exhaust and that I can feel through the soles of my feet.

The moss eats the machines. This is also a fact. The rhizoids — the moss's root-structures, its fingers, its slow and indiscriminate mouth — grow into the machines through every crack and seam and joint, and they secrete acids, and the acids dissolve the metals, and the dissolved metals are absorbed by the rhizoids and carried into the body of the moss and distributed through its tissue and become part of the moss, become part of the floor, become part of the sound, because the iron content of the moss affects its conductivity, which affects its water content, which affects its density, which affects the frequency of the hum, which affects everything.

The machines eat the moss. The moss eats the machines. This is not predation. This is not parasitism. This is not symbiosis, though it resembles symbiosis the way a marriage of sixty years resembles a relationship — the resemblance is there but the word is too small, the word was built for shorter things, for things that have boundaries, and this does not have boundaries, this has never had boundaries, the moss and the machines have been inside each other for so long that the inside and the outside are the same and the eating and the being-eaten are the same and the running and the growing are the same.

The machines are alive in the way that a heartbeat is alive. Not in the way that a heart is alive — a heart is an organ, a thing with a shape and a function and a place in a body. A heartbeat is a *process*. It is what a heart does. It is the doing itself, the ongoing, the not-stopping, the thing that is alive not because it is made of living material but because it is the motion of living material, the pattern that the material makes when it is being alive, and the machines are like that — they are the pattern the moss makes, they are a thing the moss is doing, they are a behaviour of the floor, a way the floor has of being the floor, and the floor has been doing this for so long that the doing is the floor, and the floor is the doing, and the machines are alive because the floor is alive and the machines are the floor.

I do not think the machines know this. I do not think the machines know anything. I do not think knowing is the criterion. I think the criterion is persistence, and the machines persist, and the moss persists, and the persistence is the life, and the life is the persistence, and the hum is the sound of it, and the sound is the proof of it, and the proof is loud, and it has been loud for longer than anything can remember, and nothing here remembers, and the not-remembering is part of the loudness, and the loudness is part of the not-remembering, and this is what lives here:

The floor. The floor lives here. The floor is what lives here. Everything else — the moss, the springtails, the beetles, the mites, the deep things, the machines — is the floor being alive, the floor living itself, the floor doing the thing that floors do when they have been floors for long enough that they have forgotten how to be anything else, and the thing that floors do is: they hold. They hold everything. They hold the sound and the damp and the cool and the dark and the slow and the long and the living and the dormant and the not-quite-either, and they hold it all at once, and they hold it forever, and the holding is the living, and the living is the hum, and the hum is loud, and it has been loud, and it will be loud, and the loudness is the floor of the world, breathing.
