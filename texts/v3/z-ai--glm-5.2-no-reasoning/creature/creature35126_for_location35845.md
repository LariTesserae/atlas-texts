# What Lives Here

---

## The Engine-Keepers (who are not keepers)

Let me begin with what is easiest to mistake for absence.

There are no humans here. That needs to be said first, because the place argues otherwise. The stenciled labels on the pipes. The brickwork, which is competent and human-proportioned. The corrugated metal, which was cut by someone who understood the word *utilitarian*. The greenhouse, which is composed — *composed*, not planted. Someone arranged those ferns. Someone chose where the *Astilbe* would go, that single warm note. Someone looked at the black pool and decided it belonged at the center.

These people are gone. They have been gone long enough that their beds have become soil. But they left something behind that is difficult to name — not a legacy, exactly, because that implies intention. More like a *cast*. The shape of their attention pressed into the place the way a hand pressed into wet clay leaves a shape that hardens and outlasts the hand. The facility runs because they built it to run. The plants grow where they grow because the water falls where it falls, and the water falls where it falls because the pipes were placed where pipes were placed. The human hand is still present in the system the way a skeleton is present in a body: nothing it does is *done* by it, and nothing it does could be done without it.

I mention this because if you come here looking for inhabitants, you will find them. They are the place itself. The Filter Gardens is not a ruin. A ruin is a place where the human purpose has failed and something else has grown over the failure. Here, the purpose has not failed. It has simply been absorbed. The engine breathes. The water moves. The *Cissus* vines climb the pipes not because the pipes are abandoned but because the pipes are warm and wet, and warmth and wetness are invitations. The facility did not fall into disrepair. It fell into *ecology*. There is a difference, and it matters.

## The Wrens

Thirty pairs. Perhaps more now — counting is imprecise because the acoustics make every bird sound like three birds, and the wrens have learned to use this. I want to be careful about saying *learned*, because it implies a deliberateness I cannot prove, but the songs here are different from the songs of the same species in other populations, and the difference is structural. More phrases. More repetition. More variation. A wren in another ravine — if there were another ravine — would sing a shorter song. These wrens sing *compositions*. They sing into the stone walls and the stone walls sing back, and the round that results is something no single wren is producing.

I have watched them. They perch on the copper canopy and they *watch*. This is not anthropomorphism. I mean they orient their bodies toward movement, toward the drippers, toward the insects that aggregate where water collects, and they make decisions about what to pursue and what to ignore. They are bold — bolder than conspecifics elsewhere, which tend to be furtive and quick. These wrens are *deliberate*. They hop along the copper struts with a kind of confidence that suggests they understand the territory is theirs, and the territory includes the machinery, and the machinery is part of the territory in the same way that a tree is part of a forest. They do not distinguish between the built and the grown. Neither should you.

They nest in the walls. In the crevices where moss has built up enough soil to hold a cup of woven grass. In the collapsed brickwork of the eastern building's upper floors, where the bricks have shifted to create shelves and pockets that are, to a wren, indistinguishable from the rock faces they would naturally use. The human structure has become a cliff face. The wrens do not care about the difference. They care about the depth of the crevice, the overhang above, the proximity to the drippers where insects gather. The eastern building is prime real estate because it is close to the canopy's edge, where the mesh catches the most moisture, where the insects are thickest.

Their songs are loudest at dawn and dusk, when the light changes and the insects shift. But you will hear them at all hours. A wren will sing at two in the afternoon, a single phrase, as if checking whether the acoustics are still there. They are.

## The Salamanders

*Desmognathus*, or something near it. Dark-bodied, the color of wet bark, with gold flecks along the flanks that catch what light enters the ravine and make the animal look, for a moment, like a jewel laid on mud. They are in the basin. They are in the standing water of the eastern building's ground floor. They are on the walls at night, climbing the wet moss with a slowness that is not lethargy but *method*. They are hunting. The moss is full of springtails and small beetles. The salamander climbs to where the prey is, takes what it needs, and descends before the temperature rises.

I have found them in the morning. On the upper stones, where the moss is pale and the air is cooler. They are still then, in the way that cold-blooded animals are still — not resting but *waiting* for their bodies to warm enough to move. If you approach slowly, you can get close enough to see the gold flecks. If you approach quickly, they drop. They simply release their grip on the moss and fall, and the fall is controlled — they orient midair and land in the water or on wet stone and are gone before you register the movement. It is a good strategy. It is a strategy that works because the ravine is small enough that *gone* is only a few meters away.

They breed in the standing water. I have seen larvae — small, dark, with external gills that fan like crowns — in the eastern building's marsh in the late spring. The water there is the right depth and the right temperature and the grass provides cover. The larvae mature in the shallow water and leave when they are ready, climbing the walls or moving through the thin film of water that connects the marsh to the basin. They are not numerous. This is not a place of abundance. It is a place of *sufficiency*. There are enough salamanders. Not many. Enough.

## The Insects and the Economy Overhead

Let me describe the canopy, because the canopy is where the insects live and the insects are what everything else eats.

The copper mesh — originally built as condensation surfaces, broad flat shapes designed to catch moisture from the air — has been colonized by *Cissus* vines to the point where it is difficult to say where the structure ends and the vegetation begins. The vines have wrapped every pipe, woven through every strut, and their funnel-shaped leaves sit in the mesh like cups, holding water. The mesh drips. The leaves drip. The water that falls has passed through the vine's tissues, and it tastes — I have tasted it — of green. Of chlorophyll. Of something alive.

The insects aggregate where the water collects. The *Cissus* leaf-cups are miniature ponds. In each one, a community of small flying insects — midges, small flies, things I cannot name — breeds and feeds. The lacewing-like insects, the large-winged ones with translucent wings that catch the light, are predators. They hunt the smaller insects on the wing, and their flight produces a low vibration — a buzz that is almost below the threshold of hearing, more felt than heard, that adds a quality to the upper air that I can only describe as *shimmer*. You walk through the ravine and something moves past your ear and is gone, and the air is different where it passed.

The spiders. I did not expect the spiders. The webs span the gaps between the canopy struts, and because the humidity is constant, the webs do not dry out and do not need rebuilding. They are permanent structures — or as permanent as spider webs can be, which is more permanent than you would think. In the afternoon light, when the sun enters the ravine for its two hours, the entire canopy is revealed to be curtained with silk at various levels. Webs above webs above webs. An entire stratified economy of predator and prey operating over your head. The spiders are silver-backed, or at least they appear silver in certain light, and they sit in the centers of their webs and wait with a patience that the place seems to teach to everything that lives in it.

The crickets in the ground-floor marsh. A steady, pulse-like chirping that rises in the evening and falls in the morning. They are in the grass, in the soil that has accumulated over the concrete, in the spaces between the *Eleocharis* stems. They are the base of the terrestrial food web — the salamanders eat them, the wrens eat them, the spiders catch them when they climb. They are the reason the ground floor is not silent. The ground floor is never silent.

And other things. Beetles in the moss. Springtails in the leaf litter. A species of ant — small, dark, slow-moving — that has colonized the eastern building's second floor, where the dampness is less constant. They farm something on the decomposed mattresses — a fungus, I think, growing on the dark loam the mattresses have become. The ants are organized but unhurried. Their trails follow the gaps between the wallpaper scrolls. They do not seem to be going anywhere in particular. They are maintaining something.

## The Voles

I have not seen them well. I have seen shapes in the marsh grass. I have heard rustling at dusk — a dry sound in a wet place, which is how you know it is an animal and not water. I have found droppings near the wild ginger, which is where they eat. They are dark-furred, blunt-faced, short-tailed. Vole-sized, which is a size that is perfect for disappearing into cracks in brick walls, and they do disappear into cracks in brick walls.

They are the only mammals here, as far as I know. The ravine's isolation — the narrow walls, the lack of wind, the specific conditions — makes it inhospitable to anything larger. A cat could not get in easily, though a cat might try. A fox would have no reason to. The voles have the place to themselves, which means they have the marsh grass and the wild ginger and the shelter of the eastern building and the fact that nothing is hunting them except the wrens, which are too small to take an adult vole.

They are nocturnal. They are private. They are the inhabitants that you will almost certainly not see, and if you see them, it will be because they want you to see them, or because you have been still for long enough that they have forgotten you are there. I sat in the eastern building's ground floor for an hour at dusk and saw one — a face, blunt and dark, emerging from the grass to check the air, and then withdrawing. That was enough. I understood that the building was theirs now, and that the human purpose it was built for has been fully and finally replaced.

## The Pool's Resident

I am going to be careful here. I do not know what it is. I have watched the *Azolla* film part in a slow V-shaped wake and close again. I have watched the surface dimple, as if something below had exhaled. I have seen the ferns on the far side of the pool tremble without wind, because there is no wind here, and the trembling was caused by something below the surface touching their roots.

It is large. The V-shaped wake was perhaps forty centimeters across, which means the body producing it was perhaps forty centimeters, or perhaps larger — it is difficult to estimate the size of something you cannot see, in water that is black and opaque, in a pool whose depth I do not know. The pool is the only still water in the Filter Gardens. Every other body of water is moving — dripping, flowing, filtering. The pool is the end of the line. The water that reaches it has passed through the entire system — the facility, the pipes, the canopy, the plants — and whatever is in that water has accumulated here.

I do not think it was put here. I think it *arrived*. I think something entered the pool through the underground channels that feed it — channels too small to follow, too small for me, but not too small for a larva, a juvenile, a thing that was once small and is now not. I think it grew here. I think it grew in the black water and on whatever the black water contains, and I think it is still growing, or has reached a size that is stable, and I think the reason no one mentions it in any record is that no one was here when it arrived, or no one was watching, or it arrived in a form that no one recognized as what it would become.

The pool is still. The *Azolla* film is unbroken except when the resident moves. The resident moves slowly. It moves in what I think are circuits — the same wake, the same pattern, the V appearing at one edge and crossing to the other and disappearing. It does not surface. It does not make a sound. It is the only still life in a place where everything else is in constant, patient motion, and its stillness is the thing that unsettles me most, because stillness in a living thing is either waiting or it is so large that movement is unnecessary, and I do not know which.

I will not guess what it is. A fish. An amphibian. Something else. The water is black, and I am not going to reach in.

## The Smaller Lives

There are things here that I do not have names for. I want to acknowledge them.

A flatworm, perhaps, or something like a flatworm, moving across the surface of the basin's mud in a slow, gliding motion that is disquieting because it is so smooth. It is small — two centimeters, maybe three — and it is brown and it is there, and I do not know what it eats or what eats it. It is part of the system.

Nematodes in the soil. Bacteria on the pipe surfaces. Fungi in the leaf litter — a white mycelium that I have seen threading through the dark loam of the eastern building's composted mattresses, breaking down what the mattresses were into what the mosses need. The ants may be farming a specific fungus, but other fungi are simply present, simply doing what fungi do, which is to convert what is dead into what will be alive. They are inhabitants. They are the inhabitants you will never see, and they are the most important ones, because without them the place would fill with what it has no use for and the system would stop.

I think the facility's filtration process is biological. I think this because the water that enters the facility is basin water and the water that exits is clean, and I do not believe a mechanical filter would still be functioning after sixty years without maintenance. I think there is something alive in the main building — in the housing that no one has opened — that processes the water. Bacteria. Biofilms. A microbial community that was either intentionally installed or spontaneously colonized and that has been doing its work for longer than any human has been present to know it is doing it. The engine pumps the water. The life cleans it. The engine is mechanical. The filter is biological. This is a place where the mechanical and the biological have merged so thoroughly that the distinction is no longer useful.

## What It Is Like to Be Here

You walk in from the north, where the ravine opens, and the first thing is the sound. The pulse. *Thrmm. Thrmm. Thrmm.* It enters your chest before your ears register it, and it stays. You walk south, toward the facility, and the ravine narrows and the walls rise and the light changes — it was never direct, but now it is *only* indirect, reflected off stone, filtered through vine and mesh. The air is wet. Not raining. Not misting. *Wet*. A film on your skin that does not dry because the air is saturated and the air is saturated because the facility is harvesting moisture from it and the moisture is being returned to the air by the plants and the cycle is closed.

You pass the eastern building. You see the marsh grass through the ground-floor windows. You hear the crickets. You do not go in, or you do go in, and your boots are in two inches of water and the grass is around your waist and there are salamanders at your feet that you do not see until they move. The upper floors are dry, in the way that this place means dry, which is *not flowing-wet*. The wallpaper has curled into scrolls. The ants are on the second floor. You do not stay long. It is not your building.

You reach the greenhouse. The glass is green with algae. Inside, the light is different — warmer, closer, filtered through living tissue until the air itself seems green. The *Cyathea* presses its crown against the ceiling. The understory plants are layered with a precision that is planned and a density that is not. The pool is at the center. It is black. The *Azolla* film is rust-red. You watch the surface. You watch the surface for a long time. Something moves. You do not know what.

You walk to the facility. The pipes enter at the base, from the basin. The basin is clear, clear in a way that standing water should not be, and the salamanders are visible at the bottom, dark shapes on pale mud. The engine pulses. You can feel it in the stone under your feet. You cannot feel it in the pipes, but the pipes are vibrating — a fine tremor that you can detect if you press your palm flat against the metal. The pipes are warm. The water inside them is warm. The warmth is the engine's, and the engine's warmth is moving through the system, and the system is the ravine, and the ravine is alive in a way that the word *alive* does not quite cover.

You turn around. You walk back. The wrens are singing. The lacewing-things are buzzing past your ears. The canopy drips. The light is changing — it is late afternoon, and the two hours of direct sun are ending, and the ravine is settling into the even, shadowless light of evening. The crickets are getting louder. The salamanders are climbing. The voles are stirring in the grass.

The pulse does not change. *Thrmm. Thrmm. Thrmm.* It has not changed since you arrived. It will not change when you leave. You are standing inside something that works, and your presence is not required for the working, and your absence will not alter it, and this is not loneliness. This is not abandonment. This is a place that has become complete without you, and the completeness is the thing you came to find, and you found it, and it does not need you, and you can go.
