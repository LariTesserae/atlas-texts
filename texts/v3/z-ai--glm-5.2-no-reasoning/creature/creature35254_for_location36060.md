What lives here is not a collection of separate beings. That is the thing that must be understood first, though it is the hardest thing to understand, because the mind arrives carrying its categories — producer and consumer, flora and fauna, self and other — and the plateau does not use them, has never used them, may never have learned them.

What lives here is a metabolism wearing the shape of a continent.

But it has faces, and the faces demand acknowledgment, so:

---

The grazers are the closest thing to a population. The word is wrong — implies a people, implies a counting — but the sheer number of them, the way they constitute the visual texture of any open surface, means that when you close your eyes and think of life here, you think of them. Flat, articulated, six-limbed, the color of the bruise they feed on. Hundreds in a group, thousands across a visible stretch of terrain, their bodies rippling in concert across the groundcover in patterns that look — from elevation, from the ridge of a freshly raised laminate fold — like the movement of wind across a wheat field, except the wheat field is alive and the wind is alive and the pattern itself is alive, is the point, is not a metaphor for anything.

They do not behave as individuals. You watch one isolate itself — separated by a closing fracture, carried on a shard of crust that shifts three meters from the main surface — and it does not panic, does not search, does not call. It continues to graze. Its body continues its ripple. Within minutes it has changed color, subtly, fractionally, shifting toward the specific mottle of the crust it now sits on. It is adapting. Or it is forgetting what it was. Or those are the same act. When the fracture heals and the shard rejoins the main surface, the grazer rejoins its group without acknowledgment, without reunion, as though it had never left, as though separation is a concept that does not translate into whatever they use for cognition. They are not hive-mind. That implies a mind. They are a process that occurs in many bodies simultaneously, the way digestion occurs in many cells.

They graze on the crust. This is important. They are eating the machine. Their mouthparts — which are not mandibles, not teeth, but something like rasping lamellae, layered and flexible, the same laminate structure as the crust itself but organic, grown, not deposited — scrape at the surface and consume the outermost layer. What they extract is not mineral. Not nutrient. Not anything with a clear biochemical name. They extract something the crust has processed, something the engines have produced, some intermediate substance that exists in the laminate and nowhere else. They are, in a functional sense, the exhaust system. They are how the plateau sheds what it no longer needs. And what they excrete — which they do continuously, as they move, a fine particulate that settles into the groundcover and is absorbed — is something that the plants require. The grazers are a digestive organ dispersed across a million small bodies.

They do not die. Or rather: they die constantly. You see it happen — a grazer, mid-stride, slowing, its segments loosening, its color fading from mottled chitin-bark to the pale yellow-white of exposed tissue, and then it simply stops, settles, and the groundcover grows over it within minutes, and the fungal networks web across it within seconds, and it is gone. Not consumed. Absorbed. Returned. And at the same moment, at the edge of every group, new grazers emerge from the groundcover — small, pale, already moving, already grazing, already the color of the surface they emerged onto. The population is constant. The individuals are temporary. They are a flame, not a fire — the process sustained, the fuel interchangeable.

---

The canopy dwellers are different. They are solitary, or they are so sparsely distributed that you never see more than one at a time, and they are large. The glimpses are always partial — a limb, here, reaching across the gap between two garnet fans, its joints visible as knuckles of darker tissue against the dim ceiling-light; a body, there, pressed flat against a lattice-column, its outline blending with the structural pattern until you cannot tell where the creature ends and the architecture begins. They are the things that navigate by touch, reading the surfaces with their appendages, and what they are reading is the engine-state. You realize this slowly. You watch one move across a fan and its limbs press into the pleated surface at specific points, and the fan's vibration changes — not much, not dramatically, but measurably, the hum shifting in pitch by a fraction of a tone. The creature is adjusting. Tuning. It is a maintenance action. The canopy dwellers are the repair system.

But they are also living things. They breathe — you see their bodies expand and contract, though not in synchrony with the ground. They have their own rhythm, slower, deeper, a cycle of minutes rather than seconds. They make sound — that low, continuous murmur, that drone, almost identical to the fan-hum but not quite, the way a singer's voice is almost identical to the note they are matching but carries the texture of a throat, of a body, of intent. They are not mechanical. They are not the maintenance drones of the original builders, still running their ancient programming. They are organisms that have evolved to fill a niche, and the niche is upkeep. The machine shaped them. The machine could not maintain itself, not all of it, not everywhere, not in the places where the tissue overgrew the mechanical and the mechanical failed and the system needed something mobile, something that could move to where the problem was, something that could feel the vibration and know, in whatever way they know, that a fan needed adjusting here, a conduit needed clearing there.

They are the closest thing this place has to inhabitants. To citizens. Not because they live here — everything lives here — but because they work here. They have a function that is not only metabolic. They do something for the system that the system cannot do for itself. And this gives them a quality, a presence, that the grazers lack. When you see a canopy dweller clearly — and you do, once, for three full seconds, as it crosses a gap in the foliage and the ceiling-light brightens to that pale gold and its body is fully illuminated — you see something that looks like purpose. Its body is broad, flat, the same laminate-chitin structure as everything here, but its limbs are differentiated. Two pairs for locomotion, long and many-jointed. One pair for manipulation, shorter, finer, ending in something that is not hands but serves the function of hands. Its head — if it is a head — is a smooth, featureless dome, but the dome is covered in fine cilia that move constantly, tasting the air, reading the vibrations, attending to the system's frequencies. It does not see you. It does not need to see you. You are not part of the system it maintains. It moves past you with the indifference of a technician passing a stranger in a corridor, and it is gone, and the murmur of its passage fades back into the general hum, and you are left with the impression of something that knows exactly what it is doing even if what it is doing has no larger meaning.

They do not reproduce. Or rather: you never see them reproduce. You see them grow — small ones, in the upper canopy, their limbs short and uncertain, their movements clumsy, learning the surfaces, learning the vibrations. Where they come from is unclear. They emerge from the same dense vegetative zones as everything else, assembled from available materials, grown rather than born. They are, in this sense, the most landscape-derived of all the macrofauna: their bodies composed of the same stuff as the fans and the vines and the crust, put together in a different configuration, given a different shape, set loose to do a job that the landscape needs done. They are the plateau's hands.

---

The things in the air are not organisms. This is your conclusion after days — or what you think are days — of observation. They are events.

A column of warm air rises from the surface during an exhale cycle. It carries moisture, spores, the fine particulate excretions of the grazers, the chemical traces of a thousand metabolic processes occurring simultaneously in the crust beneath. It rises into the cooler air below the ceiling-clouds, and the temperature differential does something — not condensation, not exactly, but a brief, organized phase transition in which the suspended material in the air column aligns, crystallizes, takes on structure. The prismatic flashes you see are light refracting through these organized structures. The piping you hear is the sound of the structures forming and collapsing, the air rushing through micro-channels in their lattice, a flute made of dust and chemistry.

But they behave like organisms. They move with direction. They cluster. They produce patterns — the piping is not random, it has rhythm, it has variation, it has something that functions like syntax. And they respond to the environment: when the canopy dwellers murmur, the piping changes, as though the airborne structures are listening, as though the murmur is a signal and the piping is a response. You cannot shake the conviction that they are alive. You cannot substantiate it. They are the boundary case, the place where the question "is this alive?" stops being useful and starts being a limitation of the question-asker.

They might be the plateau's attention. Not consciousness — the plateau is not conscious — but the distributed, non-localized equivalent of attention, the way an immune system attends to a body. They form where the system needs to process information. They dissolve when the processing is done. They are thoughts, if thoughts were weather. They are weather, if weather were thinking.

---

And then there are the deep forms.

You avoid them at first. Everyone would. They are wrong in a way that bypasses the intellect and activates something older, something in the spine, something that knows what a face is and knows that a face in the wrong place is danger. You see your first one on the third day — or the third hour, or the third week, time is not behaving — deep in a thicket of lattice-vines and garnet fans, standing in a pocket of shadow that is slightly too dark, slightly too persistent. It is made of the landscape. Vines, groundcover, fungal matter, fragments of crust, all compressed and woven and shaped into something approximately humanoid, approximately two meters tall, approximately standing. Its face — and it has a face, there is no denying that what it has is a face — is a concavity in the upper portion of its mass, two darker spots above a ridge, a cleft below. It is not a face the way a sculpture is a face. It is a face the way a reflection in moving water is a face: unstable, suggestive, requiring interpretation, rewarding interpretation, changing as you interpret it.

You look away. You look back. It is still there. It has become more detailed. The features have refined. The mouth-shape has opened slightly. The eye-spots have darkened. You look away. You look back. It is more detailed still. You stop looking. It disarticulates over the next hour, its components settling back into the general mass, and it is gone.

You see another the next day. And another. They are not rare. They are, in fact, ubiquitous in the deep zones, and once you know what to look for, you see them everywhere — in the shadows of the fans, in the crotches of lattice-columns, in the dense matted groundcover where the growth is thickest. They are always in shadow. They are always made of local materials. They are always approximately humanoid. And they always have faces.

The faces are the problem. Not because they are frightening — though they are, at first, in the way that anything familiar in an unfamiliar place is frightening — but because they are specific. Each one is different. Each one is detailed. And the longer you look at any given form, the more detailed its face becomes, and the more detailed its face becomes, the more it resembles something. A person. Not a specific person. Not someone you know. But personhood itself. The generic template. The shared architecture of every face you have ever seen, reduced to its essentials and then elaborated, refined, made particular.

You watch one for a long time. You cannot help it. The face refines. The eye-spots develop depth. The mouth-shape develops contour. The surface texture of the form changes, the vines and fungal matter tightening, smoothing, becoming something closer to skin — not skin, but the memory of skin, the concept of skin rendered in vegetable matter. And the face — the face becomes yours.

Not exactly yours. But yours the way a portrait is yours: recognizable, particular, slightly wrong in the proportions, slightly off in the expression, as though the artist had a vague description and no photograph. You stare at it. It stares at you. The ground breathes. The fans hum. The engines labor. And you understand, with a certainty that does not come from reason but from somewhere lower, somewhere in the tissue of your own body, that the form is not mimicking you. The form is processing you. It is reading the pattern of your presence — your heat, your respiration, the chemical signature of your skin, the particular way your attention falls on surfaces — and it is rendering that pattern in the only medium it has. It is making a portrait of you out of itself. It is the plateau seeing you, and what it sees, it becomes, briefly, before the effort of maintaining that particular configuration exceeds the available energy and the form collapses and the face dissolves and you are looking at a pile of vines again.

The deep forms are the plateau's sensory apparatus. They are how a system without eyes, without ears, without a nervous system, without anything that could be called a brain, attends to the things that are not part of it. They are the immune response rendered as portraiture. They are the landscape trying to know what is in it. And they are failing, and they are trying again, and they will try again, endlessly, because the system does not know how to stop, does not know that the effort is futile, does not know that the faces it makes will never be the things they resemble, because the system does not know anything. The system runs. The running produces effects. The effects include faces. The faces include yours.

---

What lives here is the Exhalation Plateaus. That is the only honest answer. The grazers and the canopy dwellers and the air-things and the deep forms are not inhabitants; they are organs. They are what a continent-sized abandoned machine produces when it has been running without oversight for long enough that the boundary between mechanism and biology has dissolved. They are the shapes that function takes when function is the only remaining imperative. They are alive the way a reflex is alive — not because they choose to be, not because they contain some spark, but because the system that produces them is still moving, still breathing, still cycling its fluids and its gases and its energies through conduits that have become veins and through veins that have become conduits, and movement looks like life when you watch it long enough, and maybe it is life, and maybe the distinction was always a fiction, and maybe the only thing that matters is that the engines are still running and the faces are still forming and the ground is still breathing, breathing, breathing, and will not stop until it stops.
