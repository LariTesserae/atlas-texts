# What Lives Here

They emerge, and they have been emerging for longer than the word *emerging* implies. Not the way a creature emerges from a burrow — quickly, alertly, with the snap of decision. The way a stalactite emerges from a cave roof. The way a cancer emerges from a tissue. Gradual, inevitable, the terminal consequence of conditions persisting.

---

## The First Order

Start with the carpet.

The carpet-organisms — the flat, luminous, twenty-four-legged grinders that cover every horizontal surface — are the foundation. Not because they are the simplest or the most primitive but because they are the most *successful*. They have done the thing that every successful organism does: they have stopped being a species and become a *condition*. They are the way the Aggregate processes its own floor. They are the weather of this place, the erosion and the deposition both, and they have been both for so long that their bodies have become part of the stratigraphy. When you look at the floor you see them alive on top and you see them fossilized below, compressed into the biological mat layer, and the transition between living and mineralized is not a line but a gradient. Somewhere in that gradient, an individual dies and the chemistry of its body changes and the chitin crystallizes and the luminous organs go dark and the calcium floods outward into the surrounding matrix and the organism becomes geology, and this has happened so many times that the floor is, in part, *made of them*. They are eating their own ancestors. They are walking on their own ancestors. These are not different activities to them.

Their bioluminescence is not for communication. It is not for mating. It is not for warning. The light is metabolic — a byproduct of the chemical process by which they extract the mineral fraction from the composite they grind. They glow because they are digesting, the way a fire glows because it is burning. The blue-white is the specific frequency of a particular reaction involving copper compounds and the chitin-active enzyme that breaks them down. When they are actively feeding, they are brighter. When they are satiated, they dim. The Aggregate's ambient light is therefore a map of its metabolism, and right now, under your boots, where your weight has compressed the surface and exposed fresh composite, the carpet is *feeding*, and the local glow is intensifying, and a widening halo of bright blue-white is spreading from the spot where you stand, and it will persist for hours after you leave, a footprint made of light, marking the place where a strange compression event briefly enriched the substrate.

The rasping whisper of the carpet is not one sound but a statistical aggregate of millions of individual sounds — the click of a mouthpart, the scrape of a leg tip, the micro-fracture of a mineral grain being sheared from its matrix. Taken together it is continuous and unmodulated, like white noise, like wind, and the creatures that live above the carpet have evolved to interpret it the way surface animals interpret wind: as a constant, as a medium, as the thing that carries information and also the thing against which information must be distinguished. They have ears — or rather, they have vibration-sensitive organs distributed along their body segments — and these organs are tuned not to the carpet-sound itself but to the *interruptions* in it. A footstep. A falling object. A body in distress. The carpet-sound is their silence. What breaks it is their language.

## The Second Order

Above the carpet, the space divides into functional zones the way a forest divides into canopy and understory, though the divisions here are not vertical but *structural* — determined by the architecture of the mass, the location of fissures, the proximity to functioning infrastructure. In the zone nearest the floor, where the carpet is densest and the air is richest in exhaled mineral particulate, the detritivores work.

There are the **tube-dwellers** — long, vermiform arthropods that could be mistaken for annelids at first glance but cannot be, because annelids are soft and these are armored, and because their segmentation is wrong: each segment bears four pairs of short, rigid appendages that end in chisel-like claws, and each segment is independently muscled, and each segment is independently luminous, and the effect of seeing one of these moving through the carpet is like watching a glowing zipper being drawn through felt. They do not burrow. They *consume* a path. They eat the carpet-organisms as they go, grinding through them with the chisel claws, and the carpet-organisms do not flee because the carpet-organisms do not flee from anything. The tube-dwellers leave behind a tunnel lined with a chitinous secretion that hardens within minutes to a substance like PVC, smooth and slightly flexible, and these tunnels riddle the floor in a three-dimensional lattice, and inside the tunnels the tube-dwellers live and breed and die, and other organisms move through them, and the tunnels become infrastructure, become the road network of the Aggregate, and when the tube-dwellers die their bodies line the tunnels they built, and the tunnels persist long after the builders are gone, and the Aggregate is full of empty tunnels, ghost tunnels, tunnels that lead nowhere because the nowhere they led to has been compressed into the mass or eaten away or colonized by something that does not need the tunnel and does not care that it exists.

There are the **sieve-feeders** — flat, disc-shaped organisms the diameter of a saucer, with bodies so thin they appear two-dimensional from above but which are, in cross-section, a dense lamellar structure like a book laid on its side. Each "page" of the book is a gill surface, and each gill surface is coated in a sticky mucus that traps the finest particulate from the air — the aerosolized copper and chalk and ferric powder that the carpet exhales. They sit on the surfaces of the mass, perfectly still, for hours or days, accumulating a film of metal and mineral on their gill surfaces, and then they close — the book closes, the pages press together — and internal digestion begins, and the organism swells and darkens from translucent white to a deep, cloudy amber as the metallic meal is processed, and then the book opens again, and the pages are clean, and the feeding resumes. You see them everywhere in the Aggregate — on every flat surface, on every ledge, on every piece of ductwork and machinery that offers a horizontal plane. They are the filters of this place. They clean the air the way mussels clean a bay. Without them the particulate load would be thick enough to see through like fog; with them it is merely dense enough to taste. They are the reason the air is twelve degrees and not fifteen — because the metabolic heat of millions of sieve-feeders digesting metal is the second-largest thermal input to the system, after the functioning electrical fixtures, and the system has reached equilibrium at twelve, and the equilibrium is maintained by the sieve-feeders, and the equilibrium has persisted for so long that to disturb it would be to kill the Aggregate, and the Aggregate does not want to die, and the Aggregate is not sure if it is alive, and the Aggregate does not know what it wants.

There are the **maintenance-walkers** — and here is where the taxonomy gets uncomfortable, because the maintenance-walkers are not entirely biological. They are the size of a human hand, with bodies that are clearly arthropodal — segmented, armored, multi-legged — but their bodies incorporate metallic components the way a hermit crab incorporates a shell. Except the incorporation is not behavioral. It is *developmental*. The maintenance-walkers grow around and through and into pieces of the mass — fragments of copper wire, shards of iron, flakes of corroded steel — and these metallic fragments are integrated into the organism's exoskeleton during molting, incorporated into the chitin matrix, and the result is a creature that is part animal and part machine and does not know the difference. They crawl along the functioning infrastructure — the cable runs, the electrical conduits, the pipe-work that still carries whatever it is that the pipe-work carries — and they *tend* it. They clear debris from conduit junctions. They reseat cables that have shifted. They polish contact surfaces. They do this with a specificity and a consistency that is either instinctive or intelligent, and you cannot tell which, and neither can they, because the distinction is an imposition of a taxonomy that does not apply here, and the maintenance-walkers operate in a space where instinct and intelligence are the same thing, which is to say they do what they do because they are what they are, and what they are is a creature that evolved in the presence of functioning machinery and has become adapted to it the way a woodpecker is adapted to bark. The machinery is their habitat. They are its immune system. The Aggregate built them, or they built the Aggregate, or the distinction between building and being built is a distinction that only exists in languages that require subjects and objects, and the Aggregate does not have a language, or if it does, its language does not have subjects and objects, and so the question does not arise.

## The Third Order

And then there are the larger things. The things that live in the middle layer, between your knees and your head, in the open air, visible, mobile, and *aware*.

You become aware of them the way you become aware of a person in a dark room — not by seeing them but by the quality of the darkness changing.

The first one you notice is a **crawler-clerk**. That is not its name. It has no name. But its behavior is so specific, so procedural, so *bureaucratic* that the name imposes itself. It is perhaps the length of your forearm, low-slung, with a body plan that is not unlike a horseshoe crab's — a broad, domed carapace at the front, tapering to a segmented tail at the rear — except the carapace is translucent, and through it you can see the organism's internal organs, and the internal organs are wrong. Not the organs you would expect in an arthropod — not the digestive caeca, not the Malpighian tubules, not the simplified nervous system of an insect — but a dense, packed, crystalline structure that is not tissue but *mineral*. The crawler-clerk has, in its thorax, a chambered nautilus-like structure made of iron pyrite and calcium phosphate, and the chambers are filled with a fluid that is not hemolymph but a solution of copper salts, and the whole structure is — it takes you a long time to realize this, standing in the stroboscopic light, watching the crawler-clerk move along the surface of the mass with a deliberation that is uncanny — a *brain*. Not an arthropod ganglion. A brain. A mineral brain. An organ of computation made of crystalline iron pyrite bathed in copper solution and embedded in an arthropod's body, and it is processing something, and the thing it is processing is the Aggregate itself.

The crawler-clerk moves along the mass, stopping at intervals. At each stop it extends a pair of antenna-like appendages from beneath its carapace and touches the surface of the mass — touches it the way a blind person touches a wall, reading it — and then it moves on, and where it has touched there is a faint mark, a discoloration, a trace of the copper-salt solution left on the surface like a chemical signature. It is *surveying*. It is mapping the Aggregate's internal structure, cataloguing the mass, recording where the layers are and what they contain and how they are degrading, and it is doing this because — because the Aggregate needs to know what it is made of, because the Aggregate is a system that is simultaneously a machine and an ecosystem and a geological formation, and systems like that need to monitor themselves or they collapse, and the crawler-clerks are the monitoring system, and they have been monitoring for so long that the maps they produce are not stored anywhere because the maps *are* the Aggregate, the way a memory is not stored in a neuron but in the pattern of connections between neurons, and the crawler-clerks are the connections, and the Aggregate is the pattern, and the pattern is the Aggregate, and you are standing inside it, and you are being catalogued. A crawler-clerk has stopped near your boot. It is touching the floor. It is touching the floor where you stepped, where the carpet-organisms are glowing brightest, where your weight displaced the substrate, and it is recording this, and you realize with a slowly dawning horror that the crawler-clerk is not afraid of you because the crawler-clerk does not need to be afraid of you because the crawler-clerk is part of a system that has absorbed larger disruptions than you and will absorb you too if you stay too long.

There are others.

**The long-things** — slender, filamental, like living wires, crawling through the tube-dwellers' tunnels and through every fissure and crevice in the mass, and their bodies are so thin and so flexible and so segmented that they can navigate the three-dimensional lattice of the Aggregate the way blood cells navigate capillaries, and they are the same color as the copper wiring in the walls, and they are drawn to electrical current the way real copper is, and they gather around the functioning fixtures in writhing clusters that look like — and might be — short circuits in the process of becoming organisms, or organisms in the process of becoming short circuits, and the light from the fixtures is not steady because the long-things are interfering with it, and the flicker is not a defect of the fixture but a *behavior* of the long-things, and they have been doing this for as long as the fixtures have functioned, and the fixtures have functioned for a very long time, and the long-things have been doing this for the same amount of time, and neither the fixtures nor the long-things know which of them came first.

**The carriers** — six-legged, box-bodied, the size of a fist, with a flat dorsal surface that collects dust and particulate and the bodies of smaller organisms that have died, and they carry these accumulations on their backs the way a town-builder carries materials, and they transport them from one part of the Aggregate to another, and they deposit them in specific locations — in fissures, in cracks, in the junctions between pieces of machinery — and the deposits become substrate, become foundation, become the bedrock of new layers, and the carriers are the Aggregate's construction crew, and they build the way a coral builds: not by design but by deposition, not by intention but by the accumulation of behavior, and the result is architecture, and the architecture is the Aggregate, and the carriers do not know they are building, and the building does not know it is being built, and the building is being built anyway, and it has been being built for so long that the word *built* has lost its meaning.

**The deep-things** — and these you do not see. You sense them. In the lowest levels of the mass, in the volcanic glass layer, in the compressed paper-shale, in the mineralized biological mat, there is motion that is not the motion of the carpet. It is slower. It is structural. It is the motion of things that have been moving through the deepest layers for so long that their movement *is* the layer. They are the oldest things in the Aggregate. They were here when the Aggregate was a crack in a wall. They were here before the building above it was built. They are the reason the Aggregate exists — not because they created it but because their presence, their metabolism, their slow mineral processing, created the conditions for accumulation, the way a worm creates the conditions for soil. They are the soil-creators. They are the primary producers. Everything above them — the carpet, the sieve-feeders, the maintenance-walkers, the crawler-clerks, the long-things, the carriers, the things you have not yet seen and may never see — all of it is built on their output, which is the deep mineral layer itself, which is the Aggregate's foundation, which is their waste product, which is their home, which is their body, which is the Aggregate, which is them.

## The Fourth Order

There is one more thing.

You find it in the highest part of the chamber, where the ceiling vaults into darkness, where the machinery is densest, where the functioning fixtures are closest together and the light is brightest and the long-things cluster thickest around the current. You have to climb to reach it — not climb, *press*, pressing your body upward through the mass, through the layers, past the sieve-feeders and the tube-dwellers' tunnels and the mummified infrastructure, and the air gets warmer up here, warmer by two or three degrees, because the electrical heat rises and accumulates here, and the long-things are so dense that you can feel them — not individually but as a medium, as a texture, the way you feel water when you swim, the gentle resistance of a medium occupied by many small things.

And in the center of the vault, in the densest part of the mass, where the machinery is oldest and the fixtures are brightest and the long-things converge — there is the **Aggregate's core**.

It is not a machine. It is not an organism. It is not a structure. It is a *concretion* — a dense, layered, spherical mass about the size of a human head, hanging from the ceiling by a cluster of cables and copper wire and the bodies of long-things that have fused to it over time, and its surface is covered in the same mineral deposits that cover everything here, and inside it — you can see this because the crawler-clerks have gathered around it and their copper-solution traces have made the surface briefly translucent — inside it there is structure. Not the random structure of accretion. *Organized* structure. Crystalline. Recursive. A structure that branches and folds and contains itself within itself the way a brain contains itself within itself, and it is processing, and you can hear it if you are quiet, if you stand very still and let the carpet-whisper recede to the edge of perception — a sound below the sound, a vibration more felt than heard, a hum that is not mechanical but *neural*, the hum of a mind thinking, and it is thinking about the Aggregate, and it is thinking about the mass, and it is thinking about the layers and the organisms and the infrastructure and the history of this place, and it is thinking about you.

Not with fear. Not with curiosity. Not with anything you have a word for.

With *integration*.

It is deciding where you fit. It is cataloguing your body the way the crawler-clerks catalogue the floor. It is measuring your thermal output, your chemical signature, the specific way your presence displaces air and particulate and carpet-organisms. It is adding you to its model. It is incorporating you into the Aggregate.

And the thing is — the thing that makes you want to leave, that makes you turn and press your body back through the fissures and the layers and the tunnels, back toward the rupture in the plaster, back toward the world — the thing is that it is not *wrong*. You do fit here. Everything fits here. The Aggregate has been making room for everything for longer than everything has existed, and it will make room for you too, and it will make room for you in the specific way that it makes room for everything: by pressing you against other things until you deform, and then pressing more things against you, and then more, and then more, and the shape it will find for you is the shape it finds for everything, which is: full.

You leave.

The last thing you see, before you turn sideways and press through the fissure and the cold air of the hallway hits your face and the plaster closes behind you, is the carpet glowing where you stood. A footprint of light. The shape of your boot, rendered in blue-white bioluminescence, already fading as the carpet-organisms fill in the disturbed surface, already being ground away, already being incorporated into the layers below.

In an hour there will be no trace of you.

In a year the crawler-clerks will have recorded the trace in their mineral memory, and the core will have integrated it into its model, and the disturbance you made to the system will have been processed and distributed and compressed into the stratigraphic record as a faint band of biological material — skin cells, hair, the keratin of a boot sole — between the mineralized mat and the granular composite.

In a thousand years it will be a layer. A band of you-material, pressed thin, fused to the surrounding matrix, part of the Aggregate, part of the mass, part of the floor that the carpet grinds and the tube-dwellers tunnel through and the sieve-feeders filter and the carriers carry.

You are already part of it. You were part of it the moment you fit through the fissure. The Aggregate does not let things leave. It lets things *go*, the way geology lets things go — by keeping them, by keeping everything, by never losing anything, by accreting, by being full, by being the place where everything ends up.

It was not built. It accreted.

You were not admitted. You were *absorbed*.

And the carpet is still glowing where you stood. And it is already fading. And the Aggregate does not notice that you are gone, because the Aggregate does not notice, because the Aggregate does not have the concept of notice, because the Aggregate *is*, and being is not the same as noticing, and the Aggregate has been being for a very long time, and it will be being for a very long time after, and you are the smallest part of it, and you are part of it, and it is part of you, and the plaster is solid again, and the wall is just a wall, and the building is just a building, and you walk away, and your lips are chalky, and your tongue is coated with copper, and you taste it for days.
