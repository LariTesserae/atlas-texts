# What Lives at the Rookery

## First, the Obvious

The birds.

They are what you notice because they are what you cannot help noticing, because they have made themselves into the acoustic weather of the place, because they have been here long enough to have become geography. But calling them *the birds* is like calling the ocean *some water.* It is technically accurate and entirely insufficient.

They are petrels. Or they are something that was petrels once, ten thousand generations ago, before the rookery made them into what they are now. They are the size of a fist — a human fist, closed, the knuckles forward — and they are the color of the stone they live on, which is to say they are the color of combustion, of something that has been burned and has survived the burning and wears the evidence. Soot-brown. But not uniformly. The brown has depths to it. There are individual feathers that are darker, individual feathers that are lighter, and the pattern they make is not random — it is the pattern of age, of wear, of a plumage that is replaced on a schedule that the bird's body keeps independently of any season, because there are no seasons here that matter. Each bird is a slightly different shade of the same brown, and after enough hours among them you begin to read those shades the way a forester reads rings — not as color but as history, as a record of how long this particular bird has been doing this particular thing in this particular place.

Their chests and bellies are white, but the white is not the white of snow or of cloud or of any clean thing. It is the white of guano. It is the white of their own waste, and the waste of their neighbors, and the waste of their ancestors, accumulated and dried and re-accumulated until the white is not a stain but a uniform, a livery, the outfit that every bird wears because every bird lives in the same conditions and the conditions have marked them all in the same way. You could not clean a petrel. You could not wash one and discover the true color of its chest. The guano has been there longer than the feathers it sits on. The white is as much a part of the bird as the brown.

Their eyes. You need to understand their eyes.

The eyes are dark — not black but *dark*, the way deep water is dark, the way a cave is dark, with a luminous quality that has nothing to do with actual light emission and everything to do with the structure of the eye itself. They are large for the skull. They are too large, almost, giving the birds a look of perpetual astonishment, of permanent wide-awakeness, as though the world has just done something to them that they are still processing. This is not an expression. This is anatomy. These are eyes built for the ocean, for the dim grey light of open water at dawn and dusk, for the business of spotting prey against the moving surface of a sea that is doing everything it can to hide that prey. The eyes have been built by a life spent between two darknesses — the dark of the ocean below and the dark of the sky at the elevation where these birds feed — and they have been built large and sensitive because in that gap between darknesses, every photon matters.

Up here, at the rookery, in the brutal unfiltered sunlight, the eyes are overqualified. They are too much. They take in more light than they need, and the birds squint, slightly, perpetually, a half-closing that is not relaxation but management, the best they can do with hardware designed for a different world. They look, with those half-closed too-large eyes, like they are thinking about something else. Like they are looking *through* the rookery at something far away that only they can see. And they are. They are looking at the ocean, which is where they feed, which is where their bodies are actually adapted to be, and the rookery is the place they come back to, the place where the next generation is produced, the place where the obligations of continuation are met. The rookery is their domestic life. The ocean is their real life. You can see this in their eyes.

Their bills are thin and hooked at the tip, and above the bill, the tubes — the paired nostrils that give the order its name, that make every tube-nosed bird look slightly unfinished, slightly abject, as though the face has been assembled from parts that don't quite integrate. The tubes are functional. They excrete salt. They are the reason a pelagic bird can drink seawater and survive — the excess salt is concentrated and expelled through these structures, which drip, constantly, a thin clear fluid that runs down the bill and falls or evaporates. At the rookery, this saltwater drip adds another texture to the stone, another faint wetness, another input to the system. The birds are not just living on the stone. They are *processing* the stone, altering its chemistry one drop at a time, and they have been doing this for so long that the stone is different from what it was. They have made their home by living in it.

They smell. This should be mentioned. The rookery has a smell, and the smell is the birds, and the birds smell like what they eat, which is fish and squid and the occasional crustacean, and the smell of digested fish and squid, dried and accumulated and re-wetted by condensation every morning, is a smell that is not entirely unpleasant but is entirely foreign to any olfactory vocabulary developed at lower elevations. It is not the smell of decay. It is the smell of *conversion* — of protein going through one organism and coming out as something else, of the chemical transformation that is the birds' primary ecological function. They eat the ocean and they deposit the ocean on the stone, and the stone absorbs it, and the mites process it, and the sedge grows in it, and the cycle is so complete that the smell is not a byproduct but a *feature* — the olfactory signature of a system in operation, the way that the hum of a functioning engine is not noise but evidence.

## What They Do

They call.

This is not a trivial statement. Calling is not a behavior they perform among other behaviors. It is not something they do in addition to living. Calling *is* their living, or at least the audible portion of it, the portion that exists in the frequency range that human ears can detect. They call while they sit. They call while they preen. They call while they feed their chicks. They call while they mate. They call while they sleep — or rather, they call while they rest, because sleep at the rookery is not the unconscious absence that sleep is for other animals. It is a lower-energy state, a dimming, a reduction in the intensity of calling that never reaches silence. They call with their eyes open. They call with their eyes closed. They call in such a way that the calling seems to be not a decision but a condition — the acoustic equivalent of breathing, something that happens because the bird is alive and is a bird and is at the rookery.

What the calling *means* — if meaning is the right word, and it probably isn't — is not knowable from observation. The birds do not call *to* each other in any way that a human observer can distinguish. They do not exchange information. They do not take turns. They do not respond to each other's calls in any pattern that suggests dialogue. They call *simultaneously*, and the aggregate of their calling is not a conversation but a *texture*, a sound-field that has no sender and no receiver, no message and no audience. It is possible that each individual call carries information for the individual bird's mate or chick — a location signal, a recognition signal, a statement of identity that is legible only to the one bird it is directed at and is lost in the general noise for everyone else. It is possible. But from where you stand, from any vantage point that a human body can occupy, the calls blend, and the blending is the point, and the point is that there is no point. The sound is the sound of the colony being a colony. It is the sound of togetherness, not as an emotion but as a physical fact, an acoustic proof of numbers.

They nest. The nests are not what a lower-altitude mind would call nests. There are no sticks, no grass, no constructed structures. The nests are *depressions* — shallow bowls worn into the guano-pitted stone by generations of birds sitting in the same spot, turning in the same spot, the friction of their bodies and the chemistry of their waste combining to sculpt the stone into a shape that fits a petrel's body the way a glove fits a hand. Each nest is the product of uncountable individual acts of occupation, each one adding a microscopic increment to the shape, and the shape is now stable, now fixed, now a feature of the stone that will outlast any individual bird and probably any individual generation. The birds do not choose their nests. They return to the nests they were hatched in, or to nests adjacent to those, or to nests that have been vacated by death in a pattern so orderly that it suggests not choice but *inheritance* — the passing down of a specific depression in a specific stone from one bird to the next, the way a house is passed down, the way a territory is passed down, the way a place becomes a family's place not by deed but by duration.

They mate. The mating is not visible as a distinct event. It occurs in the nests, among the calling, among the preening, among the feeding, and it is so thoroughly integrated into the general noise and motion of the colony that you cannot point to a moment and say *there, that is mating*. It is happening. It is always happening. The colony is in every stage of reproduction at all times — eggs in some nests, newly hatched chicks in others, near-fledged juveniles in others, adults preparing to lay in others — and the result is that reproduction is not a season but a *state*, a continuous process that has no peak and no trough, no beginning and no end. There are no displays. There are no courtship rituals that you can identify. The birds are past the need for courtship, if they ever had it. They have been at this rookery for so long that the elaborate behaviors of mate selection have been compressed, pared down, reduced to the essential act and the essential act alone. They mate because this is where mating happens and they are here and the mate is here and the nest is here and the system requires the next generation and the next generation requires the act and the act is performed.

They raise young. The chicks are visible — pale, downy, smaller than the adults, with the same too-large eyes but with bills that have not yet developed the full hook, with tubes that are not yet fully formed. They sit in the nests and they call, and their call is a higher version of the adults' call, a thinner sound that adds a frequency to the aggregate that is just barely distinguishable, a high, reedy overtone that sits on top of the main mass of sound like foam on a wave. They are fed. The adults return from the ocean with their crops full — the petrel's crop, an expandable pouch in the throat, is the transport mechanism, and the birds return heavy, their flight labored with the weight of partially digested fish and squid, and they land at the rookery and they find their chick — how, among thousands, among the identical nests and the identical birds and the identical calls, is a mystery that belongs to the birds and not to us — and they regurgitate, and the chick eats, and the adult leaves again.

The cycle — ocean, rookery, ocean, rookery — is the heartbeat of the place. It is the only rhythm in a system that otherwise has no rhythm. The birds come and go in streams, in funnels, in patterns that are dictated by the wind and the light and something internal that you cannot see, some knowledge of the ocean that is encoded in whatever part of a bird's brain still remembers how to find food in a featureless expanse of water. They leave in groups, and they return in groups, and the groups are of different sizes at different times, but the total number of birds at the rookery never changes appreciably. There are always approximately the same number of birds present. There are always approximately the same number absent. The system has found its equilibrium, and the equilibrium includes a ratio of present to absent that does not waver.

The birds do not notice you. This is important. This is perhaps the most important thing about them from a human perspective. You can stand among them — not in the nests, not on the nesting platforms, but among them, at the edge of the colony, close enough to see the individual feathers, the individual droppings, the individual drops of saltwater running down individual bills — and they do not react. They do not look at you. They do not call differently. They do not shift their weight or turn their bodies or in any way acknowledge that a new element has entered their environment. You are not a threat. You are not interesting. You are not anything. You are a large, warm, slow-moving object that has appeared in their visual field and that does not behave like a predator and does not behave like prey and does not behave like anything in their catalogue of things that matter, and so they do not allocate any attention to you. You are the landscape, as far as they are concerned. You are a rock that moves. You are weather.

This is not tameness. Tame implies the possibility of wildness, implies a choice that has been made not to flee. These birds have not chosen not to flee. They have not evaluated you and decided you are safe. They have simply not evaluated you at all. You are outside the set of things they respond to. You are irrelevant to the system, and the system does not acknowledge the irrelevant.

## The Chicks

The chicks deserve their own attention, because they are the part of the rookery that is most recognizably *alive* in the way that a lower-elevation mind understands alive. They are not yet part of the equilibrium. They are still in the process of becoming part of it, and the process is visible, and the visibility is moving in a way that nothing else here is moving.

The chicks are fat. They are preposterously, absurdly fat — a consequence of a metabolic strategy that prioritizes growth over all else, that takes the rich, oily food the adults bring back and converts it into body mass with an efficiency that would be alarming if it were not so plainly functional. A petrel chick at the peak of its development is a ball of pale gray down with a head and feet attached, the body so round and so dense that it seems impossible that the same species could produce the sleek, streamlined adult. The chick is a different animal. It is a creature whose entire purpose is to sit in the nest and convert food into size, and it does this with a dedication that borders on the mechanical. It eats. It grows. It eats. It grows. The adults feed it and it eats and it grows and the adults feed it more and it eats more and it grows more, and the nest — the shallow depression in the stone — becomes tighter around it, the stone pressing against the down, the chick pressing against the stone, and the chick does not care because the chick does not experience the world as a set of conditions to be cared about. The chick experiences the world as food and not-food, as warm and not-warm, as the presence of a parent and the absence of a parent, and everything else is outside its cognition.

The chicks call. Their call is the high, reedy version of the adult call — a thinner sound, a less resonant sound, the sound of a smaller instrument playing the same note. But there is something in the chick's call that is not in the adult's, or that has been lost from the adult's, and it takes time to identify it. It is *urgency*. The chicks call with urgency. The adults call with the flat, mechanical persistence of a system in steady state. The chicks call with the desperate, demanding insistence of a system that is not yet in steady state, that is still building, still accumulating, still trying to become. Their calls are higher, sharper, more variable. They rise and fall. They have a rhythm that the adult calls lack. They sound, to a human ear, like something that needs something, and the adults sound like something that doesn't.

This is the only sound at the rookery that reads as *alive* in the emotional sense. It is the only sound that triggers recognition, that makes you think *yes, I know what that is, that is hunger, that is want, that is the sound of a small thing needing a big thing.* It is the only sound here that has an inside.

## The Arthropods

The life you cannot see is the life that matters most, in the sense that without it the whole structure would collapse. The birds are the visible organism. The arthropods are the hidden one. They are the reason the guano does not bury the rookery. They are the reason the stone is not buried under centuries of accumulated waste. They are the reason the system remains a system and does not become a pile.

They are mites. They are springtails. They are a species of insect — a small, wingless, translucent creature that has no common name and that exists nowhere else, that has been here long enough to have become endemic, to have adapted to the specific chemistry of petrel guano at this elevation and to the specific temperature range and to the specific moisture regime and to nothing else, so that it is not just a species that lives here but a species that *is* here, that is of this place in a way that the birds, who spend most of their lives at sea, are not.

The mites are barely visible. They are specks. They are the size of the period at the end of a sentence, and they are the same color as the guano they live in, which means they are effectively invisible, which means that the surface of the rookery, which looks like pitted stone with a white crust, is actually a living carpet, a seething, shifting, processing carpet of organisms that are eating and excreting and reproducing and dying at a rate that the scale of their bodies makes imperceptible but that is, in aggregate, the metabolic engine of the entire place.

They do what decomposers do everywhere. They break down. They take the complex organic compounds of the guano — the proteins, the lipids, the uric acids, the calcium, the phosphorus — and they convert them, through the brute chemistry of digestion, into simpler compounds that can be transported, that can be washed by condensation into the cracks where the sedge grows, that can be broken down further by bacteria and fungi into the mineral components that the stone itself can absorb. They are the middle of a chain that goes from bird to mite to microbe to stone, and the chain is unbroken, and the chain has been unbroken for longer than any participant in it can know.

The springtails are slightly larger and slightly more visible. They are there if you look closely — if you crouch at the edge of a nesting platform and press your face to the stone and wait for your eyes to adjust to the scale of what you are looking at. They are there, in the transition zone between the guano-white of the active nests and the stone-grey of the uninhabited margins, in the narrow band where the organic input is not enough to support a full mite population but is enough to support something. They are there, and they are doing something that is not quite the same as what the mites are doing — they are grazing, not decomposing, eating something that is growing on the surface of the guano, something fungal, something bacterial, something that is itself alive and is itself feeding on the guano and is itself being eaten. The springtails are a predator in this world. They are the largest animals in the microsystem, and they hunt by the standards of the microsystem, which means they move slowly, imperceptibly, through a landscape that to them is vast and to you is a surface.

If you press your ear to the stone — and you will, because you have been told that there is a sound, and you want to hear it, and the wanting is enough to make you press your ear to the cold, guano-crusted stone of a rookery at the roof of the world — if you do this, you will hear something. Or you will almost hear something. A dryness. A crackling, like the sound of paper being crumpled at great distance, like the sound of leaves being stepped on in another room. It is the sound of exoskeletons on a granular substrate. It is the sound of mites and springtails going about their lives, and it is so small that it exists at the very bottom of the audible range, in the place where sound becomes touch, where vibration becomes pressure, where the distinction between hearing and feeling dissolves. You feel it more than you hear it. You feel it in the bone behind your ear, in the cartilage of your jaw, in the places where your skull meets the stone and the stone meets the life that is living on it.

## The Ticks

The ticks are harder to love.

They are there because the birds are there, and they are there in the way that parasites are there — not as an accident but as a feature, not as a problem but as a participant. They are hard ticks, the kind that feed on a single host and then drop off and wait for the next, and they have been feeding on these petrels for as long as these petrels have been coming here, and the relationship is not one of host and parasite in the adversarial sense but of two species that have accommodated each other, that have reached a détente, that have found a level of mutual tolerance that allows both to persist.

The ticks live in the crevices around the nesting sites. They live in the narrow gaps between the stone and the guano, in the spaces that are warm from the birds' bodies and that retain a trace of the condensation's moisture, in the micro-environment that the birds have created by their presence and that the ticks have exploited by their presence. They are there in numbers that are difficult to process. They are there in the way that grains of sand are on a beach — individually uncountable, collectively unignorable, a population that exists at a density that would be terrifying if the ticks were larger, if they were the size of something you could see clearly without crouching, but at their actual size — two millimeters, three, the size of a poppy seed — the density is merely a fact, a feature of the landscape, a thing that the stone has in addition to its other qualities.

The ticks wait. This is what they do. They wait for warmth, for the specific thermal signature of a petrel's body — the exact temperature, the exact rate of heat transfer, the exact signature that tells the tick's rudimentary nervous system that a host is present and is the right host and is worth the investment of climbing onto. When a bird settles into a nest, the ticks around that nest activate. They move toward the heat source with a slow, deliberate motion that is not much faster than the motion of the mites but that has a directionality to it, a purpose, that the mites' random foraging lacks. They climb the stone. They climb the guano. They climb the bird. They find a spot — under the wing, in the featherless tract of the neck, in the gap between the thigh and the belly — and they attach, and they feed, and when they are full, they drop off, and they return to the crevice, and they wait.

The birds do not mind the ticks. Or rather, the birds do not respond to the ticks in any way that suggests distress or discomfort or even awareness. The ticks are a cost. They are a tax. They are the price of living in a place where the conditions for nesting are perfect and the conditions for not being parasitized are impossible, and the petrels have been paying this tax for so long that it has been incorporated into the cost of doing business. A bird with ten ticks on it is not a stressed bird. It is a bird with ten ticks on it, and it calls the same as a bird with no ticks, and it flies the same, and it feeds its chick the same, and the tick burden is just another input, just another variable in a life that has no surplus and no deficit, that has exactly enough and no more.

The ticks are not only on the birds. They are on the stone. They are on the ground around the nests. They are on your boots, if you stand still long enough, and this is the moment — the only moment — when the rookery acknowledges your existence, when the system reaches out and touches you with its smallest, most intimate hand. A tick climbs your boot. It reaches your ankle. It explores the skin above your sock with the same slow, deliberate purposefulness that it uses to explore a petrel's belly, and it is looking for warmth, and it has found warmth, and it does not know that you are not a petrel and it does not care. You are warm. You will do.

You flick it off. Another takes its place. You flick that one off. The rookery does not notice.

## The Falcon

The falcon lives lower on the slope. This is important. The falcon is not *of* the rookery. The falcon is *at* the rookery, adjacent to it, exploiting it, but the falcon's home is elsewhere — a crack in the cliff face, a hundred meters below the lowest nest, a place where the stone is vertical and the wind is different and the conditions are those of a raptor's life, not a petrel's. The falcon is an outsider. The falcon is the only creature here that has a perspective on the rookery that is not the rookery's own perspective on itself. The falcon sees it from outside. The falcon sees it as a resource.

And the falcon is magnificent. There is no other word. In a landscape where everything is gray and brown and white and the colors are muted and the light is stark and the forms are worn, the falcon is a piece of a different world. It is large — larger than the petrels by a factor of four, with a wingspan that seems to belong to a different scale of thing, a different category of animal. It is dark — a deep, slate-gray that is almost black, that absorbs light in a way that the petrels' plumage does not, that makes it look solid where the petrels look hollow. It has the raptor's build: the heavy shoulders, the hooked beak that is a weapon and not a tool, the talons that are visible even in flight, trailing below the body like dropped gear.

The falcon hunts at the edges. This is the key to its strategy, and the strategy is elegant in the way that all predation strategies are elegant when they have been refined by enough time. The falcon does not enter the rookery. It does not fly into the mass of petrels, does not attempt to take a bird from the nesting platform, does not challenge the colony on its own ground. The colony is too dense, too alert in the aggregate even if individuals are not alert, too full of wings and beaks and the confusion of numbers. The falcon would be mobbed. The falcon would be driven off. The colony has a defense, and the defense is its size, and the falcon respects the defense.

Instead, the falcon hunts the margins. It hunts the birds that are returning late, that are coming in singly or in pairs, that are tired from the ocean flight, that are heavy with food for their chicks, that are at the moment of maximum vulnerability — the transition between the freedom of the air and the safety of the nest, the moment when the bird is committed to landing but has not yet landed, when its speed is reduced and its attention is on the nest and not on the sky. The falcon comes from below, using the slope as a screen, and it comes fast — faster than the petrel can react, faster than the petrel can turn, faster than the warning that the petrel's nervous system can process — and the strike is a sound like a hand clapping, like two stones being struck together, a sharp, percussive impact that is over before it is heard.

The falcon takes the petrel in the air. It hits it with its talons, and the petrel's flight stops, and the petrel falls, and the falcon catches it — a second contact, softer than the first — and carries it away, down, to the crack in the cliff where the falcon lives, and the petrel is gone.

The rookery does not react.

This is the part that is hardest to understand, and it is the part that matters most. The rookery does not react. The falcon takes a petrel from the edge of the colony, and a thousand petrels see it happen, and not one of them changes its behavior. Not one of them pauses in its calling. Not one of them lifts its head or turns toward the sound or in any way indicates that a member of the colony has just been killed. The calling continues at the same volume, the same frequency, the same unbroken, undifferentiated wall of sound. The birds on the nesting platforms do not flinch. The birds returning from the ocean do not alter their approach. The chicks do not fall silent. Nothing changes, because nothing has changed. One bird out of thousands has been removed, and the system's response to the removal of one bird out of thousands is no response at all. The loss is below the threshold. The loss is within the noise.

The falcon takes what it takes. The colony absorbs the loss. The falcon does not threaten the colony. The falcon is not a danger to the colony. The falcon is a *cost*, the way the ticks are a cost, the way the wind is a cost, the way the cold is a cost. The colony has factored the falcon into its budget, and the budget balances, and there is no need for alarm.

The falcon's cry, when it gives one, is a sound that exists on a different plane from the rookery's sound. It is a single note, descending, sharp, a sound that seems to be made of a different material than the petrels' calling — harder, colder, a sound that does not blend, that does not join the aggregate, that cuts through it the way a blade cuts through fabric. It is the only sound at the rookery that sounds like it *means* something. The petrels' calling is continuous and meaningless and beautiful in its meaninglessness. The falcon's cry is discrete and meaningful and lonely in its meaning. It is the sound of a solitary animal that lives by taking from a community and that is not part of the community and that knows it is not part. It is the sound of the outside.

You hear it once. Maybe twice. It is enough.

## The Microbes

There is life below the life. There is life below the mites, below the springtails, below the ticks, below the arthropods that are themselves below the birds. There is life in the guano itself, in the stone beneath the guano, in the interface between the organic and the mineral, and this life is microbial and it is the foundation on which everything else is built.

You cannot see it. You can infer it. You can infer it from the fact that the guano does not accumulate, from the fact that the stone is pitted and eroded in patterns that chemical weathering alone does not explain, from the fact that the air at the rookery has a smell that is not the smell of fresh guano but the smell of *processing* — of decomposition, of transformation, of organic compounds being broken into their components by organisms that are too small to see but are not too small to matter. The microbes are there. They are bacteria. They are fungi. They are the things that exist at the boundary between life and chemistry, the things that are not quite one and not quite the other, and they are doing the work that makes everything else possible.

They are breaking down the uric acid. They are breaking down the proteins. They are converting the complex, nitrogen-rich compounds of bird waste into the simpler forms that the mites can process further, that the springtails can graze on, that the sedge can absorb through its roots. They are the first step in a chain of steps that leads from the bird to the stone, and without this first step, the chain does not exist, and without the chain, the guano accumulates, and the guano buries the nests, and the nests are lost, and the colony has nowhere to nest, and the rookery fails.

The microbes are the most important thing here, and they are the thing you will never see, and they are the thing that the rookery is most truly about. The rookery is not about the birds. The birds are the visible structure. The rookery is about the invisible process — the conversion of life into stone, the transformation of the organic into the mineral, the slow, patient, invisible work of taking what is alive and making it part of what is not. The microbes do this. The mites continue it. The sedge participates in it. The stone is the product of it. And the birds — the ten thousand, the hundred thousand, the however-many-they-are — are the engine, the input, the source of the raw material that the process converts.

## The Lichen

And then there is the lichen. The lichen in the wider cracks, the foliose lichen, the bone-white rosettes that cup the morning dew. The lichen is the only thing here that seems to have a strategy, that seems to be doing something other than what it is programmed to do, that seems to be making a choice.

The lichen is not a single organism. This is worth saying, because the lichen is the most complex life form at the rookery after the birds and the falcon, and it is not even one thing. It is a partnership. It is a fungus and an alga — or in some cases a fungus and a cyanobacterium — living in a symbiosis so intimate that the resulting organism is classified as a single species, even though it is two. The fungus provides the structure, the body, the rosette shape, the cup that holds the water. The alga provides the food, the photosynthesis, the ability to turn light into sugar. Together, they are something that neither could be alone, and the something they are is a thing that can live in a crack in a rock at the roof of the world, in a place where the moisture comes for three hours a day and the light is too bright and the wind is too strong and the temperature is too low, and can not only live but persist, not only persist but maintain, not only maintain but — in its quiet, uncompetitive way — thrive.

The lichen's rosettes are perfect. This is the thing about them that strikes you most. They are geometrically regular, radiating from a center point, with ridges and folds that channel water inward, toward the center, toward the place where the alga is densest and the photosynthesis is most active. They are engineered. Not by a mind — the lichen has no mind, the lichen has no brain, the lichen has no nervous system — but by the blind, patient, iterative process of natural selection, which has been working on this form for as long as it has been working on any form, and which has arrived at a shape that is optimal for the conditions, and the conditions are: three hours of moisture, twelve hours of light, constant wind, and a crack in the rock that is a finger's width wide.

The lichen does not grow. Or rather, it grows so slowly that growth is not the right word. It *accretes*. It adds to itself at a rate that is measured in millimeters per century, a rate that makes the stone seem dynamic by comparison, a rate that is almost geological in its patience. The rosettes you see have been there for decades. Some of them have been there for longer than you have been alive. They were here before the trail was a trail. They were here before the birds were here, probably — the lichen does not depend on the birds, does not need the guano, does not benefit from the nitrogen or the phosphorus that the birds bring. The lichen needs only light and water and the minerals that the stone provides, and it has been getting those from the crack in the rock for longer than the birds have been nesting on the rock, and it will be here after the birds are gone, if the birds ever go, which they probably won't, because the birds are as much a part of this place as the stone is, and the stone is not going anywhere.

The lichen is the oldest thing here. Not the oldest individual — the individual rosettes are decades old, maybe a century, not more — but the oldest *form*. The lichen as a way of being, as a strategy for existing, has been here longer than anything else. It was here before the birds found the place. It was here before the sedge colonized the cracks. It was here when the rock was newly exposed, when the surface was fresh, when the joints were just forming, and it colonized those joints at the first moment they were habitable, and it has been here ever since, and it will be here when the rest of the system is gone. The lichen is the last thing. The lichen is the thing that will be here when the birds have moved on, when the sedge has been abraded away, when the mites and the ticks and the springtails have found other hosts or other food or have gone extinct. The lichen will be here. The lichen does not need the rookery. The lichen was here before the rookery. The lichen is the ground on which the rookery was built, and it will be the ground that remains.

## The Unseen

There is something else here. Not an organism. Not a species. Something that is present but not alive, or not alive in the way that the birds are alive or the mites are alive or the lichen is alive. It is the thing that makes the rookery what it is, the thing that is more important than any of the individual lives that inhabit it, the thing that you can feel after enough hours in the sound and the wind and the cold.

It is the system itself.

The rookery is not a collection of organisms. It is not a list of species. It is not a set of populations that happen to share a location. It is a single thing — a system, a structure, an entity that has properties that none of its components have individually, properties that emerge from the interaction of those components, from the relationships between them, from the flows of energy and material that connect them. The birds eat the ocean and deposit the ocean on the stone. The microbes break down the deposit. The mites process the breakdown. The sedge grows in the processed product. The lichen holds the water that the sedge needs. The ticks feed on the birds. The falcon feeds on the birds. The stone holds it all. The wind shapes it all. The light drives it all. And the whole thing — the whole vast, loud, stinking, teeming, motionless, ancient thing — is one organism.

It breathes. You can feel it breathing. The birds leave in the morning and the rookery exhales — the population drops, the sound diminishes, the mass of living things on the stone decreases. The birds return in the evening and the rookery inhales — the population rises, the sound swells, the mass increases. The condensation forms and the rookery is wet. The sun burns it off and the rookery is dry. The system has a respiration. It has a pulse. It has a cycle that is not the cycle of any individual species but is the cycle of the whole, the combined rhythm of every life form doing what it does at the time it does it, and the combined rhythm is a rhythm, is a beat, is a breathing that you can feel in the stone if you press your ear to it and your body to it and you let the sound enter you not through your ears but through your bones.

The rookery is alive. Not the way a bird is alive. Not the way a mite is alive. The way a forest is alive, the way a reef is alive, the way a grassland is alive — as a system, as a collective, as a thing that has no central nervous system and no single identity but that nonetheless functions, that nonetheless persists, that nonetheless has a character and a behavior and a history that are more than the sum of its parts. The rookery is alive, and it has been alive for longer than any of the individual lives within it, and it will be alive after they are gone, and it is alive in a way that does not require any of the things that we usually think of as requirements for life — it does not grow, it does not reproduce, it does not evolve in the way that species evolve. It simply *is*, and the *is* is enough, and the *is* is what you feel when you stand in the sound and the wind and the cold and you understand that you are not looking at a collection of birds on a rock but at a living thing that is ten thousand years old and that does not know you are there.

## What It Is Like to Be a Petrel at the Rookery

This is not knowable. But it is possible to imagine, and the imagining is worth doing, because the imagining is the closest you will come to understanding what lives here.

You are a petrel. You are at the rookery. You are sitting in a depression in the stone that fits your body the way your body fits your skin — perfectly, without gap, without pressure, the stone and the feather and the flesh in a continuous contact that is the closest thing you have to comfort. The stone is cold. It is always cold. You do not experience the cold as a problem. You experience it as information — the information that you are on the stone, that you are at the nest, that you are in the place where you are supposed to be. The cold is home. The cold is the sensation of being in the right place.

You are calling. You are not calling because you decided to call. You are not calling because you hear the other birds calling and are responding. You are calling because calling is what your body does when your body is at the rookery, the way your body breathes when your body is alive. The calling is not separate from you. It is you. It is the audible portion of your existence at the rookery, the part of your being-here that enters the air and joins the air and becomes part of the sound that is the place. You do not hear the other birds as individual sounds. You hear them as the sound, as the totality, as the acoustic environment that you are inside and that is inside you, and you do not distinguish yourself from it any more than a wave distinguishes itself from the sea.

Your chick is beneath you. Or beside you. Or in the nest adjacent to you. It is calling too, and its calling is a higher frequency, and you can hear it, and hearing it is not a cognitive event but a physiological one — a tightening in the crop, a shift in the posture, a readiness to regurgitate that is triggered by the specific frequency of your specific chick's specific call, which you can identify among thousands not because you are smart but because your body has been calibrated by evolution to respond to exactly this frequency and no other. Your chick calls and your body prepares. This is the extent of your parental decision-making. The call triggers the preparation. The preparation triggers the feeding. The feeding triggers the satiation. The satiation triggers the departure. The departure triggers the ocean flight. The ocean flight triggers the feeding. The feeding triggers the return. The return triggers the calling. The calling triggers the chick. The cycle is the cycle, and you are inside it, and you have always been inside it, and there is no outside.

You do not think. This is not a deficiency. This is not a lack. You do not think because thinking is not what you are for. You are for the cycle. You are for the ocean and the rookery and the calling and the chick and the guano and the stone. You are for the system, and the system does not need your thoughts, and your thoughts — if you had them, which you don't — would be redundant, would be a duplication of what the system already does without them. You are a component. You are a very sophisticated, very beautiful, very well-adapted component, and you are doing what components do, which is function, and your function is calling and feeding and breeding and dying, and you have been doing it for your whole life, and your parents did it for their whole lives, and their parents did it for theirs, and the chain is unbroken, and the chain does not need to know that it is a chain in order to be a chain.

You will die. You do not know this. You will die at the rookery, or on the ocean, or in the talons of the falcon, or on the stone with your body gradually becoming part of the guano that the mites will process and the sedge will grow in and the stone will absorb. You will die and your chick will live, or your chick will die and you will live, and either way the system will continue, and the system will not notice, and the not-noticing is not cruelty and not indifference and not neglect. It is the nature of the system, which is a nature that does not include noticing, that does not require noticing, that has been operating for ten thousand years without noticing and will operate for ten thousand more without noticing, and the not-noticing is part of what makes it work, because if the system noticed — if the system could register the death of an individual, if the system could mourn — the system would be overwhelmed by the weight of its mourning, would be crushed by the accumulated grief of ten thousand years of individual deaths, and it would stop, and it would fail, and it would be gone.

The system does not mourn. The system calls. The calling is the opposite of mourning. The calling is the assertion that the system is still running, that the components are still functioning, that the cycle is still cycling, that the living are still living and the dying are still dying and the whole thing is still whole. The calling is the sound of a system that does not know it is a system and does not need to know it is a system and is, in its not-knowing, more complete and more perfect and more functional than any system that has ever been designed by a mind that knows it is designing.

## The Rookery Itself

So what lives here?

Everything lives here. Everything that can live here lives here, and the things that live here are the things that have found a way to make a living in a place that gives almost nothing and takes almost nothing and holds its inhabitants in a balance so precise that it looks, from outside, like nothing is happening, when in fact everything is happening, all the time, at full capacity, without pause, without variation, without end.

The birds live here. The mites live here. The springtails live here. The ticks live here. The microbes live here. The lichen lives here. The sedge lives here. The falcon lives here, adjacent. The stone lives here, if stone can be said to live, if the slow, patient processes of weathering and erosion and chemical change can be called a kind of life, which they can, which they should be, because the stone is changing, the stone is being altered, the stone is being shaped by the birds and the guano and the mites and the water, and change is the only definition of life that matters, and the stone is changing.

The wind lives here. The wind is alive in the way that the stone is alive — not biologically, not organically, but as a presence, as a force, as a thing that is always present and always acting and always shaping the conditions under which the other lives are lived. The wind is the medium. The wind is what the sound travels through. The wind is what the sedge bends in. The wind is what the falcon uses to mask its approach. The wind is the thing that makes the rookery what it is, because without the wind the sound would be different and the moisture would be different and the birds would be different and the system would not be the system.

The sound lives here. The sound is alive in the way that the wind is alive and the stone is alive — as a phenomenon, as a presence, as a thing that has duration and character and history. The sound has been happening for ten thousand years. The sound is the oldest thing at the rookery, older than any individual bird, older than any nest, older than any lichen rosette. The sound predates the current configuration of the stone. The sound will outlast the current configuration of the stone. The sound is the rookery's voice, and the rookery has been speaking in that voice for longer than any of the individual throats that produce it have been alive, and the voice is the thing, and the thing is the voice, and they are the same.

What lives here is a system that is so old and so stable and so complete that it has become indistinguishable from the landscape it occupies. The birds are the landscape. The guano is the landscape. The mites are the landscape. The sound is the landscape. They are not living *in* the landscape. They *are* the landscape. They are the landscape the way that a forest is a landscape, the way that a reef is a landscape, the way that a glacier is a landscape — not as inhabitants but as components, not as tenants but as the material itself, the stuff of which this particular place is made.

The rookery is not a place where birds live. The rookery is a living place. The distinction matters. A place where birds live is a place that exists independently of the birds, a place that was there before the birds and will be there after the birds, a place that the birds occupy but do not define. The rookery is not that. The rookery is the birds. The rookery is the guano and the mites and the stone and the lichen and the sedge and the falcon and the wind and the sound, all of them together, all of them interdependent, all of them necessary, all of them parts of a whole that would not exist without any of them and that has no name except the name of the place, and the name of the place is the rookery, and the rookery is what lives here.

It is the loudest living thing you have ever encountered. It is the stillest living thing you have ever encountered. It is alive in a way that does not require growth, that does not require change, that does not require any of the narratives that we usually use to identify life. It is alive the way a mountain is alive, the way a river is alive, the way a star is alive — by existing, by persisting, by being a thing that is and that continues to be and that does not need to become anything else because it is already complete, already finished, already the thing it was going to be when it started, and it has been this thing for longer than anything alive can remember, and it will be this thing for longer than anything alive can imagine, and the sound it makes is the sound of being, and the sound is continuous, and the sound is what you hear, and the sound is what lives here.
