# What Lives Here

## The People

They are not what you expect. That is the first thing. You expect asceticism — thin, quiet, inward. You expect a people shaped by scarcity, by cold, by the reduction of the world to stone and water. You do not entirely get this.

They are short. Not unusually so — not a pygmy people, not a dwarfed population — but shorter than the mean, by perhaps ten or twelve centimetres, and it takes a while to understand why. It is not diet, or not only diet. It is heat. The plateau is cool, always cool, and the body conserves. The body draws inward. A smaller body loses less. Generations of this, and the people reflect it: compact, thick through the chest, with hands that are broad and slightly oversized for their frames, hands that can grip wet stone without slipping, hands that can hold a chisel in cold air for hours. Their feet are wide, flat, splayed on the wet rock with a certainty that is not learned but grown. They do not slip. You slip, on the same stone, in the same wet. They do not slip.

Their skin is dark. Not the darkness of latitude — they are not at a low latitude, the plateau is too high and too cool for the sun to mark them that way — but a darkness that is something else. A greyness. Their skin has a quality of stone about it, a dark grey-brown that is not quite any color you have a name for. It is not pigment alone. It is something in the texture. Their skin is slightly rough. Not scaled, not cracked, but with a quality of roughness that catches the wet air and holds it, so that in the mist their faces look like the faces of the cliffs — darkened by moisture, sheened, the features visible but softened. They are not cold to the touch, or not colder than anyone else in this air. But they feel like something that belongs to the rock.

Their eyes are the thing you notice last and remember longest. They are pale. Not blue, not grey, but a pale that is almost the absence of color, a washed-out clarity that is striking against the dark of their skin. In the flat light of the plateau, their eyes seem to gather what little light there is. When the rain comes and the world contracts to the space inside the rainfall, their eyes are the brightest thing. They do not reflect the way a cat's eyes reflect. They simply hold light when there is light to hold, and when there is no light, they go dark, and the face they are set in goes dark with them, and the person becomes a shape on the stone that you lose at twenty metres.

They wear wool. This is unexpected, because there are no sheep here, no goats, no fiber animals of any kind. But they wear wool — or something so like wool that the distinction is technical. It is the rush grass. The thin, tough rush that grows in the wet depressions and the margins of the pools is harvested, dried, and somehow processed into a thread that is rough but warm. The garments are simple: loose, layered, the color of the stone or the color of the moss, undyed or dyed in mineral pigments — iron-red, calcium-white, manganese-black — so that a person standing still against a cliff is nearly invisible. They wear hoods. They wear wraps around the neck that can be pulled up over the mouth and nose when the wind comes in cold from the sides. They wear sandals of stone — thin, flat soles of the tuff, cut to the shape of the foot and held on with rush-grass cord — and these click softly on the rock when they walk, a sound that is almost the sound of water, of drops on stone, and you wonder if this is deliberate.

It is.

## Their Number

There are perhaps two hundred of them. Maybe fewer. It is hard to count because they do not gather all in one place, and because the city — which was built for more, built for perhaps five hundred, built for a time when the water was more plentiful or the people were more numerous or both — has the quality of a thing that has more rooms than it needs. You walk through it and you count dwellings and you count hearths and you count cisterns and you arrive at a number, and then you realize that half of what you counted is maintained but empty, and the number drops.

They are contracting. Not dying — the population is stable, or near-stable, there are children, there are old people, the demographic shape is whole — but contracting in the way that a footprint contracts when the foot grows smaller. The city was built for a certain size of community and the community is now smaller than that, and the extra structures are kept standing, kept roofed, kept clear of debris and channelled and ready, but unoccupied. They are not ruins. They are rooms waiting.

You see a child in a doorway. The child is perhaps four years old. It is watching you — or watching something near you, perhaps the lichen you are standing near — with those pale eyes, and it is not afraid of you, and it is not curious about you. It is watching the way the birds watch: with an attention that is not personal. You are not a person to this child. You are a thing on the stone. The child is waiting to see what the thing does.

The child's mother, or some adult, appears behind it and speaks to it — a sound so quiet it is almost a gesture, almost a touch, almost nothing — and the child turns and goes inside, and the adult looks at you for a moment with those pale stone-eyes and then also goes inside, and the door-flap moves in the wind and you are alone in the street, if street is the word for a channel of water cut into rock between two rows of stone buildings on a high plateau where it is about to rain.

## What They Eat

They do not eat much. This is clear from their bodies, which are lean but not wasted — the leanness of people who eat enough but not more than enough, who have never had surplus and have never had famine, who exist in a narrow band between sufficiency and scarcity that has shaped them the way the water has shaped the stone: slowly, continuously, without event.

They eat the algae. The blue-green film on the surface of the pools is skimmed off with flat stone tools — thin, broad, the shape of a hand but larger — and collected into stone vessels, and left to dry in the mornings when the air is clear and cold. It dries into a thin sheet, dark green-black, with a texture like paper but heavier. It is eaten as is, or reconstituted in water and mixed with other things. It tastes of minerals. It tastes of the rock. It is not good, by any standard of good that you bring to it, but it is not repulsive. It is the taste of the place itself, concentrated.

They eat the insects. The small, dark, flightless insects that live in the rush mats are collected by hand, by the children and the old people, who sit at the edge of the wet depressions and pull apart the rush tufts and pick out the insects and drop them into stone bowls. The insects are roasted on flat stones placed near the hearth — not on the fire, but near it, in the warmth — and they crisp and darken and are eaten whole or ground into a paste with water and algae. This is the closest thing they have to a staple. The paste is dense, slightly oily, and it keeps for days in the cold air.

They eat the lizards. The lizards are caught with patience, not speed. A person sits near a crack in the rock where the lizards emerge after rain, and waits, and when the lizard comes out and positions itself on the wet stone to warm, the person takes it. Quickly. With the hand. The lizard is killed with a pressure behind the skull — a practiced, specific gesture — and is cleaned and cooked. Lizard is rare. It is not everyday food. It is food for the evening, for the time after the rain when the hearth is lit and the family gathers and the meal is something more than sustenance. The lizard is the thing they have instead of feast.

They do not eat the birds. This is important, and it is not explained to you, because nothing is explained to you, but it is visible in what they do not do. They do not hunt the birds. They do not trap them. They do not collect their eggs, if the birds lay eggs — you do not know where the birds nest, and neither, you suspect, do the people. The birds circle above the city and above the plateau and the people do not look up at them. Not because they are indifferent. Because the birds are not food. They are something else. They are the thing that is above, the way the stone is the thing that is below, and the water is the thing that is between, and the people are the thing that is in the middle of all of it, and the birds are not to be eaten. This is a rule. You do not learn it. You read it in the absence.

They eat something else, something you do not see at first, something that is not of the plateau. In one of the buildings — one of the occupied ones, near a primary seep — there is a room with a low ceiling and a floor of flat stone, and on the floor, in shallow stone trays, there is a substance that is pale and granular, like a coarse flour. It is not made from anything on the plateau. It comes from below. The plateau has an edge, and below the edge is a different landscape — lower, warmer, where things grow that do not grow up here — and the people go down. Not often. Not all of them. But some of them, sometimes, go down the escarpment, down to where the air is thicker and the soil is deeper and things grow that are not stone and moss and rush, and they bring things back. The pale flour is one of these things. It is mixed with water and baked on a hearthstone into a flat bread that is the closest thing they have to luxury. It is not everyday food. It is food for the morning, for the clear cold time before the rain, eaten in small pieces, slowly, with the algae paste, and the eating of it is a thing done with attention, with the particular quality of attention that people give to things that are rare.

## How They Live

The day is shaped by the weather, and the weather is shaped by the plateau, and the people are shaped by both. They wake early, in the cold dark before dawn, and the first sound is the sound of water — the channels, the pools, the seeps — and the first thing they do is listen. Not to the water in the channels, which is constant, but to the water in the stone. The weeps. The seeps. The sound the cliff makes. They can hear, in the sound of water emerging from rock, something about the weather that is coming. The volume changes with pressure. The mineral content changes the pitch. You cannot hear this. They can. They listen to the cliff the way a sailor reads the wind, and they know what the day will bring before the day has begun.

Mornings are for work. The work is stone. The cutting and shaping of tuff, the fitting of blocks, the carving of channels, the maintenance of the water infrastructure that is the city's skeleton. They work with stone tools — harder stone for softer stone, basalt for tuff — and the work is slow and precise and has been happening for so long that the tools are not tools but extensions of the hand, shaped to the hand, worn to the hand, passed from generation to generation until the grip of the ancestor is still in the shape of the handle. A chisel found on a ledge is not a chisel. It is a handprint in stone.

The carving of channels is the primary craft. This is what they do that is more than maintenance, more than repair. The channels are the city's expression, its art, if that word applies. The channels are cut with a precision that is not functional — the water does not need a channel to be precise to fifteen degrees of angle, does not need the inner walls to be smoothed to a polish — but the channels are precise. The inner walls are polished. The angles are exact. The junctions where one channel meets another are curved, not angled, so that the water does not turbulence but flows smoothly, and the curves are different in different parts of the city — wider here, tighter there, with a variation that is not structural but stylistic, and you realize, standing over a channel junction and looking down at the water moving through a curve that has been carved with the attention of a sculptor, that this is what they do instead of everything else. This is their sculpture. Their music. Their writing. The channels are the text of the culture, and the water is the ink, and the stone is the page, and the page has been being written for longer than anyone can remember, and it is not finished, and it will not be finished, because the water is always moving and the channels are always being refined, and the text is never done.

Afternoons are for rain. When the rain comes, the people go inside, and the city becomes a collection of hearths and warm stone and the smell of wet wool and the sound of water on the roof — every roof a concave catchment, every roof a watershed, and the rain that falls on the roof is the rain that fills the cistern, and the cistern is the water they drink, and the water they drink is the rain that fell on the roof, and this cycle is so tight, so closed, so complete that there is no gap in it, no moment when the water is not theirs, is not known, is not accounted for. They know their water. They know which roof it fell on. They know which cistern it sits in. They know how long it has been there. They drink water the way other people drink wine — with knowledge of its origin, its character, its age.

Evenings are for gathering. The rain stops and the mist sits and the air is cold and the hearths are lit and the people come out and move toward the large pools, the communal pools, and they sit beside the water and they are together. Not loudly. Not with the energy of a market or a festival. With the quiet energy of people who have been alone all morning and are now not alone, and the transition from alone to together is marked by water, by the pool, by the surface of the water that reflects the last light and the first mist and the faces of the people sitting beside it, and the gathering is not a thing with a name. It is not a council. It is not a ceremony. It is people sitting beside water in the evening, and the sitting is the thing.

They speak. They speak in a language that is not like other languages, or that is like other languages in its grammar but not in its sound. It is quiet. It is spoken at the volume of the water. The consonants are soft — the hard sounds of stone are absent, or nearly absent — and the vowels are open and long, and the words have a quality of flowing, of moving from one to the next the way water moves from one pool to the next, and the language, heard from outside, sounds like the sound of the channels, like the sound of water in stone, and you think: they have learned to speak in the voice of the place. They have made their language from the water.

They have stories. You do not know the stories because you do not know the language, but you know they have stories because you see the telling of them — the circle of people around a pool in the evening, and one person speaking, and the others listening, and the speaking going on for a long time, and the faces of the listeners changing, and sometimes a hand reaches out and touches the surface of the pool, and the surface moves, and the story continues, and the ripples move outward to the edge of the pool and return, and the story absorbs this, the rhythm of the ripples, and you understand that the stories are about water. About the water in the stone. About where it comes from and where it goes and what it remembers. The stories are the water's memory, spoken by people, in the evening, beside the pool, in the language that sounds like the water.

## The Children

The children are few. Not because of death — the children are healthy, the children are strong, the children have the same dark grey skin and broad hands and pale eyes as the adults, and they move on the wet stone with the same sureness — but because of choice. The people choose to have few children. This is not a policy. It is not a decision. It is a quality of the culture, a thing that has been true for so long that it is no longer a thing thought about but a thing simply done, the way the channels are cut and the wool is woven and the rain is waited for. Few children. Small families. The city contracts. The extra rooms are kept ready.

The children are raised by the water. This is not a metaphor, or it is a metaphor that is also literal. A child born here is brought to the primary pool — the largest, the most central, the pool where the freshest water emerges from the cliff — and the child is held over the water, and the water is touched to the child's face, and this is the beginning. The child belongs to the water. The child is of the water. The water is the child's first relation, before mother, before father, before the stone. The water.

The children learn the channels before they learn the words. A child of three can trace a channel from its origin to its end, can name the pools it passes through, can tell you which seep feeds it and how long the water has been in the stone. This is not taught. This is absorbed. The child grows up beside the channels, plays beside the channels, sleeps beside the channels — the sound of the water is the sound of the child's first sleep — and the channels become the child's first map, the child's first language, the child's first understanding of how the world is organized. The world is organized by water. The water moves from here to there. The there is a pool. The pool is a place. The places are connected by water. The water is the path. The path is the water. This is the foundation of everything the child will know.

The children do not play with toys. They play with water. They dam channels with their hands and watch the water rise and then release it and watch it fall. They float things — fragments of rush, flakes of algae, small stones — in the pools and watch them move. They pour water from one stone vessel to another. They touch the surface of the pool and watch the ripples and wait for the ripples to reach the edge and return, and they learn, from this, something about distance and time and the movement of energy through a medium, and this learning is not named and not formalized but is as real as any learning done in any school, and it is the learning that the place provides, and the children receive it, and they grow up knowing things that you do not know, things that cannot be written down or explained but can only be learned by a child sitting beside a pool on a high plateau, touching the water, and waiting.

## The Old Ones

There are old people here. This is remarkable, given the diet, the cold, the altitude, the austerity. But there are old people — very old people, people whose dark grey skin has gone almost white with the mineral deposits of decades of wet air, people whose pale eyes have gone so pale that they are almost transparent, people whose hands are still broad and strong but move slowly now, with the deliberateness of hands that know exactly what they are doing because they have done it for seventy years or eighty years or longer.

The old ones are the channel-carvers. This is the work of age, not of youth. The young cut stone and fit blocks and maintain the buildings and go down the escarpment to trade for flour and catch lizards and raise the few children. The old ones carve the channels. The old ones refine the curves. The old ones sit beside a channel junction with a chisel that is older than they are and work the stone with a slowness that is not infirmity but precision, and the stone comes away in dust and powder and the curve becomes smoother and the water flows more cleanly, and the old one watches the water and sees something in it that the young do not see, and the chisel moves again, and the curve changes again, and the water changes, and this is the work of a lifetime, and the work is not done, and the work will not be done, and the old one knows this and does not mind.

There is one — you see her in the evening, by the central pool — who is older than the others. Her skin is almost white. Her eyes are almost gone. She sits by the pool and does not speak and does not move and the people around her do not address her directly but are aware of her, the way they are aware of the water, and the children come to her and touch her hand and she touches their heads and this is a thing that does not have a name but is clearly a thing, a transaction, a passage of something from the old to the young through the medium of touch, the way water passes through the medium of stone, and the something that is passed is not knowledge and not blessing and not authority but perhaps presence, the simple presence of a person who has been here for a very long time and has absorbed the place and is now, in some way, the place itself, or the closest thing to the place that a human body can become.

When she dies — and she will die, and they know this, and the knowledge is in the way they touch her hand and the way the children come to her — she will not be buried. There is no soil. There is no digging. The dead are given to the cliff. The weeping cliff, the wet face of stone where the water emerges and the moss grows. The dead are placed in a crevice in the cliff, in a place where the water runs, and the water runs over them, and the moss grows over them, and the mineral deposit — the iron-red, the calcium-white, the manganese-black — covers them, and they become part of the text that the water is writing on the stone. They become part of the cliff. They become part of the water's memory. This is not ritual. This is geography. The dead go where the water goes. The water goes into the stone. The stone is the place. The dead are in the place. There is no separation.

## What They Are Not

They are not monks. They are not ascetics. They are not a chosen people or a cursed people or a people with a mission or a people with a grievance. They are not poor. Poverty requires a context of wealth, and they have no such context. They have enough. They have water, stone, wool, algae, insects, lizards, flour from below, and the occasional bird-circling sky and the constant wet wind and the slow growth of lichen and the slow carving of channels and the slow contraction of a city that was built for more and is now less and is not diminished by being less. They are not unhappy. Unhappiness requires a vision of something else, and they do not have a vision of something else. They have this. The plateau. The water. The stone. The rain that comes in the afternoon. The lizards that come after the rain. The pools that are always full. The channels that are always flowing. The cliffs that are always weeping. The moss that is always growing. The lichen that is always spreading, a millimetre a year, over the flat stone that is the floor of the world.

They are not waiting for anything. This is the thing that is hardest to understand from outside, because from outside, a place like this looks like a waiting place — a place where people are enduring, persisting, holding on until something changes, until the weather changes or the water changes or the world changes and they can be something other than what they are. But they are not waiting. They are being. They are in the middle of a long, slow life that has no destination because it does not need a destination, because the life is the point, the living is the point, the water in the channels and the stone under the feet and the rain on the roof and the pool in the evening and the child touching the water and the old one carving the curve and the bird circling above and the lizard on the wet stone and the algae on the still surface and the mineral script on the cliff face and the silence, the silence that is not emptiness but fullness, the silence of a place that is so full of itself that there is no room for anything else, no room for noise, no room for drama, no room for history, no room for anything but the water and the stone and the people who have learned to live at the volume of the water and the pace of the stone and the patience of the lichen, and who do not need anything else, and have never needed anything else, and will not need anything else, and are complete.
