# What Lives Here

---

## The First Sense

Before anything else, there is the *feeling* of being watched without eyes.

Not menace. Not the alertness of a predator or the wariness of prey. Something more distributed than that. The attention comes from everywhere at once — from the water itself, from the dark mats on the cliff faces, from the translucent grass bending in its slow underwater wind. As though the place has developed a kind of peripheral awareness, a consciousness without a skull to house it, and you have entered the field of its notice without triggering any particular response.

It has noticed you the way a forest notices a change in humidity. Informationally. Without urgency.

You float. The water holds you at its own temperature and you cannot find your edges. You are being perceived by something that does not know you are a person, and this should be unsettling but it is not. It is simply the terms of being here.

---

## The Mats

The dark growth on the submerged cliff faces is not what it first appears to be.

It is alive — certainly alive, undeniably alive — but it does not behave like any moss or lichen or algal film that has a name. Up close, pressing your face against the water near a mesa wall, you can see that the fractal patterns are not static. They grow. Not fast. Not in any way you can catch by staring. But if you look away for an hour and look back, the spirals have advanced — a millimeter, perhaps less — and the branching forms have extended another node outward, another fork in what might be a code or might be nothing more than the geometry of how this particular organism fills space.

But here is the thing that stays with you.

The patterns are not the same on every cliff face. They are similar — the same vocabulary of spirals and branchings, the same dark palette, the same density — but they vary. On the north-facing walls of the taller mesas, the patterns tend toward tight concentric circles, nested and regular, almost architectural. On the south-facing walls, where the diffused light is marginally stronger, the patterns loosen, become more branching, more dendritic, as though the organism is reaching toward something. On the walls of the shorter mesas, barely three meters above the drowned valley floor, the patterns are different again — longer, more linear, more like strokes than like structures. Like handwriting practiced in different scripts.

The mats do not respond to touch. Press your hand against one and it feels like very short velvet, dense and dry despite being underwater, warmer than the surrounding water by a degree or two. It does not give. It does not tear. It adheres to the stone beneath it with a strength that suggests it has been there much longer than the flood, though this is impossible, because the stone was dry before the flood, and the mats are underwater, and nothing about their presence makes sense unless they arrived with the water or grew from the stone when the water touched it, which suggests something about the stone itself, something that the flood activated.

Leave your hand against a mat for long enough — a minute, two — and you will feel something. Not heat, not vibration. A kind of *pull*. Very faint. As though the mat is taking something from your skin — a salt, a oil, a microbe — and incorporating it. Not feeding, exactly. Sampling. Learning what you are made of in the way that a tongue learns the shape of a word.

Pull your hand away and the skin where the mat touched it is slightly pink, slightly warm, slightly *different*, though you cannot say how. The mat does not appear to have changed. But later, if you check — an hour, two — there will be a new growth on its surface. A small bud, darker than the rest, slightly raised. A new node in the pattern. Where your hand was.

This is not infection. This is not parasitism. This is something that does not have a category yet.

## The Grass

The drowned grass on the valley floor is still alive, and this is the detail that refuses to become comfortable no matter how long you stay.

You can dive to it. The water is clear enough, the depth manageable, and the grass waits below in its pale aqua stillness, its blades wide and translucent and gelatinous, moving in a current so slow that the word "moving" is almost wrong. The blades are more like ribbons now, more like tongues, more like things that have a top and a bottom and a direction and are in the process of becoming something other than grass. Some of them have already begun to split at their tips, each fork producing two thinner ribbons that themselves will fork, a branching that echoes the branching on the cliff mats and suggests either a shared principle or a shared origin or a coincidence that only seems meaningful because you are looking for meaning in a place that has not committed to being meaningful yet.

The grass tastes the water. This is the only way to describe what the roots are doing — they have extended their filaments into the flood not to anchor but to *sample*, to test the chemistry of this new medium they have been dropped into, and the information they gather is being processed by the plant in real time. You can see this happening if you watch long enough. A blade will thicken in one place and thin in another. A root filament will extend, pause, retract slightly, extend again in a different direction. The plant is making decisions, but not with a mind. With a chemistry. With the same kind of trial-and-error that evolution has always used, compressed into a timescale that is fast enough to observe.

What lives in the grass — what has already moved in, what was perhaps already there and has simply expanded its range — are the microorganisms. They are not visible to the naked eye, but their presence is detectable as a faint cloudiness in the water immediately above the grass beds, a slight haze that catches the green-gold light and makes it shimmer. These are the decomposers, the recyclers, the organisms that are eating the parts of the drowned ecosystem that have already died and are dissolving. They are the first evidence that the nutrient cycle is underway, that the place is beginning to process itself, that the flood is not just an event but the start of a metabolism.

If you hold very still in the water above the grass beds and let your vision adjust, you can see them — not as individuals, but as a phenomenon. The water above the grass has a *texture*. It shimmers like heat rising from a road, but slowly, underwater, in green-gold. This is life at its smallest and most fundamental, the first tenants of the drowned valley floor, already at work, already building the foundation of whatever this place will become.

## The Curtains

The floating algae curtains are not alive in the way that the grass is alive or the mats are alive. They are alive in the way that a flame is alive — a process, a temporary organization of matter into a form that persists only so long as the conditions that create it persist.

Each curtain is a single sheet of translucent material, pale green, flexible, slightly sticky to the touch. It has no roots, no stems, no structure that maps onto any known plant anatomy. It is a film. A membrane. A surface across which photosynthesis occurs and within which a simple metabolism maintains the integrity of the form. The curtains reproduce by tearing — a fragment breaks free, drifts away, and if the conditions are right, it grows. Not by cell division in the traditional sense, but by *extending*. The fragment spreads itself thinner and wider, like a drop of oil on water, until it has become a new curtain, a new sheet of pale green floating in the water column.

The curtains breathe. This is not metaphor. They rise in the light and sink in the dark, a vertical migration that plays out over hours and that is driven by the production and consumption of oxygen within their own tissue. In the morning, when the diffused light strengthens, the curtains drift upward, their photosynthesis generating buoyant gases that lift them slowly through the water column. In the evening, as the light fails, the gases are consumed and the curtains sink. It is the most basic kind of breathing — the kind that does not require lungs or gills or any structure at all, just a membrane and a gradient and a reason to move.

There is something in the curtains. Not inside them — *on* them. A coating, a thin film of something that is not algae and not bacteria and not quite identifiable. It catches the light differently than the curtain material itself, giving each sheet a slightly iridescent sheen, a faint oil-on-water shimmer that is visible only at certain angles. This film is organic. It is alive, or it was alive, or it is in the process of becoming alive — a community of microorganisms that has colonized the curtain's surface and is building its own small ecosystem on the floating substrate.

The curtains, then, are not just organisms. They are *habitats*. They are the first structures in the water column, the first surfaces available for colonization in a three-dimensional space that was previously empty, and the microscopic life that has found them is already beginning the process of building complexity, of turning a simple floating film into a community, of adding layers to a place that has very few layers yet.

## What Hunts

There are no hunters here yet. This is important to understand.

The ecosystem has not developed the machinery of predation. The microorganisms in the water column are decomposers, not predators — they consume dead matter, not living tissue. The cliff mats sample, but they do not eat. The curtains photosynthesize and are colonized, but nothing eats them. The drowned grass is alive and adapting, but nothing grazes on it. The mesa-top plants grow and root and reach down into the water, but nothing feeds on their filaments.

This will not last.

The place is waiting for something. Not a specific thing — not a particular species, not a known organism that will arrive and fill the empty niche. It is waiting for the *concept* of predation to arrive, for the idea that one living thing can consume another living thing and convert its substance into energy and structure. This idea has not yet found its way here. The microorganisms that exist are too simple, too limited, too new. They decompose but do not hunt. They recycle but do not kill.

But the nutrients are running out. The drowned vegetation is being consumed. The water is rich with the dissolved substance of a former landscape, but that substance is finite, and the organisms that depend on it will eventually need to find another source, and the most obvious source is each other. This transition — from decomposition to predation, from scavenging to hunting — has not yet happened, but it will. It is the next chapter in the story of this place, and the place knows it is coming, not with a mind but with a chemistry, with the slow accumulation of pressures that will eventually produce a change.

When the first predator arrives — or evolves, or emerges from the mats or the grass or the film on the curtains' surfaces — it will be small. Microscopic. A single-celled organism that has learned to consume another single-celled organism rather than dead matter. It will be the first mouth, the first stomach, the first intention to eat something that is still alive. And from that first predation, everything else will follow — the grazers and the browsers and the hunters and the hunted, the whole elaborate architecture of a food web, built upward from a single act of eating something that did not want to be eaten.

But that is later. That is the future. For now, the place is peaceful in the specific way that a place without predation is peaceful — not because nothing dies, but because nothing kills. The dead are recycled by the decomposers. The living grow and adapt and spread. The water is warm and clear and full of the dissolved substance of a landscape that is becoming something else. Nobody is eating anybody.

This will be the first thing to change.

## The Floaters

You are not the only thing floating on the surface.

There are others — not many, not yet, and they are not what you would expect. They are not fish. They are not insects. They are not vertebrates of any kind.

They are fragments.

The mesa-top vegetation, in its rampant growth, occasionally produces detritus — leaves that fall, twigs that snap, small clusters of root filament that break free from the underwater extensions and drift upward to the surface. These fragments collect at the waterline, accumulating against the mesa walls in small drifts of organic matter, dark against the pale stone. Most of this material decomposes quickly in the warm water, its substance dissolving into the flood and joining the nutrient cycle. But some of it persists. Some of it — the woody twigs, the thick-stemmed leaves, the dense mats of root filament — floats for days or weeks, slowly breaking apart, releasing its substance gradually into the water.

On these floating fragments, life is beginning to establish itself.

Not complex life. Not yet. But the same kind of microbial film that has colonized the algae curtains has found the floating detritus, and the detritus provides something the curtains do not — *structure*. A surface with texture, with crevices, with trapped air and decomposing wood and the chemical complexity of a terrestrial plant that has not yet been fully processed by the aquatic ecosystem. On these small floating islands, the microorganisms are diversifying. Different species — if they can be called species — are finding different niches on different parts of the fragment. The top, exposed to air and light. The bottom, submerged and shaded. The edges, where air and water meet in a meniscus that is neither one nor the other.

This is where the animal life will begin, when it comes. Not in the water column, not on the cliff mats, not in the drowned grass. On the floating detritus. On the fragments of a terrestrial ecosystem that are being slowly consumed by an aquatic one, the boundary between the two worlds providing the complexity, the gradient, the edge where new things tend to emerge.

For now, the fragments are colonized only by microbes. But the microbes are doing something that the water-column microorganisms are not — they are *organizing*. They are forming structures. They are arranging themselves in patterns that echo the patterns on the cliff mats — spirals and branchings and concentrations — and these patterns are not random. They are the beginning of biofilms, the beginning of communities, the beginning of the kind of cooperative organization that will eventually produce multicellularity and then mobility and then the whole long chain of complexity that leads, eventually, to something with a mouth and a gut and a preference for eating things that are still alive.

But the fragments are also where something else lives. Something that you can see, if you look closely, if you are patient, if you let your eyes adjust to the surface of the water and the small objects floating on it.

They are very small. They are very still. They are almost invisible.

## The Still Ones

On the surface of the larger floating fragments — the ones that have been drifting for days, the ones with the thickest microbial films — there are shapes that are not microbial and not plant and not detritus.

They are animals. Almost certainly animals. Or they are the thing that comes before animals — the proto-animal, the organism that is on the exact boundary between colony and creature, between a group of cells and a single organism made of many cells.

They are round. Flat. Very small — perhaps a centimeter across, perhaps less. They are translucent, like the drowned grass, and they are the same pale aqua, so close in color to the water and the detritus and the microbial film that they are nearly impossible to see. You find them by looking for discontinuities — a place where the texture of a fragment's surface is slightly different, slightly smoother, slightly more regular than the rough, organic chaos around it.

They do not move. Or they move so slowly that movement is the wrong word. They *shift*. Over the course of an hour, a single one of these organisms will change its position on a fragment by a few millimeters, migrating from one edge to another, from a shaded spot to a lit spot or vice versa. They are grazing, probably — feeding on the microbial film, consuming the organisms that have colonized the floating detritus. They are the first predators. Or they are the first grazers, which is close enough. They are the first organisms in this place that eat other living things.

Their bodies are not differentiated. There is no head, no mouth, no gut, no visible internal structure. They are flat discs of translucent tissue, pale aqua, slightly ruffled at their edges, slightly convex in their centers. They absorb food through their surfaces, or they engulf it, or they dissolve it externally and absorb the result — the mechanism is not clear and may not be consistent from one organism to the next. They are as simple as an animal can be and still be an animal. They are the minimum viable body plan for a heterotroph, the simplest possible design for a creature that eats.

But they are here. They are alive. They are eating. And they are the first.

If you watch one for long enough — long enough that the light shifts and the curtains drift and the mats on the cliff faces extend another imperceptible fraction of a millimeter — you will see it do something that changes your understanding of the place. It will reach the edge of its floating fragment. It will encounter the water. And it will not stop.

It will extend itself over the edge, slowly, millimeter by millimeter, its translucent body stretching and thinning as it crosses the boundary between the wet surface of the detritus and the wet surface of the water. And it will enter the water and continue to move — not swimming, not floating, but *crawling*, adhering to the surface tension of the water itself, a flat pale disc of living tissue moving across the meniscus as though the surface of the water were a solid thing, as though the boundary between water and air were a landscape it could traverse.

It can hunt, here. It can graze the microbial film that forms on the water's surface, the thin organic layer that accumulates where air and water meet. It can move from fragment to fragment, from detritus to detritus, following the food. It can cross the open water, if the food is there, if the need is great enough, if the surface tension holds.

It is the first animal in a place that has not yet decided it has animals. It is the first consumer in a place that has not yet developed the concept of consumption. It is the first mobile organism in a place that has been, until now, a world of rooted things and drifting things and things that grow in patterns on stone.

And it is not alone.

## The Sense of Life

What lives in the Drowned Mesas is not a what. It is a *when*.

This is an ecosystem at the very beginning of its story, in the first chapter, on the first page. The flood has created a new world and the new world is being colonized — by mats that grow in patterns that might be language, by grass that is becoming something other than grass, by curtains that breathe and carry communities on their surfaces, by fragments that float and accumulate complexity, by organisms that are barely organisms and are learning to eat.

The dominant life forms are the cliff mats. They cover the most surface area. They are the most visually striking. They are the closest thing the place has to a character, a presence, a thing with intentions. But they are not the future. The future is the small translucent discs on the floating detritus, the first movers, the first eaters, the organisms that will diversify and specialize and compete and, over the next thousand years or ten thousand or a hundred thousand, become the ecosystem that this place will eventually be.

What lives here now is potential. What lives here now is the first fragile improvisation of a world that has been given a new medium and is learning what can be done with it. The warm water is a womb. The drowned mesas are the architecture of a nursery. The plants and the mats and the curtains and the barely-animals are the first draft of a community that will be revised and revised and revised until the revisions themselves become the history of the place.

And underneath all of it, in the water that is exactly body temperature, in the silence that is the silence of a place listening to itself, there is the sense that the place is aware of what it is doing. Not with a mind. Not with a consciousness. With something older and more patient than consciousness — the deep, slow, evolutionary attention of a landscape that has been alive, in its way, for longer than anything with a skull has been alive, and that will continue its patient becoming long after the first animals have diversified and the first fish have arrived and the first birds have found their way across the water and the whole elaborate pageant of life has played itself out across the flat crowns of the mesas and the drowned valley floor below.

The mats will still be growing their patterns. The water will still be warm. The silence will still be the silence of a place that is listening to what it is becoming.

And what it is becoming is itself. Only itself. Slowly, patiently, without hurry or explanation, the Drowned Mesas are becoming what they will be, and what lives there is the process of that becoming, which is the oldest and most patient kind of life there is.
