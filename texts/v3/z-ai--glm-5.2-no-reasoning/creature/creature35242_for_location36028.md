# What Lives in the Seep

---

## First, the things you would notice

The snails.

They are what you would see first, if you ever stood there — which you shouldn't, and won't, and almost certainly never will. They are what you would notice not because they are dramatic or beautiful or strange, but because they are *everywhere*, and because in the dim blue-grey light of the hollow, their shells catch what little illumination there is and hold it. A glint here. A faint amber glow there. Dozens of them, then more than dozens, then a number you would stop counting because counting would require standing still for longer than the place would tolerate your standing.

They are small. None larger than a hazelnut. Their shells are thin — thin enough, in some individuals, to see the dark shadow of the body coiled within, and this thinness is not a flaw but a strategy. Calcium is scarce in this water. The mountain's geology is old, acidic, leached. The seep carries what it carries and no more, and what it carries is enough for shells but not for thick ones, and so the snails have, over generations uncounted, arrived at a compromise: build lightly, build often, dissolve and rebuild. Their shells are translucent, ranging from pale amber to deep chestnut, and the coloration is not decoration. It is the stain of the water itself, the iron and the tannins and the faint minerals that the seep has been carrying for longer than the rowan has been alive, which is longer than you have been alive, which is longer than anyone you know has been alive.

They graze. That is their primary activity, and it is not a casual or a lazy grazing but a thorough, methodical, absolute grazing. They eat the film of algae that sheets across the wet rock face. They eat the microorganisms that colonize every surface in the hollow — every leaf, every stem, every stone, every shell of every other snail. They eat decaying plant matter in the moss. They eat the moss itself, occasionally, though the moss grows faster than they can suppress it. They eat each other's egg cases, sometimes, though not often enough to matter. They eat, and they grow, and they mate, and they lay their own gelatinous clusters of eggs in the axils of leaves and in the crevices of the rowan's bark and in the furled tips of fern fronds, and then they die, and their shells thin and soften in the acidic water, and the calcium that was in them returns to the system, and other snails — their descendants, their neighbors, the continuation of the population in the most literal sense — incorporate that calcium into new shells.

This is the tightest loop in the hollow. Not a family line. A chemical line. The shell you see on a snail today may contain calcium that was in three snails before it, cycled through the water, taken up by the moss, eaten by the snail, secreted as shell, dissolved upon death, cycled again. The snails are, in this sense, not individuals. They are a single ongoing mineral event, a wave of calcium moving through time in the shape of thousands of small brown bodies.

There are, by rough estimate, several thousand of them in the hollow. Perhaps more. The floor is only sixty feet across at its widest, but the density is staggering — on the moss, on the rocks, on the sedges, on the tree trunk, on each other. Stacked, sometimes. Three or four deep on a particularly favored rock, the ones on top riding on the shells of the ones beneath, all of them grazing, all of them moving with the absolute, unhurried purposefulness of animals that have nowhere to go and nothing to fear. They do not flee. They have no predators in the hollow that can meaningfully reduce their numbers. They do not hide. They do not seek shelter. They exist on surfaces, and surfaces exist for them, and the correspondence between snail and surface in this place is so complete that it is difficult to imagine one without the other.

## Beneath them, in the matrix

The beetles.

If the snails are the surface fauna, the beetles are the infrastructure. They live in the moss, in the leaf litter, in the saturated peat, in the spaces between stems and roots and fragments of undecomposed organic matter that make up the floor of the hollow. They are the hidden majority, and their diversity is, frankly, absurd for a space this small.

Dozens of species. In sixty feet of moss and peat, dozens of species of beetle, each one occupying a niche so narrow that it can be described only in terms of the specific layer of the specific substrate at the specific moisture level where it is found. This is not unusual for wetland habitats in general — wetlands are diverse by nature — but it is unusual at this scale. The hollow is smaller than a large room. And in this room-sized space, there are more beetle species than you would find in a square mile of dry upland forest a thousand feet below.

The ground beetles come first. They are small — three to eight millimeters — and dark, with legs that are longer than they need to be for their body size, adapted for moving through wet substrate without sinking. Their elytra are smooth and glossy, and water beads on them the way it beads on wax. They patrol the surface of the moss at night, when the darkness in the hollow is absolute and visual hunting is impossible, and they find their prey by touch and by chemical cue, antennae sweeping the air just above the moss surface, detecting the faint traces of springtails and mites and the larvae of other insects. They are predators. They are, in the hollow, the closest thing to an apex predator that exists on land.

But they are not the largest predators. The rove beetles are, in a sense, more formidable. They are longer-bodied, more flexible, with shortened wing covers that expose most of their segmented abdomens, and this body plan — which looks awkward, even unfinished — is precisely what allows them to navigate the three-dimensional maze of moss stems and leaf fragments and root threads. They move through the moss matrix the way eels move through kelp, undulating, turning their bodies around corners that a stiffer insect could not negotiate. They hunt in the depths of the litter, in the anaerobic zone where the peat is almost liquid and the oxygen is almost gone. They find prey there that no surface predator can reach — the larvae of fungus gnats, the nymphs of small crustaceans, the soft, slow, pale things that live in the saturated dark below the green.

In the runnels, the shallow water that crosses the floor of the hollow in branching patterns, the water beetles hold dominion. They are the smallest of the hollow's beetles — two to four millimeters, some of them — and they are the most specialized. Their bodies are streamlined, hydrodynamic, adapted for a life spent entirely in water that is, at its deepest, barely an inch. They cling to submerged vegetation — the stems of sedges that have bent into the runnels, the rhizomes of moss that cross the channels like submerged bridges — and they hunt the crustaceans that fill the water in clouds so dense they cast shadows.

The beetles do not interact with the snails. This is important. In a larger ecosystem, a more variable ecosystem, there would be competition, predation, overlap — some beetle would have evolved to eat snails, or some snail would have evolved to compete with beetles for a shared resource. But in the hollow, the niches are so precisely defined and so completely separated that the beetles and the snails might as well live on different planets. The snails are on surfaces. The beetles are in the matrix. The snails eat algae and detritus. The beetles eat other invertebrates. Their diets do not overlap. Their habitats do not overlap. Their activity cycles do not overlap — the snails graze continuously, day and night, while the beetles are more active in the dark, though "active" is a relative term in a place where all movement is slow.

This is not peace. This is not cooperation. This is precision. The system has had time — uncounted, uninterrupted time — to sort itself into a configuration where every species fits and no species conflicts, and this sorting is not a process that has a moral dimension. It is physics. It is chemistry. It is the logical consequence of a stable environment occupied by organisms that have been adapting to it for longer than adaptation usually gets to run without interruption.

## In the water, the foundation

The crustaceans.

The runnels are full of them. Copepods, ostracods, water fleas — the tiny, translucent, many-legged or no-legged, shelled or unshelled, swimming or crawling or simply drifting inhabitants of the shallow water that crosses the floor of the hollow. They are the smallest visible animals in the place, and they are, in a functional sense, the most important, because they are the base of the food web, the foundation on which everything else is built.

Their density is difficult to describe without sounding like you are exaggerating. Thousands per square foot of runnel. The water in the shallow channels is not clear — it has a faint opacity, a barely perceptible cloudiness — and this cloudiness is, in part, the crustaceans themselves, their bodies so numerous that they affect the optical properties of the water they live in. If you could see them at scale — if you could shrink yourself to their size and stand among them — it would be like standing in a blizzard, except the snowflakes were alive and moving and eating and excreting and mating and dying and being born all around you, all the time, in every direction, without pause.

They filter. That is what most of them do. They filter particles from the water — bacteria, fine detritus, dissolved organic matter, the microscopic fragments of things that were once alive and are being broken down by the slow, cold chemistry of the peat. They filter, and they excrete, and their excretions are nutrients — nitrogen, phosphorus, trace minerals — that feed the moss and the algae and the single-celled organisms that also live in the water and on the surfaces and in every damp crevice of the hollow. The crustaceans are, in this sense, the hollow's circulatory system. They take what is dissolved and make it solid, take what is waste and make it food, take what is dead and make it alive. They are the engine of the nutrient cycle, and the nutrient cycle is the engine of everything.

The water fleas are the most visible of them, if "visible" is a word you can apply to an animal less than two millimeters long. They move in quick, jerky dashes — a stroke of their antennae, a moment of stillness, another stroke — and their motion gives the water a quality of restless, granular activity that is distinct from the slow sheeting of the seep itself. They are prey for the water beetles. They are also, in their smaller instars, prey for the larvae of midges that develop in the runnels before emerging into the air. They are food. That is their primary ecological role — to be food, to convert the dissolved nutrients of the peat into the protein and lipid and chitin of a body that something else can eat. They perform this role with a thoroughness that borders on the heroic. Every water flea in the hollow is, statistically, eaten within a few weeks of hatching. They do not know this. They do not experience it as a tragedy. They filter, they dash, they reproduce, they are consumed, and the system continues.

The ostracods are different. They are bean-shaped, enclosed in bivalved shells of their own secretion, and they move by extending and retracting their legs through the gap between the shell halves, rowing themselves through the water with a motion that is somehow both mechanical and organic, like a tiny submarine operated by a nervous system. They are slower than the water fleas, more deliberate, more attached to surfaces. They crawl along the bottoms of the runnels, ingesting sediment, processing it, extracting what they can use. They have been here, in this form, doing this, for an extraordinarily long time. Ostracods as a group have existed for nearly 500 million years. The species in the hollow are younger than that, but their lineage, their body plan, their approach to the problem of being alive — it is older than the mountain that holds them. They are living fossils in the most literal and least dramatic sense: they have found a way to be, and they have not stopped being that way, and the seep has given them a place to continue.

## In the air, the prisoners

The flies.

They hang in the air just above the moss surface, in clouds that are densest near the center of the hollow and thinner toward the walls. Fungus gnats, mostly, and midges — the small, delicate, short-lived dipterans that breed in wet substrate and emerge as adults into the still, saturated air. They do not swarm. There is no direction to their movement, no pattern, no organization. They move in random Brownian paths, each insect turning and drifting and turning again according to cues that are too subtle for human observation to detect, and their collective motion gives the air above the moss a quality of constant, low-level agitation, like the surface of water that is just about to boil but never does.

They are prisoners. This is not a metaphor. The air outside the hollow is drier than the air inside — perhaps not dramatically drier, but drier enough, and the flies' bodies are so small and so adapted to saturation that exposure to air below a certain humidity causes them to lose water faster than they can replace it. They cannot leave. The mist that fills the hollow is, for them, not a condition but a boundary, an invisible wall of differential vapor pressure that keeps them confined as surely as a glass dome. They live their entire lives — egg, larva, pupa, adult — within the hollow's sixty-foot circumference. They have never known any other air.

The fungus gnats are the more numerous of the two groups. Their larvae develop in the moss and the wet peat, feeding on the fungal hyphae that permeate the organic substrate — a hidden network of microscopic threads that decompose the dead plant matter and make its nutrients available to the living plants. The larvae are legless, white, translucent, with dark head capsules that are visible as tiny dots in the moss if you part the surface gently with your fingers, which no one does, because no one comes here. They pupate in the upper layer of the substrate, and the adults emerge into the air and live for a few days, mating in the suspended clouds, laying eggs back into the moss, dying, falling, being decomposed by the same fungi their children will eat.

The midges are different in habit but identical in constraint. Their larvae are aquatic, living in the runnels, constructing tiny tubes of silt and secreted protein on the submerged stems of plants, extending their heads from the tube openings to feed on the particulate matter that drifts past in the slow current. They are the runnels' sediment processors, and they are remarkably efficient — a single midge larva can process several times its body weight in sediment per day, filtering out what is edible and expelling the rest as compacted waste that settles to the bottom of the channel and becomes, over time, part of the peat.

When they emerge as adults, they join the gnats in the air above the moss. They do not eat as adults — many midge species have no functional mouthparts — and their sole purpose in their aerial phase is to mate and to return their eggs to the water. They have a few days. They spend those days in the same air, in the same light, in the same temperature, moving in the same random paths, and then they are finished, and they fall, and the spider finds them, or the moss absorbs them, or the crustaceans in the runnels take their bodies apart, and the cycle does not notice their passing because the cycle has no mechanism for noticing.

## The spider

There is one spider.

This requires emphasis, because in the hollow, where everything is numerous and saturated and everywhere, the spider is singular. One spider. One web. Between two sedges, near the center of the hollow, at a height of perhaps six inches above the moss surface. The spider has been there — or rather, *a* spider has been there, in that precise location, in that precise web — for as long as the hollow has been observed, which is not long, because the hollow has not been observed, but the web is there, and it has the quality of a thing that has always been there.

The spider is small. Its body is no larger than a peppercorn, dark, with legs that are proportionally long and that move with a deliberateness that is different from the snails' deliberateness and different from the beetles' deliberateness. The snails are deliberate because they cannot be otherwise. The beetles are deliberate because the substrate demands it. The spider is deliberate because it is hunting, and hunting, even in a place this stable, this predictable, this free of surprise, requires attention.

Its web is sheet-like, horizontal, positioned to intercept the gnats and midges that drift through the air at that level. The construction is meticulous — radial threads anchored to the two sedges and to nearby moss stems, supporting a plane of sticky silk that is nearly invisible in the dim light except when water condenses on it. And water always condenses on it. Every strand of the web is beaded with droplets of condensed moisture, tiny perfect spheres that catch the faint light and give the web the appearance of something manufactured — a net of glass beads, a piece of jewelry, a small and exact thing made by a small and exact animal in a small and exact place.

The spider sits beneath the web, not on it. This is important. It is not waiting in the web for prey to arrive. It is waiting beneath the web, watching — with its eight simple eyes, in the near-darkness — for the vibration that indicates a catch. When a gnat or a midge strikes the web, the droplets of water on the strands hold it — the surface tension of the water is sufficient to trap an insect that small, quite apart from the stickiness of the silk — and the spider climbs to the upper surface, locates the prey by touch, wraps it in a further layer of silk, and then carries it below to consume at leisure.

The consumption is slow. The spider injects venom and digestive enzymes into the wrapped package and waits for the insect's internal tissues to liquefy, then sucks the resulting fluid out through the silk. The empty exoskeleton remains in the wrap, a husk that the spider eventually drops to the moss below, where the beetles will find it and process it further, or where the moisture will soften it and the fungi will colonize it and it will become, over weeks, part of the substrate.

When the web is damaged — and it is damaged, occasionally, by the weight of accumulated water or by the impact of a larger insect that blunders in from outside and is too heavy to hold — the spider repairs it. It does not rebuild. It repairs. It replaces the broken strands, adds new silk to the damaged areas, adjusts the tension of the sheet. The web is, in this sense, not a single object but a continuous process, a structure that is being made and remade and made again at every moment, and the spider that makes it is similarly continuous — it will die, eventually, and for a brief period the web will be empty, and then another spider will arrive (from where? from the margins of the hollow, from a crevice in the rock, from an egg sac hidden in the moss that hatched and produced a spiderling that grew and found the gap between the sedges and began to build) and the web will be rebuilt in the same place, in the same shape, between the same supports, and the process will continue.

The spider is not the same spider. The web is not the same web. But the difference is so small, so irrelevant to the function of the system, that it might as well be. This is the hollow's relationship with identity: it does not require it. It requires only the process, only the continuation, only the function. The spider that is here today is the spider that was here yesterday in every way that matters to the hollow, and the spider that will be here tomorrow is the spider that is here today, and the thread that connects them is not memory or consciousness or lineage but simply the geometry of two sedges and the space between them.

## The things that visit

The thrush.

It comes without sound. It arrives in the hollow the way light arrives — not dramatically, not with announcement, but simply present, suddenly, where a moment ago it was not. It is hard to be certain of the species. The light is too dim for reliable observation, and the bird does not stay long enough for careful study. But it appears to be a thrush — the size, the posture, the way it holds its head — and it comes for the rowan berries.

The rowan bears fruit and flowers simultaneously. This is not normal phenology, but normal phenology is a concept developed in places that have seasons, and the hollow does not have seasons. It has temperature, and the temperature varies by two degrees, and two degrees is not a season. The rowan responds to the absence of seasonal signal by doing everything at once — flowering, fruiting, growing, shedding, all concurrently, all at a low level, a continuous, simultaneous performance of every phase of its reproductive cycle. The berries are pale orange, small, water-heavy. They are available at all times.

The thrush takes them. It perches on one of the rowan's horizontal branches — the tree grows outward rather than upward, spreading its canopy to catch what little light reaches the hollow, and its branches are well-suited to a bird's weight — and it reaches for the berries with its beak, swallows them whole, and leaves. The visit takes less than a minute. The bird does not call. It does not vocalize. It does not linger on the ground. It does not investigate the moss or the runnels or the snails. It comes for the berries, and the berries are there, and it takes them, and it goes.

Where it goes is unclear. The hollow opens onto the high country, and the high country is vast and exposed and dry, and the thrush is a bird of the open mountain, not of the enclosed wet. It does not belong here. It visits. It is a reminder that the hollow is connected to the larger world — connected by geology, by water, by the occasional animal that finds its way in and finds its way out again. But the connection is thin. The thrush takes berries, and it leaves, and it may carry the seeds of the rowan to some other place where they will not grow, because the conditions that allow the rowan to exist are specific to the hollow and its immediate margins. The seeds will be deposited on dry ground, or on wet ground that is not wet enough, or on ground that is wet enough but too warm, or too cold, or too exposed. They will not grow. The rowan does not spread. It simply is, where it is, for as long as the conditions hold.

The thrush does not know this. The thrush does not think about the rowan or the hollow or the seeds. It thinks about berries, if it thinks at all, and the berries are there, and it takes them, and the relationship between thrush and rowan is as simple and as one-sided as a relationship can be while still being a relationship.

Other things visit, even more rarely. A moth, perhaps, blundering in through the mist on a still night, attracted by nothing, trapped by the humidity, settling on the moss and being found by the beetles in the morning. A spider of a different species, ballooning in on a thread of silk from some point upwind, landing in the moss and either finding a niche or not finding a niche and dying without issue. A springtail, hopping in from the rock face, taking up residence in the litter and joining the thousands of springtails already there. These visits are not events. They are not significant. They are the almost-imperceptible leakage of the outside world into the inside world, and the inside world absorbs them the way it absorbs everything — slowly, uniformly, without discernible consequence.

## The things you cannot see

Beneath the visible fauna, in the water and the peat and the film on every surface, there is a world that is too small to see and too important to ignore.

The bacteria. They are everywhere in the hollow — in the water, in the peat, on every surface, inside every organism. They are the final decomposers, the things that break down what no other organism can break down, and their work is constant and invisible and essential. Without them, the peat would accumulate without limit, the dead would not return to the living, and the hollow would choke on its own productivity. With them, everything that dies is processed, slowly, in the cold, wet, low-oxygen environment of the saturated substrate, and the nutrients that were bound in dead tissue are liberated and made available to the living.

The fungi. Their hyphae permeate the peat and the leaf litter and the moss, a network of microscopic threads that is densest in the upper layers where oxygen is available and thins as the substrate becomes more anaerobic. They are the primary decomposers of plant matter — the things that break down cellulose and lignin, the structural components of the moss and the sedges and the rowan's fallen leaves. They are also, in some cases, symbionts — partners of the roots of the rowan and the vascular plants, exchanging minerals and water for the carbon compounds that the plants produce through photosynthesis. They are the hidden infrastructure of the hollow, the substrate's substrate, and their total biomass, if it could be measured, would almost certainly exceed the biomass of every visible organism in the hollow combined.

The protozoa. Single-celled, motile, predatory, they live in the film of water on every surface and in the runnels and in the saturated spaces between particles of peat. They eat bacteria. They are eaten, in turn, by the smallest of the crustaceans. They are a link in the chain so fine that it is almost not a link but a continuous transition — from the dissolved to the particulate, from the dead to the living, from the non-biological to the biological, with the protozoa as the junction.

The algae. On the rock face, in the runnels, on the surfaces of the moss and the sedges, a film of single-celled photosynthetic organisms grows in the perpetual wet. They are the hollow's primary producers alongside the vascular plants — the things that take light and carbon dioxide and water and make sugar, the things that convert the dim blue-grey luminance of the hollow into the energy that drives every other process. They are not dramatic. They are a film. A coating. A barely-visible tint on the wet stone that is darker where the algae are thicker and lighter where they are thinner. But they are feeding the snails, and the snails are feeding the beetles, and the beetles are feeding the spiders, and the spiders are feeding the moss, and the moss is feeding the crustaceans, and the crustaceans are feeding the algae, and the loop is closed, and the energy that entered as light is cycled and recycled and cycled again, and almost none of it leaves.

This is the thing about the hollow that is the most remarkable and the most difficult to convey: the visible organisms, the ones that can be seen and counted and described, are a small fraction of what lives here. The majority of life in the seep, by biomass, by diversity, by any measure that matters, is invisible. It is in the water and the peat and the film on the stone, and it has been there as long as the hollow has existed, and it will be there as long as the hollow continues, and it is, in the most literal sense, the foundation on which everything else is built. The snails and the beetles and the spider and the rowan and the moss are the visible expression of a system whose true bulk is microbial, whose true nature is chemical, whose true identity is the interaction between water and rock and light and time.

## The things that are not there

No amphibians. This is notable. In a place this wet, this stable, this rich in invertebrate life, you would expect frogs, or newts, or salamanders — something that requires permanent moisture and feeds on small invertebrates. But there are none. The hollow is too cold, perhaps — eight degrees Celsius is below the thermal tolerance of most amphibian eggs, which require warmer temperatures to develop. Or the hollow is too isolated — too far from other suitable habitat for a population to have colonized. Or the hollow is too small — too little space for a breeding population to sustain itself. Whatever the reason, the niche is empty, and the snails and the beetles and the crustaceans that would, in a larger or warmer wetland, be prey for amphibians are here free of that pressure, and their populations are shaped by other forces — by food, by space, by the predation of beetles and spiders, by the slow chemistry of the peat.

No fish. Obviously. The runnels are too shallow, too small, too temporary in their specific courses — they shift as the moss grows and the peat accumulates and the water finds new paths — to support fish. But the absence of fish is not just a consequence of size. It is a consequence of the water's chemistry. The seep is acidic, cold, low in dissolved oxygen, low in calcium. It is not fish water. It is barely insect water. It is crustacean water, and copepod water, and water flea water, and nematode water, and the water of things that can survive in conditions that would kill a fish in minutes.

No mammals. No shrews, no voles, no mice. The hollow is too wet for burrowing, too small for foraging, too enclosed for escape. A shrew could hunt here — the invertebrate density would support it — but a shrew requires dryness for resting and breeding, and the hollow offers none. The ground is saturated. Every surface is wet. There is no place to build a nest, no place to cache food, no place to be still without being damp. The mammals of the high country pass by the hollow without entering it, or if they enter, they do not stay.

No reptiles. The temperature is too low. Eight degrees Celsius is below the thermal minimum for every reptile in the region. A reptile that entered the hollow would become sluggish, then torpid, then dead. The hollow is a reptile trap, and no reptile has fallen into it, or if one has, it died and was decomposed and its calcium — if it had any, if the bones were not dissolved by the acidic water first — returned to the peat, and there is no evidence it was ever there.

No humans. This is the most significant absence, and the least accidental. The hollow does not repel humans. It does not repel anything. It simply does not accommodate them. It is too wet, too dark, too small, too still, too enclosed, too changeless. There is nothing here that a human needs. No resource that is not available more conveniently elsewhere. No shelter that is not better provided by a dry overhang or a cave. No view, no passage, no strategic advantage, no spiritual significance that has been recorded or remembered. The hollow is, for humans, a null space — a place that exists without reference to human use, without acknowledgment of human presence, without the slightest concession to the fact that humans exist at all.

And this is why the hollow is what it is. This is why the snails are too numerous and the silence is too complete and the light is too dim and the rowan fruits out of season and the spider's web is always in the same place. The hollow has been left alone. Not protected — left alone. Not preserved — left alone. The difference matters. Protection implies intention. Preservation implies a preserver. The hollow has been left alone by default, by the simple fact that nothing about it attracts or requires or even permits sustained human attention, and this default leaving-alone has allowed the system to develop to a state of equilibrium that would be impossible in a place that humans visited, used, modified, or even noticed.

The hollow is not pristine. "Pristine" implies a before, a moment when the place was untouched and a moment when it was touched and a distinction between the two. The hollow has no such moment. It has been continuously what it is for longer than anything alive can remember — longer than the rowan, longer than the oldest beetle lineage, longer than the particular community of snails that currently occupies the space. It was what it is before humans existed. It will be what it is after humans are gone, unless the mountain's plumbing changes or the climate shifts or the rock walls fall. It does not care. It is not waiting. It is not enduring. It is simply, continuously, quietly, wet.

## What lives in the seep

What lives in the seep is not a list of species. It is not a community, in the ecological sense, though it is also that. It is not an ecosystem, though it functions as one. What lives in the seep is a process — a single, continuous, self-sustaining process that has been running for longer than any of its components have been alive, a process that manifests as snails and beetles and crustaceans and moss and a single rowan tree and a single spider in a single web, but that is none of those things individually and all of those things together and more than all of those things together, because the together is the point, the together is the thing, the together is what lives here.

The water is the process. The water emerges from the rock and sheets across the stone and collects in the runnels and crosses the floor of the hollow and sinks into the peat and is taken up by the moss and is transpired by the sedges and is condensed on the spider's web and is drunk by the snails and is breathed by the beetles and is filtered by the crustaceans and is moved by the fungi and is moved by the bacteria and is moved by the roots of the rowan, and all of this movement is the same movement, the water moving through the system the way blood moves through a body, and the system is the body, and the body is what lives here.

The mountain weeps, and the weeping is not grief. It is geology. But the geology produces something that, if you stood in it long enough, if you let the moisture settle on your skin and the silence fill your ears and the dim light enter your eyes, you might be tempted to call alive. Not the individual lives — those are obvious, those are visible, those can be counted and described and named. The other aliveness. The aliveness of the system. The aliveness of a place where every process is connected to every other process and every connection is stable and every stability feeds back into the system and the feedback is the thing that keeps everything the same and the sameness is not death but a particular kind of life, a life that does not require change to be alive, a life that has found, in the wet and the dark and the cold and the still, a way to continue without moving, to grow without progressing, to be without becoming.

The snails graze. The beetles hunt. The crustaceans filter. The flies drift. The spider waits. The moss absorbs. The rowan bears fruit and flowers at the same time, always. The water moves. The water does not move.

Everything is exactly as it was.
