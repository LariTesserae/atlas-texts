# The Keeper of the Cistern of Voices

She was not the first. The groove in the passage wall — left side, at hip height, where a hand has trailed for generations — is deeper than her hand could make. Others came before. Others sat where she sits. Others tended the brazier and listened to the water and watched the frogs and understood what the cistern was for.

But she is the one here now.

---

## Her Name and Not-Her-Name

She has a name she was given and a name she took. The given name belongs to the world above — to the settlement, the family, the register of persons. She uses it when she climbs the passage and emerges blinking into daylight to trade small frog-preserved things for oil and cloth and the iron rods she reshapes into pins for the brazier. She uses it when someone asks. People ask less often than they used to.

The name she took is not a word. It is a posture. A way of sitting with her back against the warm stone and her hands open on her knees, palms up, receiving the sound. She found this posture in the first weeks after she came down, and it became a name because she began to answer to it — not with a sound, but with a settling, a recognition. The cistern named her. Or she named herself by the way she fit into the cistern. The distinction does not matter to her.

She was a potter before. This matters.

---

## How She Came to Be Here

The cistern had been without a keeper for perhaps a decade — long enough that two of the channel-spouts had clogged with windblown debris, long enough that the brazier had rusted through its base and left an orange shadow on the stone floor, long enough that one of the stone plugs in the sealed passages had been pushed inward by groundwater pressure and left a slow seep to widen into a trickle that flooded the third basin and drowned its mineral patterns under a centimeter of extra water. The frogs had managed. The insects had managed. The lizard had managed. The cistern does not need a keeper to survive. It needs a keeper to remain itself.

She came because she was looking for a place that was already in its own state. She had been making pots — good pots, fired hard, glazed in the pale green that the local clay produces when you push the kiln past the point where most potters pull — and she had begun to feel that the pots were not the point. The point was the kiln. The point was the state the kiln reached when it had been hot for hours and the heat was no longer something she was doing but something the kiln was being. The point was the moment when a system reaches equilibrium and you can stop forcing it and simply witness it.

She wanted to live in that moment. Someone told her about the cistern. She came down through the throat of fitted stone and did not leave.

That was — she is not certain. Long. She measures time by the brazier's fuel consumption and by the slow migration of the light-slots' illumination across the floor as the seasons turn. She has seen the illumination reach its northernmost point and return to its southernmost point perhaps eleven times. Or twelve. The ambiguity does not trouble her.

## What She Looks Like

She is not what you would expect, if you expected a hermit. She is not wasted. She is not wild-haired. She is not wearing rags or skins or the conventional costume of withdrawal.

She is compact. Not small — compact. The density of a body that has been shaped by its environment. Her shoulders have narrowed to fit the passage. Her calves have thickened from the constant descent and ascent. Her hands are rough but not broken — the stone is smooth, and rough hands come from rough work, and her work is mostly fine. She tends small things. She clears channel-spouts with a sliver of bone. She lifts frogs from the edge of the brazier's heat and returns them to the basins. She scrapes the mold back from its boundary when it advances past the line it has held for longer than her tenure.

Her skin has darkened unevenly. The parts of her that face the fire — left side, left arm, left cheek — are darker and drier than the parts that face the water. The right side of her face is paler, dampened by the mist that rises from the basins nearest her sitting place. She is two-toned. Fire and water. She did not plan this. It happened.

Her hair is short. She cuts it with a stone flake when it interferes with her work. The result is not a style but a series of accidents that average to a rough cap. It has gone the color of the sandstone — pale, bone-dust — though it was dark when she came down. She does not know if this is age or mineral. The water she washes with is heavy with calcium and iron. It may be changing her the way it changed the basin lips. She is being deposited upon. Slowly. Over years.

Her eyes have adapted. She can see in the amber-gray light as if it were full daylight. When she climbs the passage and emerges into the sun, she is blind for several minutes. She has learned to sit at the passage's mouth and wait. The people from the settlement have learned to wait with her. They do not speak during this time. They have understood, without being told, that she is between states and should not be addressed until she has arrived.

She wears cloth. Plain, undyed, wrapped in layers that she can add or remove as the cistern's temperature shifts — and it does shift, slightly, with the seasons and with the volume of water flowing through. She washes the cloth in the second-highest basin, which is not used for settling because its channel-spout clogged long ago and she has not cleared it. She likes the stagnant water. It has a different quality. The cloth comes out mineral-stiff and she wears it that way until it softens, then washes it again. The cloth is slowly becoming stone. She finds this appropriate.

## What She Does

The brazier is the center of her practical work. She tends it the way a potter tends a kiln — not constantly, but with an awareness of its state that operates beneath conscious attention. She knows by the smell of the air when it needs fuel. She knows by the color of the iron when the draft has shifted. She knows by the way the frogs behave — they move closer to the brazier when the chamber cools and away from it when it warms, and their distribution across the basins is a thermometer she reads without thinking.

She feeds it with charcoal she makes herself, in a small pit outside the passage's mouth, from scrub wood and dried moss. She carries the charcoal down in a leather bag that she has patched so many times it is more patch than bag. The brazier holds enough for a day and a half of low burning. She does not let it flame. Flame is waste. She keeps it at the orange-black edge of combustion, the state where heat is maximum and light is minimum. This is the kiln-keeper's instinct. She has not stopped being a potter. She is simply firing a different vessel.

She clears the channel-spouts. This is delicate work. The spouts are finger-width, cut into the stone blocks at angles that were precise when they were cut and have shifted slightly as the stone has settled. She uses a thin tool — the rib of a small animal, sharpened and shaped — to probe the blockages. Most are silt. Some are mineral — calcium deposits that narrow the spout over years. She chips these carefully, restoring the flow without widening the channel. The flow rates matter. The polyrhythm of the cistern depends on each spout delivering water at a different speed, and if she standardizes them, the sound will flatten. She learned this in her first year when she cleared three spouts to the same diameter and the chamber's voice went dull for two weeks until the deposits re-established the variation.

She does not clear all the spouts. Two remain clogged — the one in the third basin, which she has left because the seep from the broken plug provides enough water there, and one in the highest basin, which she has left because its silence is part of the rhythm. The absence of sound is a sound. She understands this. The cistern taught her.

She manages the ecosystem. Not intensively — she is not a farmer. But she intervenes when the balance shifts. When the frog population in the lowest basin grows too dense, she moves individuals to the higher basins, where the water is warmer and the insects are fewer. When the mold on the upper walls advances past its boundary — which happens in the wet season, when the airflow through the vents slows — she scrapes it back with a flat stone, carefully, leaving the root-filaments intact so it can regrow to its line and hold. When the lizard's wall-groove begins to collect water and grow algae that would make the surface too slick for its feet, she dries the groove with a cloth and scratches it rough again with the tip of her bone tool.

She does not think of these actions as maintenance. She thinks of them as conversation. The cistern speaks through its states — the water level, the frog density, the mold line, the lizard's path — and she responds. The responses are small. A nudge. A correction. The cistern does not need her to function. It needs her to remain itself, and the difference between functioning and remaining itself is the difference between a clock that is running and a clock that is keeping time.

## What She Hears

She hears the cistern the way a musician hears an orchestra — not as a single sound but as a structure of sounds, each one distinct, each one related to the others by intervals and durations that she has come to know so well that variation is immediately apparent.

The seventh spout from the left on the western wall has a catch in its flow — a momentary interruption every fourth beat, caused by a small irregularity in the channel that creates a bubble. She hears this. She would notice if it stopped.

The lowest basin has a resonance. The water that drains through the square hole at its center produces a low tone — not a pitch, exactly, but a hum, a vibration that she feels in the stone she sits on more than she hears with her ears. This tone changes with the water level. When the flow is heavy, the tone drops. When the flow is light, it rises. She has come to use this tone as a barometer. She knows the weather above by the tone of the drain. Rain is coming. The tone tells her before the spouts do.

The frogs are percussion. Their jumping is not random — it follows the rhythm of the water, or perhaps the water follows the rhythm of their jumping. She has watched long enough to suspect that the frogs prefer to jump at the intervals between the spouts' strikes, filling the silences the water leaves. If this is true, it is not intention. It is adaptation. The frogs have learned to jump when the sound is lowest because the sound of their landing is least likely to be masked, which means their neighbors hear them, which means the population distributes itself by sound. She does not know if this is what is happening. She suspects. She has been suspecting for years. The suspicion is enough.

The insects are a drone. A constant, a ground, the thing beneath the thing. She has stopped hearing them as separate. They are the cistern's breath, the way the air movement through the vents is the cistern's breath. The insects are the sound of life at its smallest, and they do not vary, and their constancy is what allows the other sounds to vary against them. They are the silence that is not silence. She values them for this.

She hears the lizard. Not its movement — it is silent on the stone — but the dimple in the insect cloud when its tongue strikes. A tiny absence of sound, a hole in the drone, lasting less than a second. She counts these. On a good day, the lizard eats forty times. On a bad day, twenty. She does not know what makes a good day or a bad day for the lizard. She watches. She will know eventually. She has time.

## What She Knows

She knows the cistern was built by people who understood it better than she does. The engineering — the settling basins, the flow rates, the drain — is beyond her. She can maintain it. She cannot replicate it. The people who built it were not potters. They were something else. Something she does not have a word for. Engineers of sound and water and stone, people who could hear the polyrhythm they were building before it existed, who placed the spouts at different heights not by calculation but by ear, who shaped the basins to follow the stone's preferences because they understood that stone has preferences and that ignoring them is a kind of violence.

She knows the cistern is old. Older than the settlement above. Older than the language spoken there. The stone plugs in the sealed passages are shaped with a precision that her bone tools cannot match. The blocks of the walls are fitted so tightly that she has tried, idly, to slide a hair between them and failed. Whoever built this place had a relationship with stone that she cannot imagine. They were not separate from it. They were in conversation with it, the way she is in conversation with the cistern, but deeper, more intimate, a conversation that produced fitted blocks and cork-shaped plugs and basin lips that follow bedding planes as if the stone itself were asked where it wanted to break.

She knows the cistern is not only a cistern. The light-slots prove this. The acoustics prove this. The fact that someone sat here, for generations, and listened — this proves it. The cistern is a place. A place where people came to hear something. She does not know what they heard. She hears what the cistern sounds like now, in its age, in its equilibrium, in its long after-state. She does not know what it sounded like when it was young, when the stone was fresh and the basins were unstained and the water ran clear and the polyrhythm was being tuned for the first time.

She suspects it sounded worse. She suspects the cistern needed centuries to become itself. She suspects she is hearing it in its maturity, its voice settled and complex the way an old voice is settled and complex, and that the people who built it did not hear what she hears. They built it to hear it. They did not get to hear it. This is the nature of building something that takes longer than a life to mature.

She finds this beautiful. She finds it the most beautiful thing she knows. They built a thing they would not live to hear. She lives in a thing she did not build. This is the trade. This is the conversation across time. She does not know their names. They did not know hers. The cistern knows both and neither and does not care.

## What She Does Not Do

She does not write. She does not carve. She does not leave marks. The cistern has enough marks. Her presence is recorded in the worn spot where she sits, in the ash of the brazier, in the cleared spouts and the managed mold. She does not need to add to this. She is a mark. The cistern is the surface. She will be absorbed.

She does not pray. She has been asked about this by the people from the settlement, who assume that living in a place like this must be a religious act. She does not know how to explain that it is not. The cistern is not a god. It is not a temple. It is a place. Prayer requires a direction — outward, upward, toward something else. The cistern is here. It is entirely here. There is nothing beyond it, nothing above it, nothing it points to. It points to itself. It is a place that is about being a place. She sits in it and is about being a person in a place. This is not prayer. This is presence.

She does not leave. She leaves the cistern — she climbs the passage, she trades, she returns — but she does not leave it. The distinction is this: when she is above, she is still in the cistern. She carries it. The tone of the drain hums in her sternum. The frogs' eyes glow behind her own. The amber-gray light colors everything she sees, so that daylight is a temporary condition, a departure from the real light, which is the light of fire and stone and water meeting in a small warm space underground. She returns because she never fully left. She returns because the cistern is where she already is.

She does not explain this. She has tried. The words make it smaller. The cistern is not small. The cistern is the size of itself, which is the size of a modest chapel, which is the size of a world, which is the size of whatever you can hold when you stop trying to hold more than what is here.

---

## The Night

The daylight slots go dark. The brazier's fire becomes the only light. The chamber contracts. The walls, which were pale bone in the daylight, become amber and then umber and then a darkness that is not black but warm-brown, the color of the stone's heat made visible. The frogs' eyes catch the firelight and become a constellation — dozens of gold points scattered across the basins, some clustered, some solitary, some moving in slow arcs as their owners swim or drift.

She sits. The stone is warm against her back. The water flows. The spouts strike their surfaces. The polyrhythm continues. The insects orbit. The lizard has retired to a crack between two blocks near the ceiling, its pale belly pressed to the stone, its eyes closed or not closed — she cannot tell, and it does not matter.

She is not waiting. She is not meditating. She is not performing solitude. She is sitting in a place that is alive, and she is part of its life, and the life is the sound of water on stone and the movement of small bodies in small basins and the slow breath of air through vents and the steady heat of the rock and the fire and the decomposition and the growth.

She is the keeper. She keeps the cistern the way you keep a breath — not by holding it, but by staying with it, by letting it move through its cycle, by being the body in which it moves.

The cistern speaks. She listens. This is enough. This has been enough for eleven years or twelve. This will be enough for however many more she has, and then she will be gone, and the cistern will continue, and someone else will come down through the throat of fitted stone, and the frogs will jump, and the water will flow, and the sound will be the same and different and the same.

The cistern does not need her. She has needed it. This is the shape of her life: a need that found its answer and stayed.
