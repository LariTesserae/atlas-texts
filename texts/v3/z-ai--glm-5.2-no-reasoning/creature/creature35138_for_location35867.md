# The Workers

They are not a people. That distinction matters to them, though they would never say so, because the word *people* implies a origin, a history that predates the place, and they do not have one that anyone can verify. They are not born here. They do not die here, exactly. They arrive.

---

Arrival is unremarked. Someone is simply present at the processing platform one morning, or standing at the lock gates watching the barge return, or sleeping in a dome that had been empty the night before. The others notice the way you notice a new pool in the root-mat — the ground was wet there, and now there is water. It was always likely. No one remarks on it. The new one is handed a saw and shown where to stand so the sound of cutting does not carry to the far wall.

Where they come from is not discussed. Not because it is forbidden but because the question does not seem to produce answers. Ask a worker where they were before the Reedworks and they will pause, and the pause is not evasion — it is the genuine blankness of someone trying to remember a dream. They were somewhere. They have a sense of having been somewhere. But the specifics dissolve, or were never specific. A few retain fragments: a dry wind, a horizon, a white sky. One worker, Maret, who has been here longest, remembers standing water and the smell of salt, and believes she came from a coast, though she cannot say which coast or name the body of water. She does not seem troubled by this. The Reedworks consumes specifics. It is a place that reduces language to the present tense and the practical mood.

This is not amnesia. It is not damage. It is more like the way the root-mat absorbs fallen stalks — the material is taken in, broken down, and incorporated. The workers are not hollow. They have memories. But the memories are increasingly of the Reedworks: the morning the press jammed and Pol had to reach into the rollers up to his elbow, the sound of his forearm bone resetting, the week the channel nearly closed and every worker spent eighteen-hour shifts in the water cutting reed by hand. They remember each other. They remember the work. The rest becomes, over time, the way the outside world is beyond the reed forest — known to exist, unreachable, increasingly hypothetical.

---

There are seventeen now. The number has been as low as nine and as high as twenty-three. Workers leave, though *leave* is the wrong word, as *arrive* is the wrong word. A worker is present, and then they are not. Sometimes this happens during sleep — they go to their dome and do not wake, and in the morning the dome has already begun to close, the trained reed coils tightening, the metabolic heat inside rising as the plant reclaims the space. By afternoon there is a sealed green nodule where a shelter was, and within days it is indistinguishable from the perimeter wall. Sometimes a worker simply stops. Mid-task, mid-sentence. They set down the saw or the bundle of sheets or the kettle and they stand, and they do not move, and their eyes do not focus, and the others guide them to the root-mat near the wall and leave them there. By morning they are part of the ground. The root-mat takes them the way it takes fallen reed: slowly, completely, without fuss.

This is not death. The workers do not have a word for it. When Maret was asked, once, by a newer worker — one who still remembered enough of outside to have the concept — what happens to those who stop, she said: "They go to the roots." She did not say it with sorrow. She said it the way she would say "the channel needs clearing" or "the engine wants fuel." A statement of mechanical fact.

Pol, who was standing nearby, added: "Everything here goes to the roots." He touched the root-mat with his foot. "This was probably a person once. This part here." He did not mean it as philosophy. He was identifying a resource.

---

They are not young. They are not old. They appear to be what used to be called middle-aged, though the category means less here. Their bodies are lean in the way of people who work constantly and eat enough but not more. They eat reed, of course — the young shoots, the pale stems that have not yet accumulated silica, boiled in kerosene pots over small fires built in metal trays on the processing platform. The shoots are bland and faintly sweet, with a texture like bamboo. They provide enough. No one is hungry, and no one is full.

Their hands are the thing to notice. Every worker's hands are the same: broad-palmed, thick-fingered, the skin dried and retextured by constant exposure to reed-water and silica dust. The fingertips are calloused to the point of numbness, which is useful, because the reed cuts — the edges of split stems are sharp as glass, and a worker who is not careful will open their hand on a fresh-cut stalk without feeling it until they see the blood, which is bright and strange against the green. The blood falls on the root-mat and is absorbed. The reed does not mind blood. The root-mat takes it the way it takes everything.

Their skin has a quality that is difficult to describe. Not green — nothing so dramatic. But there is a tint, a subcutaneous suggestion of chlorophyll, or of something like chlorophyll, that becomes more pronounced the longer a worker has been here. Maret's forearms, where she has rolled her sleeves past the elbow, show a definite olive undertone that is not tan. Pol's shins, bare below his cut trousers, have the same. Newer workers do not have it. It develops over months, over years. No one mentions it. It is not clear whether the workers are aware of it, or whether it matters.

Their hair grows slowly. Their nails grow slowly. Their wounds heal slowly but they heal. Pol's forearm, the one that was caught in the press, healed over six weeks into a thickened, ridged scar that is darker green than the surrounding skin. He can still use the arm. He favors it slightly. He says it aches when the engine is off, as if the silence presses on the scar the way the root-mat presses on everything.

---

Maret is the closest thing to a leader, which is to say she has been here the longest and she knows the sound-map of the hollow perfectly and she can sense when the channel is narrowing before anyone else can — a change in the air pressure, a thickening, she says, like the hollow is holding its breath. She is not large. She is not commanding. She moves through the space with the unhurried certainty of someone who has walked the same twenty meters a thousand times, and her voice, when she speaks, is pitched to the acoustic bright spot near the drying frame, so that it carries to the far wall without effort. She is the one who teaches the new workers. Not by instruction — she does not explain — but by proximity. You stand near Maret. You watch. You learn.

She has a habit of touching the reed. Not the cut stalks, not the processed material. The living reed. She will place her palm flat against a stalk at the perimeter wall and stand for a moment, eyes half-closed, as if listening. She says she can feel the water moving inside. She says the reed tells her when it is going to push inward, which sections are weak, which are about to calcify. The other workers do not do this. They do not doubt her. They simply do not have, or have not developed, whatever it is that allows Maret to read the reed through her skin. When asked about it she looks confused, as if asked how she knows which way is down. "It's right there," she says. "It's always saying something."

---

Pol is the engineer, though that is not a title anyone uses. He maintains the engine. He built the press, or rebuilt it, or modified it — the original design predates anyone's memory and the machine has been repaired so many times that no original components remain, which makes it the same machine or a different one, a question that does not interest Pol. He is interested in tolerances. He is interested in the way the reed sheets curl if they are pressed at the wrong temperature. He is interested in the firebox, which cracks every few months and must be patched with reed-pulp cement that he mixes himself in a ratio he has never written down. He is not a large man but he has a physical confidence that comes from understanding mechanical systems: the press will do what the press will do, and his job is to know what that is before it happens.

He talks to the engine. Not in words — in the sounds he makes back at it. When the chuffing changes rhythm he will match it with a low hum from his chest, as if he and the machine are breathing together. He can diagnose the engine by feel, by vibration through the platform, by the way the steam smells. He says the engine is the only thing in the Reedworks that is not alive, and he says this with a tenderness that suggests he considers this a vulnerability rather than a virtue.

He sleeps near the engine. His dome is the one closest to the processing platform, and he has cut a small window in the reed-coil wall — the only worker who has done this — through which he can see the dim shape of the press in the green phosphorescence. He says the engine is quieter when it knows it is being watched. He is not joking. He is not entirely serious. The distinction does not apply.

---

There is a worker named Sev who arrived, or appeared, during a night when the engine was down and the hollow was silent and the green glow was at its brightest. Maret found them standing at the center of the hollow, barefoot on the root-mat, looking up at the canopy. Their feet had already begun to sink. Maret pulled them onto the platform and gave them Pol's spare clogs and they have been here since.

Sev does not speak. This is not a vow, not a disability, not a choice that anyone can identify. Sev can hear, understands instructions, follows the work rhythm. They communicate by gesture, by pointing, by a system of hand signs that seems partly invented and partly borrowed from something none of the others recognize. When something needs to be said that cannot be said with hands — which is rare, in this place — Sev will take a stick and draw in the fibrous pulp on the processing platform. The drawings are crude but sufficient. REED PUSHING AT NORTH WALL. ENGINE WANTS WATER. The messages are always practical. They are always about the Reedworks. Sev has never drawn anything that was not about the work.

Sev is the one who works the channel. This is the hardest labor and the most necessary: standing in the flat-bottomed barge, waist-deep in the narrow waterway, cutting the reed that grows inward from both sides, the stalks emerging from the water like fingers reaching. The channel is perhaps three meters wide and the reed narrows it by a centimeter a day and Sev spends four hours each morning cutting it back, the saw making a sound in the enclosed channel that is muffled and wet and distant. The reed in the channel is different from the reed in the hollow — darker, more flexible, growing from the root-mat below the water's surface, and it is this reed that Sev cuts and loads into the barge and takes back through the lock to the processing shed. Channel reed is softer, less silica, easier to press. It makes the best sheets.

Sev knows the channel the way Maret knows the reed. They can navigate the waterway in the dark, which they sometimes do, when the green glow is enough and the lantern fuel is low. They know every curve, every place where the bottom is shallow, every section where the root-mat has risen and needs to be cut back. They work the channel alone. No one joins them. This is not a rule. It is simply understood that the channel is Sev's, the way the engine is Pol's, the way the reed-reading is Maret's. The labor divides itself.

When Sev returns from the channel, dripping, their skin flushed darker from the tannin-stained water, they stand for a moment at the lock and look back down the waterway. No one knows what they are looking at. No one asks. They step onto the platform, set down the saw, and begin unloading the barge.

---

The workers sleep in the domes, two or three to a dome, on matting woven from stripped reed fiber. The domes are warm — the living reed generates metabolic heat, and the interior temperature is several degrees higher than the hollow, which is itself warmer than the channel or the forest. They sleep deeply, the way people sleep when their bodies are tired and their minds are empty. They do not dream, or if they dream, they do not remember. Maret once said she dreamed of a sound, a low hum, like the reed-growing sound but louder, and she woke to find that the reed-growing sound was, in fact, louder — a section of the perimeter wall had thickened overnight and was pressing inward. She did not consider the dream significant. She considered the wall significant.

They do not pair. They do not form bonds in the way that implies exclusivity, though there is a gentleness between them that could be called love if it were larger, or if it needed a name. They touch rarely. When they do it is brief and practical: a hand on a shoulder to move someone from a dead acoustic spot, a grip on an arm to steady a worker reaching for a high stalk. Pol sometimes rests his hand on the engine housing the way another person might rest a hand on a sleeping animal. Maret touches the reed. Sev, once, was seen standing in the channel with their eyes closed and their hands submerged, palms up, in the dark water.

They share the work. They share the food. They share the space. There is no hierarchy — Maret's authority is the authority of experience and acoustics, nothing more. If she says "the north wall needs cutting" the workers go to the north wall because she is right, not because she has told them. If Pol says "the engine needs an hour" the press stops for an hour because Pol knows the engine, not because Pol has the power to halt production. Decisions are made by proximity and competence. The person closest to the problem solves the problem. There is no other system. There has never needed to be.

---

They do not have a religion. They do not have ceremonies. They do not mark time in days or weeks or months — time here is measured in the growth of reed, in the narrowing of the channel, in the cycle of the press. A "day" is the period between one sleep and the next, which is roughly the time it takes for the channel to narrow enough to need cutting again. The engine's rhythm is the clock. When it runs, it is day. When it stops, it is night. This is sufficient.

But there is something. Not a practice, not a belief — a posture. When the engine is off and the hollow is in its green silence and the reed-growing hum is audible, the workers who are awake will sometimes stop what they are doing and stand still. Not all at once. Not coordinated. One will stop, and then another, and they will stand in the dark-green light and listen, and the listening is not directed — they are not listening *to* something. They are listening *in* something. In the way you stand in rain and do not try to feel individual drops. The hum is in the ground and in the air and in the root-mat under their clogs and in the bones of their feet, and they stand in it for a moment, or for several moments, and then they resume.

Maret calls this "hearing the reed think." She does not mean it metaphorically.

---

They are not unhappy. This must be understood clearly, because the description of their lives — the labor, the silence, the dissolution of memory, the slow assimilation into the place — describes something that, from outside, resembles captivity or decline. It is not. The workers are not trapped. They are not diminished. They are adapted.

Adaptation is not the same as resignation. The reed forest is a closed system, and the workers have become part of the closure. They are the only part of the system that cuts. The reed grows; the workers cut. The reed consumes its dead; the workers make the dead into sheets and send them out through the lock to wherever the channel goes, which they do not know and do not ask about. They are the system's way of producing something other than more of itself. They are the exception. They are small and they are few and they are temporary, and they know this, and they work.

The Reedworks does not need them. But the Reedworks is different because they are here. The channel exists because Sev cuts it. The sheets exist because Pol presses them. The wall is maintained because Maret reads it. The reed would grow inward and the hollow would close and the place would be forest again, and the forest would not notice, and the workers know this, and they cut the reed anyway, and the channel stays open another centimeter, another day, and the engine chuffs in the green dark, and the water falls from below, and the sound of growing is the sound of everything except them, and they are the sound of cutting, and the two sounds together are the sound of the Reedworks, which is a place that exists because something alive is being kept from closing.

---

They are quiet people. They work. They go to the roots. They are replaced. The engine runs.
