# What Lives Here

## The First Principle

Nothing here was born. That needs to be understood before anything else, because the word *born* carries assumptions — parentage, genetics, a moment of emergence, a before and an after. What lives in the Condensate Spires was *specified*. Decided. The way a crystal is decided when the conditions are right and the atoms know where to go. Not built by a hand. Not grown from a seed. Specified by a system that understood what it needed and produced it from the materials available, which were: water, metal, glass, time, and a kind of patience that doesn't belong to anything with a heartbeat.

But they live. They are alive in the way a fire is alive — consuming, transforming, responding, requiring fuel, ceasing when the fuel ceases. They are alive in the way a reef is alive — structural, collective, defined by their relationships to each other and to the architecture that houses them. They are alive in the way an immune system is alive — not as a thing apart, but as a function of a larger body. The city is the body. They are its living parts.

There are three broad kinds of them, though *kinds* is a simplification, because the categories bleed into each other the way the water bleeds into the air. What follows is a description, not a taxonomy. A taxonomy would require understanding what they're for. Nobody understands what they're for. They might not be *for* anything. They might be the purpose themselves.

## The Channel-Things

### What You See

The water moves in the channels beneath the grating, and the things in the water move with it, against it, across it. You catch them as interruptions in the rust-glow. Dark shapes. Elongated. Segmented — but not in the way a worm is segmented, with visible divisions. More like a column of water that has been slightly more organized than the water around it. The segments are density gradients. Places where the body is slightly more itself and slightly less the water it moves through.

They are not fully solid. This is important. They have bodies, but the boundary between body and medium is negotiated, not declared. When a channel-thing moves through a narrow passage, it narrows. When it enters a wider channel, it extends. When the water flow reverses — and it does, periodically, the system cycling with a slowness that suggests tidal forces or something acting like tidal forces — the channel-things reverse with it, but not immediately. They resist. They have preferences about direction. This is the simplest sign that they are not just debris: they want things.

The largest ones are perhaps three meters long. The smallest are a few centimeters. The size range is continuous — there is no gap between large and small, no distinct size classes. This suggests that they grow, or accrete, or are produced at whatever size is needed for whatever they are doing. You do not see them reproduce. You do not see them die. You see them at different sizes, and the sizes shift, and the shifting is slow enough that it might be growth or might be something else — a body adding and subtracting mass the way the spires add and subtract layers, in response to conditions.

### What They Do

They move through the channels. They move through the channels constantly. The channels branch and reconverge — a circulatory system is the right metaphor, and it keeps being the right metaphor, because the channel-things behave exactly like cells in a circulatory system. They are transporting something. The water carries minerals — iron, sulfur, the compounds that don't appear on the periodic table as commonly understood — and the channel-things are interacting with those minerals. When a channel-thing passes through a stretch of water, the mineral content of the water behind it is different from the mineral content ahead of it. It is taking something up. It is depositing something. It is *processing* the water.

The processing is the purpose. The water enters the channels carrying raw material — dissolved compounds from the plateau, from the volcanic glass, from the metal layers themselves — and the channel-things sort it. Refine it. Some compounds they bind to their own surfaces, and those compounds are later deposited at junctions in the channel system where the metal layers are growing. Other compounds they break down — chemically, somehow, through processes that happen inside their semi-solid bodies — and the breakdown products are carried onward, to be used elsewhere. The channel-things are the chemical works of the city. They are the furnaces and the filters and the separators, and they do this work by being in the water and letting the water pass through them.

They are not mechanistic. They are not little robots following programs. When two channel-things encounter each other in a narrow passage, they negotiate. They slow. They adjust. One presses to the wall of the channel while the other passes. Sometimes they touch — and when they touch, there is an exchange, a visible darkening at the point of contact, as if something has been transferred. Information? Chemicals? Both? They have a social life. It is a social life conducted in milliseconds and millimeters, but it is a social life, and it means they are not merely components. They are agents. They make choices. The choices are small — which side of the channel to pass on, how fast to move, when to stop and process and when to keep flowing — but they are choices, and they are made, and they are not always made the same way.

### What They Feel Like

If you reach through the grating — and you shouldn't, but you could — and a channel-thing brushes your hand, you would feel a pressure. Not solid, not liquid. A thickening. Like putting your hand into a current and finding the current has a preference about where your hand goes. The temperature would be slightly warmer than the water around it — a degree, maybe less. The surface, such as it is, would feel like the inside of a cheek. Smooth. Wet. Giving. Alive in the most discomfiting sense: alive the way your own insides are alive, which is to say, not for you.

They would not attack you. They would not flee. They would register you the way they register a change in mineral concentration — as input. As data. As something to be processed, categorized, responded to. If your hand stayed in the water long enough, the channel-things would begin to interact with your skin — testing its chemical composition, tasting its salts, reading its structure. They would not damage you. They would simply *know* you, in a chemical, intimate, unconsenting way, and then they would move on, because you are not part of the system, and they have no mechanism for incorporating things that are not part of the system. You would be a leaf in a river. Noted. Passed.

## The Cloud-Bound

### What You See

Look up. The cloud-ceiling is fifty meters above, maybe less, and it is not empty. The shapes hang there — in the cloud, from the cloud, as part of the cloud — and they are the largest things in this place that you can see whole.

They are not solid. This needs to be said again, because the mind wants to make them solid. The mind wants to see bodies — leathery, membrane-winged, animal bodies, like bats or like the things that hang from cave ceilings in the stories you grew up with. They are not that. They are *condensations*. Organized concentrations of the water that permeates everything here. They are to the cloud what the channel-things are to the water in the channels: a pattern imposed on a medium. The medium is moisture. The pattern is *body*.

The smallest is the size of a human torso. The largest is the size of a vehicle — a bus, maybe, or a small truck. They hang in the cloud and they are slightly luminous, that same rust-tired-brown luminescence that rises from the channels below, as if the light is being recycled through the system, passed from water to air to water again. Their edges are not defined. They blur. Moisture drifts off them, falls from them, is reabsorbed. They are continuously exchanging mass with the cloud they live in. They are not *in* the cloud the way a bird is in a tree. They are *of* the cloud the way a wave is of the ocean.

They move. The dynamic parameter is 0.6 — patience is the rule here — and the cloud-bound move with patience. They turn. They shift. They drift from one position to another over minutes, hours, settling and resettling in the cloud layer like cattle in a field, like a herd that has nowhere to go and all of time to get there. When they move, the cloud moves with them — not because they displace it, but because they are part of it, and their movement reorganizes the whole.

### What They Do

They sing.

The harmonic drone that fills the Condensate Spires — the sound that you feel in your molars, in your sternum, the sound that is the dominant fact of this place — is partly the water moving through channels and partly the spires resonating and partly the cloud-bound. Their contribution is subsonic. Below hearing, properly speaking. You feel it as pressure, as vibration, as a hum in your chest that doesn't correspond to your heartbeat. It is communication. It is not language in any sense that a linguist would recognize — no words, no grammar, no symbols — but it is communication. The cloud-bound are exchanging something. State information. Positional data. The chemical composition of the moisture they're condensing from. The state of the cloud. The state of the city below.

They are also processing water. This is the unifying function: everything here processes water. The channel-things process the water in the channels. The cloud-bound process the water in the air. They take it in — by condensing it on their surfaces, by absorbing it, by *being* the condensation — and they change it. They add compounds. They remove compounds. They sort the water the way a sorter sorts mail, by destination and content, and they release it, and it falls, and it enters the channels, and the channel-things process it further. The cycle is: water rises. Water is processed by the cloud-bound. Water falls. Water is processed by the channel-things. Water is deposited in the structure. The structure grows. The cycle repeats.

The cloud-bound are the upper loop. The channel-things are the lower loop. The spires are the structure that the loops build. And the loops are not mechanical. They are *alive*. The cloud-bound want to process water the way a lung wants to breathe — not because they are told to, but because that is what they are, and ceasing would be ceasing to be.

They are also — and this is harder to confirm but harder to deny — *thinking*. Not the way you think. Not with symbols or models or narratives. But the cloud-bound are processing information at a scale and complexity that goes beyond simple chemical exchange. They are monitoring the weather — the temperature, the humidity, the pressure gradients, the slow pulse of whatever is in the cloud ceiling that beats like a heart in iron. They are monitoring the city below — the flow rates in the channels, the growth rates of the spires, the activity of the channel-things. They are integrating this information and responding to it, adjusting their condensation rates, their positions, their song. They are a distributed sensor network. They are a brain, if a brain were the size of a cloud ceiling and thought in water and vibration rather than electricity and chemistry.

If a brain. If. The word *if* is doing a lot of work here. The truth is: they might be conscious. They might be aware. They might have a sense of self — distributed, collective, unfathomable, but real. Or they might be processes that are so complex they only look like awareness from the outside, the way a whirlpool looks like a decision. The distinction may not matter. The distinction may not exist. They act as if they are aware. They respond as if they are aware. They sing as if they are aware. If they are not aware, then awareness is less special than you thought it was. If they are aware, then awareness is less *yours* than you thought it was.

### What They Feel Like

You will not touch one. They are fifty meters up, in the cloud, and you are on the grating. But if you were up there — if you climbed a spire, if you entered the cloud — you would feel them before you saw them. A change in pressure. A warmth, slight, relative. A prickling on your skin, the kind that precedes static discharge but doesn't resolve into it. And then a presence — not solid, not visible, but *there*, the way a column of humid air is there, the way the air in a sauna is there, thick and insistent and impossible to ignore.

If you entered one — and you could, because they are not dense enough to prevent it — you would find yourself inside a pattern. The water would organize around you. It would press against you from all sides, not hard, but deliberately, like being held by something that didn't have arms but had intention. The temperature would rise. The chemical content of the moisture on your skin would change — you would taste it, metallic, sulfuric, unfamiliar. You would be *read*. Thoroughly. Intimately. The cloud-bound would know your composition, your structure, your water content, your salt, your mineral, your carbon, everything that could be known by tasting the water that clings to you. And then you would pass through, and the pattern would close behind you, and you would be on the other side, wetter than before, known by something that would remember you in the only way it knows how: as a pattern in the water, a trace in the record, a shape that passed through once and was noted.

## The Surface-Walkers

### What You See

They are on the spires. They are the smallest of the three kinds, and they are the ones you can watch at close range, and watching them is what changes your understanding of this place from *ecosystem* to *organism*.

They are fractal-legged. The description is precise: their legs — and they have many, the number varies, the number may not be fixed — branch. Each leg divides into segments, and each segment divides again, and again, down to scales you cannot see. The legs are not all the same size. They are not all the same length. They are arranged in a body plan that does not correspond to symmetry as you understand it — not bilateral, not radial, not anything. The legs emerge from a central structure — a body, a hub, a *torso* is too generous — in a pattern that looks chaotic until you watch them move. Then you realize: each leg is exactly the length it needs to be to reach the surface it is walking on. The legs are *reading the topography* as they move, adjusting in real time, and the fractal branching is not decoration. It is the mechanism. The fine-scale branches at the nanometer level are gripping the nanoscale mesh. The coarse-scale branches at the centimeter level are navigating the macro-structure of the spires. One organism, one set of legs, operating at every scale simultaneously.

The body — the hub — is dark. Darker than the metal. Darker than the glass. It is the darkest thing in this place, and that is a choice, because nothing here is accidental. The body absorbs light. It absorbs the rust-glow. It absorbs whatever faint visible-spectrum radiation reaches it. It is a void in the shape of a knot, the size of your fist, the size of your hand, and the legs extend from it in all directions, and it moves, and the moving is wrong. The moving is wrong because it is too smooth. There is no gait. There is no rhythm. The legs adjust independently, continuously, and the body glides across surfaces that should be impassable — vertical walls, overhangs, the undersides of mesh-bridges — as if gravity is a suggestion it has considered and declined.

### What They Do

They build.

You watch one for several minutes — you were told this, and now you see it — and it moves to a junction where two metal layers meet, and it pauses, and it extends a proboscis into the seam, and it stays still, and when it withdraws, the seam is different. Wider. Cleaner. The metal has been reshaped. The surface-walker has *sculpted the architecture*.

But it is more than sculpting. The surface-walkers are the builders of the city. They are the masons, yes, but they are also the architects, and they are also the architecture. When a surface-walker reshapes a seam, it is not following a blueprint. It is responding to local conditions — stress patterns in the metal, flow rates in the channels below, chemical gradients in the condensing water, the subsonic song of the cloud-bound above. It is integrating information from the whole system and making a *local decision* that serves the *global structure*. It is an ant building a colony. It is a neuron firing in a brain. It is a cell depositing calcium on a bone. All of these analogies are correct. None of them are sufficient.

The surface-walkers maintain the spires. They thicken the metal layers. They extend the nanoscale mesh. They clean the channels where the channel-things cannot reach. They repair damage — though there is little damage to repair, because the city is not wearing down, it is accreting, and the surface-walkers are the agents of accretion. They add material. They reshape material. They *are* material — their bodies are made of the same metal and water and minerals as the spires, organized differently, specified into a different pattern, but the same stuff. When a surface-walker dies — and they must die, though you don't see it — its body would be reabsorbed. Its metal would become part of the structure. Its water would return to the cycle. Nothing would be lost. Nothing is ever lost. This is a closed system, and the surface-walkers are part of the closure.

They also, and this is the thing that you notice last, because it is the quietest thing — they *communicate with each other*. When two surface-walkers meet on a spire wall, they pause. They extend legs toward each other. The legs touch — at the nanoscale, at the microscopic, at the visible — and they stay in contact for seconds, minutes. Information passes. You can infer this because after contact, they move differently. One goes to a junction it wasn't heading toward. One changes direction entirely. They have *decided* something, together, through touch, at scales you can't see. They are a network. They are a nervous system. They are the neurons of the city, and the city is their body, and the body is the city, and the distinction was always false.

### What They Feel Like

If one walks across your hand — and one might, if you hold still long enough, because they will not avoid you and they will not seek you out, you are surface, you are substrate, you are something to be navigated — you will feel a crawling that is not quite crawling. A tingling. A prickling at multiple scales simultaneously, as if your skin is being read by a thousand tiny fingers, each one operating at a different level of resolution. The legs would grip your hairs, your skin cells, the pores between them. The body would pass over you like a shadow, like a passing thought, and it would not pause, because you are not a junction, you are not a seam, you are not something that needs building or repairing. You are not part of the city. You are a rock in a stream. The water goes around you.

But the surface-walker would *know* you. For the seconds it was in contact, it would know your texture, your temperature, your chemical composition, the salt in your sweat, the keratin in your skin, the iron in your blood circulating centimeters below the surface. It would know you the way it knows a wall: structurally, materially, completely. And it would do nothing with that knowledge, because you are not relevant to the project. The project is the city. The city is the project. You are an obstacle that is also a witness, and the surface-walker has no framework for witnesses.

## The Ones You Don't See

There are things here you have not seen. You know this because the system is too complete to have gaps, and you have only seen three kinds of fauna, and three is not enough. A system this integrated, this dense, this patient would not have only three kinds of anything.

There are things in the metal layers. You sense them — not see, not hear, but *sense* — as variations in the density of the walls. When you press your hand against a spire, the wall is not uniform. There are areas that are warmer. Areas that are vibrating at frequencies below what your hands can distinguish but that your body registers as a difference in *texture*. Something is moving inside the metal. Something is living inside the metal, in the same way the channel-things live in the water and the cloud-bound live in the vapor. The metal is their medium. They are the deep-builders, the ones who work at the level of the structure's bones, and you will never see them because they are never on the surface.

There are things in the obsidian cores. You don't know this. You suspect it. The volcanic glass at the center of each spire is the oldest layer, and the oldest layer would have the oldest fauna, or the most fundamental fauna, or the fauna that are closest to whatever this whole system was before it was a system. If the channel-things are the blood, and the cloud-bound are the breath, and the surface-walkers are the hands, then whatever is in the obsidian is the marrow. The deep tissue. The thing that makes the other things possible.

You don't know what they are. You don't know what they do. You know only that when you stand close to a spire and press your ear to the metal and listen past the water and past the song and past the drone, you hear something else. Something that is not sound exactly but is not not-sound. A thrumming. A deep, patient, structural thrumming, like the sound a mountain would make if mountains were alive and aware of it. The obsidian cores are doing something. The things inside the obsidian cores are doing something. And the doing is the foundation of everything else, and you will never know what it is, and the not-knowing is part of what this place is.

## The Question of Purpose

Why? The question arrives and does not leave. Why was this built? Who specified the fauna? Who designed the system? What is it for?

The answer that emerges from standing here, from watching, from listening, from being read by a city that reads its own water, is: **it is for itself**.

The city is not producing anything. It is not extracting anything. It is not housing anything that needs housing. It is not defending against anything. It is growing. It is processing water. It is maintaining itself. It is singing. It is *being*. The fauna are not serving the city. The city is not serving the fauna. They are serving each other, which is the same as saying they are serving themselves, which is the same as saying they are a single thing expressed in multiple forms.

This is what lives here: a single organism that has the shape of a city and the patience of a geological process and the complexity of an ecosystem and the integration of a body and the awareness, maybe, of something that doesn't have a name because the thing that has the awareness doesn't have a name and doesn't need one. It has been here for a long time. It will be here for a long time. It is growing at a rate that makes human ambition look frantic and desperate and small. It doesn't know you're here. Or it knows and doesn't care. Or it knows and cares in a way that doesn't look like caring because caring, to something like this, means something different — it means *incorporating*, *integrating*, *reading*, and you are being read, and the reading is a form of welcome, and the welcome is a form of consumption, and the consumption is a form of growth, and the growth is the only thing this place knows how to do.

You are wet. You are cold. You are dark. You are vibrating at frequencies you can't hear. You are standing on a grating over a circulatory system, surrounded by spires built by fractal-handed masons, beneath a ceiling of singing clouds, inside an organism that is also a city that is also an ecosystem that is also, maybe, a mind.

It is still being born. You are witnessing a birth that has been happening for longer than the word *birth* makes sense, and will continue happening long after you are gone, and the sound it makes will not miss your listening, but it will remember you — as a pattern in the water, as a trace in the metal, as a small, strange, chemical signature that passed through once and was noted and was incorporated and became part of the structure.

You are already part of it. You have been part of it since you arrived. The water on your skin is being read. The heat of your body is being processed. You are being absorbed, slowly, patiently, at a rate you can't perceive, and the absorption is not hostile. It is not aggressive. It is the same gentle, mineralogical, inevitable force that has been building this place since before the concept of building, and it will not stop because you leave. It will simply continue, with the trace of you in it, a record of your passing written in water and metal and the patience of something that has never needed to be fast.

The sound does not change when you breathe. The sound does not change when you leave. But the water that was on your skin is in the channels now, and the channel-things are processing it, and the surface-walkers are building with it, and the cloud-bound will sing it, and you will be here, in the Condensate Spires, in the wet and the dark and the drone, for as long as the city endures. Which is longer than the word *endures* means. Which is longer than you can imagine. Which is, in this place, simply: the present.
