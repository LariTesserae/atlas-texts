# What Lives in the Resonant Expanse

---

## The Listeners

There is a thing the Expanse has done to them that you can see in the shape of their skulls.

They are low-built. That is the first thing — the wind has made them low, has pressed them toward the ground the way the water has pressed the stone, not violently, not in a single generation, but with the patience of a force that never relents and never varies. Their limbs are short and densely packed with a kind of muscle that does not waste. You can see the architecture of it when one of them moves across an open stretch of mat in the moments before dawn, when the wind drops to its lowest ebb and the organ-tones sink to a register that is almost silence: the haunches are disproportionate, the forequarters narrow, the spine a smooth arch that distributes the animal's weight across a center of gravity kept deliberately, always, *insistently* low. They are the size of large dogs, the text said. The text was wrong. They are the size of large dogs the way a river stone is the size of a fallen fruit. The comparison describes the dimensions and misses the substance entirely. They are dense. They are *composed.* Everything about them speaks of compression, of a body that has been shaped by the removal of everything that could not survive here.

Their heads are broad and flat. The cranium is extended laterally — not in the manner of a hammerhead or any such grotesquerie, but in a subtle widening that you might not notice unless you saw one alongside an animal from a softer place. The temporal bones flare. The zygomatic arches are thick, almost ridged. This is a skull built to conduct vibration. The jaw is heavy and the teeth are unusual — broad in the back, almost flat, with a complexity of ridging that does not correspond to any carnivore or herbivore pattern you can name. They eat the mats. They eat the dark globular masses that cling to the stalks, nuzzling them free with a motion that is gentle, almost tender, the heavy jaw working with a delicacy that contradicts its architecture. And they eat something else — something in the stone, or on the stone, or that emerges from the mats when they are broken. You have seen the patches where the mat-surface has been torn. You thought it was grazing. It is. But the grazing is precise, selective, and it follows a pattern: they tear the mat to reach what is beneath, and what is beneath is a thin layer of — you do not have the word. A film. A residue. Something that lives between the vegetative mat and the quartzite, something that is neither plant nor mineral but exists in the interface, and something that the animals know how to find and how to expose and how to consume without destroying the mat that produces it.

There are not many of them. You suspect this because you have walked for days and seen — what? Eight? Ten? The glimpses are always partial. A shape passing behind a curve in a slot corridor. A movement in the mats that is not wind. Once, from a ridge, you saw one descending into a secondary channel perhaps three hundred meters away, and you watched it for nearly a minute before the wind pushed you sideways and you had to brace, and when you looked again it was gone. In that minute, you learned more than in all the glimpses combined.

It moved with a gait that was not a walk and not a trot but something between — a pace that covered ground without committing to speed, a locomotion that seemed to *listen* its way forward. Its feet touched the mats and you could see the sponginess of the contact, the way the surface compressed and then released. It did not lift its feet high. It did not need to. The channels are smooth. Everything here is smooth. Nothing has a sharp edge except the wind.

Its color was the color of the mats at their darkest — the deep, almost black-green that the oldest growth achieves — and you understood, watching it disappear, that it did not vanish because it was fast. It vanished because it belonged there, because the Expanse had grown it the way it had grown the mats and the stalks, from the same substrate, under the same forces, toward the same end, which is persistence.

## How They Hear

You do not know this. You are guessing. But the guessing has been forced on you by the shape of the skull and by a behavior you have observed and cannot explain otherwise.

The wind drops sometimes. Not often, and not for long, but in the transition hours — the thin light before dawn, the copper moment after sunset — the collision of weather systems above the plateau reaches a temporary equilibrium and the exhalation falters, and the organ-tones sink to their lowest, longest notes, and for a few minutes the Expanse is merely loud instead of deafening.

In those minutes, you have heard them.

Not the grunt-sigh that was mentioned. That was real, and it was one sound, once. But in the quiet hours, standing at the lip of a slot corridor with your hands on the stone and your jaw aching from the vibration, you have heard a sequence of sounds that the Expanse does not make. Short. Tonal. Rhythmic. They are low — below the range where you can easily locate them, more felt than heard, which means they are close, which means they are produced nearby, which means they are produced by something that lives nearby. The sounds are not complex. They do not resemble language as you understand it. They resemble the organ-tones of the plateau itself — single notes, sustained, then cut. As if the animals have learned, or been shaped to learn, that the Expanse communicates through vibration, and they have answered it in its own tongue.

The skull confirms this. Those lateral temporal bones, that heavy jaw, the architecture of a head built to receive and interpret vibration through the bone rather than through the air — this is not an ear you are looking at. It is a seismograph. They feel the Expanse. They feel the wind in the stone and the water in the flues and the organ-tones as they shift from dry to wet to dry, and they navigate by this feeling the way you navigate by sight. The slot corridors are not mazes to them. They are songs. Each corridor has its own frequency, its own resonance, its own character, and the animals know them all, know which channel leads to which pool, know where the wind shears worst at the ridge-tops, know where the mats are thickest and the residue beneath them sweetest — they know all of this not by seeing it but by hearing it with their entire bodies.

This is why they are invisible. Not because they hide. Because they move through a landscape that is, to them, rich with information that you cannot access. They know you are coming. They have felt your footsteps in the stone for a kilometer or more. They have moved to other channels, other corridors, other pools, and they have done so without urgency, without alarm, because you are not a threat. You are a vibration. A new note in a landscape that has been sounding for longer than anything can remember. They have heard new notes before. They will hear them again. They wait for the notes to leave, and they return.

## The Young

You saw one once, and only because the wind had dropped to its lowest ebb and you had been sitting motionless for an hour against the wall of a tertiary channel, and you had become — not invisible, but irrelevant, a vibration that had gone still, a note that had decayed.

It came down the channel from the north. It was smaller than the adults — half the size, perhaps less — and it was not alone. There was an adult ahead of it and an adult behind it, and they moved with the same listening pace, the same ground-covering deliberation, and the young one moved between them with an unsteadiness that was not weakness but learning. It placed its feet with care. Each footfall was deliberate, testing, as if it were confirming something about the stone beneath the mat. Its skull was narrower than the adults', the temporal bones not yet flared, the jaw not yet heavy. It was, you realized, still being tuned. Still growing the architecture that would let it hear the Expanse the way its parents heard it.

The adult ahead paused at a bend in the corridor and produced a sound — one of the short, tonal calls, a single sustained note, low, held for perhaps three seconds, then released. The young one stopped. It stood motionless. You could see its head tilt — not toward the sound, but toward the stone, toward the floor of the channel, as if it were listening not to the call but to what the call did when it entered the rock and traveled through it. It was learning the corridor. It was being taught the song of this place, and the song was not in the air. It was in the stone.

Then the adult behind the young one nudged it forward — a gentle press of the broad skull against its flank — and they moved on, and the wind rose again, and the organ-tones swelled back to their full volume, and you sat against the wall with the stone vibrating against your spine and you understood that you had seen something that the Expanse had decided to show you, and you did not know why.

## The Insects and What They Are

The small, dark, flat-bodied things that move across the surface of the mats are not insects. They have six legs, and they are small, and they move quickly, and the word *insect* is the closest available container, but it is the wrong container.

They have no wings. They have no eyes that you can identify. Their bodies are oval, flattened, the color of the pale stone that shows through the mats where the growth thins. They are, in fact, the same color as the exposed quartzite, and when they move across the bare stone — which they do, more often than they move across the mats — they are nearly impossible to see. They are the color of the Expanse itself, which is to say they are the color of survival in a place where visibility is death, though nothing here seems to hunt them.

They feed on the mats, or rather on the moisture the mats hold. You have seen them cluster at the edges of a torn patch where an animal has exposed the underlying layer, and they drink — not eat, drink — from the surface of the film that the mats produce. They are the cleanup crew. The recyclers. When an animal tears a mat, the insects arrive within hours and they work the exposed edges, and the mat seals over the wound, and the insects move on.

They do not make sound. In a landscape where everything makes sound, where sound is the primary medium of existence, they are silent. This is their adaptation. They live below the notice of the vibration. They are too small to register in the stone, too light to leave a frequency, too silent to be heard. They have become the thing that the Expanse does not perceive. In a place where everything is felt, they are the unfelt. This is not a flaw in their design. It is the design.

There are other invertebrates — or the things that occupy the invertebrate niche — but they are fewer and more cryptic. Something lives in the permanent pools, something that distorts the surface of the water in patterns too fine for your eye to resolve, something that is perhaps the size of a fingernail and perhaps much smaller. Something lives in the mats themselves, in the deep layers where the sponginess holds moisture longest, a thread-like organism that might be a worm or might be a fungus or might be a filament of the mat-organism itself, and you cannot tell where the animal ends and the plant begins because the categories have not been useful here for a very long time.

## The People Who Built the Shelters

They are not gone. This is the thing you have to say, even though the text said *absorbed,* said *vanished,* said *dispersed.* They are not gone. They are below.

Not in a metaphorical sense. Not in a spiritual sense. In a literal sense, in the sense of stone and depth and the conduits that riddle the plateau like marrow in a bone.

You found the entrance by accident. A slot corridor that you had been following for an hour opened — not into another corridor, not into a wider channel, but into a *shaft.* Vertical. Cut into the quartzite with the same methodical patience that the water had used, but cut by hands, by tools, by the shaped spine-edges of the fibrous stalks worked against the stone for months or years or generations. The shaft descended at a steep angle into the body of the plateau, and the walls were smooth — not water-smooth, but tool-smooth, with the faint striations of parallel cuts, the record of labor so patient it makes your breath catch.

The organ-tones are different here. Deeper. Closer. The sound does not come from the walls; it comes from *below,* rising through the shaft like heat, and you understand something you did not understand before: the people who built the shelters on the surface did not live on the surface. The shelters were way-stations, yes. Temporary structures for temporary use. The people lived inside the plateau. They lived in the conduits and flues that the water had carved, and they had widened and smoothed and shaped those conduits into something that was not quite architecture and not quite cave, but something in between — a habitation that used the Expanse's own body as its structure, its own resonance as its light.

Because light, down there, is not what you think. There is a kind of luminescence in the deep conduits — not the bioluminescence of caves you have known, not the glow of fungi or minerals, but something stranger. The mats, or a subspecies of the mats, or a deeper expression of the same organism, grow on the interior walls of the conduits where moisture seeps through from above. And in the deepest, most resonant passages — the ones where the organ-tones are loudest, where the vibration of the stone is so intense you can feel it in the fluid of your eyes — the deep-mats glow. Faintly. A deep, cold green that is not enough to see by in any useful sense but is enough to orient by, enough to suggest the shapes of the walls, enough to walk by if you know where you are going and do not need to see the path.

They do not need to see the path. They hear it.

## What They Are Now

You did not go down. The shaft was too steep, the sound too intense, and something in you — something older than reason, older than language, something in the fluid of your joints and the marrow of your bones — told you that descending into the Expanse's interior was a transition you could not reverse. This is not a fear you can justify. It is a knowledge you can feel.

But you sat at the edge of the shaft for a long time, and you listened, and you heard — below the organ-tones, below the wind, below the rattle of the stalks and the percussion of the rain that was beginning to fall — you heard the sounds that the animals make. The short, tonal calls. The sustained notes. The rhythmic sequences. But *organized.* Organized in a way that the animal calls above were not. Organized into patterns that repeated and varied and repeated again, and the variations were not random, they were *deliberate,* and the deliberation was the deliberation of minds that had been thinking in this medium — in vibration, in resonance, in the sustained note and the cut note and the silence between — for longer than the surface structures had been standing.

The people are still here. They are in the stone. They have been in the stone for long enough that their skulls have widened, their jaws have thickened, their bodies have compressed and lowered and grown dense, and their eyes — their eyes have gone the way of the insects' eyes, which is to say they have gone *away,* because in the deep conduits there is nothing to see, and everything to hear, and they have chosen — or the Expanse has chosen for them, which may be the same thing — to become the thing that hears the Expanse most fully.

They are the animals. The animals are them. This is the thing you have to say, and you do not want to say it, and the saying of it does not make it less true: the four-legged, low-built, broad-skulled, dense-bodied, vibration-listening things that move through the slot corridors and drink from the permanent pools and tear the mats to eat the residue beneath — they are the people who built the shelters. They are the people who cut the notches. They are the people who lashed the stalks and shaped the pegs and counted the water-levels and lived on the surface of the Expanse for a span of time that you cannot calculate, and then, at some point — some point that is not marked by anything you can find, some point that the stone does not record — they went down.

They went down into the conduits, into the flues, into the body of the plateau, and they did not come back, and over the time that followed — the time that the Expanse measures not in years but in the slow re-shaping of stone and the slow re-shaping of bodies — they changed. They became what the Expanse needed them to become, or what the Expanse allowed them to become, which is an animal that hears the world through the rock and navigates by resonance and lives in the deep places where the organ-tones originate and the deep-mats glow and the water seeps through the stone in the dark.

The shelters on the surface are the relics of their surfacing. They still come up. The animals you have seen — the ones that move through the corridors, that drink from the pools, that graze on the mats — they still climb, sometimes, to the surface. They still use the shelters. They still maintain the lashings, replace the bindings, check the notches. Or some of them do, sometimes, for reasons that the deep conduits know and the surface does not.

The young one you saw in the channel — the one with the narrow skull, the un-flared temporal bones, the jaw still un-heavy — it was learning the surface, because it was still young enough to learn it, because the transition happens slowly, over years, over the growth of the body, and the young are still ambivalent, still capable of the open air, still able to see, dimly, the things that the adults have stopped seeing. It was learning the corridors by their surface-song, and it was being taught, and the teachers were patient, and the lesson was not *this is where you live.* The lesson was *this is where you lived once, and you will not live here again, but you should know it, you should feel it, you should carry the song of this place into the deep stone so that when you hear it again, rising through the conduits, you will know what it means.*

## What the Expanse Is

You do not say this easily, and you do not say it with confidence, and you do not say it as a fact but as the shape that the facts have pressed themselves into in your mind, the way the wind has pressed the stone, the way the water has pressed the channels, the way the sound has pressed everything:

The Expanse is alive. Not the life of organisms, not the life of the mats and the stalks and the deep-mats and the animals and the people-becoming-animals. Those are lives, plural, individual, each with its own trajectory. The Expanse is *alive* in the way that a thing is alive when it has been resonating for so long that the resonance is not something it does but something it is. The organ-tones are not music. They are not communication. They are the sound of a system that has achieved a state of sustained, self-reinforcing vibration, and that state has properties, and one of the properties is that it shapes the things that live within it, shapes them toward the vibration, toward the resonance, toward the hearing of it, and the things that are shaped — the mats, the animals, the people — become part of the system, become extensions of the vibration, become the Expanse hearing itself.

The people heard it. They heard it for a long time on the surface, and they built shelters and counted the water and lashed the stalks, and the whole time they were hearing it, and the hearing changed them, not suddenly, not catastrophically, but the way the water changed the stone — slowly, with patience, with the persistence of a force that never stops. They began to hear the interior. They began to hear the deep conduits, the flues, the resonance-chambers that the water had carved and the wind had tuned, and they heard them the way you hear a voice calling from inside a house, and they went to the voice, because the voice was the Expanse, and the Expanse was where they lived, and they went home.

And home was inside the stone, and inside the stone the sound was everything, and over the time that followed — the time that has no marker, no notch, no count — they became the thing that the sound needed them to be. Not demanded. Not forced. *Needed.* The way a note needs a resonant body. The way an organ needs a chamber. The Expanse had been vibrating for inconceivable spans of time, and it had been producing its tones, and the tones had been traveling through the stone, and the stone had been shaping itself around the tones, and then a thing entered the stone that could *hear the tones,* could receive them, could hold them in a body that was itself a chamber, a resonator, an instrument, and the Expanse — not deliberately, not with intent, but with the blind, patient, total shaping of a system that has been doing one thing for longer than anything can remember — made use of it.

Made use of them.

## What Remains

This is what lives in the Resonant Expanse:

An organism that carpets the stone and holds the water and grows toward the light. Stalks that rattle in the wind and make a sound that is the fourth voice in the roar. Leaf-forms that drink with precision. Dark, globular, warm masses that may be fruiting bodies or may be something older than fruiting. Small, flat, silent things that live below the notice of the vibration. Thread-like organisms in the deep mats that blur the line between plant and animal and mineral. Things in the pools that you cannot see but can infer.

Animals that were people, that are still people in some way that the deep conduits preserve, that live inside the stone and hear the world through vibration and come to the surface to teach their young the song of the place they used to live. An Expanse that is a condition, a state of sustained resonance, a single note that has been sounding for so long that the world has shaped itself around it, and the living things within it have shaped themselves around it, and the people who came here — whoever they were, whenever they came — heard the note and were changed by it, and went down into the stone, and became part of the instrument.

And you. For a little while. A new note, passing through, feeling the vibration in your teeth and your joints and the fluid of your eyes, sitting at the edge of a shaft that descends into the body of the plateau, listening to sounds below the sounds below the sounds, and knowing — not thinking, not reasoning, but *knowing,* the way the stone knows its own resonance — that the Expanse is hearing you, and has been hearing you since before you crested the ridge, and will go on hearing you after you leave, your footsteps in the stone fading, decaying, becoming part of the record, a small, brief vibration in a body that has been vibrating since before anything you can name began.

You leave. The Expanse continues. The wind bifurcates around the place where you stood and finds no resistance and closes behind you like water.
