# What Lives Here

## The People of the Upper Tier

They are not what you would call diminished. That word implies a before-state of fullness, a baseline against which the present is measured and found lesser. The residents of the upper tier have no such baseline. They are what they have always been, as far back as their generational memory reaches — which is not far, because generational memory here is not a narrative but a set of reflexes. How to read the water. How to taste the mineral content and know which deep system is cycling. How to move through acoustic space without disturbing its frequencies. These are the things that pass between them. Not stories. Not histories. A body of knowledge that lives in the body.

They are lean. Not gaunt — the word implies a deficit, a deprivation — but *efficient*, the way a tool becomes efficient through use. The humidity is a tax on everything, and they pay it in the coin of metabolism. Their bodies have adapted to extract more from less, to retain what the environment strips away. Their skin is warm. Not the warmth of exertion but of conduct — you realize, after spending time among them, that they are thermally coupled to their surroundings in a way that goes beyond comfort. They wear the heat of the walls the way you wear a coat. It enters them. They do not regulate against it. They regulate *with* it.

Their coloring is the coloring of the biofilm. This is not metaphor. Over generations of proximity, of sleeping against mineral surfaces that have been coated for centuries by the same warm-toned ecology, their skin has taken on a quality that is not quite pigmentation and not quite patina. It is deeper in the creases — the knuckles, the inner elbow, the lines around the eyes. It is paler where the skin is flat and exposed. In the pale light, they look like they have been carved from the same material as the walls, and then polished by the same water, and then left to develop the same living film. They do not think this is strange. They do not think about it at all.

Their hands are remarkable. This is the first thing you notice. Their fingers are longer than yours by a proportion that is not dramatic but is unmistakable — an extra centimeter, perhaps, on each digit, with joints that are more flexible and nails that are thicker and flatter, almost hoof-like in their density. They use their hands the way you use your whole body. They climb the wet surfaces of the structures — the verticals, the overhangs, the conduits — with a fluidity that makes the surfaces look grippable, and the surfaces are not grippable, not for you, but they are for these hands, which find purchase where yours would find only slick mineral and sliding panic. They grip the biofilm the way a gecko grips glass: not through adhesion but through a geometry of contact that distributes force across a thousand micro-points, each one holding, each one releasing in sequence as the hand moves.

They speak the way the city sounds. This is not poetic observation. Their language is tonal — not in the way Mandarin is tonal, where pitch changes meaning, but in the way music is tonal, where frequency, amplitude, and harmonic content all carry semantic weight simultaneously. A single vocalization can contain a noun, a temporal marker, and an affective qualifier, layered into the fundamental frequency and its overtones. They learn it the way birds learn song — through immersion, through repetition, through the gradual calibration of the throat to the acoustic properties of the space. A child's first words are not words. They are frequencies. Matched to the standing tone of the canal fly gyre in the district where the child was born, which is the district where the child will likely live, which is the district whose acoustic signature will become, for that child, the sound of home.

They are not many. You knew this from the empty housing units, but being among them makes the knowledge specific. A residential corridor that could house forty contains twelve. They are not dispersed — they cluster, the way the flickers cluster around the warm outflows. They live near each other. They live near the maintenance access points that connect their tier to the systems below. They live near the water channels that provide transport and, more importantly, the particular acoustic channels that the water creates — the corridors of clear sound that carry their voices farthest.

They age, but they age the way the mineral deposits age. Slowly. In layers. The oldest among them — and there are some who are old by any standard, three score and more — have a quality that is hard to name. Not frailty. Something like density. As though the years have not worn them but added to them, the way the calcite adds to the deep structures, millimeter by century, until the original surface is no longer the surface but something older and more stable has taken its place. The old ones move more slowly but more surely. Their voices are lower. Their hands are surer. When they speak, the younger ones adjust their own frequencies to accommodate the elder's tone, the way the city adjusts its drainage to accommodate a new water source. Not deference. Calibration.

## What They Do

They maintain. This is the word they use, and it is precise in a way that maintenance is not usually precise. They do not repair. They do not build. They do not plan. They maintain.

The distinction matters. Repair implies a prior state of integrity that has been compromised. Building implies the creation of something new. Planning implies a future that is other than the present. The residents of the upper tier do none of these things. They maintain, which is to say they participate in the ongoing process of the mass continuing to be the mass, the systems continuing to process, the water continuing to find its level.

This looks, from the outside, like work. They move through the tiers with tools — or things that function as tools, which is to say extensions of their hands that have been shaped by use into forms no designer would have chosen but no designer could improve. They scrape the biofilm from specific surfaces. Not all surfaces — never all surfaces — but specific ones, chosen by a logic that is not written down and not spoken but is shared among them the way the acoustic frequencies are shared: through proximity, through repetition, through the gradual formation of consensus that does not require discussion. They scrape the biofilm because if they did not, the channels would narrow, the outflows would constrict, and the circulatory system of the city would develop clots. They know this because they have seen it happen. Not often. But enough.

They also — and this is the part that is hardest to describe from outside — they *listen*. Not metaphorically. They press their ears, and sometimes their whole bodies, against the surfaces of the mass, and they listen to what is happening inside it. The deep systems have a diagnostic language. It is the language of vibration, of temperature differential, of the particular quality of water that emerges from different outflows at different times. The residents can read it the way a doctor reads a pulse. They can feel when a system is cycling normally. They can feel when it is laboring. They can feel when it is — the word they use is *hungry*, and they use it without irony, without anthropomorphism, with the clinical specificity of someone describing a technical state — when it is hungry, and they respond by adjusting the inputs. Opening a channel here. Restricting one there. Adding mineral substrate to a feed point. Redirecting warm water to a zone that has cooled.

They do not know what the deep systems are processing. This is not ignorance. This is the nature of their relationship to it. They know the inputs. They know the outputs. They know the schedules and the sounds and the failure modes. They do not know what happens inside, because the inside of the mass is below the water table, below the bedrock, below the point where human access is possible, and has been for longer than the residents have been residents. They maintain the systems the way a person maintains a body — from the outside, by feel, by inference, by long familiarity with the patterns of something that is alive in a way that does not require comprehension.

When you ask them about the deep systems — and you do, because you cannot help it — they respond in a way that is neither defensive nor evasive but something else. Patient. The way you would be patient with someone who asked you to explain breathing. They explain the maintenance. They explain the schedules. They explain the sounds. They do not explain the purpose, because purpose is a category that does not apply. The deep systems process. That is what they do. Asking why is like asking why the heart beats. It beats because that is what a heart is. The systems process because that is what the systems are.

## The Children

There are children. Not many — the population is stable, which is to say slowly declining, which is to say in a state of equilibrium with the systems' requirements — but there are enough that you see them, and seeing them changes your understanding of the place.

They are not like the adults. This is true of all children everywhere, but it is specifically and particularly true here. The children are loud. They have not yet learned to tune their voices to the acoustic environment, and so they broadcast — raw, unmodulated, at frequencies that clash with the standing tones of the canal fly gyres and the hydraulic pulse of the deep systems. The adults do not correct them. The adults listen to the children the way they listen to the systems: with attention, with diagnostic interest, with the knowledge that the sound is information. A child's unmodulated voice tells them something about the child's development, about the maturation of the throat and the acoustic palate, about the degree to which the child has begun to absorb the frequencies of their district. The children who will become maintenance workers — which is most of them, which is all of them — are the ones whose voices gradually disappear into the sonic fabric. You watch a six-year-old call to a friend across a channel, and the call is barely distinguishable from the ambient sound. It is not that the child is quiet. It is that the child has learned to become part of the sound.

The children play in the water. This is not recreation in the way you understand it. It is calibration. They are learning the channels — which ones are warm, which ones carry current, which ones are navigable and which are not. They race the flat-bottomed craft through the second-tier market, and the market vendors adjust around them without comment, the way a stream adjusts around a stone. The children are learning to be stones. They are learning to be in the current without being moved by it. They are learning the thing that all the residents know, which is that you do not fight this place. You do not shape it. You are shaped by it, and the shaping is not loss but becoming.

A child born in the upper tier will never see the sky. This is not a tragedy. This is not even a fact that registers. The sky is the pale gray light that filters down through the tiers, and that light is sufficient, and the world above the overlook — the world of open air and distant horizon and the terrible openness of the uncontained — is not a place the children imagine, because nothing in their experience provides the material for that imagination. They do not feel confined. They feel *held*. The city is around them and below them and above them, and it is warm, and it is wet, and it is loud, and it is alive, and they are part of it, and it is part of them, and the word for this is not enclosure. The word for this is home.

## The Canal Flies

They are not insects. You called them that for convenience, and the name stuck because names do that here — they stick, or they don't, and the ones that stick are the ones that fit the acoustic space, and "canal fly" fits because it has the right number of syllables and the right tonal weight for the frequency at which it must be spoken to be heard above their own sound.

They are flat. Transparent. Their bodies are a single layer of cells, organized into a planar structure that is more membrane than organism — a living film that flies. They have no internal organs in the way you would recognize. Their metabolism is distributed across their surface, which is also their respiratory system and their reproductive system and their sensory system. They are, in a sense, flying biofilm. They are what happens when the biofilm that covers every surface in the Confluence decides — or is driven, or evolves, or is shaped by pressures you cannot see — to detach and take to the air.

They feed on the biofilm. Not by consuming it but by *grazing* it — landing on the thick mats and absorbing nutrients through their ventral surface, which is adapted to make contact with the living film and draw from it without killing it. The biofilm regrows. The canal flies graze. It is a stable relationship, a pact between two forms of the same kind of life, and it has been running so long that neither party could survive without the other.

They reproduce in the gyres. The rising columns of warm air that form above the basin floor are not just flight paths — they are courtship arenas, mating grounds, nurseries. When the canal flies spiral upward in their hundreds of thousands, they are not feeding. They are breeding. The gyre is a single organism made of ten thousand bodies, and within it, pairs meet and exchange genetic material through surface contact — a brief, instantaneous touch of ventral surfaces, so fast it looks like a collision — and the fertilized individuals peel away from the gyre and descend to the surfaces below, where they deposit a film of eggs so thin it is invisible, so thin it becomes part of the biofilm itself, so thin that the biofilm and the next generation of canal flies are, for a time, the same substance.

They live for days. Most of them. Some live for hours. The ones that live longest are the ones that find the warmest updrafts, the richest biofilm surfaces, the safest roosts under the overhangs where the drifters cannot reach. They die the way they live — on surfaces, against surfaces, becoming surfaces. The dead canal flies accumulate in the crevices of the structures, and the biofilm absorbs them, and the cycle is not a cycle but a continuous process, a flow of matter from surface to air to surface, and the canal flies are not individuals in this flow. They are momentary states. They are what the biofilm looks like when it is flying.

## The Flickers

You see them best from the second tier, where the drainage channels widen and the water slows and becomes, for brief stretches, still enough to reflect the amber light from below. They move through these slow channels in schools that shift and reform with a coordination that is not instinct in the way you understand instinct — not a fixed action pattern, not a genetic program — but something more like conversation. They are talking to each other. You are sure of this. Not through sound, or not through sound that you can hear, but through the same photonic quality that gives them their name. Their bodies flash. Dark to bright, bright to dark, in patterns that are too fast and too structured to be reflexive. They are exchanging information. The information is about food, probably. About temperature. About the location of the warm outflows where the feeding is best. But it might also be about other things. You cannot know. You do not have the physics for their language any more than you have the physics for the drifters' flight.

Their bodies are — you have to be careful here, because the word that comes to mind is *made*, and that implies a maker, and you do not want to imply a maker. Their bodies are composed of something that is not tissue in the biological sense and not metal in the mineral sense but something between. Something that the mass produces, perhaps. Something that emerges from the same substrate that the deep systems process, in the same way that the biofilm emerges and the canal flies emerge and the calcite deposits emerge. The flickers might be a product. An output. Something the deep systems make as a byproduct of their processing, the way a furnace makes ash, the way a river makes silt. They might be waste. They might be purpose. They might be the same thing.

They feed at the warm outflows. They cluster there — dozens of them, pressing against the grating where the heated water rises from the deep systems, their bodies flashing in unison, the school becoming a single strobing entity that lights up the channel in patterns that the residents above can see through the floor gratings. The residents read these patterns. Not consciously — not the way you read text — but peripherally, the way you read the light through curtains when you wake. The flicker patterns at the outflow points are diagnostic. A certain rhythm means a system is cycling normally. A different rhythm means it is laboring. A stillness — the total absence of flickers at a normally active outflow — means something is wrong, and the maintenance workers respond the way firefighters respond to an alarm: with speed, with focus, with the knowledge that the system they are maintaining is also the system that maintains them.

The flickers produce particulate. When they feed, they excrete — a fine, warm dust that drifts upward through the water and settles on the biofilm above, and the biofilm absorbs it, and grows thicker, and the canal flies graze it, and the drifters skim the canal flies, and the chain of consumption is not a chain but a loop, not a hierarchy but a circuit, every output an input, every waste a resource, everything feeding everything else in a system so closed it might as well be a single body.

## The Drifters

They are the ones that stay with you.

You see them first from the overlook — distant, flat, diamond-shaped, turning in the upper air with a slowness that is not lethargy but deliberation. They look like kites. You keep thinking this. You keep being wrong.

They are not kites. They are not animals in the way you understand animals. They are large — three meters across, sometimes four, sometimes more, the ones that are highest and oldest and have been drifting longest — and they are thin. Unbelievably thin. A centimeter at their thickest point, tapering to a membrane so fine it is translucent at the edges. Their bodies are not bodies in the sense of volume, of mass, of the three-dimensional occupancy you associate with living things. They are surfaces. They are two-dimensional beings in a three-dimensional medium, and they maintain themselves in air — not by buoyancy, not by lift in the aerodynamic sense, but by something their skin does.

Their skin is the thing. It is the answer to the question of how something that large and that thin stays aloft, and the answer is not an answer because you do not have the physics for it. Their skin is a gas-exchange surface — this much the residents can tell you, and this much you can observe, because when a drifter passes over a warm updraft, the air beneath it shimmers, and the shimmer is not heat distortion but something chemical, something metabolic, the visible signature of a surface that is constantly exchanging gases with the atmosphere in a way that produces lift as a byproduct. They are breathing. They are always breathing. Their flight is respiration. They stay aloft the way you stay alive — by breathing, constantly, without thinking about it, without being able to stop.

They feed at dusk. This is not tied to the sun — there is no sun here, only the pale gray membrane of the sky — but to the cycle of the deep systems, which has its own rhythm, its own days and nights, measured in the thirty-second thrum that builds and peaks and releases. The release is dusk. The canal flies scatter. The drifters descend.

The descent is the thing you will remember.

They come down slowly. Not gliding — they do not have the control surfaces for gliding — but *sinking with purpose*, rippling their bodies in long peristaltic waves that move from their leading edge to their trailing edge, and each wave adjusts their altitude by centimeters, and the centimeters accumulate, and over minutes — over tens of minutes — they descend from the upper air to the upper surfaces of the structures, and when they arrive, they arrive with a sound.

The pulse. The low, rhythmic pulse that you feel in your sternum. It is the sound of their bodies flexing against the air at close range — not vocal, not mechanical in the sense of gears or pistons, but mechanical in the sense of muscle, of a living surface doing work against a fluid medium. A single drifter's pulse is a slow, deep throb. Two drifters create interference. Three create a beat. Five or more — and there can be more, many more, when the canal flies are thick and the feeding is good — create a sound that is not a sound but a pressure event, a rhythmic displacement of the air that you feel in your chest and your sinuses and the fluid of your eyes.

They feed. Their ventral surfaces — the same surface that breathes, the same membrane — press against the upper surfaces of the structures, and they skim. The biofilm comes away. The canal flies come away. The drifter absorbs them through its skin, and its skin darkens as it feeds, from translucent to amber to a deep, rich brown, and when it has fed enough — and enough is a lot, enough is hours of contact, enough is a skin-full that visibly weighs it down — it releases from the surface and rises, slowly, rippling, pulsing, back to the upper air where it will drift and breathe and metabolize until the next descent.

The residents do not fear the drifters. They respect them. The distinction is important. Fear implies a threat that is external, an adversarial relationship. Respect implies a recognition of scale, of consequence, of the fact that something large and powerful and fundamentally indifferent to your existence shares your environment. The drifters do not attack people. They do not notice people. People are beneath their attention — too small, too dense, too slow. But a drifter descending to feed can pin a person against a surface, and the weight of a feeding drifter — three meters of wet membrane pressed flat by the pressure differential that holds it in place — is not survivable for long. The residents know this. They teach their children this. They read the signs of descent — the change in the air pressure, the shift in the canal fly patterns, the low pulse that begins as a vibration in the walls — and they move. They go inside. They go below. They wait for the feeding to end and the drifters to rise, and then they come out again, and the surfaces where the drifters fed are clean — stripped of biofilm, stripped of canal flies, pale and bare and new-looking in a way that nothing else in the Confluence ever looks.

The oldest drifters are different. You see them from the overlook — the ones that fly highest, that drift in the pale light near the membrane of the sky. They are larger. Their edges are ragged. Their bodies carry deposits — mineral accumulations, calcite growths, patches of thickened biofilm that have colonized their dorsal surfaces and that they cannot reach to clean. They are encrusted with the place. They have been here so long that the place has begun to grow on them, and they carry it with them, and they drift, and they breathe, and they are slowly becoming part of the same process that they feed on.

No one knows how long the drifters live. The residents do not think in these terms. The drifters are — like the canal flies, like the flickers, like the biofilm — a condition of the place. They exist because the conditions exist. They will persist as long as the conditions persist. When one dies — and they do die, sometimes, in the upper air, their bodies becoming too heavy, their breathing too slow — it falls. It drifts down, rippling, but the ripples are irregular, the pulse is arrhythmic, and the descent is not a descent but a settling, a slow, tumbling, flat descent that ends on the upper surfaces of the structures, where the body lies — huge, thin, still — and the biofilm colonizes it, and the canal flies graze the biofilm, and the flickers feed at the boundary where the drifter's body meets the warm water, and the mass, eventually, absorbs it, and what was a drifter becomes a surface, and what was a surface becomes part of the city, and the city grows by one layer, and the layer is thin, and the layer is almost nothing, and it is enough.

## What Lives Here, Ultimately

The mass.

The mass is what lives here. Not the people, though they live here. Not the canal flies, the flickers, the drifters, the biofilm, though they live here. Not the deep systems, though they live here. The mass. The continuous, self-organizing, self-maintaining substrate that underlies everything and expresses everything and is everything. The people are an expression of the mass. So is the biofilm. So are the canal flies, the flickers, the drifters. So is the water. So is the sound. So is the light, which is not sunlight, which is not electric light, which is the light of the mass itself — the warm amber glow of metabolism, of process, of a living thing doing the work of staying alive.

The mass does not think. The residents are clear about this. It does not plan. It does not want. It processes. It takes what comes in — water, mineral, heat, the organic substrate that enters from somewhere below — and it transforms it, and the transformation is not purposeful but *consequential*, the way digestion is consequential. The mass does not eat because it is hungry. It eats because it is a mouth. It does not process because it needs to. It processes because that is what it is.

And what lives here lives here because the mass is hospitable. Or rather — and this is the harder truth, the one that the residents understand and you are only beginning to — the mass is not hospitable in the way a host is hospitable, offering space to a guest. The mass is hospitable in the way a body is hospitable to its own organs. The things that live here are not visitors. They are not colonists. They are not adaptations to an environment. They are *part of the mass*. They are what the mass looks like when it walks, when it flies, when it swims, when it scrapes biofilm from a channel wall with long fingers and sure hands and a voice tuned to the frequency of home.

This is what lives at the Confluence. A single thing, wearing many shapes, doing a single thing — persisting, processing, staying alive in the only way it knows how. The sound does not stop.
