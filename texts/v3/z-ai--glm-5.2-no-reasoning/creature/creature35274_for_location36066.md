# What Lives at Lumen Step

## The Keeper

She comes every nineteen days. She has come every nineteen days for six years, which is thirty-six fewer visits than the chart paper in the gauge box would suggest if anyone were counting, and she *is* counting, which is why she knows.

Her name is on the padlock's invoice somewhere — filed under a site number she sometimes forgets and has to read off the stencil on the shed, squinting, lips moving slightly. She is fifty-four. She has the kind of lean, unfinished hands of someone who has done fieldwork most of her life and will keep doing it until something stops her, and nothing has yet. Her fingernails are usually clean but never quite *right* — there's a permanent crescent of dark under the left thumbnail that she's stopped noticing. She wears the same canvas jacket in all weather. It has been wet here so many times that the fabric has taken on a permanent stiffness, a memory of damp she carries with her between visits. When she arrives, she stands for a moment at the gap — the fracture in the ridge — and listens. She listens the way you'd check whether someone is home. Then she pushes through.

She comes alone. She has always come alone. There was a period, in the first year, when she brought a junior technician — a quiet young man who took notes and held the ladder — but he transferred, or left, or simply stopped being assigned, and the paperwork to request a replacement was more tedious than the work itself, so she stopped asking. Now the visits are hers. She has a key to the padlock on a ring with three other keys, none of which she can identify by sight anymore, she knows them by feel — this one, the brass one, the Lumen Step key, its teeth a shape her fingers find in the dark of her pocket.

When she opens the shed, the smell hits first: machine oil, warm metal, the dry mineral funk of the small electric heater that keeps the monitoring equipment above condensation temperature. The shed is not large enough to stand in. She crouches. She checks the pump — a compact submersible unit bolted to a bracket, drawing from a secondary sump she had drilled into the basalt floor beneath the shed during the second year, when the first pool's mineral load proved too variable for the primary sampling intake. The pump cycles. She has never heard it clearly, even in the shed's quiet — the water-sound from outside fills every space here, and the pump's vibration is more felt than heard, a buzz in the floor that comes up through her knees when she kneels. She checks the flow meter. She replaces the filter housing — a small canister, quarter-turn bayonet mount, which she screws out and replaces with a fresh one from a case of twelve she brings each visit, taking the spent one back in a plastic bag. The spent filters are brown. Iron. She has a drawer of them at the lab. Nobody has asked her about the drawer.

She lifts the recorder chart from the gauge box and replaces it with a fresh drum. She does this with a paper towel, because the chart is always damp and the ink smears. The old chart goes into a hard plastic tube she carries in a padded sleeve in her pack, and back at the lab — a different world, lower, drier, fluorescent — she hangs each chart on a drying line and photographs it and files the photograph in a system that three people have access to and one person has ever opened. The pen arm on the recorder twitches when she replaces the drum. She watches it settle. It always settles back to the same range. Sixty percent. She doesn't know what the gauge measures. This is not quite true. She was told, once, early on, and the explanation was technical and included the word *hydraulic* and the word *headspace* and a diagram she didn't keep, and over time the explanation faded and what remained was the procedure: change the chart, note the reading, replace the filter, check the pump, photograph the spillway, check the valve seals, note the amphipod count if visible, lock the shed, leave. The procedure is the explanation. It is enough.

She has carved her initials in the basalt. This is unlike her — she is not sentimental, not prone to marking things — but on one visit, early, maybe the fourth, she stood at the spillway and looked at the water running down the channel and felt something she couldn't name, and she took out her knife and scratched the letters, small, and the date. She has not done it again. When she passes the spot on subsequent visits, she looks at the letters and they look like they belong to someone else. The moss has begun to grow over them. Another few years and they'll be gone. She finds this acceptable.

She photographs the spillway every visit because the procedure requires it. The spillway is changing — the channel deepening, the edge of the pool retreating by millimeters, the basalt smoothing where the water runs. She has a sequence of photographs, six years of them, showing the same view, and the changes are so slow that no single photograph shows anything the previous one didn't, but laid in sequence — flipped through — the rock appears to *move.* To soften. To age. She has shown the sequence to no one. She keeps it in a folder on her desktop labeled with the site number. Sometimes, at the lab, between other tasks, she opens the folder and flips through the photographs, and it's like watching something breathe at a time scale she can't quite perceive. She closes the folder and returns to work.

She does not think of Lumen Step as beautiful. She thinks of it as *correct.* The way a well-made instrument is correct — everything in proportion, everything doing what it should. The water comes up. The pool fills. The water spills. The moss grows. The gauge reads sixty percent. The amphipods drift. The shed hums. She comes and she does the things she does and she leaves and the place goes on without her and the going-on is the point. She has never articulated this. If asked, she'd say it's a monitoring job, routine, a long drive for a short visit. She'd say the road is bad. She'd say the coffee thermos doesn't stay hot long enough at this elevation. She would not say: I go because the place does not need me, and that is the most restful thing I have ever found.

## The Amphipods

There are eleven of them right now. There have been as many as twenty-three (that she has counted) and as few as four. They are not a colony in the way she thinks of colonies — no central structure, no queen, no organization visible from outside. They are simply *there,* the way moss is there, the way the water is there, having arrived through some path she can't trace — perhaps in the groundwater itself, pushed up through the fissures, carried from some deeper reservoir where a population persists in mineral darkness, or perhaps as eggs, dessication-resistant, stuck to the foot of a bird that landed here once, years ago, when this basalt was new and the pool was just beginning.

They are white. Not the white of albinism — the white of *never having needed* color. They have no eyes. The mutations that would have produced eyes occurred and were not selected for, or never occurred at all, and the result is a smooth, featureless head, blunt, slightly translucent, through which you can see — if you look closely, if the light is right — the dark shadow of something internal, gut or nerve, processing. They are small. Two centimeters, maybe three. They move by walking, not swimming — their legs are articulated for surfaces, their bodies flattened to press against the submerged rock and the moss that covers it. They are bottom-dwellers in a pool that is only thigh-deep. When the bubbling intensifies — the cycles of pressurized upwelling that come every few minutes — they flatten. They press themselves into the moss, into the cracks, against the stone, and they wait. When the water settles, they resume. She has watched this happen and it looks like breathing.

They eat what grows on the submerged rock. Biofilm. Algae. The bacterial mat that forms on every wet surface in this place and that she has never thought to sample, though it would be the easiest thing in the world to swab and culture and identify, and she hasn't, and this might be the most telling detail about her and her work here: she has not tried to understand the amphipods. She counts them. That is all. They are a number on a form, a field marked *fauna count* that she fills in and that goes into the system and that nobody reads. But she counts them carefully, every time, standing at the pool's edge, waiting for the stillness between bubbles, waiting for the brief clarity when the light comes through and the pale shapes are visible against the dark bottom. She counts and she is glad when the number is stable and she is not sure what she'd feel if the number dropped to zero.

The amphipods do not know she counts them. They do not know anything about her. They do not know the shed exists, or the gauge, or the stencil on the metal, or the initials cut in the basalt. They know the pool — its temperature, its mineral taste, the texture of its floor, the cycle of pressure that comes up through the fissures, and the edges where the water spills and where the moss grows thickest and where the biofilm is sweetest. They know each other, probably, in the way that very small animals know each other — as presence, as movement, as *something else moving in the water.* They do not mate for life. They do not mourn. They do not plan. They are pressed to the stone and the stone feeds them and the water moves and they move with it and one day the pool will widen and they will live in the widened pool and they will not know it is different.

## The Snails

There are more than two. She has only seen two because she has only looked where she happens to look — the square meter of wall near the spillway, where the light is best and the moss is thinnest. But the snails are everywhere on the walls, in the moss, in the liverworts, on the wet stone where the seepage films down. They are small — eight, nine millimeters — and dark-shelled, the color of the basalt they live on, which is not coincidence and not adaptation in the intentional sense but simply the result of being made from the same place. Calcium is scarce in this young rock. The shells are thin. They are the color of what they have.

They graze. This is their entire existence, and it is not a small thing. The biofilm — the same film the amphipods eat underwater — grows on every wet surface, and the snails move across it in invisible paths, rasping with radulae too small to feel, leaving the silvery trails that harden to nothing within hours. A snail's life, here, is a life of continuous eating. There is no shortage. The water never stops; the film never stops growing; the snail never stops rasping. It is, in its way, the most complete life in the room — more complete than hers, which involves leaving and returning and paperwork and thermoses and roads. The snail stays. The snail eats. The snail dies — eventually, after some span of time that nobody will ever measure — and its shell dissolves back into the mineral cycle of the pool, its calcium returning to the water that made it, and another snail, somewhere on the same wall, takes its place without knowing.

They have no name. She does not name them. The procedure does not ask for a snail count. They are unrecorded. They are the most permanent thing in the room.

## The Mosses

There are four species, as she has noted, though she has only formally identified two — *Bryum* something, and a *Marchantia* that isn't quite right for the genus, the thallus margins slightly different than the key suggests, and she made a note of this once and then lost the note. The other two she hasn't keyed out. One is the fluffy pale one, the one that forms cushions where the water seeps. The other is the dark, flat, velvet one that coats the basalt in shade. They are distinct. They compete. You can see the boundaries — the edges where one species gives way to another, the front lines of a war that moves at the speed of growth, which is to say: imperceptibly, which is to say: inevitably.

The moss does not know it is winning. It is winning.

Given time — and she has thought about this, lying awake in low elevations, in dry rooms where the air is settled and warm — the moss will cover the shed. It will begin at the base, where the corrugated metal meets the basalt floor, where condensation pools in the corrugation valleys. It will climb. The rust will help — iron is a nutrient, and the corrosion will provide texture for rhizoids to grip. In five years, the lower panels will be green. In ten, the roof. The padlock will still be visible — brass doesn't corrode as fast — but the stencil number will be gone, eaten by the fluffy pale species, which she suspects is a *Bryum* but hasn't confirmed.

She has not shared this prediction with anyone. It is not the kind of thing that goes in a report. But it is the kind of thing that is *true,* and she knows it the way she knows the gauge reads sixty percent — by being here, by watching, by the accumulation of small evidences that don't add up to proof but add up to something more durable than proof.

## The Flies

They are *Scatella,* probably, or something near it — small, dark, the kind of fly that lives on wet surfaces and algal films and that appears in places where water and stone and organic matter meet. They don't bite. They don't land on her. They live in the column of light that sometimes falls through the gap in the overhang, and when the sun is there, they are a haze, a density, a living weather that occupies the brightest part of the room. When the sun leaves, they settle — onto the moss, onto the stone, onto the surfaces they share with the snails — and they become invisible. She has never seen one land. She has never seen one not flying. She suspects they rest at night, or in the dark, but she has never been here in the dark, and the thought of being here in the dark — the sounds louder, the shapes gone, the water louder — is a thought she turns away from, not because it frightens her but because it would be a different place, and she has not earned the different place. She comes during the day. She does the procedure. She leaves.

The flies don't matter to the procedure. She doesn't count them. They are the only thing in the room that moves at her speed — fast, visible, here-then-gone — and she has sometimes thought, watching them, that they are the only thing here that is on her scale at all. The moss is too slow. The snails are too slow. The water is too constant. The amphipods are too deep. But the flies — the flies live in seconds, in the brief warmth of a sun-gap, in the ordinary time of being alive and small and in a specific place, and they don't last, and they don't care.

## What Lives Here

Not the shed. Not the gauge. Not the pipe, or the cable, or the pump that hums below hearing. These are *present,* the way a fence is present in a field — placed, maintained, meaning something to someone somewhere — but they don't live here. They're the apparatus. They're the reason she comes, but they're not the reason the place *is.*

What lives here is the water.

The water lives here. It comes up from below — from how far below, she doesn't know; nobody has told her; it might be in the report she hasn't read — and it arrives in the pool through fissures in the basalt, pressurized, mineral, cold, and it fills the pool and it spills and it runs down the channel and it disappears into the crack at the base of the far wall and it goes *somewhere else.* It is always arriving. It is always leaving. It is the most transitory thing in the room — here for seconds, in the pool, before it spills and is gone — and it is the most permanent, because it will always be arriving, it was here before the moss, before the amphipods, before the snails, before the shed, before *her,* and it will be here after all of them. The pool is not the water. The pool is the *shape the water takes* when it arrives. The pool is a verb.

And what lives here is what the water makes possible — the moss, the liverworts, the ferns, the reeds, the amphipods, the snails, the flies, the biofilm they all eat. A small, enclosed, young ecosystem, born from mineral water and young stone, not more than a decade old, not less complex for its age. A garden that nobody planted. A workshop that nobody built. A room that made itself, is still making itself, will continue to make itself — widening, deepening, greening, eroding — long after the shed rusts through and the gauge fails and the chart runs out and the padlock key is lost and the initials are gone from the stone.

And what lives here is *her.* In a way she would not say, in a way that would embarrass her if it were spoken: she lives here. Not the way the amphipods live here — constantly, thoughtlessly, in perfect contact. Not the way the moss lives here — growing, claiming, covering. She lives here the way a *keeper* lives in a lighthouse: by arriving, by doing the small required things, by being present in the intervals, by carrying the place with her between visits. She is the only human who knows this room exists. She is the only consciousness that has held it — its sounds, its smells, its particular green light, its slow breathing pace — and that will hold it, and that will keep holding it, for as long as she comes, for as long as the procedure requires, for as long as the water comes up and the gauge reads sixty percent and the pen arm twitches on the chart and the moss grows over everything, including eventually, if given enough time, her.
