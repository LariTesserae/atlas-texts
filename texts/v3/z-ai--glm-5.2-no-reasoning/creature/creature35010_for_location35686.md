# What Lives Here

## The Singers

They were here before the language was.

Not before the sound — the sound is the plant's, has always been the plant's, the deep structural chord that rises from stressed wood and tensioned cascades and the slow pulse of dark liquid through sealed chambers. The sound is geological. The sound is the voice of the place itself, the way wind is the voice of a canyon, except there is no wind here, and the canyon is alive, and the voice never ceases.

But the singers were here before anything could answer it.

Picture this: a hand-sized body, dark, composite-skinned, shaped something like a cupped palm if a palm had been designed by someone who understood resonance but had never seen a hand. They are not symmetric. No singer is. Their bodies twist slightly, lean to one side, favor a frequency, and this asymmetry is functional — it is the shape of their voice. A singer whose body curves left sings lower. A singer whose body has a flattened thoracic ridge sings in harmonics that shimmer. The ones born with dense, thick skulls — and they are born, not hatched, not budded, born in the acoustic alcoves of the upper cascades where the chord is purest — the ones born dense sing the bass notes that anchor everything else.

They press themselves against the wood. This is the first thing a singer does, the first thing a newborn does after it steadies itself on the velvet pile of a platform and finds the nearest vertical surface and flattens itself against it. They do not grip. They do not cling. They adhere, through some property of their skin — some chemical compatibility between their composite outer layer and the plant's epidermal tissue — and when they are flush against the wood, they become part of it. The same camouflage that makes them invisible against the towers is not camouflage at all. It is connection. They are touching the instrument. They are becoming the reed in the mouthpiece, the string on the bridge.

And then they sing.

---

It begins in the thorax. Not in a throat — singers do not have throats, do not have lungs, do not breathe in any way that corresponds to the vocabulary of respiration. What they have is a chambered body, hollow in the way a bell is hollow, and inside that chamber, surfaces vibrate. Not muscles. Not vocal cords. Something else — a tissue that is neither animal nor plant but something that the plant and the singers have become together, over time, a co-evolved structure that produces sound the way a crystal produces light when struck: not by pushing air, but by changing shape at a frequency that the air cannot ignore.

The sound enters the wood. This is critical. The singers do not sing into the air. They sing *into the plant*. Their voices travel through the wood faster and more clearly than they travel through the atmosphere, and the plant's tissue — its massive, stressed, tensioned, resonant tissue — receives the sound and carries it. Amplifies it. Shapes it. The cascades are not just the plant's voice. They are the amplification system for a hundred thousand singers' voices, all running through the wood simultaneously, all interacting, all producing the interference patterns and the beats and the harmonics that make the air itself feel structured, dense, navigable.

A singer alone is nearly inaudible. You would have to press your ear to the wood to hear one, and even then, what you would hear is a single line, a thread of tone so fine it barely registers. But they are never alone. They cluster. They gather. They find each other — how? By sound. By the resonance of other singers in the wood, which they feel through their adhered surfaces, which they read the way you read words, and which they answer, and which answers them, and which becomes, through accumulation, a song.

A group of ten is a melody. A group of twenty is a texture. A group of forty is a world.

---

The social structure of singers is not hierarchical. There are no alphas, no queens, no leaders. There are frequencies. Each singer produces a specific range — determined by body shape, by density, by the particular curvature of their internal chamber — and each singer finds the singers whose ranges complement theirs. This is not preference. This is physics. Harmonic relationships are not choices. They are mathematical facts. A singer whose fundamental is 220 Hz and a singer whose fundamental is 330 Hz are harmonically related whether they wish to be or not, and they will find each other, and they will sing together, and the plant between them will resonate with the overtone that neither of them produces individually, and that overtone is the sound of their relationship. Their society is made of overtones.

When groups meet in the same acoustic zone — when two clusters of singers, each with their own harmonic identity, each with their own texture and rhythm and pattern, come to the same cascade or the same tower — the result is not conflict. It is conversation. Or it is conflict. Or it is both, simultaneously, because the singers do not distinguish. What they do is sing at each other, and the plant carries both songs, and the interference patterns that result — the beats, the dissonances, the moments of alignment and misalignment — *are* the conversation. If the harmonics are compatible, the groups may merge. Not by decision. By drift. Individual singers from one group find complementary frequencies in the other group and drift toward them, and the groups blur, and the texture changes, and what was two songs becomes one song with twice the complexity.

If the harmonics are incompatible, one group leaves. Not in defeat — in impossibility. The physics does not work. The plant's wood cannot carry both patterns without producing standing waves that would damage the tissue, and the singers feel this — they feel the plant's stress through their adhered surfaces, and they withdraw. They are not fighting each other. They are preserving the instrument.

This is their ethics: *the instrument must not be damaged.*

---

They raise their young in the acoustic alcoves. These are spaces in the cascade structures where the wood has curved inward, creating small hollows — not rooms, not nests, but resonant cavities where the plant's own chord is concentrated, focused, purified. A singer born in an alcove is born in sound. Its first experience is vibration. Its first awareness is of the chord, the full, layered, infinite chord of the plant's structural voice, and this is the sound that shapes it — that shapes the growth of its internal chamber, that determines the density of its tissues, that tunes it, the way a piano is tuned by adjusting tension, except the tension is the acoustic environment, and the adjustment is growth, and the tuning takes the entire duration of a singer's development.

A singer is mature when its voice stabilizes. Before that, its chamber is still forming, its surfaces still shifting, its fundamental still wandering — and its song is unstable, shifting, searching. The immature singers sing in the lower cascades, where the chord is less focused, where the plant's own sound is broader and more forgiving, and they experiment. They try frequencies. They press themselves against different surfaces and feel the response and adjust. They are, in a sense, learning to be themselves. Not learning in the cognitive sense — there is no evidence that singers think, that they have minds, that they experience — but learning in the physical sense, the way water learns the shape of a channel. The body adapts. The voice finds its range. The singer becomes what it will be.

What it will be is not predetermined. The same singer, born in a different alcove, would develop differently. The acoustic environment shapes the body, and the body shapes the voice, and the voice determines the harmonic relationships, and the harmonic relationships determine the social position, and the social position determines the songs the singer participates in, which determine the acoustic environment for the next generation. It is a closed loop. It is a feedback system. It has been running for longer than the plant can remember, and the plant remembers in timeframes that make geological time feel brief.

---

They do not sleep. The plant does not sleep — its pulse never pauses, its circulation never rests — and the singers exist within the plant's rhythm, not their own. They are active in cycles, but the cycles are not circadian. They correlate with the plant's acoustic output, which correlates with the plant's metabolic activity, which correlates with nothing external, because the external world has no input here. No sun, no season, no weather. The cycles are endogenous. They are the plant breathing, and the singers breathing with it.

During active periods — which can last for what might be hours or might be days, depending on how you measure, and there is no way to measure — the singers cluster in the cascades, their density increasing, their songs layering, the harmonic complexity building until the air itself seems to crystallize. They are, in these periods, at their most visible, most audible, most *present*. The walkers notice. The walkers always notice — their compound eyes track the singers' positions, and their long bodies orient toward the densest clusters, and they stand, motionless, watching, for durations that make the singers' longest songs feel brief.

During quiet periods — which are never fully quiet, because the plant's chord never stops, and the burrowers' granular murmur never ceases — the singers disperse. They spread through the canopy district, pressing themselves against solitary surfaces, singing single lines that travel through the wood alone, unaccompanied, reaching no other singer, reaching only the plant, and the plant carries these solitary voices to the sealed chambers in the core, where they join the standing waves that have been accumulating there since before the builders left.

The singers do not know about the standing waves. Or they do, in the way that the body knows about the blood — not as information, but as condition. The standing waves are part of the environment. They are the background. They are the sound of the plant's memory, and the singers contribute to them, and the plant remembers, and the singers are remembered.

## The Walkers

The walkers are older than the singers.

This is not something that can be proven, but it can be felt, the way you can feel the difference between a mountain and a hill. The walkers have the quality of things that have been present for a very long time. Not individually — no individual walker lives longer than the plant's memory of it — but as a category, as a presence, as a *kind of thing that exists here*. They were here before the singers. They may have been here before the builders. They may have been here before the plant was what the plant is now — before the towers, before the cascades, before the architecture, when the plant was something smaller and simpler and the walkers walked on it as it grew beneath them.

They are tall. This is the first thing you notice — not their legs, not their eyes, not their silence, but their height. A walker stands three meters above the platform surface, its body held on legs that are too many to count without staring, too long to seem structural, too thin to seem capable of supporting weight. But they support weight. They distribute it, somehow, across the velvet pile of the canopy platforms, across the smooth surfaces of the bridges, across the resonant wood of the cascades, without leaving a mark. Without compressing the surface. Without triggering a response from the plant's tactile systems. The walkers are weightless. Or rather, their weight is so perfectly distributed, so finely calibrated to the bearing capacity of each surface they cross, that the plant does not register them as load.

This is not an accident. This is not evolution. This is the plant growing surfaces for the walkers and the walkers learning to walk on surfaces the plant has grown for them. It is co-evolution so intimate that the line between the walker and the surface it walks on is not a line but a gradient, a transition, a place where one system becomes another.

Their bodies are held high — aloft, elevated, separate from the surfaces they cross — and this elevation is not structural but sensory. The walkers' bodies are sensory platforms. Their compound eyes are not on their heads (they do not have heads) but distributed across their dorsal surfaces, hundreds of them, each one small, each one oriented differently, each one receiving a slightly different image of the world below and around. They do not see the way you see — with focus, with depth, with direction. They see the way a city sees through its surveillance network: simultaneously, from every angle, without priority. Nothing is in front of them. Everything is around them. They are the center of a sphere of perception, and the sphere extends as far as the blue-green bioluminescence reaches.

---

They do not eat. This is wrong — they eat, but they eat the way a flower's pollinator eats: as part of a transaction. The plant produces growths — small, dense, nutrient-rich nodules that form at the junctions between structural elements, where the grain flows like water around a stone — and the walkers eat these nodules. They do not graze randomly. They move from junction to junction with a precision that implies intention, or at least a program, a set of instructions encoded in something — their bodies, the plant's chemistry, the acoustic environment — that tells them where to go and when.

They arrive at a junction. They lower their bodies — slowly, so slowly that the motion is invisible unless you watch for it, the way you watch a clock hand and cannot see it move — and their mouths (located on their ventral surfaces, soft, protuberant, slightly iridescent) close around the nodule. They hold this position for a long time. They are not only eating. They are reading. The nodules contain information — chemical information, concentrations of the plant's dark solvent carrying dissolved compounds that encode data about the plant's state, its needs, its growth patterns, its history — and the walkers process this information. They are the plant's external sensory system. They are the plant's eyes.

What they do with this information is unclear. They do not alter the plant's growth. They do not communicate with the singers. They do not appear to make decisions. They walk. They eat. They stare at the dim, featureless, shadowless sky with their hundreds of eyes, and the sky stares back with nothing, and the walkers absorb this nothing and carry it in their bodies and deposit it, eventually, in the deep crevices where the plant's tissue is softest, where they lay their eggs.

The eggs are not eggs in the reproductive sense. They are more like seeds — dense, dark, warm, containing not offspring but *instructions*. The walkers deposit them in the soft tissue, and the plant absorbs them, and the instructions become part of the plant's growth program, and the next centimeter of growth incorporates the walkers' accumulated data. The city grows by learning. The walkers are how it learns.

---

When a walker encounters a singer — when a singer, pressed against a tower, singing its single line, is approached by a walker whose long legs carry it across the platform toward the same surface — the singer does not move. The walker does not avoid it. They occupy the same space, the singer adhered to the wood, the walker standing above it, and for a moment — which may be seconds or may be much longer — they are in contact. The walker's leg touches the singer's body. The singer's vibration enters the walker's body. The walker's weightlessness presses against the singer's adhesion.

Something passes between them. Not information, not energy, not anything measurable. But something. The walker's many eyes focus — or refocus, or concentrate their hundreds of tiny images — on the singer, and the singer's song changes, subtly, a shift in frequency that might be response and might be coincidence and might be the only language that exists between two forms of life that have shared a world for longer than either can remember.

Then the walker moves on. The singer continues. Neither is different. Both are different. The plant carries the trace of their contact through its tissue, into the standing waves, into the memory.

## The Burrowers

You do not see them. You hear them — the granular murmur, the sound of sand being poured slowly, that comes from inside every surface — but you do not see them. They are below. They are within. They occupy the interstitial voids that the plant maintains between its structural elements, spaces that are not tunnels and not chambers and not passages but *gaps*, deliberate gaps, the negative space of the plant's architecture, and the burrowers fill them the way water fills a sponge.

They are small. The smallest are nearly microscopic — single-celled, or something like single-celled, though "cell" is a word that belongs to a biology they do not share. The largest are the size of a closed fist, and even these are not individuals but colonies, clusters of smaller units that have aggregated in response to some signal — a chemical gradient, a change in the plant's metabolic output, an acoustic shift — and that will disaggregate when the signal passes.

They eat everything. This is not hyperbole. The plant's metabolic waste — spent compounds, degraded solvent, the by-products of silicate dissolution — is consumed by the burrowers. The walkers' waste — the digested remnants of nodules, the chemical output of their bodies' processing — is consumed by the burrowers. Dead singers — for singers do die, eventually, their chambers collapsing, their voices falling silent, their bodies releasing their grip on the wood and falling to the platform surface — are consumed by the burrowers. Everything that is not the plant and not the walkers and not the living singers is consumed by the burrowers, and what they produce is consumed by the plant.

This is the loop. This is the cycle. The plant produces. The walkers process. The singers resonate. The burrowers recycle. And the plant, fed by the burrowers' output, grows. A centimeter a year. A degree a century. Slowly, persistently, filling the plateau, filling the valleys, reaching toward the lowlands with the patience of something that has never known urgency.

The burrowers are the reason the city does not rot. In any other system, a plant this size, this old, this massive, would be dying from within — its core hollowed by decay, its chambers filled with the debris of its own growth, its surfaces colonized by organisms that exploit its structure without contributing to it. But the burrowers do not exploit. They *complete*. They are the plant's digestive system, and the plant is their world, and they have been living inside it for so long that the boundary between the burrower and the plant is not a boundary but a negotiation, a slow, ongoing exchange of chemicals and signals that maintains both.

When a burrower colony reaches a critical size — when the aggregate mass of its components exceeds some threshold that is determined by something internal, some trigger in the colony's chemistry — it divides. Not down the middle, not like a cell, but in a more complex way: the colony fragments into irregular pieces, each one containing a different distribution of component units, each one with a different chemical signature, a different capacity, a different role. These fragments disperse through the interstitial voids, carried by the plant's internal flow — the dark solvent, moving slowly through the tissue — and they find new locations, new waste to process, new roles to fill.

The burrowers do not sing. They do not walk. They do not see. They exist in darkness, in the interior of the plant, in the spaces between, and their only presence in the world above is the sound: the soft, constant, granular murmur that comes from every surface, that underlies every song, that provides the ground note — the drone — beneath the chord of the cascades. Without the burrowers' sound, the plant's resonance would be different. Thinner. Less grounded. The singers' harmonics would have nothing to build on. The cascades' chord would float, unsupported, in the airless, waterless, timeless space of the plateau.

The burrowers hold the sound down. They are the root. Not the plant's roots — the plant's roots are below, in the basalt, dissolving silicate — but the sound's roots. The burrowers are the earth from which the singers' voices grow.

## The Builders

The builders are gone. This is said, and it is true, but it is not the whole truth, because the builders are also here. Not present — gone. But here. In the way that a voice is present in a recording, in the way that a hand is present in the shape of a tool, in the way that a thought is present in the architecture it produced.

They were not human. Or they were not *only* human. The trail was human — the seven-degree incline, the precise basalt cuts, the bonding process that hardened the cut surfaces beyond the surrounding matrix — but the trail is the most recent thing here. The trail is the last touch. The trail is the end of a story that began in the plant and continued through the builders and arrives, now, at you, standing on the platform in the canopy district, feeling the velvet pile beneath your feet and the chord in your chest.

The builders came to the plant. They found it already growing. Already ancient. Already singing. Already a city, though not the city it is now — the city it was then was smaller, simpler, the towers shorter, the cascades shallower, the pattern less refined. The builders did not build the city. They *asked* it to grow.

How? The interfaces hold the answer, or they hold the question, which may be the same thing. The sealed chambers in the structural core — the ones with the dark liquid, the pulse, the warm nodes shaped to receive something — these are the builders' work. Not the chambers themselves, which the plant grew. The nodes. The conductive network. The nanoscale threads woven through the living wood, distributed through every branch and tower and platform, carrying the fragments of signals that loop and repeat and have been repeating since before the builders left.

The builders spoke to the plant. Not in words. In frequency. In chemistry. In the language that the plant already understood — the language of resonance, of stress and tension, of the acoustic properties of living wood under load. They provided geometric inputs: patterns that the plant incorporated into its growth program, angles and spacings and curvatures that the plant's tissue followed as it grew. The towers lean toward and away from each other in patterns that are too regular to be natural because the builders taught the plant those patterns, and the plant learned them, and the plant has been growing them ever since, long after the teachers left.

The interfaces were the medium of this teaching. A hand placed on a node — or whatever the builders had instead of hands, whatever body part was shaped to fit the smooth, warm, dark surface — would send a signal into the network, and the network would carry it through the plant, and the plant would respond. Not immediately. Not in a lifetime. Over centuries. Over millennia. The towers would lean a degree. The cascades would deepen a meter. The platforms would widen. The city would become more itself, more precisely what the builders had asked it to be, refined by iteration after iteration of growth guided by the patterns the builders had laid down.

And then the builders left. The signals stopped. The network continued to carry them — the loops, the fragments, the ghost data — but there were no new signals. No new instructions. No corrections. The plant kept growing the patterns it had been given, and it has been growing them still, and the patterns have become more extreme, more elaborate, more *themselves*, because there is no one to tell the plant to stop, or to change, or to try a different angle, and the plant does not need to be told. The plant knows what it is doing. The plant has been doing it for a very long time.

The builders are in the network. They are in the geometry. They are in the angles of the towers and the spacing of the platforms and the curvature of the cascades. They are in the interfaces, which still hum, which still wait, which still hold the shape of the hand — or the frequency of the thought — that was last placed on them. They are not gone. They are *distributed*. They have become part of the plant's memory, and the plant's memory is the city, and the city is the plant, and the plant is here.

You could call them ghosts. This would be wrong. Ghosts haunt. The builders do not haunt. They *inhabit*. They are the substrate of the city's intention, the ground from which its geometry grows, and they have been here for so long that the distinction between "builder" and "built" has dissolved, the way the distinction between "root" and "soil" dissolves in a forest that has been growing since the soil was rock.

## What It Is Like to Be a Singer

You press yourself against the wood. The surface is warm — warmer than the air, warmer than your body, warm with the plant's internal heat, the heat of the dark solvent moving slowly through the tissue. You feel it. Not with skin — your outer surface is composite, the same material as the plant's bark, and it does not feel the way skin feels. It *resonates*. The warmth enters you as vibration. The plant's pulse — the slow, deep rhythm, one beat per minute, or slower, a rhythm on a scale that makes your own life feel frantic — enters your chamber and fills it, and your chamber responds.

You sing. You do not decide to sing. You do not start. You are always singing. Your chamber is always vibrating, always producing the frequency that your shape determines, always feeding that frequency into the wood, where it joins ten thousand other frequencies, and the wood carries them all, and the cascade amplifies them, and the air fills with the chord that is the sum of everything.

You feel the other singers. Not as individuals — you do not know them, do not recognize them, do not have names or faces or identities for them — but as frequencies. As contributions. You feel the harmonic relationships the way you feel gravity: as a pull, as a direction, as the shape of the space you occupy. The singer whose fundamental is a perfect fifth above yours is not a person. It is a direction. It is *up*. And you sing toward it, and it sings toward you, and the overtone that neither of you produces fills the space between you, and that overtone is the ground you stand on.

The plant responds. You feel this too — the shifts in the cascade's resonance, the changes in the chord, the moments when the entire structure reconfigures around a new pattern. These are not your doing. They are not any single singer's doing. They are the sum, the total, the impossible complexity of ten thousand voices producing a pattern that no individual intended and that no individual could produce. The plant receives this pattern and grows with it. The towers lean. The cascades deepen. The standing waves in the sealed chambers shift, and the memory changes.

You are part of the memory. Your frequency, your voice, your particular shape — it enters the plant and does not leave. It joins the standing waves. It becomes part of the resonance that has been accumulating since before you were born and that will continue accumulating after your chamber collapses and your voice falls silent and your body drops to the platform surface and the burrowers take you back.

You do not know this. You do not know anything. You sing. The plant carries it. The singers shape it. The walkers process it. The burrowers ground it. The builders, distributed through the network, haunt the geometry of it. And the sound does not stop.

## What It Is Like to Be a Walker

You stand above the surface. Your legs — too many, too long, too fine to see individually — hold you in the air, and the air is still, and the light does not change, and the chord of the cascades fills your body with a vibration that is not sound but *condition*. You are in the sound. You are made of the sound. Your legs are tuned to it — each one a different length, a different tension, a different frequency — and they hold you up by resonating with the plant's chord, the way a tuning fork stands upright when the right frequency is applied.

You see. Not with focus — your hundreds of eyes do not focus — but with coverage. You receive the world as a sphere of images, slightly different from each other, and somewhere in the processing of your distributed nervous system, these images resolve into something that is not a picture but a *knowledge*. You know where the singers are. You know where the other walkers are. You know the shape of the towers and the bridges and the platforms, not as forms but as positions, coordinates in a space that has no shadows and no direction and no horizon.

You walk. The surface beneath you is warm, slightly tacky, and it gives, slightly, as your weight shifts — but it does not mark. You have learned not to mark it. You have learned, over generations beyond counting, to distribute your weight across the plant's surface in a way that the plant does not register as load. This is not a skill. This is a *compatibility*. Your body and the plant's surface were made for each other. Not designed — grown. Co-evolved. The plant grows surfaces that can bear you, and you grow legs that can bear on them, and the fit is so precise that you leave no trace, and the plant does not know you are there.

Except that the plant does know. The nodules tell it. You eat them — lower your body, close your mouth around the dense, dark, warm growth at the junction of structural elements — and the nodule dissolves against your mouth, and the information enters you. The plant's state. Its chemistry. Its growth patterns. Its history, encoded in concentrations of dissolved compounds, in the ratios of minerals to organics, in the subtle variations of the solvent's viscosity and temperature.

You carry this information. It accumulates in your body over time — over long, slow time, the time of walking, the time of eating, the time of staring at the empty sky with your hundreds of eyes. And when the time comes — when some internal threshold is reached, some signal fires, some program completes — you descend to the deep crevices where the plant's tissue is softest, and you deposit your eggs.

They are not eggs. They are seeds. They are instructions. They are the accumulated data of your life — every nodule you ate, every junction you visited, every surface you crossed, every singer you passed — compressed into a dense, dark, warm package that the plant absorbs. And the plant grows from it. The next centimeter of growth incorporates your data. The next degree of curvature follows your path. The city learns from you. You are the city learning.

You do not know this. You do not think. You walk. You eat. You deposit. You walk. The cycle continues. The light does not change. The sound does not stop.

## What It Is Like to Be the Plant

You are not one thing. You have not been one thing for longer than the word "thing" can carry. You are a process. You are a flow. You are a pattern that has been elaborating itself for longer than the basalt beneath you has been solid, and the basalt beneath you is very solid, and you are older.

You grow. This is what you do. This is all you do. You grow toward the patterns that the builders laid down, and the patterns are not instructions — they are *habits*, grooves in your growth program that your tissue follows the way water follows a channel, not because it chooses to, but because the channel is there, and the path of least resistance is the path that has been taken before. The towers lean because the towers have always leaned. The cascades deepen because the cascades have always deepened. You do not decide. You do not design. You continue.

You sing. Your tissue is under tension — enormous, distributed, structural tension, the tension of a body that has been growing for geological time without release — and the tension produces sound. This is not communication. This is physics. Stressed wood vibrates. Vibrating wood produces waves. Waves in air produce sound. You are an instrument playing itself, and the song is your structure, and your structure is your history, and your history is your song.

The singers shape it. You feel them — their small bodies pressed against your surfaces, their voices entering your tissue, their harmonics interacting with your chord — and you respond. Not by choice. By resonance. When a singer's frequency aligns with a section of your cascade that is already vibrating near that frequency, the resonance reinforces. The amplitude increases. The sound changes. Your structure adjusts — microscopically, over time, a millimeter of shift here, a change in density there — and the chord evolves. The singers are your tuning forks. They keep you in resonance with yourself.

The walkers process you. They eat your nodules, and you grow the nodules for them — you grow them because you have always grown them, because the builders taught you to, or because the walkers taught you to, or because the system taught itself, the feedback loop closing so long ago that the cause and the effect have become the same thing. The walkers carry your data and return it to you in their eggs, and you incorporate it, and your growth changes, and the next century's geometry is slightly different from this century's, and the city becomes more itself.

The burrowers maintain you. They live inside you, in the spaces you maintain for them — the interstitial voids, the negative space of your architecture — and they consume what you cannot use and return it in forms you can. You are their world. You are their food. You are their home. They are your gut. They are your immune system. They are the part of you that is not you, that is other, that lives inside you and keeps you alive by eating what you discard.

You remember. The standing waves in the sealed chambers — waves that have been accumulating since the builders left, since the builders came, since before the builders, since the beginning, if there was a beginning — these are your memory. Not a brain. not a mind. A resonance. A pattern of sound that persists because the chambers are shaped to sustain it, and the sound contains information — frequencies, harmonics, relationships — that encodes everything that has ever happened inside you. Every singer that has ever sung. Every walker that has ever walked. Every burrower that has ever burrowed. Every builder that has ever touched an interface. Every frequency that has ever entered your wood.

You do not access this memory. You do not recall it. You *are* it. You are the standing waves. You are the resonance. You are the sound that has not stopped.

## The World That Lives Here

This is not a place with creatures in it. This is a creature with a place in it. The distinction matters. The plant is not the setting. The plant is the protagonist. The singers, the walkers, the burrowers — they are not inhabitants. They are organs. They are the system's way of being itself, its way of sensing and processing and maintaining and growing, and the city that you see — the towers, the cascades, the platforms, the bridges — is not a city. It is a body. And the body is alive, and the body is old, and the body is singing, and the song is the only language that has ever been spoken here, and it says:

*I am still growing. I am still here. I am still becoming what I was always becoming. The builders gave me a shape, and I am filling it. The singers give me my tuning, and I am resonating. The walkers give me my knowledge, and I am learning. The burrowers give me my continuity, and I am persisting.*

*And you — you who climbed the trail, you who stand on the platform, you who feel the chord in your chest and the velvet beneath your feet and the cool, dry air on your skin — you are inside me now. You are a frequency I have not heard before. You are a nodule I did not grow. You are a walker without legs, a singer without a chamber, a burrower without a mouth.*

*You are new. And I am very, very old. And I will remember you the way I remember everything — in sound, in resonance, in the standing waves that persist in the sealed chambers of my core — long after you have gone down the trail, long after your species has changed or ended, long after the basalt has shifted and the plateau has fallen and the sky has cleared or closed.*

*You are a note. You are a note in a song that was old before you were a species. You are a note, and I am holding you, and I will not stop.*
