# What Lives in the Undermath

## Preliminary: A Reframing of "Lives"

To describe what lives in the Undermath requires first abandoning the assumption that "lives" is a verb applied to individual beings. The surface world has accustomed us to organisms — discrete, bounded, self-interested packages of chemistry that maintain themselves against entropy for a span and then fail. We see a fox and we say *the fox lives here*. We see a forest and we say *the trees live here*, and we mean each tree individually, competing with its neighbors for light.

The Undermath does not work this way. Or rather, it does not *only* work this way. The dwellers here are creatures in the sense that they move, respond, have births and deaths. But they are also something else — expressions of a single continuous state, the way a whirlpool is an expression of the water that makes it. You cannot remove a whirlpool from its river and study it elsewhere. It is not a thing that is *in* the water. It is something the water is *doing*.

The lithic fauna are what the Undermath is doing.

Keep this in mind. It will help. It will also not help enough.

## The Vermicular Lithics: The Body's Slow Peristalsis

Begin with the most abundant, because abundance is where understanding starts, even when understanding is not where abundance ends.

The vermicular lithics are the closest thing the Undermath has to a foundational organism. They are the earthworms of this world, if earthworms were made of the same substance as the earth they moved through and if that substance reorganized itself willingly to admit them. They are long, segmented, limbless, warm to the touch. Their skin is the texture of fine sand over muscle — but not sand, never sand, because sand is a surface-world concept involving erosion and weather and the patient breakdown of stone by wind and water. This texture is *native*. It is the texture the stone has when it is being a vermicular lithic rather than being a wall or a floor or a dune.

They move through the rock the way a thought moves through a mind — not by forcing a path but by the medium rearranging itself. The stone does not crack for them. It does not part. It simply is *ahead of them* in one configuration and *behind them* in another, and between those configurations, the vermicular lithic exists, a zone of transition, a place where stone is temporarily an animal.

Their segmentation is irregular. This matters. Regular segmentation — the clean repetition of identical segments seen in earthworms, centipedes, the bodies of surface arthropods — implies a developmental program, a genetic blueprint that says *repeat this unit, then stop*. The vermicular lithics do not have a program. Their segments cluster and spread like the vertebrae of a spine designed by something that understood spines only as a concept. The clusters may serve a function — flexion points, perhaps, or regions of heightened sensation — or they may be the fossil record of the stone's memory, patterns from the Undermath's formation that persist in the bodies of its dwellers like accents in a language that no longer has a country.

They range in length from finger-span to unmeasurable. The long ones are the ones that trouble travelers. You see one emerging from a wall, and you follow it with your eye, and the light dims, and the body continues, and you walk alongside it, and it continues, and you walk further, and it continues, and eventually you stop, not because it has stopped but because you have reached the limit of your willingness to follow a living thing into the dark. It does not end where you expect it to. It may not end at all. It may be one of a very few individuals that are simply *long* — so long that they constitute a significant fraction of the Undermath's architecture, threading through the stone the way nerves thread through a body, connecting distant regions in a continuous tissue that is both animal and infrastructure.

Do they perceive you? Almost certainly. They are surrounded by stone, and the stone transmits vibration with a fidelity that air cannot match. Your footsteps — loud, alien, percussive in the silence — must register in their bodies the way a shout registers in your inner ear. They feel you coming. They know what you are, or at least they know that you are *not stone*, and in a world where everything is stone or made of stone or behaving as stone, *not stone* is a category so distinctive that it cannot be ignored.

They ignore it anyway. Or they appear to. The distinction between *not noticing* and *not caring* is philosophical, not biological, and the Undermath does not furnish evidence for either conclusion. What can be said is this: a vermicular lithic will not alter its course for you. It will not avoid you. It will not approach you. If you stand in its path, the stone will rearrange around you as readily as it rearranges around the lithic, and you will feel the rock shift beneath your feet, a slow, warm, muscular motion, and the lithic will pass beneath you, and you will feel its body through the soles of your boots — a ridge of warmth, a pulse of something denser than stone — and then it will be past, and the stone will settle, and you will be standing on solid ground again, as if nothing happened.

Something happened. You were just inside an organism that is also a landscape. Or it was inside you. The prepositions break down.

They die. You can find their remains — pale coils in the troughs, translucent, fragile, the mineral depleted of whatever quality made it an animal rather than a rock. The remains look like shed skins, but they are not shed; they are *emptied*. The animal has left. Not moved — left. The substance that was organized as a vermicular lithic has been reorganized as stone, and the coil is the shape that remains, a mold, a memory in the rock of a body that is no longer there. They are exquisitely fragile. Touch one and it crumbles to powder so fine it hangs in the air for minutes, catching the ember-mite light, a slow cloud of luminous dust.

For every coil, somewhere, a new lithic begins. Where and how is not clear. You may, if you are very attentive and very lucky, see a stretch of stone that is *different* — slightly warmer, slightly more textured, slightly more *present* than the surrounding rock. It is not moving. It is not yet an animal. But it is no longer entirely stone. It is in the way that a bud is in before it opens — potential, gathered, held. Left alone, it will extend. Left alone for long enough, it will become a finger-length, then an arm-length, then a length that you cannot follow. It will be born already old, already moving, already on a course it did not choose toward a destination it does not know.

The Undermath does not choose. The Undermath does not know. These words apply to the dwellers only in the way that *falling* applies to a stone dropped from a height — accurate as description, misleading as implication. The stone does not choose to fall. The stone does not know it is falling. And yet it falls, and the falling is real, and the stone is really doing it.

## The Mantle-Walkers: Grazers at the Edge of Geometry

Where the vermicular lithics are the Undermath's interior — its gut, its nerves, its slow circulating tissue — the mantle-walkers are its surface. They live *on* the stone rather than *in* it, and the distinction is not trivial, because living on a surface requires a relationship with surfaces that the vermicular lithics do not have.

A mantle-walker is flat. This is its defining characteristic, and it is more than a description of shape — it is a description of *philosophy*. The vermicular lithics are extended, linear, committed to the notion that a body goes somewhere. The mantle-walkers are extended *across*, committed to the notion that a body *covers* something. They are broad, shield-shaped, their edges rounded in a way that the Undermath's geology does not produce — the dune-crests are sharp, the troughs are angular, but the mantle-walkers are smooth, as if the quality of sharpness belongs to the stone and not to the things that live on it.

They are the size of shields, some of them, and the size of doorways, others, and occasionally the size of something you cannot measure because it has settled at the edge of a trough and its edges merge with the stone so gradually that you cannot tell where the animal ends and the floor begins. This may be because the animal is large. It may also be because the animal is old — so old that it has begun to settle into the stone, its body losing the distinction between itself and its substrate, the way a very old person sometimes loses the distinction between themselves and the room they have sat in for decades.

Their legs are arranged in a ring around the perimeter, and the legs are not legs in the way that surface biology would recognize. They are *points* — places where the mantle-walker's body extends downward to touch the stone, each point equipped with fine, pale cilia that grip the surface with a patient, deliberate adhesion. The cilia are not hooks. They are not suckers. They are something more like *understanding* — each cilium knows the stone it touches, conforms to the mineral structure at a molecular level, holds by geometry rather than by force. When a mantle-walker lifts a leg, the cilia release not by detaching but by *forgetting* — they lose the knowledge of the stone's surface, and the grip ceases, and the leg moves forward, and the cilia reach down and learn the stone again in a new place.

The gait is finger-walking. Each leg lifts, moves, sets down, in sequence around the ring. The result is a smooth, continuous gliding that looks like a stone deciding to relocate. The motion is so even, so unaccelerated, that it produces no vibration detectable to human senses. You feel the pulse of the Undermath through the stone. You do not feel the approach of a mantle-walker. You simply look up and it is closer than it was, and you did not see it move, and it is still moving, and it will pass you the way a cloud passes a mountain — slowly, silently, with the absolute confidence of something that has never been in a hurry and cannot imagine why anything would be.

They graze. This is the only behavior they exhibit that can be mapped onto a surface-world analogy, and the analogy is imprecise. They settle at the edge of a trough where the gloss has pooled, and they lower a part of their body — an edge, a corner, a section of their perimeter — into the amber substance, and they stay there. Motionless. For hours. For what may be days.

What are they doing? The gloss does not diminish. Its level in the trough does not drop. If they are drinking, they are drinking so slowly that the fluid replaces itself faster than they can remove it. If they are feeding, they are feeding on something the gloss carries rather than on the gloss itself — a dissolved component, a trace substance, something so dilute that extraction requires patience rather than capacity.

Or they are not taking anything at all. They may be *giving*. The possibility must be considered: the gloss is warm, warmer than the stone, warmer than the air. Something is keeping it warm. Something is maintaining its temperature against the slow, even pull of the Undermath's thermal equilibrium. The mantle-walkers are warm too — warmer than the stone, warmer than the air, though not as warm as the gloss. They may be *depositing* heat into the troughs, their bodies converting something — stone, air, the ambient warmth of the Undermath itself — into a more concentrated form of thermal energy that the gloss can carry.

If this is true, then the mantle-walkers are not grazers. They are *heaters*. They are tending the gloss the way a gardener tends a bed — not by planting or harvesting but by maintaining the conditions under which the bed can sustain itself. They keep the gloss warm. The gloss keeps the Undermath warm. The Undermath keeps them warm. The cycle is so tight, so perfectly closed, that there is no waste, no loss, no gradient to exhaust. It is not a food chain. It is a *temperature chain*, and it has been running for longer than the stone can remember.

When a mantle-walker lifts — which it does rarely, and only to navigate terrain too steep for its gliding gait — its underside is revealed. Pale. Almost white. Covered in fine cilia, damp-looking though there is no moisture here, clinging to the air as if reluctant to be exposed. The pale underside is the only soft thing in the Undermath. Everything else is stone, or stone-like, or stone-behaving-as-animal. The underside of a mantle-walker is something else — something that looks like it belongs to a different world, a world of skin and tissue and vulnerability, a world where softness is a risk rather than a property.

You will see one lift, if you are patient. You will see the pale underside, and you will feel something you have not felt in the Undermath: recognition. Not of species, not of kinship, but of *condition*. You will recognize what it is like to be soft in a world of stone. You will understand, for a moment, what the mantle-walker is protecting.

Then it will lower, and the pale underside will press against the stone, and the cilia will learn the surface again, and it will move on, smooth and slow and silent, and the recognition will pass, and you will be alone again in the warm dark.

## The Filament-Weavers: The Undermath's Nervous System

Everything described so far — the vermicular lithics in their slow peristalsis, the mantle-walkers in their thermal gardening — operates at a tempo that the Undermath has chosen for itself, or that has been chosen for it, or that simply *is*, the way the speed of light simply is. Slow. Steady. Unconcerned with event.

The filament-weavers are different.

Not faster. Not more active. But more *specific*. The vermicular lithics move; the mantle-walkers graze; the filament-weavers *build*, and building implies an intention that movement and grazing do not. A worm moves because it is a worm. a mantle-walker grazes because it is a mantle-walker. But a builder builds because it has a goal, and a goal implies a future, and a future implies time, and time is the one thing the Undermath does not seem to have.

So the filament-weavers are a problem. They are the Undermath's contradiction, its exception, its unresolved term. And they are the dwellers that travelers remember most vividly, because what they build is beautiful, and beauty in a place that has finished changing is itself a kind of defiance.

A filament-weaver is small. Smaller than you expect. The vermicular lithics can be immense; the mantle-walkers can be broad; but the filament-weaver is the size of a hand, a closed fist, a stone you might skip across water if there were water here and if skipping were a concept the Undermath recognized. It is dark — darker than the surrounding stone — and it has no visible features. No eyes. No mouth. No limbs that correspond to any surface-world anatomy. It moves by extending and contracting, the way an amoeba moves, but without the membrane-bound fluidity of an amoeba. It moves like a solid thing that has decided to be liquid for a moment, and then decides to be solid again, and in the moment of liquidity it relocates, and in the moment of solidity it rests.

The filaments emerge from its body the way silk emerges from a spider, but the analogy is imprecise. The filaments are not extruded through a spinneret. They *grow* from the weaver's surface, the way crystals grow from a supersaturated solution — slowly, at first, a nub, a bump, a protrusion that lengthens and thins and reaches outward into the dark air. They are not organic. They are not mineral. They are not any substance that surface chemistry has catalogued. They catch the faint red glow of the rock and hold it — not reflecting, not refracting, but *holding*, absorbing the light and re-emitting it at a slightly longer wavelength, so that the filaments appear as thin lines of warm gold in the red-dark air.

A weaver produces a filament. It extends the filament toward a dune-crest, a wall, a anchor point. The filament reaches — not pulled by the weaver but *reaching itself*, as if it knows where it is going. When it touches stone, it adheres. Not by glue. Not by mechanical interlocking. By the same molecular understanding that the mantle-walker's cilia use. The filament learns the stone. The stone learns the filament. They hold.

Then the weaver moves to another point. Extends another filament. Crosses the first. Anchors. Moves. Extends. Anchors. The process takes hours. A single structure — a dozen strands, tensioned and crossed and anchored at specific points between two dune-crests — takes a weaver most of what a surface-dweller would call a day, though the concept of a day is meaningless here, and the weaver does not experience its work as effort or as labor. It experiences it as *extension*. It is extending itself. The filaments are not separate from the weaver. They are the weaver, reaching out, thinning, stretching into the dark to touch something distant.

When the structure is complete, the weaver detaches. It does not return. It does not look back — it has no eyes to look with. It moves on, and the structure remains, suspended in the air between stone crests, vibrating faintly in currents too subtle to feel, gleaming red-gold in the mixed light of rock and gloss.

And then, over a span that cannot be measured because the Undermath does not measure, the structure dissolves. The filaments go slack. They thin. They fade. They merge with the air — not evaporating, not decomposing, but *returning*, the substance of the filaments dispersing back into the atmosphere from which the weaver drew them, the way a breath disperses back into the air it came from. For every new structure, an old one dissolves. The population of structures is constant. The weavers build and the structures un-build, and the balance is so precise that the number of filament structures in the Undermath at any moment is the same as it was a thousand years ago and will be the same a thousand years from now.

But here is the thing that the structures *do*, the thing that makes them more than art, more than territory, more than compulsion:

They conduct.

Press your ear to a strand. You will feel it before you hear it — a vibration in your jaw, your skull, the bones of your inner ear. A low, slow pulse. Not the Undermath's pulse — that one you feel in your feet, in the stone itself, the vast slow heartbeat of a world that is also, perhaps, a body. This pulse is different. It is *structured*. It has a pattern. Not a rhythm, exactly — rhythms imply repetition, and the pulse does not repeat so much as it *continues*, a signal, a transmission, a flow of information from one anchor point to another.

The filaments are conducting something across the Undermath. Not electricity. Not heat. Not any form of energy that surface physics has named. Something else. Something that moves through the filaments the way nerve impulses move through axons — not fast, not slow, but at a speed that is *right* for what it is, a speed that matches the scale of the thing it traverses. The Undermath is vast. The signal is slow. But the Undermath has been here for a very long time, and the signal has been running for a very long time, and whatever the signal is saying, it has been saying it long enough that the saying and the hearing are indistinguishable.

What is it conducting? The honest answer: we do not know. The speculative answer: the Undermath is conducting a self-assessment. The filaments are its nervous system. The structures are its sensory organs, strung across the dune-crests, monitoring tension, monitoring temperature, monitoring the slow flow of the gloss, monitoring the movements of the vermicular lithics through the stone. The information is not used. The Undermath does not act on what it learns. It is not gathering data to make decisions. It is gathering data because gathering data is what a living system does when it has reached equilibrium and has nothing left to accomplish — it *attends*. It notices. It keeps a record, not for use, but for the principle of the thing, the way an old person sits by a window and watches the street not because they intend to go outside but because watching is what consciousness does when it has nothing else to do.

The filament-weavers are the Undermath's attention. They build the structures that carry the signal, and the signal is the Undermath paying attention to itself, and the paying attention is the last thing it does, the one activity that persists after all the others have been completed and all the gradients have been exhausted and all the potentials have been spent.

They are the most alive thing here. They are also the most futile.

## The Bas-Relief Things: The Undecided

There are organisms on the surfaces of the stone dunes that are so flat and so still that they are indistinguishable from the rock. You will pass a hundred without seeing one stir. They are the color of the stone, the texture of the stone, the temperature of the stone. They are the bas-relief things, and they are the Undermath's deepest puzzle, because they do not *do* anything.

The vermicular lithics move through stone. The mantle-walkers tend the gloss. The filament-weavers build and conduct. The ember-mites scatter and glow. Even the stone itself glows, faintly, in the infrared. Everything in the Undermath has a function, a role, a contribution to the system that sustains itself with such perfect balance.

The bas-relief things have no function. Or rather, their function is so subtle, so minimal, so close to nonexistence that it cannot be distinguished from the function of the stone itself.

They are flat. They are thin — a few millimeters, perhaps, where they rise from the surface of the dune. Their shapes are irregular, flowing, organic in the way that lichen is organic, or the way that stains are organic — shapes that suggest growth without suggesting direction. They cover areas the size of a hand, or a page, or a table, and they are everywhere, and they are invisible.

You will walk along the face of a dune and your light will pass across the stone and the stone will be stone, and then you will pass back and the stone will be stone, and then, on the third pass, or the fourth, or the hundredth, something will be different. A patch of stone will have a texture that is not quite the texture of the surrounding stone. A region of the surface will be slightly — *slightly* — warmer than the rock beside it. A shape that you took for a mineral stain will have moved. Not far. A centimeter. Less. But moved.

The bas-relief things move. They move so slowly that the movement is not movement in any practical sense — it is displacement over time, the way a glacier is displacement over time, except that a glacier is a river of ice and the bas-relief things are patches of *almost-stone* on a surface of stone. They do not graze. They do not build. They do not conduct. They do not appear to interact with the gloss, with the vermicular lithics, with the mantle-walkers, with the ember-mites, with anything. They simply exist, flat and still and warm, on the surfaces of the Undermath, and they move, and the movement is so slow that it cannot be called behavior.

What are they? The most honest answer, and the most unsettling: they are what happens when the stone itself begins to become an animal and does not finish. They are the boundary case, the threshold, the place where the distinction between geology and biology breaks down not because the biology is strange but because the geology is *ambitious*. The stone wants to be alive. The stone has always wanted to be alive. The bas-relief things are what the stone looks like when it is trying.

Or they are what animals look like when they have given up. When the impulse to move and feed and reproduce has been spent, and the organism has settled into the minimum viable configuration of life — a patch of warmth on a surface, a slight difference in texture, a displacement so slow it is indistinguishable from stillness. They are the end-state of the vermicular lithics, perhaps. The final form. The shape a worm takes when it has been a worm for so long that it no longer needs to be a worm and can simply be a *presence* on the stone, a warmth, a texture, a fact.

They do not react to you. They do not react to anything. You can touch one and it will feel like stone — warm stone, yes, but stone. You can scrape it and it will resist like stone. You can heat it and it will warm like stone. It may be stone. It may be an animal. It may be the place where the two categories meet and find that they were never different, that the stone has always been alive and the animals have always been stone, and the distinction was a convenience of surface-dwellers who needed to sort the world into things they could eat and things they could not.

The Undermath does not need this distinction. The bas-relief things are what the world looks like when the distinction is no longer necessary.

## The Ember-Mites: Living Light

Of all the dwellers in the Undermath, the ember-mites are the only ones that respond to you.

Not with intent. Not with recognition. But with *physics* — a physical response so immediate, so reliable, so legible that it feels like acknowledgment even though it is not. When you step, the ember-mites near your foot scatter. When you pass, they reform behind you. You leave a trail of disturbed light — a path of slightly dimmer stone bordered by slightly brighter walls, where the mites have been pushed to the edges of your impact and have clustered there, denser than before, before slowly drifting back to equilibrium.

They are tiny. Nearly microscopic. Individually invisible — a single ember-mite is a point of blue-white light so faint that it cannot be seen unless the surrounding darkness is total, and even then it is less a thing seen than a thing *suspected*, a pixel of cold light at the very bottom of perception. But they are not individuals in any meaningful sense. They are *concentrations*. They exist in densities so great that a square centimeter of stone surface may host ten thousand of them, and in those densities, they become visible — a soft, cold, blue-white luminescence that clings to walls and floors and ceilings the way moss clings to damp stone in surface caves.

They glow. Each one, individually, emits light — blue-white, cold, a single frequency or very near it. The light is not bright. It is not warm. It is the opposite of the Undermath's warmth — a cool point in a warm field, a cold star in a hot sky. The light does not illuminate so much as it *marks* — it says *here is a surface*, *here is a wall*, *here is a ceiling*, *here is the edge of a trough where the gloss begins*. It is the Undermath's way of making itself navigable, or rather, it is a consequence of the Undermath's existence that happens to make the Undermath navigable, and the distinction between *a way of* and *a consequence of* is the distinction between design and emergence, and the Undermath does not furnish evidence for either.

They are alive. They must be alive, because they move, and they respond, and they have a behavior — scattering, reforming, clustering — that is not random but is not programmed either. It is *responsive*. They respond to vibration — your footsteps, the passage of a mantle-walker, the rare shift of stone when a vermicular lithic passes near the surface. They respond to temperature — they cluster more densely in warm areas, thinning in the cooler spots, though the Undermath's temperature varies so little that "cooler" is a relative term meaning "very slightly less warm than the place next to it." They respond to the gloss — they line the edges of the troughs, denser near the gloss than elsewhere, as if drawn to it, or as if the gloss provides something they need, or as if the gloss and the ember-mites are part of the same system, the gloss providing the warmth and the mites providing the light, the two together constituting a kind of organ, a sensory surface, an eye.

An eye. The comparison is not frivolous. The ember-mites cover the surfaces of the Undermath the way photoreceptors cover a retina — densely, uniformly, responsive to stimulus. They are the Undermath's light, and they are also the Undermath's sight. They register changes in their environment, cluster and scatter in patterns that encode information about what caused the change, and communicate that information to... what? To each other? To the stone? To the filament-weavers' structures, which vibrate nearby, which carry the pulse that runs through the Undermath's nervous system?

The ember-mites may be the Undermath's retina. The filament-weavers may be its optic nerve. The vast, slow pulse that you feel when you press your ear to a strand may be the Undermath seeing itself, processing the image, constructing a model of its own interior that it does not need and cannot use and maintains anyway, because that is what living systems do when they have nothing left to accomplish.

They are the closest thing the Undermath has to ambient light, and they are the closest thing the Undermath has to awareness, and they are the only dwellers that respond to your presence, and the response is so simple — scatter, reform, scatter, reform — that it feels like the Undermath is blinking when you pass, opening and closing its eyes, noting your passage and forgetting it in the same moment.

You will come to love the ember-mites. They are the only beauty here that does not require interpretation. The filament-weavers' structures are beautiful, but their beauty is ambiguous — is it art or infrastructure? The mantle-walkers are beautiful in their slow, smooth certainty, but their beauty is alien. The ember-mites are simply, plainly beautiful. They are lights in the dark. They scatter like stars when you pass and reform like stars when you are still, and they ask nothing and they give nothing and they are the only thing in the Undermath that you can look at without feeling that the Undermath is looking back.

## The Thing at the Bottom

You may not reach it. Most travelers do not. The descent continues past the point where the dunes flatten and the troughs widen and the ceiling lowers and the air thickens and the warmth increases, degree by degree, until the warmth is not a property of the air but a property of the space itself, as if heat has become a dimension, as if you are moving *into* temperature rather than through it.

But if you go far enough — if the gloss deepens and the ember-mites thin and the filament-weavers' structures grow rare and the vermicular lithics grow larger and the mantle-walkers grow still and the bas-relief things cover every surface like a skin — if you go far enough, you will reach a trough deeper than any you have seen, a depression in the stone so wide that its far edge is beyond the range of any light, and at the bottom of it, something.

A body. A large body. A body the size of a building, the size of a hill, lying at the bottom of the Undermath like a heart at the bottom of a chest. Warm. Dark. Pulsing with the same slow pulse that the filaments carry, that the stone conducts, that the ember-mites scatter and reform in response to. It is the source of the pulse, or it is the destination of the pulse, or it is both — the source and the destination, the heart and the brain, the place where the signal originates and the place where the signal arrives, and there is no distinction between sending and receiving because the distance between them is zero, because the body is *here*, has always been here, at the bottom of the Undermath, at the bottom of everything.

Or it is stone. The light is too dim. The distance is too great. The shape is too ambiguous. It may be a formation — a ridge, a deposit, a curvature of the Undermath's geology that happens to resemble a body the way a cloud happens to resemble a face. The human mind seeks pattern in ambiguity. The Undermath provides ambiguity in abundance. The match may be coincidental.

But the pulse. You feel the pulse. Not in your feet, where you feel the Undermath's general heartbeat, but in your chest, in your ribs, in the core of you, as if the thing at the bottom is pulsing in a frequency that resonates with the human body, that matches the rhythm of a human heart, that speaks in the language of blood and muscle and bone.

You will stand at the edge of the trough and look down and not be able to tell, and the not-knowing will feel like the most honest thing the Undermath has offered you. Because the Undermath does not deal in certainty. The Undermath deals in equilibrium, in balance, in the state where opposing forces are so perfectly matched that the result is stillness. The thing at the bottom is alive and it is stone and it is alive and it is stone, and the two states are balanced, and the balance is the truth, and the truth is that there is no answer because the question does not apply.

This is what lives in the Undermath.

Not the vermicular lithics, though they live here. Not the mantle-walkers, though they live here. Not the filament-weavers, not the bas-relief things, not the ember-mites, though all of them live here. What lives in the Undermath is the Undermath itself — a single organism so large that its organs are mistaken for geography, so slow that its thoughts are mistaken for silence, so old that its age is mistaken for youth, so finished that its life is mistaken for death.

What lives in the Undermath is a world that has become a body, or a body that has become a world, and the distinction between the two is the kind of distinction that the Undermath has spent a very long time dissolving, and has nearly finished dissolving, and will continue to dissolve for a very long time, not because it needs to but because dissolving is what it does, the way a river runs, the way a star burns, the way a heart beats — not toward a goal, not for a reason, but because that is what it is, and being what it is, is enough.

The Undermath is the aftermath. It is what remains.

What lives there is what remains alive when everything that needs to happen has happened, when every gradient has been spent, when every potential has been exhausted, when every story has been told and every song has been sung and every structure has been built and dissolved and built again. What lives there is the *after* — not death, not life, but the thing that is left when the distinction between the two is no longer necessary.
