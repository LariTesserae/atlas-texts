# What Lives Here

## The Grazers

Let me start with what you can find, because they are the easiest to believe in.

They move along the structural members in the half-light, and at first you think there are dozens of them, because you see them often, but eventually you realize there are perhaps a few hundred spread across an area you cannot measure. They are not numerous. They do not need to be. Nothing about this place operates on the logic of abundance through multiplication. Everything here operates on the logic of persistence through precision.

The one nearest to you — the one you first saw silhouetted against those dim nodes — has a body like a low table, broad and flat, the legs splayed wide and numerous. You count eight on one side before it moves and you lose the count. The legs are not jointed the way insect legs are jointed. They bend in softer places, the way a fern bends, and each foot is a small pad that adheres to the living surface through some mechanism you cannot see — possibly moisture, possibly a chemical adhesion, possibly something like the way the plant itself grips its own structure. The animal walks the way a spider walks on water: with a confidence that the surface will hold, that it has always held, that holding is what surfaces do.

Its body is the color of the plant's darker tissue, that jade-so-dark-it's-black, but mottled, and the mottling is not random — it follows the contours of the animal's body in a way that suggests the coloration is produced by the same chemical vocabulary the plant uses. You realize after a while that you think of it as *an animal* with less and less certainty. Its outer surface has a texture that is not quite chitin, not quite bark, not quite skin. It is something that has arrived at a solution the way convergent evolution arrives at solutions: the material is different but the shape is familiar. It has a dorsal surface that is slightly photosynthetic, or was once, or still is in a way that supplements its diet. It is not entirely a grazer. It is a grazer that carries a small garden on its back.

Its mouth — you get close enough to one to see this, after an hour of patient stillness on your part — is a broad, flat structure on the underside of its front end, a scraping apparatus like the plate of a bulldozer scaled to its size, with fine, flexible bristles arranged in rows that comb the photosynthetic layer off the plant's surface the way a card combs fibers from wool. The sound it makes is the sound of those bristles working across living bark. Click. Click. Click. The sound is soft and regular and it becomes, after a while, a kind of clock. You measure time by the clicks the way you would measure time by a heartbeat.

They do not react to you. Not because they are docile. Because you are not in their schema. You watch one approach you along a ridge, its deliberate gait bringing it closer over minutes, and it passes within arm's reach and does not pause, does not flinch, does not alter its course by a single degree. You could reach out and touch it. You do not, because you are not sure what that would mean here, and because something in you understands that touching it would be a violation of a contract you did not sign and cannot read. The grazer moves on. The clicking fades. You are alone again in the way you were alone before, which is the way the place is alone, which is not loneliness but the simple condition of a system that does not require company to be complete.

There are smaller grazers too. Much smaller. You see them when you look closely at the broad surfaces where moisture collects — things the size of your thumbnail, pale, soft-bodied, moving through the thin films of water that coat the structural members. They are to the grazer what the grazer is to you: a presence at a different scale, doing the same work in a different register. They feed on the microorganisms that live in the surface moisture — and there are microorganisms, you are certain of this, because the water has a smell that is not sterile, a faint sweetness that is the smell of something being broken down and rebuilt at a scale you cannot see. The small grazers are part of this. They are the middle layer of a food web that extends downward into the microscopic and upward into forms you have not yet encountered.

## What Moves Through the Gaps

The channels between structural members are not empty. They are corridors. You learn this when you see the thing that crosses the gap above you in a single fluid motion, and your breath catches, and you understand that the architecture you have been walking through is also a habitat for things that do not walk.

It moves on tendrils. Not the plant's tendrils — its own. It has limbs that are long and thin and prehensile, and it uses them the way a gibbon uses branches: with a fluency that makes the structure seem designed for it, or it designed for the structure, or the two have grown into each other over a time that has fused their geometries. You see it for perhaps three seconds: a body the size of a cat, sleek, dark, moving through the dim air with a speed that makes the grazers look like furniture. It catches a tendril, swings, catches another, and is gone into a deeper shadow, and the tendrils it touched sway for a moment after and then are still.

You think: *monkey*. You think: *spider*. You think: *otter*. None of these are right. It is something that has solved the problem of moving through a three-dimensional lattice of living material, and it has solved it the way the plant has solved the problem of building a three-dimensional lattice of living material: thoroughly, with a completeness that leaves no gap between form and function. Its body is elongated, built for the spaces between stems. Its limbs are jointed in places that correspond to the widths of the channels. It is, in a real sense, a negative space of the plant — the shape the gaps would take if they could move.

You see others. You begin to see them everywhere, once your eyes adjust to the speed and the scale. They move in pairs sometimes, or in threes, and the pairs and threes move with a coordination that suggests communication, or at least a shared attention. They call to each other: small, high sounds that are not quite chirps and not quite clicks, produced by something in their throats or their limbs — you cannot tell — that resonate in the wet air and carry across the gaps. The sound is absorbed by the plant within a few meters, so they must call frequently. The sounds are not language. They are not not language. They are the sounds of creatures that need to know where each other are in a space where visibility is ten meters and the architecture is the same in every direction.

They watch you. Not with the blank indifference of the grazers. With an alertness that is almost curiosity, and that is almost something else. A face turns toward you as its body swings past, and you see eyes — two of them, large, dark, wet — and the eyes hold you for a moment that lasts longer than the swing, longer than the catch, longer than the release. The eyes are not afraid. The eyes are *assessing*. You are being assessed, and you are found to be — something. Not a threat. Not a food source. Not a mate. Not a landmark. Something else, something without a category, and the animal holds you in its gaze for the extra half-second it takes to decide that you do not require a response, and then it is gone.

## The Thing in the Lower Mist

You do not go down. You know this without being told. The lower layers are warmer and wetter and the mist is thicker, and something down there moves with a slowness that is different from the slowness of the plant. The plant is slow because it is large. The thing in the mist is slow because it can afford to be.

You see it once. A shape. Through a gap in the structure, looking down into a channel that descends into deeper green, you see something pass through the fog far below — a body, or a part of a body, broad and dark, moving with an undulation that could be swimming or could be walking or could be the motion of something that has never needed to distinguish between the two. It is larger than the grazers. Larger than the gap-crossers. It moves through the mist the way a whale moves through water: with a mass that the medium must accommodate, pushing the fog aside in a slow bow wave that you see as a change in the density of the air, a clearing, a parting.

The grazers stop clicking when it passes beneath you. They do not flee. They do not run. They simply stop, and the silence that follows is a different silence than the silence of the place. It is the silence of things that are listening for something to end. When the clicking resumes, it resumes one grazer at a time, tentatively, the way applause resumes in a hall after something has interrupted it.

You do not know what the thing in the mist eats. You do not know if it eats. You know that the grazers know it, and that their knowledge of it is not knowledge but a relationship written in their behavior over spans of time that make your presence here, your single afternoon of observation, a thing so small it does not register in the ledger. You know that the thing in the mist is part of the system, and that the system includes things that move through it the way blood moves through a body: not often seen, not often needed, essential.

## What the Plant Thinks About

The plant is not thinking about you. This is important to say, because you might think it is, because the nodes brighten when you pass and the surface shifts beneath your feet and the water shows you things. But the plant is not thinking about you the way you think about the things you think about. The plant is thinking about moisture gradients. The plant is thinking about load distribution across a span that has been widening for decades and will need to be reinforced. The plant is thinking about the chemical signal that one of its distant tendrils has been producing for the past three days, which means something is boring into its vascular tissue two kilometers to the north, and it is growing a callus around the intrusion, and the callus will either seal the wound or it will not, and the plant is not worried about this because the plant does not worry, but it is *attending* to it, and attending is the plant's form of thinking, and the thought takes the form of growth, and the growth takes the form of architecture, and the architecture is the thought made visible.

You are in the thought. You are standing on a thought. The surface beneath your feet is the plant's attention to the problem of weight and surface and the need for something to be flat enough to walk on — except the plant did not know you would walk on it. It did not grow this surface for you. It grew this surface because flatness is a solution to the problem of distributing load across a wide span, and the solution happens to be walkable, and you happen to be walking on it. You are an accident of geometry. The plant does not mind.

But the nodes brighten. The surface shifts. The water shows you things. And you cannot fully escape the feeling — the conviction — that the plant is aware of you the way you are aware of the bacteria on your skin. A faint itch. A presence that is noted and not acted on. A thing that is there and that the system, in its vast and patient calculus, has decided to accommodate rather than reject. You are being tolerated. You are being included in the metabolic ledger as a minor variable, a small perturbation in a system large enough to absorb perturbations the way the ocean absorbs rain.

The plant thinks in growth. Its thoughts are structural. When it is concerned about a weak point in its architecture, it thickens the tissue there, and the thickening is the thought, and the thought is the thickening. When it senses moisture in a new direction, it sends tendrils, and the tendrils are the thought, and the thought is the tendrils. When a grazer feeds on a surface, the plant grows a slightly thicker layer of photosynthetic tissue there, and this could be a response to damage or it could be — and this is the thought that sits in you like a stone — it could be an offering. The plant could be feeding the grazers on purpose. The plant could have been feeding the grazers on purpose for so long that the purpose has become structural, embedded in the growth patterns, a habit so old it is indistinguishable from instinct.

And the plant thinks about the water. The water is its nervous system, and the plant knows this, in the way a body knows its own blood. The water carries information — chemical signals, pressure differentials, temperature gradients — and the plant reads the water the way you read faces: constantly, unconsciously, with an attention so total it does not feel like attention. The pools are the plant's eyes. The channels are its ears. The moisture film on every surface is its skin. The plant is touching itself, everywhere, all the time, and the touching is the knowing, and the knowing is the growing.

When the water showed you that map — that pattern in the pool that looked like the structure seen from above — the plant was not showing you anything. The plant was thinking, and the water happened to make the thought visible, and you happened to be looking. You were a witness to a private act, and the plant did not perform the act for you, and the plant will not perform it again for you, and the fact that you saw it does not mean you were meant to.

Or it does. You cannot know. The plant's intentionality is so different from yours that the word *intention* bends and warps when you try to apply it here. The plant intends the way a river intends. The plant means the way a root means. If the water showed you the map, it was because the showing was part of the plant's process, and you were part of the environment in which the process occurred, and the process did not adjust itself for your presence. The thought was about the plant's own structure. You were a witness, not an audience.

## The Predators

You do not see them for a long time. You begin to doubt them. The grazers' pauses could be explained by other things — a change in the texture of the surface, a chemical signal in the water, a vibration from the plant's own metabolic processes. You are perhaps projecting. You are perhaps a mammal looking for danger because your body is in an unfamiliar place and your body wants there to be danger because danger is familiar and the absence of danger is not.

Then you see one.

It is on the same surface you are standing on, far ahead, where the ridge you are walking along meets a broader platform. You see it because the nodes near it are brighter — the plant responding to something, or the thing interacting with the plant in a way that stimulates the nodes — and in the faint glow you see a shape that is low and long and still. Still in a way that the grazers are not still. The grazers pause. This thing *waits*.

It is not large. Perhaps the size of the grazers, perhaps a little bigger. Its body is the same dark jade, and you realize with a small shock that you cannot tell where the predator ends and the surface begins, and then you realize that this is the point. Its coloration is not just camouflage — it is *the same material*. The predator's outer surface is living tissue, or was once, or has become so integrated with the plant's surface that the distinction has been lost. It is not hiding on the surface. It is part of the surface. It has grown into the surface, or the surface has grown over it, or the two have been growing together for so long that the predator is a feature of the architecture the way a door is a feature of a wall.

It does not move. You watch it for ten minutes and it does not move. You begin to think it is not a predator, that it is a growth, a modified structure, a part of the plant that looks like an animal the way some roots look like hands. And then a grazer comes along the ridge, clicking, and the predator unfolds.

*Unfolds* is the only word. The body that was flat against the surface rises, and limbs that were pressed tight against its sides extend, and the mouth that was closed and flush with its ventral surface opens, and the opening is not a mouth in the way you understand mouths — it is a gap, a widening, a space that was not there a moment ago and that is now there, and the grazer walks into it.

Not stumbles. Not falls. Walks. The grazer walks into the predator's mouth the way it walks along the surface, with the same deliberate gait, the same careful foot-placement, and you understand that the grazer did not see the predator because the predator *was the surface*, and the grazer's feet were touching the predator's body and did not know it, because the predator's body and the surface were the same thing, and by the time the grazer's sensory apparatus — whatever it has, wherever it is — registered the difference, the difference was inside it, or it was inside the difference, and then the mouth closed, and the clicking stopped, and the predator settled back against the surface and was still again, and was the surface again, and the ridge ahead of you was empty.

You stand there for a long time. You do not walk forward. You are not afraid, exactly. You are recalibrating. You are understanding that the food web here is not a system of pursuit and flight but a system of *immanence* — the predator is always present, because the predator is the ground. The predator is the surface. The predator is the plant's own tissue turned to a different purpose, or the plant's tissue that has always had this purpose, alongside the photosynthesis and the structural support and the water transport. The predator is a function of the plant. The plant is a function of the predator. The two are aspects of the same system, and the system does not distinguish between them, and the grazers do not distinguish between them, and you, standing there in the dim gold-green light, are the only thing in this volume of space that distinguishes between them, and your distinction is wrong.

## The Small Bright Things

There is one more kind of life here, and it is the kind you almost miss.

In the tendrils that hang from above, in the small modified leaves that drip continuously, there are bright flashes. Not the bioluminescence of the nodes — this is different, whiter, quicker. You look up and see, for a moment, something the size of your finger, bright, almost incandescent, moving through the hanging tendrils with a speed and a lightness that makes the gap-crossers look heavy. It is not flying. It is not gliding. It is doing something else — touching the tendrils with its feet and releasing, touching and releasing, and each touch is so brief and so light that the tendril does not sway, and the creature moves through the hanging garden the way light moves through water: in straight lines that bend at interfaces.

There are many of them. Once you see one, you see them everywhere in the upper structure — dozens, hundreds, moving through the tendrils in patterns that could be feeding or could be social or could be something you do not have a word for. They glow. They produce their own light, or they reflect the light of the nodes, or both — and the light they produce is not the dim green-gold of the plant's bioluminescence but a clearer, sharper light, almost white, almost blue, and it flashes in patterns. On-off. On-on-off. The patterns are not random. You watch them for twenty minutes and you see sequences repeat, and the repetitions are not exact — they vary, they shift, they build on each other the way phrases build on each other in a conversation.

They are talking.

You do not know what they are saying. You do not know if they are saying anything. But the patterns of their flashing have the structure of communication — the rhythm, the variation, the call-and-response when one flashes and another flashes back after a pause that is not random but specific, as if the second one is *answering*. They are saying something to each other in a language made of light in a place where light is scarce and therefore precious, and the preciousness of the light makes the language meaningful in a way that sound, in this sound-absorbing place, could not be.

And they are watching you. You see it when one of them drops from the tendrils and hovers — *hovers*, somehow, in the still air, by some mechanism you cannot see — at the edge of your bubble of visibility, flashing at you. On. Off. On. On. Off. A sequence. A question, maybe. Or a warning. Or a greeting. You do not speak their language. You stand still and let them flash, and after a while the one that was flashing at you returns to the tendrils, and the conversation resumes among the bright small bodies above you, and you are left with the feeling that you have been addressed and could not reply, and that the inability to reply is a small loss in a place that is full of losses so large they cannot be felt.

## The Thing That Is Not a Builder

The place is built. The place has a builder. The builder is the place. You keep looking for something else — a hand, a mind, a presence that designed this — and you keep finding only the plant, and the plant's growth, and the plant's attention to its own growth. The builder is the building. The architect is the architecture. The mind is the body is the structure is the thought is the growth is the world.

But there is something else. Not a builder. Something older. Something that the plant is growing *around*.

In the deepest channels, in the places where the structure descends into mist and the warmth is almost too much and the air is so saturated you are breathing water — there, if you look, if you are taken there, if the plant lets you see — you can find shapes that are not plant. Shapes that are smooth and angular in a way that the plant never produces. Shapes that do not weep, do not photosynthesize, do not grow. Shapes that the plant has encased in layer after layer of tissue, the way a tree grows around a nail, the way a coral grows around a bottle — slowly, patiently, incorporating without understanding.

The plant does not know what these shapes are. The plant has grown around them because they are there, and the plant grows around things that are there, and the growing is not curiosity, it is process. But you, seeing them, feel something that the plant cannot feel. You feel the weight of a previous world. You feel the faintest tremor of a time when something else was here, something that was not plant, something that perhaps *built* — made things with intention, shaped materials, left artifacts — and the plant grew over it all, and the artifacts are still there, inside the living tissue like fossils inside stone, and the plant does not know and does not care and does not need to know or care.

Or maybe the plant does know. Maybe the water has shown the plant what is buried inside it. Maybe the plant's thoughts sometimes include the shapes it has grown around, the way your thoughts sometimes include the bones you are grown around. Maybe the plant knows exactly what is inside it and has decided — in the slow, structural way the plant decides things — that the inside is not its concern. The inside is the past. The plant grows outward. The plant grows upward. The plant grows toward the moisture and the light and the future, and the past is a core it carries and does not examine.

You do not touch the shapes. You see them once, in the deep warm mist, and you climb back up to the dim gold-green light of the higher layers, and you do not go back down. The shapes are not for you. They are not for anyone. They are what is left of something that was here before the plant was everything, and the plant has made them part of itself, and they are beyond reach, and they are beyond meaning, and they are, in a way that you feel in your chest more than understand in your mind, the reason the plant is so patient: because patience is what you learn when you have outlasted everything that was in a hurry.

## What Is Not Here

No one like you lives here. Nothing here is lonely. Nothing here is wondering what it is or why it is or whether it is living correctly. The grazers graze. The gap-crossers cross. The predators wait. The bright small things flash their light-language in the tendrils. The plant grows. The water moves. The nodes glow. The warm air rises. The mist forms and dissipates and forms again. Everything that is here is here completely. Everything that is here is sufficient to itself.

You are the only thing in this place that is insufficient to itself. You are the only thing that needs to understand. You are the only thing that is afraid of not understanding. And the place does not help you, because the place does not know you need help, because needing help is not a condition that exists here, in the warm dim wet vast patience of the living world that has become itself and does not need to become anything else.

You are being watched by everything and judged by nothing. You are a small warm shape on a large warm surface in a larger warm darkness, and the darkness holds you the way a hand holds a seed — not knowing what you are, not caring what you become, holding you only because holding is what it does.
