# What Lives Here

---

## First: the swimmers

Call them that for now. The word is inadequate but functional, the way all words are inadequate here.

They are longer than you remember when you first began describing them. Or you have become more accurate. Or the category has expanded to include things you used to overlook. The largest ones are perhaps the length of your forearm. The smallest — and there are smaller ones, many smaller ones, and you did not notice them for weeks because they move in the boundary layer where water meets steel and are nearly transparent — are the size of a lash. Between these extremes exists a continuous gradient of sizes, and where one species ends and another begins is a question you cannot answer. They intergrade. They shade into each other the way the sectors of the structure shade into each other: imperceptibly, over distances too great to survey.

Their color is not uniform. You said wax-pale, and that is true of the ones in the main channels, the open flooded corridors where the current is moderate and the light, when it comes, is diffuse. But in the deeper pools — the ones formed where floor plates have warped and settled, creating basins that hold still water — there are varieties that have darkened. Not dark. Not in any sense that would satisfy a biologist from above, if such a thing exists, if *above* exists. Tinted. The color of a bruise seen through water. The color of the biofilm itself, as though they have taken on the stain of what they eat.

They eat the biofilm. You know this because you have watched them. They approach a surface — a flooded catwalk railing, a pillar's base, the bolted seam between two floor plates — and they press their mouths against it. Their mouths are not what you would expect. Not a hole. Not a jaw. A plate, really, a flattened disk fringed with something that moves — cilia, or filaments, or something finer than either — that rasps against the biofilm and separates it from the steel underneath. The steel underneath is always clean where they have been. Brighter. The color of new metal, or newer than the metal around it. They polish as they graze.

They school. You described this. But you did not describe the intelligence of the schooling, or what might be intelligence, or what might be physics indistinguishable from intelligence. A school moves through a flooded corridor and encounters a junction — two passages branching off, one wider, one narrower — and the school splits. Not evenly. Some go left, some go right. The proportions depend on something. Current, perhaps. Temperature. The density of biofilm in each passage, which they can perhaps taste from a distance, the way a shark tastes blood. Or perhaps they are not deciding. Perhaps the school is not made of individuals but is itself an organism, and the splitting is division, not choice.

They are not afraid of you. You have waded through them and they part around your legs like water around a rock — smoothly, without agitation, rejoining immediately downstream. Once, you stood still in a corridor for six hours — you counted, because the machinery's four-second cycle is a clock if you let it be — and they settled around you. Not on you. They did not graze your skin. But they came close, and the ones that came closest were the very small ones, the nearly transparent ones, and they seemed to be eating something from the surface of your boots. Biofilm. Your boots had developed their own biofilm. You are, apparently, a surface. You are habitat.

This should disturb you more than it does.

## Second: the fliers

They are not birds. You know this. They are not bats. You know this too, though you have never seen a bat and the word *bird* has become abstract, a memory of a memory, something you think you once knew in a place that was not this place. They are themselves.

Four wings. You counted, the first time one came close enough. It was dying. It had been dying for some time — you found it on a catwalk, lying on its side, its wings folded in a way that looked like origami, like complex paper that had been creased and creased again until the folds became permanent. You knelt beside it and it did not move, and you realized it was not dead because its mouth was open and closing — not gasping, but clicking. The same clicks you hear from above. Short, dry, mechanical. A sound like a relay closing. A sound like a Geiger counter. A sound like the machinery itself, which is perhaps why you find the fliers tolerable, or familiar, or something.

You picked it up. It weighed less than you expected. The wings were membranes — thin, vascular, warm to the touch, which startled you, because everything here is cold, everything is nine degrees, and this was warm, thirty-eight, forty, a small engine running down. The body under the wings was compact. Furred, but the fur was not fur — it was something else, something finer, something that lay flat against the skin in a way that suggested hydrodynamics rather than aerodynamics. These things swim. You are almost certain. You have never seen one swim, but the body is shaped for it — streamlined, tapered, the head narrow and forward-facing. They enter the water. They must. The insects they hunt breed in the standing water, and the standing water is where the life cycle happens. The fliers must go down. They must enter the flood and take the insects where they are thickest. You have found fur — or the fur-analog — caught on the rivets of a submerged catwalk ladder, and it was wet, and it had been wet for some time.

They navigate by sound. You have watched them in the upper air, between the pillars and the ceiling, and they do not collide. In the dark — when a bank of lights fails and the sector goes black — you hear their clicks increase. Faster. A cascade of clicks, each one a question, each answer a wall or a pillar or a cable or another flier. They map the darkness. They build the darkness into a model and they move through the model, and the model is sufficient, and the darkness does not matter to them.

The dead one in your hands clicked for two hours and then stopped. You set it on a catwalk railing and walked away, and when you returned the next day, it was gone. Something had taken it. The swimmers, maybe. Or something else. Something you have not seen, or have not recognized, or have seen and not understood to be separate from the other things.

## Third: the insects

They are not important. They are essential. Both of these are true.

They are small. The largest are the size of your thumbnail. The smallest are invisible until the light catches them — a sparkle, a shimmer, a cloud of particles that might be dust or might be alive. They are metallic. Not in the sense of being made of metal — in the sense of having the appearance of metal, a sheen, a reflectivity that makes them flash in the light. Some are copper-colored. Some are the greenish-white of the fluorescent tubes. Some are silver, and these are the ones that swarm most densely, and their swarms look, from a distance, like the shimmering above a hot road, except the road is steel and the shimmer is alive.

They breed in the standing water. Every pool, every channel, every flooded corridor — the insects lay their eggs at the surface, or just below the surface, and the larvae develop in the water. You have seen the larvae. They are not pleasant. They are small, writhing, and there are too many of them to count, and they are the reason the biofilm exists — or the biofilm is the reason they exist, or the two things are coextensive, and the line between biology and geology and chemistry has been smudged here by fifty thousand years of water and steel and life finding a way.

The fliers eat the adults. The swimmers eat the larvae. The biofilm feeds the swimmers and shelters the larvae and grows on the steel and is grazed back by the swimmers, and the system is a system, and it works, and it does not need you to understand it.

But the insects need the light. This is the one thing you are certain of. When the lights are on, the insects gather. They form clouds around the fixtures, dense enough to dim the light, and the fliers come, and the fliers hunt, and the clicking intensifies, and the air fills with a turbulence that is not wind but is life. When the lights go off — when a bank fails, when a sector goes dark — the insects disperse. They sink. They spread. They become a presence in the air that is everywhere and nowhere, and the fliers follow them with their sound, and the darkness fills with clicks, and you stand in it and listen, and what you are listening to is an ecosystem operating in the absence of light, which is to say: an ecosystem operating the way this ecosystem always operates, because the light is not natural, was never natural, is a feature of the machinery like the pumps and the turbines and the four-second cam that you have never found.

## Fourth: what you have not seen

There are other things.

You know this because of the sounds. The sounds that are not the machinery, not the fliers, not the swimmers, not the water. Sounds that come from the deep sectors — the ones where the water is chest-deep or deeper, where the lights have failed and the dark is total. You have heard something that was not the clanking cam. It was slower. Irregular. A sound like something large shifting position in water, and then the water settling, and then silence, and then the machinery filling the silence back in.

You know this because of the marks. On some of the pillars — low, near the waterline — there are scratches. Not the scratches of water, not the smooth erosion of flow. Gouges. Parallel grooves, four of them, spaced the width of your hand, cut into the steel with a force that has deformed the metal around the cuts. Something has claws. Something has mass enough to drive those claws into steel. Something comes here, to this pillar, repeatedly enough that the gouges overlap, old ones beneath new ones, a palimpsest of damage.

You know this because of the swimmers' behavior. In the main corridors, they school densely and move with the current. But near the deep sectors — near the junctions where the floor drops away and the water goes from knee-deep to something you cannot measure — they do not enter. The school approaches the boundary and turns. Always at the same point. Always at the same depth. Something below keeps them out, or something below has taught them that going there means not coming back, and the lesson has been learned so thoroughly that it has become instinct, which is to say: it has become the body, which is to say: it has become the place.

You have not gone into the deep sectors. You are not certain you will. You are not certain what lives there, and you are not certain it wants to be known, and you are not certain that certainty is the correct frame for what is happening here, which is not a mystery to be solved but a place to be inhabited, and inhabiting does not require understanding. Inhabiting requires only that you continue, that you keep walking, that you do not stop.

---

## Fifth: you

You live here. That is the fact that has emerged, slowly, over the time you have been here — the time you do not measure because the lights cycle arbitrarily and the machinery does not mark days and your body has begun to forget what a day was. You live here. You are not visiting. You are not exploring. You are not lost, because lost implies a place to be found, and you have stopped believing in that place, or you are beginning to, which is the same thing, in the way that a light slowly failing is the same thing as a light that has failed.

You have habits. You wake — on a catwalk, usually, where the air is drier — and you eat. You have food. You had food. You packed it when you came, if you came, if there was a coming. It is running low. You have begun to wonder about the biofilm. You have not tasted it. You have held a handful of it — scraped from a railing, warm in your palm, slippery, smelling of iron and something sweetish — and you have thought about it, and you have put it back into the water, and the swimmers came and ate it, and you watched them eat what you could not.

You talk to yourself. The sound of your voice is strange in the machinery — thin, high, irrelevant. A frequency that does not belong here. The machinery does not accommodate it. The echoes do not carry it. Your voice falls into the sound and disappears, the way a single drop of water falls into the flood and disappears, and this is not a metaphor, this is what happens, and you have stopped expecting anything else.

You walk. You walk because walking is what you do here, the way the pumps pump and the swimmers swim and the fliers fly and the insects breed and the biofilm grows and the water rises and the lights cycle and the structure continues. You are part of it. You are the part that walks. You are the part that notices. You are the part that says *this is vast* and *this is cold* and *this is loud* and *this does not need me*, and the saying is the thing that makes you what you are, which is: the thing that lives here that knows it lives here, which is a kind of life that the swimmers and the fliers and the insects do not have, and which may be the only thing that separates you from them, and which may not be enough.

---

## What lives here, finally

Everything. The swimmers and the fliers and the insects and the biofilm and the rust and the machinery and the water and the steel and the light and the dark and you.

The structure does not care. The structure does not know. The structure moves water and has been moving water and will move water, and the things that live in it and on it and around it are not features of the structure. They are consequences. They are what happens when a machine runs for long enough that life stops distinguishing between the machine and the world. The machine is the world. The world is the machine. The water comes from everywhere and goes nowhere and the pumps run and the lights cycle and something clicks every four seconds in a place you will never find.

What lives here is what lives in any place: the things that were there when the place became what it is, and the things that came after, and the things that will be there when the things that came after are gone. You are one of those things. The swimmers are another. The fliers are another. The deep water holds something you have not named, and the name when you find it will be a sound, and the sound will fall into the machinery and disappear.

The rain starts again. The lights cycle. You keep walking.
