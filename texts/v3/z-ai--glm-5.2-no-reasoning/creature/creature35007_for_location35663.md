# The Hollow Choir of Vesh

## What Lives Here

They are not silent people. That is the first thing to understand, and the hardest for anyone who has not been inside the shell to truly grasp. They are people who live inside sound the way fish live inside water — so thoroughly saturated that the medium has become invisible to them, has become *them*. Their vocal cords have not simply atrophied from disuse; the tissue has thinned and softened over generations until a child born in Vesh who was taken to the surface and stood in a quiet room could not produce a sound louder than a breathy wheeze. The architecture of their throats has changed. The larynx sits slightly higher, shifted by generations of non-use, and the muscles that once controlled pitch and volume have been recruited for other purposes — for the subtle, precise contractions of breath-control that the people of Vesh use to regulate their bodies against the pressure of the chord, which is always pressing, always vibrating, always testing the seal of their lungs.

They breathe differently. You would notice this immediately if you watched them, though you would not know what you were seeing. Their respiration is rhythmic in a way that ordinary human breathing is not — or rather, it is rhythmic in a way that ordinary human breathing *is*, but more so, more consciously, more deliberately synchronized. They breathe *with* the chord. Not in time to it, not matching a beat, but in sympathy with it, finding the resonant frequencies that let their chest cavities open and close without fighting the pressure waves that roll through the air. A visitor would find themselves gasping, coughing, their diaphragm spasming against vibrations it does not understand. The people of Vesh do not gasp. They have never gasped. They were born into the sound and their first breath was a breath taken inside a bellows, inside a drum, inside the mouth of an instrument, and their bodies learned before their minds did.

They are small. Not stunted — not malformed, not deficient — but compact, dense, built close to the ground. Generations of climbing, of living in vertical space, of navigating narrow steps cut into calcite while carrying tools and fiber and wafers of wax, have produced bodies that are efficient in their proportions: short-limbed, thick-ankled, wide-handed. Their hands are remarkable. The fingers are long in relation to the palm, the pads broad and sensitive, the skin of the fingertips thinner and more finely innervated than the skin of any other part of their bodies. These are their primary instruments of communication, and they have been shaped by that use the way a violinist's left hand is shaped by the neck of the instrument. The pad of a Vesh thumb can distinguish textures that a surface dweller would need a magnifying glass to perceive. The edge of a Vesh palm, pressed against the inside of another's wrist, can transmit information at a speed that approaches speech — not because they have a shorthand, not because they have compressed their language, but because the skin itself has become a medium of sufficient bandwidth that nuance and tone and subtext can travel through it the way they travel through air in a spoken language, through the minute variations of pressure and duration and rhythm that their hands have learned to produce.

They touch each other constantly. This is not intimacy. This is not affection, though it can be both. It is *speech*. A child in Vesh learns, before it learns to walk, that the flat of a hand against its back means *come*, that a thumb pressed to the inside of its wrist means *wait*, that the slow, circular stroking of a finger behind its ear means *you are safe*. The child's skin is its ears. The child's skin is its mouth. By the time a Vesh child is old enough to climb the steps alone, the skin of its arms and hands and neck has been spoken to and has spoken back so many thousands of times that the boundary between touch and language has dissolved entirely. They do not think of their sign language as sign language. They think of it as *talking*. They do not think of speech — mouth-speech, the kind the visitors who never come would use — as something they have lost. They think of it the way you might think of a tail, or gills: something that belongs to a different creature, a different environment, something that has nothing to do with them.

---

They are craftspeople, but that word is insufficient. They are *refiners*. They are the last link in a chain of making that stretches back so far that the chain has become a circle, the forms so settled into their materials that the distinction between the maker and the made has blurred.

Consider a bowl. A Vesh bowl is carved from a single piece of calcite, cut from the inner wall of the shell in a shape that follows the grain of the mineral — a grain that is not geological but biological, or was biological once, when the shell was a thing that grew. The bowl is not designed. It is *released*. The carver — the woman, the man, the child who has been given this task for the day — runs their hands over the raw surface, feeling for the place where the calcite wants to separate, where the crystal lattice has a weakness that corresponds, somehow, to the curve that a bowl requires. They do not think in words about this. They think in pressures, in tensions, in the way the stone pushes back against their fingertips. The knife — a blade of harder calcite, ground to an edge so fine it is almost invisible — follows the line of least resistance, and the bowl emerges. No two bowls are identical. No two bowls are very different. They are variations on a theme that the stone itself has been proposing for centuries, and the carvers have been listening.

The cloth is the same. It is woven from the fibrous stems of the pale vegetation, stripped and dried and worked against the edge of a stone until the fibers separate and can be laid parallel and pressed together — not spun, not twisted, but *fused*, the waxy residue of the leaves acting as a binding agent that holds the fibers in parallel without the need for a loom. The resulting fabric is dense, slightly damp, cool against the skin. It does not dry out. It does not crack. It has the quality of something alive — something that has been killed and reassembled, and that retains, in its stillness, a memory of the living thing it was. The people wear it wrapped around their bodies in loose, untailored lengths, and it moves with them the way the leaves move in the wind: with a slight, constant, almost-imperceptible trembling.

The wax they harvest from the leaves is the third material, and the most enigmatic. It is not secreted by the plant. It is not produced by any gland or pore. It appears — accumulates, condenses — on the surface of the broadest leaves during the hours of peak light, when the shafts are pouring their fullest illumination and the salt floor is bouncing it back with maximum intensity. It is a product of the light, or of the interaction between the light and the leaf, or of something else that no one has named because no one has needed to name it. The wax is pale, translucent, slightly warm to the touch. It hardens slowly when removed from the leaf, and in its hardened state it has a quality that the people of Vesh value above all others: it *resonates*. A piece of leaf-wax, shaped and thinned and mounted against a surface that vibrates, will vibrate in sympathy, amplifying and coloring the tone, and the people use it for this purpose — to build instruments that are not played, that have no player, that simply *are*, that sit in niches and corners of the shell and add their voices to the chord.

These instruments are not music. They are *architecture*. They are built into the city itself, mounted in the dwellings and on the terraces and along the edges of the shafts, and they modify the sound the way a window modifies light. Some of them have been in place for generations. Some of them were made by people whose names — whose touch-signatures, whose identifying patterns of pressure and rhythm — are still remembered, still taught to children, still recognized when a hand presses against the wax surface and feels, in its resonance, the echo of the hand that shaped it.

---

They do not have names. Not in the way you have a name — a sound, a word, a thing that can be called across distance. They have *touch-signatures*: the specific, unique, unrepeatable pattern of pressure and rhythm and duration that each person's hands produce when they speak against another's skin. A touch-signature is not chosen. It is not given. It *emerges*, in the first years of life, as the child's hands develop their particular character — the weight of the palm, the speed of the fingers, the preference for certain gestures over others. A mother knows her child's signature before the child knows it. A lover knows it the way you know a voice: by feel, by instinct, by the deep recognition that bypasses language entirely.

When a person dies, their signature does not die with them. It is *remembered* — not recorded, not written, but carried in the hands of those who knew them, who can reproduce it by pressing their own hands against their own skin in the pattern they remember. This is how the dead are named. This is how they are spoken to. A Vesh mourner will sit in the light, in the full blast of the chord, and press their own palm against their own wrist in the pattern of the person they have lost, and the pattern is the person, for a moment, and the mourning is the same as speaking to them, and the speaking is the same as holding them, and the holding is the same as letting them go.

---

They are not unhappy. This must be said, because the temptation to pity them is strong and the pity would be wrong. They do not experience their silence — the silence of the mouth, the absence of voice — as a loss. They do not experience the sound as a burden. They experience it the way you experience weather: as the condition of the world, the thing that is simply so, the medium through which all other things are experienced. A hot day is not a tragedy. A rainy day is not a defect. The chord is the weather of Vesh, and the people who live in it have adapted to it the way people adapt to any climate: by building their lives around it, by making it the ground on which they stand.

They have culture. They have history. They have something that functions, for them, the way literature functions for us — a body of transmitted knowledge and story and song, carried not in books but in hands, in the patterns of touch that pass from generation to generation, each hand adding its own variation, each variation becoming part of the pattern, so that the stories of Vesh are not fixed but *accretive*, growing richer and more complex with each telling, each hand that speaks them adding a layer of nuance that the next hand will carry forward. A story in Vesh is not a text. It is a *lineage*. It is the chain of hands that have spoken it, and to hear a story — to receive it, to have it pressed into your skin by someone who received it from someone who received it from someone — is to feel the weight of that lineage, the accumulated pressure of every hand that has ever spoken it.

They have something that functions as song. It is not vocal. It is not instrumental. It is *choreographic*. A Vesh singer is a person who moves through the space of the cavity in a pattern that interacts with the chord — walking a specific route along the terraces, passing through specific shaft-frequencies at specific moments, so that their body — which is a resonant cavity, which is a drum, which is an instrument — catches and modifies and redirects the sound as it passes through them. The effect, for a listener, would be subtle: a slight shift in the texture of the chord as the singer walks, a momentary brightening or darkening of a particular harmonic, a sense that the sound has been *shaped* by a human intention. For the people of Vesh, the effect is profound. They can feel the singer passing through the sound the way you can feel a current in a river — not by seeing it, not by hearing it separately, but by the way the medium itself changes around the moving body.

The singers are old. Not in years, necessarily, but in practice. They have been doing this for so long that their bodies have become calibrated to the acoustics of the shell the way a violin is calibrated to the acoustics of a concert hall. They know, without thinking, which step on which terrace will produce which modulation, which angle of the body will catch which frequency, which breath, taken at which moment, will add its own small resonance to the chord. They are not performing. They are *praying*. The sound is the god, or the medium of the god, or the body of the god, and the singer is the person who has learned to move inside that body the way blood moves inside a vein.

---

They love. Of course they love. They love the way all people love — desperately, specifically, with the full weight of their attention — but they love *in touch*, and the touch is the love, and the love is the touch, and there is no distance between the two. A Vesh lover does not say *I love you*. A Vesh lover presses their palm against the back of their beloved's neck and holds it there, and the pressure of the palm is the statement, and the warmth of the skin is the answer, and the sound that rolls through them both, the chord that presses against both their chests simultaneously, is the context in which the statement is made, the world in which it is true.

They grieve. A Vesh mourning is a silent thing — silent even by Vesh standards, which is saying something, because Vesh is already silent in all the ways that surface-dwellers measure silence. A mourning in Vesh is a cessation of touch. The mourner withdraws their hands. They do not speak against skin. They do not receive speech against their own. They sit in the light and the sound and they are alone, and the aloneness is the grief, and the grief is the aloneness, and the people around them know to leave them be, to let the silence of their hands say what it needs to say, to wait — sometimes for days — until the mourner reaches out and presses their palm against someone's wrist and the touch-signature that emerges is not the one they were mourning but their own, their own, changed by the grief but still recognizably theirs, still alive.

---

They do not leave. This is the fact that visitors — if visitors came, which they do not — would find most incomprehensible. The shell has openings. The shafts are large enough to climb through, with difficulty, with risk. The surface is out there — dry and bright and empty, but traversable, and beyond it, somewhere, are other places, other people, other ways of living. The people of Vesh know this. They are not ignorant of the outside world. They have a history — a transmitted, hand-carried history — that includes the memory of arrival, of a time when their ancestors were not of the shell but came to it, from the surface, from the dry land, from the silence that they have since forgotten.

They do not leave because the shell is not a prison. The shell is a *body*. They live inside it the way a soul lives inside a body — not trapped, not confined, but *housed*, made possible by the structure that contains them. To leave the shell would be to leave the sound, and to leave the sound would be to leave the medium in which they exist, the thing that carries their breath and their touch and their songs and their stories. They would not survive the silence. Not because the silence would kill them — though it might, the shock of it, the sudden absence of the pressure that has shaped their lungs and their bones and their thoughts — but because they would no longer be *themselves*. A fish cannot leave the water and remain a fish. A person of Vesh cannot leave the chord and remain a person of Vesh.

They know this. They do not discuss it, because there is nothing to discuss. The shell is. The sound is. The light is. The leaves are. The salt floor is. These are the facts of the world, and the people live inside the facts the way they live inside the shell, and the facts do not require commentary.

---

There is one more thing. There is the creature — the small, pale, translucent, clicking thing that enters through the shafts in certain seasons and eats the leaves and leaves again. The people of Vesh do not hunt it. They do not fear it. They do not worship it, though the temptation to say they do is strong, because their behavior toward it has the quality of devotion.

When the creature comes, they stop. They stop working, stop climbing, stop eating, stop whatever their hands are doing against each other's skin. They turn their faces toward the clicking — and they can hear it, or rather they can *feel* it, the thin, sharp, rhythmic pulses cutting through the chord like a needle through cloth — and they *attend*. They stand in the light and the sound and they attend to this small, alien, temporary thing that has come into their world from outside, and their faces — which are weathered, which are bright-lit, which have been shaped by the light into something that is almost not faces anymore, almost masks of calcite and salt — their faces soften.

They love it. They love it the way you love a bird that lands on your windowsill — not because it is yours, not because it will stay, but because it is *evidence*. Because it proves that the world is larger than the room you live in. Because it carries, in its clicking, in its translucence, in its brief and alien presence, the news that somewhere, beyond the shell, beyond the sound, beyond the light, there are other things living, other ways of being alive, and the knowledge of this does not make them want to leave. It makes them want to stay. It makes the staying bearable, the way a single star makes the night sky bearable, the way a single voice in a silence makes the silence bearable, the way a single touch on the wrist in a dark room makes the dark bearable.

The creature leaves. The shafts close behind it. The chord resumes. The people turn back to their work, their bowls, their cloth, their wax, their lives. But something has changed. Something has been renewed. The sound is the same sound it was before, but it is also different, because they have heard it *interrupted*, and the interruption has reminded them that it is a sound, a specific sound, a sound that could, in theory, stop — and the knowledge that it could stop, that it does not, that it has not for as long as anyone can remember, is the closest thing they have to gratitude.

They are grateful. For the sound. For the light. For the shell. For the leaves that sing back. For the salt that scatters brilliance like snow. For the creature that comes and goes. For each other, for the touch of hand on hand on wrist on neck, for the signatures that identify and the pressures that comfort and the silences between gestures that carry more meaning than the gestures themselves.

They are grateful, and the gratitude is not spoken, because nothing in Vesh is spoken, because speaking is a thing that happens in other places, in other bodies, in other ways of being alive. The gratitude is *pressed*. It is pressed into the skin of the person next to them. It is pressed into the stone of the terraces. It is pressed into the wax of the instruments that resonate in the corners. It is pressed into the salt floor, which receives it and scatters it upward, into the light, into the chord, into the shell that contains them, and the shell takes it and makes it part of the sound, and the sound carries it, and the carrying is the gratitude, and the gratitude is the carrying, and it goes on and on and on, and it does not stop.
