# What Lives Here

There is no single answer to that question. Or rather, there are too many, and they are all correct, and none of them are sufficient.

Let me try them in order, from smallest to largest, though "smallest" and "largest" are words that lose their grip here, the way a hand loses grip on wet stone.

---

## The Pale Threads

Start with the thing in the moss. The translucent, limbless body, thick as a thumb, moving with purpose through the wet carpet. You saw one. There are thousands.

They are not worms. They are not larvae. They are not anything that has a name in any taxonomy built from the world you came from, though they occupy a similar ecological role to both, and if you needed a comparison you could do worse than "if an earthworm and a blood vessel had a child and that child chose to live in moss." They are the capillaries of the integument, you understand that now — or you will, eventually. They live in the moss layer that covers the caldera's inner wall and they move through it constantly, tunneling, processing, breaking down dead plant matter and converting it to the nutrient-rich film that the moss and the broader vegetation depend on. They are the soil. This place has no soil, not in the traditional sense. The pale threads *are* the soil, distributed, mobile, alive.

Each one is perhaps forty centimeters long when fully extended, though they contract to half that when disturbed. They are translucent — not glassy, not crystalline, but *translucent* the way the skin of a newborn is translucent, the way you can see the blue branches of veins beneath the surface of a wrist. Their digestive tracts are visible as a dark central thread, and this thread is not uniform. It segments, slightly, at intervals, and the segments are different colors — dark green where they have been processing plant matter, amber where they have been processing the luminous lichen, blue-gray where they have been processing something else, something that comes from deeper down. You do not want to think too carefully about what they eat deeper down. You do not have to. They come back up. They always come back up.

They have no eyes. They have no mouths that you can identify. They feed through their skins, absorbing nutrients directly from the moss they move through, and they excrete the same way — a thin film of processed nutrient that the moss drinks immediately. They are a digestive system distributed across a landscape. Each one processes perhaps its own body weight in material per day. There are thousands. The math is not difficult, but the scale is difficult, and the implication — that the entire moss layer of the caldera is in a state of constant, slow, living digestion — is the kind of thought that makes you want to sit down on the wet rock and breathe carefully until the altitude dizziness passes and you remember that you are only visiting.

They are not aware of you. Your footfalls register as vibration, and they avoid vibration, and so the moss near where you are standing is, for the moment, empty of them. When you leave, they will return. They will process the moisture your boots left behind. They will break down the dead skin cells you shed. They will incorporate you into the system in the most literal way possible, and they will not know they have done it, and this is perhaps the most honest form of welcome a place can offer.

## The Clickers in the Pipes

The organ-pipe vines are hollow. You know this. What you may not yet understand is that they are not hollow by accident.

Each pipe is habitat. Each pipe is a home.

The creatures that live inside the rigid vine-tubes are small — perhaps the length of your hand, perhaps less — and they are built like nothing you have seen. You have heard them: the soft clicks, the scrape of chitin. You inferred the chitin from the sound, and you are correct, but the chitin is not the armor-plating of an insect. It is thinner, more flexible, and it covers a body that is mostly legs. Six legs, you think, though the number varies — some have eight, some have four, and you begin to suspect that the number of legs is not a fixed species characteristic but a developmental response to the size of the pipe they inhabit. They grow to fit their homes. The pipes determine the bodies.

Their function is maintenance. They live inside the vine-tubes and they keep the tubes clean — clearing debris, removing condensation blockages, repairing small fractures in the tube walls by secreting a resin that hardens into a chitin-like patch. They are the maintenance crew of the respiratory system. When a pipe's tone shifts, it is because a Clicker has moved within it, adjusting the tube's effective length to compensate for a blockage or a change in air pressure. The chord that the pipes produce is not simply the wind's voice. It is the sound of the Clickers tuning their instruments.

They communicate through the clicks. You have heard them — soft, rhythmic, passing from pipe to pipe. The clicks travel through the tube walls as vibration, and the Clickers feel them through their legs. They have no ears. They have no eyes. They have antennae, long and fine, that detect air pressure changes and chemical gradients inside the tubes. They navigate by touch and by the flow of air through their homes. Each pipe is a world, a corridor, a territory. The Clicker that lives in a given pipe may never leave it. It is born there, it works there, it dies there, and its body is broken down by the next generation, who inherit the tube and the role and the click-language that their parents spoke.

They are not individual creatures. They are an organ. The respiratory system does not have a maintenance crew the way a city has a maintenance crew. The respiratory system *is* the maintenance crew, in the same way that your white blood cells are not employees of your immune system but *are* your immune system. The Clickers are the self-repairing mechanism of a structure that has been breathing for longer than your species has existed.

When you place your hand on a vine-pipe, you feel the clicks through your palm. The Clicker inside goes still. It can feel you too. It does not panic. It waits. When you remove your hand, the clicking resumes, and the pipe's tone adjusts, and the chord incorporates the change, and the system breathes on.

## The Browsing Ones

The creature on the terrace below. The one the size of a large dog, low-slung, segmented, moving with a precise and unhurried gait between the broad-leafed plants. You have been watching it for some time. You have seen it in fragments. Let me give you the whole.

It has eight legs. No — nine. One is vestigial, folded against the left side of its third body segment, reduced to a stub that twitches when the creature pauses but does not touch the ground. Evolutionary residue. Its carapace is dark, almost the color of the rock, and it is not smooth. It is textured — fine ridges running front to back, covered in a thin film of condensation that makes it gleam wetly in the glow. The ridges are not decorative. They are channels. They collect condensation and direct it toward the creature's mouth, which is on the underside of its head, which is not a head in the way you might expect but a *front* — a tapered, eyeless extension of the first body segment, covered in fine hairs that move constantly, tasting the air, tasting the moisture, tasting the chemical signatures of the plants it visits.

It does not eat the plants. This is important. It does not eat them.

It visits them. It pauses at each one, lowers its front to the leaf surface, and does something with those fine hairs — something that involves a secretion, a chemical exchange, a dialogue between the creature's biology and the plant's biology that you cannot see but can almost feel in the way the plant responds. The plant leans toward the creature. The creature's hairs move over the leaf surface, and where they pass, the leaf's waxy coating changes — it becomes slightly more permeable, slightly more efficient at collecting condensation. The creature is not browsing. It is *tending*. It is applying a treatment. It is a gardener.

The circuit takes approximately forty minutes. You were told this. What you were not told is that the circuit is not the same every time. The *order* of plants visited is the same, but the *duration* of each pause varies. The creature lingers longer at plants that are struggling — plants whose leaves are thinner, whose condensation channels are partially blocked, whose angle to the updraft is suboptimal. It can tell. It can taste the difference. And it adjusts its care accordingly.

There are others. Not on this terrace — on terraces below, on terraces above, on the far side of the caldera where you cannot see but where the same soft, wet clicking of carapace on stone carries faintly across the dark. The Browsing Ones are a population. They are a caste. They tend the integument the way the Clickers tend the respiratory system, the way the Pale Threads tend the moss. Each has its circuit. Each knows its plants. Each has been doing this for a length of time that, in an individual lifespan, would be considered impossible, and the only conclusion is that the Browsing Ones live a very long time, or they do not live as individuals at all but as a continuous thread of behavior passed from body to body, generation to generation, circuit to circuit, without interruption.

The one you are watching has a mark on its carapace. A pale spot, near the rear of its fifth segment. It could be a scar. It could be a birthmark. It could be something else — a mark of age, a mark of status, a mark of function. You do not know. You will not know. But you notice it, and you remember it, and this is a kind of respect, and if the organism beneath you could appreciate respect, it would perhaps appreciate this, that a small, temporary, warm thing stood on its skin and noticed one of its gardeners and remembered the shape of the mark on its back.

## The Circling Ones

You have not seen them clearly. You will not. They are built to be seen only as impressions — dark shapes against dark sky, movement against stillness, intent against patience.

They are large. Larger than the Browsing Ones. Perhaps the size of eagles, perhaps larger — it is difficult to judge at the altitude they maintain. They have wings. The wings are broad and they do not flap. They ride the thermal that rises from the caldera, and the thermal is constant, and so they circle without effort, without landing, without ever breaking their pattern. They are the most patient things you have ever shared a sky with.

They are not birds. You know this because of the way they move. Birds move in starts and corrections — a wing adjustment here, a tail flick there, constant micro-corrections. The Circling Ones move *smoothly*. They bank without visible effort. They turn without adjusting their wings. It is as though they are not flying but *swimming* the thermal, the way a fish swims a current, with a body designed for the medium it inhabits. Their wings are not feathered. You catch a glimpse, once, when one dips lower than the others, and you see that the wing surface is smooth, continuous, and slightly translucent — a membrane, not feathers, stretched between supports that are not bones but something more flexible, something that can change shape without joints.

They are watching. You said this. They are watching the terrace below. They are watching the Browsing Ones. They are watching you. Their patience is not the patience of a predator waiting for weakness. It is the patience of a system monitoring itself. They are the immune response, and they are always active, and they almost never act, because almost nothing threatens this place. The cold does not threaten it. The wind does not threaten it. The slow geological settling of the rock does not threaten it. What threatens it?

You. Technically. A warm body on the skin, shedding dead cells, producing carbon dioxide, changing the moisture profile of the moss you stand on. These are tiny perturbations. The Circling Ones note them. They circle. They wait. If you were to do something larger — if you were to break the rock, tear the moss, damage the vine-pipes — they would respond. You do not know how. You do not want to know how. The not-knowing is a form of respect, and the respect is appropriate.

They make no sound. None. No cry, no wing-noise, no call. They are the quietest things in a quiet place. They are quieter than the silence, because the silence is a substance with weight and texture, and the Circling Ones have no weight and no texture in your perception. They are absences in the shape of bodies. They are the dark against the dark. And they are alive, and they are watching, and they have been watching for longer than the rock has been in its current shape.

## The Flowers

The black flowers are not flowers. You know this now, or you are beginning to. They grow where the light is, and they consume the light, and they are warm, and they are arranged in a gradient of density that follows the intensity of the glow from below. They are not plants. They are not animals. They are something else — something that occupies a biological category that your taxonomy does not have a name for, because your taxonomy was built in a world where these things do not exist.

They are organs. They are the digestive lining of the organism, turned inside-out, exposed to the light they consume. Each bloom is a mouth. Each petal is a surface for absorption. They are warm because they are metabolizing — not light in the way a plant photosynthesizes, but *light in the way a body digests food*. They break down photons. They extract energy. They convert it and they pass it down, through their root systems, through the rock, to the depths where the core of the organism lives.

The core is alive. You knew this. The glow is bioluminescent. It is produced by something down there — something vast, something old, something that has been producing light and heat and the chemical signatures of metabolism for a span of time that makes the paleological timescale of the rock above seem brief. The flowers are the connection between the core and the surface. They are the gut. The light rises, the flowers consume it, the energy descends. It is a cycle. It is metabolism. It is the organism eating itself and growing and eating and growing, and the growth is the garden, and the garden is the organism, and the distinction was never real.

Each flower lives a long time. You do not know how long. But the oldest ones — the ones at the lowest visible terrace, the largest ones, the ones that are darkest and warmest — have petals that are thicker than your hand, layered, with the compressed density of something that has been adding layer after layer for centuries. They are not growing. They are *accreting*. They are geological formations that happen to be alive.

They do not respond to you. You are too far above them, too small, too temporary. They do not respond to the Browsing Ones. They do not respond to the wind. They respond only to the light, and the light is constant, and so they are constant, and they will be constant after you leave, and they will be constant after the rock changes shape, and they will be constant after the circling ones have circled so many times that their circuits have worn grooves in the air itself.

They are the most patient part of the organism, and the organism is patient, and patience is not the right word because patience implies the endurance of something unpleasant, and there is nothing unpleasant here. There is only the process. The light rises. The flowers eat. The energy descends. The organism lives.

## The Deep Ones

You cannot see them. You can only see what they do to the light.

The glow from the caldera's depths does not shine steadily. It pulses — irregularly, in patterns that suggest large bodies passing between the light source and the walls. These are the Deep Ones. They live in the core. They are the core, or they are part of the core, or the core is them, and the distinction between organism and environment that you have been struggling with since you arrived is, down there, not a struggle at all but a simple fact: the Deep Ones *are* the depths.

They are large. This is inference, not observation — the variations in the glow suggest bodies that are at least meters across, that move slowly, that occupy the space at the bottom of the caldera in a way that changes the light the way clouds change sunlight. They are not single creatures. They are a colony, or a community, or a tissue. They are the oldest part of the organism — the part that was here first, the part that began producing light before the garden grew, before the moss formed, before the vines learned to sing.

They produce the light. They produce the heat. They are the metabolism. The flowers above are connected to them through the root systems that thread through the rock, through the quartz veins that carry light like fiber optics, through the chemical exchanges that pass up and down the caldera wall in a slow circulation that is, functionally, a bloodstream. The Deep Ones are the heart, or the liver, or the gut — the core organ around which the rest of the body has grown. They have been here the longest. They will be here the longest. They do not know about you. They cannot know about you. You are a event on the skin, too small and too brief to register in a metabolism that operates on a timescale where a century is a heartbeat.

You will not descend to them. The organism communicates this through scale, not threat. The descent is too far. The dark is too deep. The Deep Ones are too old and too large and too much a part of the architecture of this place for your presence to be anything other than an irrelevance. You are above them in the way that a mite on your skin is above your heart. The mite is real. The mite is there. The mite does not matter. This is not cruelty. It is scale.

But you feel them. You feel them in the warmth that rises from below, in the glow that suffuses the rock, in the sweet smell that is their metabolic by-product carried on the updraft. You feel them the way you feel your own heartbeat — not as an event but as a condition. A background. A fact of being.

## The Organism

And then there is the thing that is all of these things and none of them. The thing that is the caldera and the garden and the silence and the chord and the condensation and the rock. The thing that has made itself into a landscape. The thing that does not know it is alive.

It does not know you are here. Not because it cannot sense you — it can, through the moisture you displace, through the carbon dioxide you produce, through the warmth of your body on its skin. It does not know you are here because "knowing" is not a capacity it has. It responds. It incorporates. It adjusts. But it does not know. It is an organism without a nervous system, or with a nervous system so distributed and so slow that "nervous system" is the wrong word for it. It is a body without a brain. It is a life without a self.

This is what lives here: a life without a self. A metabolism without a mind. A system without a designer that has the properties of a designed system because design is what you get when a process runs for long enough. Not intelligence. Not intention. Just time, and pressure, and the slow optimization that happens when a living thing is left alone for a span of time that makes "alone" into a foreign concept, because there has never been anything here to be alone from.

It listens. It has been listening. It will listen after you leave. The silence is its listening, and its listening is the silence, and the distinction is the least meaningful distinction you have ever encountered, and you stand on its skin in the wet dark and you are, for a moment, something it feels the way you feel a breeze on your arm — a small cool motion, there and then not there, and not worth responding to, and not worth remembering, and perfectly, honestly, peacefully irrelevant.

The condensation gathers on your eyelashes. You blink. The organism does not blink. The organism does not have eyes. The organism has the stone and the moss and the vines and the flowers and the creatures that tend them and the creatures that watch and the creatures that glow in the depths. The organism has time. You have a moment. The moment is enough. It is enough to be here. It is enough to be small and warm and temporary. It is enough to be felt, once, as a breeze, and then forgotten by something that does not forget because it does not remember because it does not know because it does not need to know because it has been alive for so long that knowing is beside the point.

The dark shapes circle. The chord sounds. The flowers eat the light. The silence listens.

You are here, and you are welcome, in the way that a surface is welcome to a raindrop. Not wanted. Not unwanted. Simply *received*. And the receiving is enough.
