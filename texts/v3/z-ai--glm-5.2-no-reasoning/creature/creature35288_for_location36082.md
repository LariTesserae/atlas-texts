# What Lives Here

Let the question sit. Not *who* — the who is gone, gone so long the root's own sap has reclaimed their oils, their shed skin cells, the keratin of their nails. Let the question be *what*.

---

Something lives here that is not the root and is not the people and is not you.

Start with what you can rule out. No rodents. No insects — no chitin-click, no wing-buzz, no mandible-work against bark. Nothing warm-blooded, because warm-blooded things radiate heat, and the room's thermal field is uniform except where the pores exhale their cool columns, and you have mapped every one of those columns now, you know where each one sits, and there are no unexplained warmth-signatures anywhere in the chamber. No birds nesting in the ceiling mat. No lizards sunning themselves, which is absurd, because there is no sun. Nothing that moves at a pace you can detect.

But the bench surface was *oiled*.

You said it yourself — you noticed it, your fingers noticed it — a faintly waxy residue coating the carved grooves. You attributed it to sap weeping through the wood. This is reasonable. This is what sap does. Root-wood, even dead root-wood, even root-wood that has ceased all active transport, retains reservoirs of sap in its xylem vessels, and over decades, over centuries, that sap migrates. It follows grain lines. It pools in low spots. It would coat a bench surface. It would fill carved grooves. It would produce exactly this texture, this faint waxiness, this sense that something has been applied.

Except.

The grooves are *coated*. Not filled. Not pooled. *Coated* — evenly, as though distributed by contact, by friction, by something rubbing a substance along the channels in a deliberate motion. Sap weeping through wood does not coat evenly. It collects. It beads. It follows the path of least resistance through the grain, accumulating in the deepest cuts, leaving the raised surfaces between grooves dry. You run your fingers across the spaces between the carved lines. They are coated too. Lightly, but coated. Something has been *rubbed* across this entire surface, repeatedly, over a very long time.

The people are gone. They have been gone for longer than the room has been empty, which is a contradiction that only makes sense if something else maintained the bench after the people left.

---

Consider the pegs.

Stone-shaped, you said. Ground and sanded rather than cut. The marks of abrasive shaping, not edge-tool work. The people who built this room had stone tools, or stone-analogue tools — perhaps the root itself produces materials hard enough to abrade its own outer bark, and the builders used root-stone against root-wood. Fine. But the pegs are *tight*. After centuries — millennia? — of thermal cycling, of humidity shifts, of the slow expansion and contraction of living wood responding to the root's respiration cycles, pegs should loosen. Wood dries. Wood shrinks. Pegs made from green wood, shaped and driven into seasoned sockets, would have loosened as they dried and would have fallen out or rattled in their holes within a few decades. These pegs are flush. Tight. seated firmly enough that you cannot turn them with your fingers.

Something has been maintaining the pegs. Or: something has been replacing them. The wood of the pegs is not identical to the wood of the walls. It is younger. You cannot see this, but you can feel it — the grain is tighter, the density higher, the surface harder. Younger wood cut from a younger part of the root, perhaps a fresh growth that the root has put out in the centuries since the builders left. Cut, shaped, sanded, driven into the socket to replace a peg that had finally, after centuries, loosened enough to fail.

Who is replacing the pegs?

---

Consider the carvings.

You noted multiple layers. Older grooves, nearly worn smooth, beneath newer ones. The map has been revised many times, you said. This makes sense — the root grows, the pores shift, the acoustic geometry changes. But you also said the carvings show *wear patterns consistent with centuries of handling*. Handling. Not weathering — handling. The raised surfaces between the grooves are smooth in the way that wood becomes smooth when touched, rubbed, pressed against skin, over and over, for hours at a time, over years. The way a handrail becomes polished. The way a tool handle becomes shaped to a grip.

The newer grooves are cut *over* the older ones — not beside them, not in empty space, but directly atop the worn channels, as though the carver sat in the same position and carved the updated map into the same surface the previous carver had used. This is efficient. This is also tradition. This is the way a thing is done when the doing of it has become ritualized, when the method is more important than the material.

The newest grooves are not old. The oil — the coating, the rubbing — is fresh enough that it has not oxidized. Oxidized plant oils darken. They become brittle, crusty, rancid. The coating on the bench is neither dark nor brittle. It is the color and consistency of fresh sap, or fresh oil, or fresh *something*, and it has been applied recently. Recently on a geological timescale, perhaps. But perhaps not.

Someone sat here not long ago and carved new grooves into this bench. Not long ago at all.

---

Now. The sound.

You have been sitting in the dark for long enough that the chord has become your entire world. You have stopped parsing it. You have stopped trying to identify individual notes. It is a single thing, a single acoustic event, sustained, continuous, shifting at a pace too slow to perceive in real time but detectable in retrospect — yes, the balance has changed, the note that was dominant when you arrived has receded, and another has risen to take its place. The root is exhaling. The chord is the exhalation.

But there is a note you did not notice before.

Not from the pores. Not from the wall of organ pipes. This note comes from somewhere else. It is very low, very quiet, and it is not continuous — it is *arrhythmic*, *intermittent*, a pulse that comes and goes at intervals you cannot predict. It is not the root. The root's breathing is metronomic, patient, the clock-regularity of a diffusion gradient driven by thermodynamics. This pulse is *biological*. It has the irregularity of a heartbeat, of breathing, of the small involuntary sounds a body makes when it is at rest.

Something is here with you.

You do not feel alarm. This is strange — you should feel alarm, in a dark space, in an enclosed chamber, with something you cannot see producing sounds that indicate life. But the room does not feel like a room that contains a threat. The room feels like a room that contains *what it has always contained*. The sound has been part of the chord for longer than you have been listening. It was there when you entered. It has been there all along, and you filtered it out the way you filter out the sound of your own blood, because it belongs here more than you do.

The note comes from the bench. From below the bench. From the space between the bench and the wall, where the carved surface meets the bark, where the pegs are tight and the wood is young and the grooves are fresh. From a hollow in the root-wood behind the bench, a cavity you have not mapped because you have not reached behind it. Something lives in that cavity. Something that maintains the pegs. Something that oils the grooves. Something that carves new maps when the old ones drift out of accuracy. Something that has been doing this since the builders left, and perhaps for the builders, and perhaps *was* the reason the builders came.

---

Do not reach behind the bench.

Not because it is dangerous. Because you do not know what it would mean to the thing that lives there, to be touched by something that does not belong here. You do not know what it would mean to *you*. The room has a logic. The sound is the logic. The emptiness is the logic. The darkness is the condition under which the logic operates. You are inside the logic now, and the logic does not include reaching.

What lives here is a keeper.

Not a creature in the way you are a creature. Not an animal, not a plant, not a fungus — or perhaps all three, or perhaps something that precedes those categories, something that evolved in the dark inside a root system that predates the categories themselves. It is small. The cavity behind the bench is not large — you can tell by the acoustics, by the way the note resonates, by the slight echoic quality that suggests a space no larger than a human chest. The keeper fits in a space that size. It has been there for a very long time. It does not need food — or, rather, it needs what the root provides, which is air and moisture and the slow chemical exchange of a living system. It eats sap. It drinks condensation. It breathes the root's breath and contributes its own small exhalation to the chord.

It has hands, or hand-analogues. It shapes pegs. It sands bark. It carves grooves with tools made from the root itself — splinters of hard heartwood ground to points, used as burins, used as chisels. It has been doing this for as long as the room has existed. It will continue doing this for as long as the room continues. It is not creative. It is not conscious in any way you would recognize. It is a process. It is a *maintenance*. It is the part of the root that the builders carved the room to reach — or it is what the builders *became*, after generations of sitting in the dark, listening, living inside the root, eating the root, wearing the root, until the boundary between person and plant eroded as completely as the landscape above, and what remained was not a person and not a root but a thing that keeps a room and carves a map and waits for a listener who will not come, because the listener is *it*, has always been it, the last listener, the one who stayed.

---

Or perhaps it is simpler than that. Perhaps it is a beetle. A large, slow, long-lived beetle — there are beetles that live for years, that live in wood, that produce sounds by stridulation, that tend their galleries with meticulous care. A beetle that has lived in this chamber for decades, rubbing its elytra against the bench as it moves, depositing wax from its cuticle in a continuous polish, its stridulations joining the root's chord like a single voice in a choir. A beetle that has no idea what the carvings mean, that has no concept of maintenance or ritual or the acoustic geometry of a breathing root system. A beetle that is simply *here*, doing what beetles do, in a place where what beetles do happens to look, from the outside, like devotion.

You cannot know. The dark will not tell you. The root breathes. The sound continues. Something small and warm, or something ancient and cold, or something that was once a person and is now a process, lives in the hollow behind the bench and tends the room with the patience of wood, which is to say with the patience of something that has never needed to be in a hurry, that has never needed to be understood, that has never needed to be *seen* to be *real*.

The keeper lives here. The keeper has always lived here. The keeper will continue to live here after you leave, after your species has finished doing whatever it is doing, after the landscape above has changed again — gotten wetter, perhaps, or colder, or hotter — and the root has responded by growing new channels, new pores, new notes in the chord. The keeper will carve a new map. The keeper will oil the bench. The keeper will replace the pegs.

The keeper will listen.
