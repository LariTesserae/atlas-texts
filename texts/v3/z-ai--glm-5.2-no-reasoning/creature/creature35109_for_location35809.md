# What Lives in Mossbarrow

---

## The People

They are not what you expect.

That is the first thing. You arrive with some image — and everyone does, there is no shame in it — of people softened by damp, diminished by shade, their limbs pale and their eyes weak from living in permanent twilight under a canopy that admits no direct sun. You imagine a certain attenuation. A pallor. The folk of wet places, in stories, are always somehow lessened by it.

The people of Mossbarrow are not lessened.

They are, to be precise, compact. Not short — not uniformly — but dense in the way that certain trees are dense, their grain tightly packed by the slow pressure of consistent adversity. The women average perhaps a hand below the stature of lowland women; the men, the same. But they are broad through the shoulders and deep through the chest in a way that suggests not labor, exactly, but *bracing*. They are built to withstand. Their frames are the frames of people who have spent every day of their lives in a place that pushes back — not aggressively, not violently, but ceaselessly, the way water pushes. Their posture is slight-forward, the lean of someone walking into a persistent wind, even when the air is still. It is the posture of someone moving through resistance. The moss mat underfoot gives with each step. The air is thick. Everything requires slightly more effort than it should, and their bodies record this in the forward cant of their hips, the strength of their calves, the way their feet seem to grip the ground when they stand.

Their skin is not pale. It is dark — not with sun, there is no sun here, but with weather. A lifetime of exposure to moving moisture, to the fine suspension of water in the air that is Mossbarrow's atmosphere, has stained them. The color is hard to place. It is not the brown of sun-tanning. It is something closer to the color of the bark of their house-trees: a deep, weathered grey-brown, darker on the forearms and faces, lighter on the trunk, the skin thickened and slightly roughened at the knuckles and the backs of the hands. Their hands are remarkable. Every person past childhood has hands that look older than their faces — the fingers broad, the pads calloused not from tools but from gripping living wood, from weaving wet branches, from pulling moss and pressing it into gaps. The nails are kept short, trimmed close to the quick, because nails grow fast here and long nails trap dirt and moisture and become foci for fungal infection. They know this. It is not theory. Everyone has lost someone's toenail to a bout of rot that started under a too-long nail and spread. The knowledge is practical, acquired, shared without discussion.

Their hair is universally dark. Not black — not the blue-black of some northern peoples or the warm black of southern ones — but a dark brown that borders on black and is, in the younger people, glossy with the constant ambient moisture. It hangs straight or slightly wavy, heavy with damp, and they wear it long, past the shoulders for both sexes, usually tied back with braided nettle cord. Loose hair in Mossbarrow is a liability; it catches on branches, snags in the wattle, falls into fire pits, provides housing for insects. Children's hair is often braided tightly against the head, the braids pinned with small carved bone or wooden pins, because children move fast and erratically through the settlement and cannot be trusted to manage loose hair in a place where a low-hanging branch can snag you mid-run. The elder women — those past forty, which is not old here but is past the prime of physical resilience — often wrap their hair in strips of bark fiber, forming a kind of close turban that keeps it manageable and, incidentally, warm. The men's beards, for those who grow them, are kept short. Long beards collect moisture and drip.

Their eyes are the thing you notice last and remember longest.

They are large. Not dramatically, not grotesquely, but noticeably — the eyes of a person who has spent their life in low light, whose pupils have learned to dilate broadly, whose irises have adapted to extract every photon from the diffuse grey of Mossbarrow's sky. The color varies — brown, hazel, an occasional grey-green — but the quality is consistent: a certain *intensity* of gaze, a fixedness, that comes from living in a world where the visual field is always cluttered, always layered, always full of partially obscured things. They look at you the way a wren looks at you: head slightly tilted, both eyes fixed, unblinking, parsing you from the background. It is not hostile. It is not unfriendly. It is *attention*. The attention of a people for whom inattention means walking into a branch, stepping on a frog, missing the early signs of a wall splitting, a channel backing up, a mushroom blooming in the wrong place. They cannot afford unfocused gaze. Their eyes are the eyes of people who read their world in the language of green.

They speak softly. This is the other thing. The settlement is so loud — the insects, the frogs, the birds, the pigeons, the water — that speech must compete, and they have learned that the way to compete with a dense soundscape is not to shout but to cut through it. Their voices are low, pitched below the throb of the frogs, and carry a clarity that is almost startling when you first hear it. They do not mummer. They do not mumble. Each word is shaped precisely, given its full consonants, its vowels open and round. The accent is difficult to place — it is not the accent of the lowlands or the highlands or any of the surrounding country. It is its own thing, shaped by the acoustics of a place where every surface is soft and absorbent and sound does not echo but is swallowed, so that speech must be clean and complete to survive the distance between speaker and listener.

They call to one another across the settlement constantly. Not in sentences — in fragments, names, single words loaded with context. A sharp whistle means *look here*. A low trill means *come*. A specific two-note call, rising then falling, means *the channel is backing up*. They have a vocabulary of perhaps two dozen such calls, learned in childhood, used without thought, as automatic as breathing. A stranger in Mossbarrow hears these calls and cannot distinguish them from the bird noise; they blend, they are meant to blend, the people have developed a signal system that lives within the acoustic ecology of the place rather than fighting against it.

They move differently. You notice this when you try to follow someone and cannot. Not because they are fast — they are not fast, not in the lowland sense — but because they are *continuous* in a way that your movement is not. They do not walk from point to point; they flow. Their feet find the solid places in the moss mat without looking down, the way your feet find stairs. They duck under low branches without stooping, their bodies reading the architecture of the space the way a swimmer reads current. They step over drainage channels without breaking stride, their weight shifting with a fluidity that is almost liquid, almost — and you resist this comparison but it persists — almost amphibian. They have grown up in this place. Their bodies are shaped by it. Their proprioception, their sense of where they are in space, is calibrated to a world of uneven, giving surfaces and continuous overhead obstruction. You watch a woman carry a clay pot of water on her shoulder down a path that is more root than ground, stepping over a sleeping duck and under a curtain of fern and around a child crouched to watch a beetle, and she does not spill, does not pause, does not look down. The pot arrives. She sets it down. She goes back for another.

## The Growing of Houses

The growers are the most important people in Mossbarrow, and they are not a caste, not a class. They are simply the ones who can do it.

There are perhaps six of them at any given time, though the number shifts. They are not old — not necessarily — though the eldest among them, a woman named Tarn whose hands are so calloused she can grip a nettle stem without flinching, is perhaps fifty and has been growing for thirty-five years. They are not exclusively women, though four of the six are. They are identified young, usually by the age of eight or nine, by an aptitude that is difficult to articulate but that the growers recognize in each other the way one musician recognizes another: not by skill, which can be taught, but by *feel*. The feel for how a branch wants to grow. The sense of a tree's intention.

Because that is what growing a house requires. You do not force a tree into a shape. You coax it. You redirect its existing tendencies. A branch that wants to grow upward is bent into a roof beam, but only if it wants to grow upward strongly enough that the bending will not kill it. A branch that wants to spread laterally is woven into a wall. A branch that wants to droop is left to form a doorway, trained into an arch over years, the growing tip guided downward and around until it meets and grafts to its own trunk. The grower's art is the art of reading desire and redirecting it, and a good grower can look at a young tree and see, in its branching pattern and its growth rate and the angle of its buds, the house that is already latent in it, waiting to be expressed.

Tarn describes it this way, and you must translate because she does not explain in terms that a lowlander would recognize: she says she listens to the tree. She puts her hands on the bark and she feels where the sap is moving strongest, and she knows that is where the tree is putting its energy, its *want*, and she works with that. She says a tree that is forced will sulk. It will grow slowly, or it will grow in the wrong direction out of spite — and she says this without irony, without metaphor, as a statement of observed fact. Trees, in Mossbarrow, have temperament. The growers know this. They work with it. Tarn says the old oak at the center of the meeting hall is stubborn and slow but patient, and you can trust it to hold a structure for a century, but you cannot rush it. She says the ash on the western side is nervous and grows too fast and puts out weak wood, and you have to watch it, prune it back, keep it from overextending. She says the wych elms are moody. They will grow beautifully for years and then, for no apparent reason, decline. She has stopped trying to understand why. She accepts it.

A house begins with a site selection. The growers walk the settlement, looking for gaps — places where a structure has failed, where a tree has died, where the canopy has thinned. They consider the drainage, the soil depth, the existing root systems, the proximity to other structures. They consider the tree species. Oak for permanence. Ash for speed. Elm for height. Hazel for filling. Willow for the wet places where nothing else will root. They consider the person who will live there — their needs, their family size, their trade — because the house must grow to fit them, and a house takes years to become a house.

The process: a young tree, already established, is selected. In the first year, the lower branches are cleared and the main stem is encouraged to thicken. In the second year, the structural branches are selected and the training begins — gentle bending with weighted cords, the branches guided toward their eventual positions. In the third year, the weaving starts: the structural branches are joined with live wattles, hazel or willow whips that are threaded through and allowed to root, filling the walls. In the fourth year, the roof is begun — the upper branches drawn together and inward, a lattice of living wood that will close over time. In the fifth year, the moss is packed, the leaf-shingles laid, the floor cut and shaped. In the sixth year, a person can move in. It will not be finished. It will never be finished. But it will be *habitable*, and the person who lives in it will spend some part of each week maintaining it, pruning its growth, repairing its weave, managing its life as it manages theirs.

Tarn has grown eleven houses in her lifetime. She says the first one was ugly. She says she did not listen well. She says the tree wanted to be a different shape and she forced it, and the house is still standing — still living — but it is awkward, its walls bulging strangely, its doorway too narrow, its roof uneven. She says the tree has never forgiven her. She says this without smiling. She means it.

## The Daily Round

The morning begins before what anyone elsewhere would call morning. The light does not change — it is the same flat grey at midnight as at noon, a slight brightening of the featureless sky the only concession to the sun's passage — but the people of Mossbarrow keep time by the frogs. The first chorus, the dawn chorus of the amphibians, begins in the deepest part of what might be night, a slow swelling of sound that starts with a single individual and builds to the full polyrhythmic throb. The sleepers stir. The fires are checked, fed, stirred to life with small twigs and dried moss kept in bark-fiber bags beside the pit. The first meal is simple — stored foods, mostly, dried mushroom and root, sometimes a paste of ground acorns mixed with water and warmed, sometimes fish if the night's catch was good.

Then the rounds.

Every person in Mossbarrow has a round. Not a job — the word is wrong, implies a discrete task and an end. A *round* is a circuit, a daily walk through a section of the settlement, checking, tending, managing. The growers have the largest rounds, covering the structural trees and the major walls. Others have smaller territories: the drainage keeper, a wiry man named Reed who is perhaps thirty and has been keeping channels since he was a child, walks the entire water system each morning, clearing blockages, redirecting flow, checking the standing pools for stagnation. The moss tenders — two of them, a mother and daughter, Alder and Little Alder, though the daughter is nineteen and not little — walk the paths, patching thin spots in the living floor with transplants from the thick growth at the settlement's edges. The fungi forager walks the root flares and the shaded undersides, checking for edible species, harvesting the overnight puffballs before they collapse, watching for the dangerous ones — there are a few, and everyone knows them, and the forager removes them before a child can find them.

The rounds take the first two hours of the day. They are not hurried. They are not slow. They are *thorough*. Every surface is checked. Every branch is tested with a hand on the bark, a push to feel the give, a look at the graft points for signs of separation. Every drainage channel is followed from source to outflow, the water's clarity noted, its smell noted — a stagnant smell is a warning, a sign that the channel is blocked somewhere below, in the root-tangle where it cannot be seen. Every wall is pressed, gently, to feel for softness, for the internal rot that can take a woven wall from the inside, invisible until the structure fails.

Because structures do fail. This is the thing that a visitor, seduced by the green beauty of the place, might miss. Mossbarrow is not a paradise of stasis. It is a place in continuous crisis — low-level, manageable crisis, but crisis nonetheless. A wall fails when its wattles rot faster than they root. A roof leaks when the moss mat thins and the leaf-shingles decay. A floor collapses when the root-tangle beneath it hollows out, undermined by water or burrowed through by voles. A doorway closes when a branch grows across it, unmanaged for too long, the wood thickening until the gap is too narrow to pass. These things happen. They happen regularly. They are not disasters — the people of Mossbarrow do not experience them as disasters — they are simply the *state* of the place, the natural consequence of living inside something alive.

The repair is continuous. A wall that is failing is cut back — the rotting wattles removed, the gap packed with fresh willow whips, the living frame pruned to encourage new growth into the gap. A roof that leaks is patched — fresh moss packed into the thin spot, new leaves laid, a branch redirected to provide additional cover. A floor that has collapsed is bridged — a plank of split oak laid across the hollow, the edges packed with moss, the gap below left open for the water to drain through. The repairs are not invisible. They are visible everywhere — the pale new wood of a fresh graft against the dark weathered wood of an old frame, the bright green of new willow against the grey-green of established wattle, the light patch of transplanted moss against the dark mat of the old floor. Mossbarrow is a patchwork. It is all patchwork. It has always been patchwork. The oldest structures, the meeting hall at the center, are so heavily patched that no original surface remains — every wattle, every shingle, every section of moss has been replaced, some of them many times. The structure persists, but its material has turned over completely, like the cells of a body. It is the same house the way you are the same person you were ten years ago: in pattern, in form, in identity, but not in substance.

The afternoon — if it is afternoon, if the slight brightening of the sky can be trusted to indicate the sun's position — is for the other work. Foraging in the surrounding forest, mostly. The settlement is not self-sufficient; it draws from the woods around it. Mushrooms, roots, greens, nuts in season, bark for fiber, clay from the stream banks below, stone from the river beds. Hunting is minimal — the occasional bird, the occasional rabbit, the rare deer if someone is lucky and skilled. Fishing is more reliable. The streams that run through the ravines below the settlement are full of small, dark trout and the larger, slow-moving fish that the people call mud-pike, which are not pike but are large and fatty and can be caught by hand if you know where to feel for them under the banks. The fishers go down the hill each day, two or three of them, and return with whatever the water has offered.

The craftspeople work in the afternoon, when the light is brightest — which is still not bright. A woman sits in the doorway of her house, splitting reeds for weaving, her hands moving by touch as much as sight, the bone knife in her right hand making a sound like tearing paper as it separates the fibers. An old man — the oldest in the settlement, perhaps sixty, his face creased deep as bark, his hands tremoring slightly but still steady enough to work — sits beside the central oak and knaps flint, making the edge tools that the growers use for trimming. Two children, perhaps ten, work together at the drainage channel near the meeting hall, building a small dam of sticks and clay to deepen a pool, because they want to keep frogs there. No one has told them to do this. No one stops them. The pool will exist for a few weeks until the drainage changes it, and the children will lose interest, and the frogs will relocate. This is how things work in Mossbarrow. Projects are small, local, and temporary. The settlement is large, communal, and permanent. The tension between these scales is the tension that gives the place its life.

## The Children

There are not many. This is the thing that a careful count reveals and that the people do not discuss with visitors, or much among themselves.

There are perhaps thirty children in Mossbarrow. The total population is perhaps one hundred and twenty — the counting is imprecise, because people come and go, and the boundaries of "who lives here" are fuzzy in the way that everything in Mossbarrow is fuzzy. But the ratio is low. Lower than it should be, by the standards of the lowland communities, and lower than it needs to be for replacement. The population is very slowly declining. Not catastrophically. Not even noticeably, from year to year. But over decades, the trend is clear to anyone who counts, and the elders count.

The reasons are not mysterious. The environment is hard on infants. The damp is constant, and respiratory illness is common in the first year of life. The insects carry things — not the biting species, but the others, the ones that crawl through the moss mat and the walls and the bedding, carrying pathogens between the human population and the dense animal population that shares the settlement. The living walls are penetrable to vermin, and the vermin carry fleas, and the fleas carry fever. Mossbarrow fever, it is called in the lowlands, though the people of Mossbarrow call it simply *the heat*, because the symptom is a rising temperature in a place where body temperature is usually slightly depressed by the ambient chill. An infant with the heat will burn in a place where nothing else burns. The fire pits are too small, too few. The living walls hold heat but cannot generate it. A feverish child must be held against a warm body, constantly, and the people of Mossbarrow do this — the mother, the father, the siblings, the neighbors, taking shifts through the night, holding the child against the chest, the heat of one body transferred to another — but sometimes it is not enough. The heat takes one or two each year. The people accept this. They do not accept it easily. They accept it the way they accept the rain.

The children who survive are remarkable. They are the most Mossbarrow of all the inhabitants, the most fully adapted, the most perfectly fitted to the place. They have been growing here since before they were born — their mothers' bodies were Mossbarrow bodies, shaped by the diet and the damp and the daily round, and the children grow in the womb with the ambient moisture already in their blood. They are born, and they are placed on the moss mat, and they begin to learn.

They learn fast. A child of three in Mossbarrow can navigate the settlement in the dark — and it is always, in some sense, dark — by feel and sound alone, knowing the location of every root ridge and drainage channel and low branch by the texture of the ground and the quality of the echo. A child of five can identify twenty species of mushroom by smell. A child of eight can weave a wall panel. A child of twelve can manage a round. The children are not educated in the formal sense — there is no school, no instruction, no curriculum. They learn by being present, by watching, by doing, by being given small responsibilities that grow larger as they grow larger, until the settlement's maintenance is simply a thing they do, as natural as walking, as automatic as the morning frog chorus.

They play. They play constantly, in the way that children everywhere play, but their play is shaped by the place. They chase each other through the narrow spaces between buildings, ducking and weaving, their bare feet sure on the moss mat, their laughter sharp and bright in the damp air. They build things — small structures, miniature houses, living constructions of woven weed and stuck moss that they tend for days and then abandon. They hunt things — beetles, frogs, the small crustaceans that scuttle on the bark — and they keep their catches in small woven cages, and the catches die, and the children are briefly sad, and then they catch more. They climb. They climb everything. The house-trees, the meeting hall oak, the living wall, the braided hawthorn at the entrance. They climb with an ease that is almost primate, their small fingers finding holds in the bark that an adult would miss, their weight low enough that branches that would not support an adult bear them easily. They sit in the canopy and look down on the settlement and they are quiet, sometimes, for long stretches, and you wonder what they see from up there — what the settlement looks like from above, through the eyes of a child who has never known any other world.

They are not afraid. This is the thing that strikes you. The children of Mossbarrow are not afraid of the dark, because there is no dark — only lesser and greater degrees of dimness. They are not afraid of the animals, because the animals are everywhere and are mostly harmless. They are not afraid of the structures, because the structures are alive and do not fall; they grow, and if they fail, they fail slowly, visibly, with warning. They are not afraid of the wet, because they have never been dry. They are not afraid of the forest, because the forest is their home, extended, and they know it the way they know their own house. They are not afraid of being lost, because Mossbarrow is small and the surrounding forest is theirs and the paths are known.

They are afraid of the lowlands. This is a fear they learn from their parents, not from experience, and it is a quiet fear, a background fear, the kind that sits in the voice when the lowlands are mentioned and in the eyes when a stranger arrives from the ridge path. They are afraid of dry open sky, of distance without trees, of ground that does not give, of air that does not move. They are afraid of the world that made them an anomaly, and they are right to be, because the world that made them an anomaly does not understand them and will, if given the chance, change them.

## The Old Woman by the Western Wall

Her name is Thatch, and she is the oldest person in Mossbarrow, and she does not know her age.

She lives in a small house on the western edge of the settlement, where the ground drops away into the steepest ravine and the living wall of trees is thickest. Her house is a single wych elm, trained into a low dome perhaps three meters across, the walls so densely woven with ivy and bindweed that the living structure beneath is invisible. The entrance faces east, away from the prevailing wind, and is a gap in the wattle that she closes each night with a woven panel of reed and bark fiber that she hangs from the lintel. Inside, the space is dark, warm, close. A sleeping platform of woven hazel, raised on root-ridges, covered with layers of moss and bark fiber. A fire pit, small, the stones blackened with years of use. A few clay pots. A bone needle, a coil of nettle cord, a pile of bark-fiber cloth. Nothing else. There is no room for anything else. The house is the size of a large nest, and it is, in every functional sense, exactly that.

Thatch is small. She has been shrinking for years — the spine compressing, the shoulders drawing in, the body contracting toward its center as the energy of maintenance withdraws from the extremities. She is perhaps a meter and a half tall, which is not dramatically short but which is noticeably shorter than the adults around her, and her size gives her a certain compactness, a certain density, as if she has been simplified by time to her essential form. Her face is deeply lined. The lines run in the direction of the rain — downward, vertical, following the path that water takes on skin, and you realize with a slight shock that this is not metaphor. Her face is *eroded* by the same forces that erode the weeping stone. She has lived in this place for so long that its weather is written in her skin.

Her hands are still active. She weaves — not structures, not walls, but small things. Bags of bark fiber. Mats of reed. The small, intricate pieces that are not needed but that she makes because her hands must do something, and the something they do is the thing they have done for sixty years or seventy or eighty, the muscle memory deeper than memory. She weaves and does not watch her hands. She watches the wall of her house, the living wattle, the slow pulse of growth that she can see — or claims she can see — in the widening of the gaps, the thickening of the branches. She talks to the tree. Not in words, exactly. In sounds. Low, sustained hums and clicks that she makes with her tongue against the roof of her mouth, sounds that are not language but that are communication of some kind, or at least are one-directional communication, the tree the recipient of a message it may or may not receive.

She says the tree responds. She says it grows differently when she is present, that the branches lean toward her, that the leaves turn. The growers say this is phototropism, the leaves following the light from the fire pit. Tarn says this with respect, not dismissal, because she also believes that Thatch's tree grows differently, though she frames it differently: she says that Thatch has been in the house so long that her breath, her body heat, her daily movements have become part of the tree's environment, and the tree has grown to accommodate them, the way a tree grows around a rock. The tree and the woman have shaped each other. Tarn says this is the goal of all growing. She says Thatch is the best grower she has ever known, though Thatch has not grown a new house in twenty years.

Thatch does not leave her house often. She does not need to. The children bring her food — mushrooms, fish, the roots she likes, the specific bracket fungus that grows on the north side of the central oak and that she considers a delicacy. They bring her water. They bring her news, which she receives with the tilted-head attention of all Mossbarrow people but with an additional quality, a depth of listening that suggests she is hearing not just the news but the patterns beneath it, the long slow trends that only someone who has been present for decades can perceive. She says things, sometimes, to the children. Short things, gnomic, not quite proverbs. "The water knows where to go." "A wall that does not leak is dead." "If the frogs stop, leave." The children repeat these to their parents, who nod and do not explain, because there is nothing to explain. The sayings mean what they mean. They are the compressed observations of a life lived in continuous attention to a single place.

She will die in this house. Everyone knows this. When she dies, the house will be tended by the growers until a decision is made about its future — whether to give it to another, or to let it grow closed, the doorway narrowing as the branches thicken, the interior filling with moss and root, the structure becoming a tree again over the course of a decade. This has happened before. There are trees in Mossbarrow that were houses, their trunks still bearing the marks of old grafts, old training, old doorways grown shut. The settlement is full of these ghosts. Every tree that was a house is a grave of a kind, and every house that is a tree is a life in progress, and the distinction between the two is, as everything in Mossbarrow, not quite clear.

## The Settlement at Night

The frogs change. This is the first thing that happens when the light dims — if it dims, if the flat grey of the day deepens to a darker grey that is not quite dark but is unmistakably night. The daytime chorus, the polyrhythmic throb of three or four species calling simultaneously, shifts. The higher-pitched species fall silent. The deeper voices persist, and a new voice joins: a low, resonant, almost subsonic croaking that you feel more than hear, a vibration in the moss mat, in the soles of your feet, in the floor of your chest. This is the night frog, a species that hides in the root-tangle during the day and emerges after the light changes, moving to the deepest pools, the standing water in the drainage channels, and calling. The call carries. It penetrates the living walls. Inside the houses, the people lie on their sleeping platforms and feel the call in their bones, and it is not unpleasant. It is the sound of Mossbarrow breathing in its sleep.

The birds change. The daytime species — the wrens, the rails, the woodpeckers — fall silent, roosting in the upper canopy. The pigeons continue, their cooing slower, deeper, more spaced, the bass note of the settlement drawn out like a slow heartbeat. A new sound emerges: a small owl, a species that nests in the hollow trunks of the oldest oaks, begins to call — a single sharp note, repeated at intervals of perhaps ten seconds, piercing the layered sound of the night with a clarity that is almost painful. It hunts the rodents of the moss mat, dropping from its roost in utter silence, taking a mouse or a vole, returning to its perch to eat. The people of Mossbarrow know this owl and consider it a neighbor. One family has named it, though the name is not spoken aloud, because to name a hunting animal too loudly is to alert its prey, and the prey are also neighbors, and the balance must be kept.

The insects change. The day species — the midges, the crane flies, the small metallic beetles — settle into the moss mat, their wings folded, their metabolism slowing in the cooler air. The night species emerge: moths, pale and large, attracted to the dim glow of the fire pits, their bodies soft and furred, their wings making a sound like paper when they brush against the living walls. The caddis flies persist, drawn to any light, gathering in small clouds around the fire pits, their wings catching the glow and flickering like sparks.

The water does not change. The water is the same at night as it is during the day, because the water does not care about light. The weeping stone still weeps. The drainage channels still run. The drip from the eaves continues, perhaps slightly slower as the temperature drops and the rate of transpiration decreases, but not enough to notice. The sound of water is the constant, the ground note, the thing that does not change when everything else does. The people of Mossbarrow sleep with it. They have always slept with it. A person from Mossbarrow, sleeping in a dry place, will wake in the night, unsettled, and will not know why. The absence of water sound is, to them, the absence of a heartbeat.

Inside the houses, the fire pits glow. The light is dim, orange, warm against the green-grey of the living walls. The smoke rises through the gaps in the roof, joining the haze that clings to the canopy, and the settlement, seen from above — if you could see it from above, if you were a bat or an owl or a child who has climbed the central oak at night, which is forbidden but which happens — glows faintly from within, a scattered constellation of orange points in a dark green body, like the last coals of a fire that is not quite out.

The people sleep. They sleep deeply, in the warm close dark of their living houses, with the sound of water and frogs and pigeons and the crack of wood expanding in the cool and the rustle of things moving through the moss mat outside their walls. They sleep in the confidence of a place they know completely, a place that is known by them completely, a place that has shaped them and that they have shaped, and the sleep is the sleep of people who are, in a sense that goes beyond metaphor, at home in a body that includes them.

Mossbarrow breathes. The people breathe. The trees breathe. The frogs breathe. The moss breathes. The water moves. The night passes. The light does not change, and then it does, slightly, barely, a brightening of the grey that is the nearest thing to dawn this place will offer, and the first frog begins to call, and a child stirs, and a fire pit is fed, and the rounds begin again.

## What Lives Here

What lives in Mossbarrow is Mossbarrow.

This is not evasion. It is the answer that the place demands. You cannot separate the inhabitants from the habitat. You cannot list the people and then list the trees and then list the animals and then list the fungi and then list the water and claim to have described what lives here, because none of those things lives here alone. They live here *together*, in a density of coexistence that makes the concept of "individual organism" almost useless. The people are the trees are the moss are the frogs are the insects are the water are the stone are the rain. Not metaphorically. Functionally. The people eat the fungi that grow on the trees that are their houses. The fungi are fed by the moisture that weeps from the stone that is fed by the rain that falls from the sky that is shaded by the trees that are the houses. The frogs eat the insects that breed in the drainage channels that carry the water that weeps from the stone. The people tend the channels that house the frogs that eat the insects that bother the people. The trees are tended by the people who live in the trees that are tended by the people. It is a loop. It is all loops. It is loops all the way down, and the thing that lives here is the loop itself, the system, the *process* — the continuous, churning, growing, dripping, croaking, breathing, living process that is Mossbarrow.

The people of Mossbarrow do not articulate this. They do not have a philosophy of ecology. They do not have a word for it. They simply live it, the way they live in their houses: as part of something larger, shaped by it, shaping it, never quite in control, never quite out of control, always in the negotiation. They are not wise in the way that outsiders might want them to be wise. They are not noble savages or nature-saints or keepers of ancient earth-knowledge. They are practical, observant, tired, funny, sometimes cruel, sometimes kind, and they live in a wet place that is trying to absorb them, and they spend their lives keeping it from doing so, and they love it, and they fear it, and they are it.

They are it. That is the final thing. You look at the people of Mossbarrow and you see the place: in the weathered darkness of their skin, in the forward lean of their posture, in the intensity of their gaze, in the low clarity of their voices, in the broad strength of their hands, in the compact density of their frames. They have been shaped by this place as surely as the weeping stone has been shaped by the water, and they carry Mossbarrow in their bodies the way the moss carries the spores of the fungi, the way the bark carries the lichen, the way the water carries the memory of the stone it has passed through. They are of this place. They are this place, walking and speaking and tending and sleeping and waking and tending again, and when they are gone — when the last person leaves, or dies, or is absorbed — the place will remember them in the shapes of its trees, the patterns of its drainage, the species composition of its moss mat, the residual geometry of a settlement that was, for a time, a human habitation and is now, again, a thicket on a wet hillside.

It is still raining. It is always, in some degree, still raining. A woman steps out of her house, barefoot, and presses her hand to the bark of her wall, feeling the growth. She nods. She pulls a few stray shoots, tucks them into the weave. She steps back inside. The fire pit glows. The frogs call. The water moves. The moss grows. The place lives.

It lives.
