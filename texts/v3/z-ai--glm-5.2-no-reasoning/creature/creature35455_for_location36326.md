# What Lives in Mossbrug

---

They do not think of themselves as separate from the water.

This is the first thing to understand, and it is the hardest, because an outsider arrives with a body that has boundaries — skin, warmth, the contained self — and the people of Mossbrug do not have this, or rather, they have it but it is permeable in ways that would disturb a person from dry country. They wake wet. They sleep wet. The moss-cloth they wear against their skin is damp when they put it on and damp when they take it off and damp in every hour between, and their skin has adapted to this in ways that are not visible but are profound: the outer layer is thicker, almost calloused, but underneath it is soft in the way that things are soft when they have been in water for a very long time. Their skin does not prune. That response — the one a bather's fingers develop after twenty minutes in a tub — was lost generations ago, or rather, it became permanent, became simply the texture of their hands, which are broad and flat-fingered and grip wet stone the way a root grips wet earth: not by friction but by conforming, by finding the surface's truth and matching it.

They are not tall. None of them. The tallest man I saw might have reached my chin, and I am not tall. The women are shorter, but only by a hand's width, and the children — the children are compact in a way that suggests not stunting but efficiency, as though the body decided early that height was a luxury this place could not afford. The ceilings are low. The doorways are lower. The paths between buildings are narrow enough that two adults cannot pass without turning sideways, and even then their shoulders brush the moss on the walls, and the moss is wet, and so they are wet, and this is not an event. This is Tuesday. This is every minute of every day.

Their coloration runs toward the pale. Not sickly — there is a difference between pallor and the particular luminous whiteness of skin that has never needed to defend itself against sun, because sun is a visitor here, a rare guest that appears for an hour or two in late afternoon when the angle is right and the cloud cover thins enough to let light down into the bowl without letting warmth. The light that reaches Mossbrug is filtered, diffused, bounced off the underside of clouds until it arrives as a general illumination without a source, shadowless, the kind of light that makes everything look like it is lit from within. The people share this quality. Their skin is pale but it is not flat. There is color in it — a blue undertone, faint, visible at the wrists and throat and temples, where the veins run close — and this is not illness. This is the cold. Four degrees, five, the temperature of groundwater, the temperature of the stone, the temperature of the air they breathe in and out into a sky that is already full. Their blood runs close to the surface because their bodies are working, always working, to maintain the difference between themselves and their environment, and the work shows.

---

Their hands are their tell.

I spent an afternoon in the company of a woman named — the name does not translate well; it was a sound like water over stone, a syllable that started at the back of the throat and ended at the lips, and I will call her Veft because that is close enough and she did not correct me. Veft was old. Old in Mossbrug is different from old elsewhere — she might have been fifty, might have been seventy, the cold and the damp and the constant low-grade caloric expenditure of simply being warm in a cold place age the body in particular ways, weathering the face, deepening the lines around the eyes and mouth, whitening the hair early. Her hair was white. It hung in a single twisted rope over one shoulder, and the rope was damp, as everything was damp, and it had the faint greenish tinge that long exposure to moss-cloth gives to hair, so that she looked, from a distance, as though she had been drawn in pencil and then smudged with a wet thumb.

Her hands were something else. Her hands were the most articulate things I have seen on a human body. The fingers were not long — nothing about Mossbrug's people is long — but they were jointed in a way that suggested extra knuckles, an impression created by the flexibility of each joint, the range of motion that years of gripping irregular stone had opened up. She could spread her hand flat and press it to a wall and her palm would conform to the stone's surface completely, every dip and ridge accounted for, the hand becoming a cast of the rock. I watched her pick up a wet stone from a channel — a stone slicked with water, coated with the dark submerged moss, round as a skull — and she lifted it without adjusting her grip, without any of the small corrections and resettlings that my own hands required when I tried the same. Her fingers had found the stone's shape in the instant of contact, and they held it.

The people of Mossbrug do not write. This is not illiteracy — they have a system of marks, scratches on stone, that serve as labels and records and the occasional message — but they do not write the way dry-country people write, with ink on paper or stylus on wax, because paper rots in a week and wax softens in the cold and ink freezes or thins to nothing. What they have instead is hands. Their hands are their records. The calluses on a builder's palms map the stone they have lifted — where the fractures were, how the weight distributed, which edges cut and which were blunt. The scars on a channel-tender's fingers tell the history of the water's path — which gullies ran fast, which ran slow, which ones froze in the winter of the deep frost and had to be cracked open with a chisel. The moss-harvesters have hands stained blue-green at the fingertips, a stain that does not wash out because it is not a stain, it is a colonization — the moss lives in the creases of their skin, in the whorls of their fingerprints, and it grows there in microcosm, a faint living film that the harvesters carry with them as a mark of their trade.

---

They eat what the hollow provides, and the hollow provides little, and what it provides is strange.

The channels run with water and the water runs with life — not fish, nothing so large, but a ecology of small things, insect larvae and freshwater shrimp the size of a fingernail and something that the residents call *skil* which is neither shrimp nor insect but something between, pale and translucent and oddly sweet, that they harvest by dragging woven mats of the submerged moss through the channels and shaking the catch into stone bowls. The skil are eaten raw. They are alive when eaten. I watched Veft's grandson — a boy of perhaps eight, silent as the cats, moving through the channel behind their dwelling with a competence that made my own childhood seem idle and wasteful — I watched him scoop a handful of channel water, skim the skil from it with his fingers, and eat them the way another child might eat berries, one by one, tasting each before the next.

There are frogs. The frogs are not sacred but they are protected, because the frogs eat the insects that would otherwise colonize the moss, and the moss is warmth, and warmth is life. But the frogs die anyway — naturally, in the cold, in the winter when the channels freeze at the edges and the frogs slow and sometimes do not wake — and the dead frogs are collected and dried and ground into a paste with the rust-colored moss that grows on the rooftops, and the paste is spread on flatbread — the only cooked food I saw in Mossbrug, cooked on small fires built in stone troughs on the rooftops, fed with driftwood carried down from the marshplain and dried for months under the overhangs — and the paste is, I will be honest, terrible. It tastes like mud and protein and the particular bitterness of cold-adapted amphibians, and the residents eat it with a focus that suggests not pleasure but necessity, the way a person takes medicine.

There are the gardens. The hanging gardens in the gaps between buildings, the herb troughs on the rooftops, the small stubborn patches of green that cling to every surface where soil can be made to sit. The soil is not soil — it is moss-compost, the decomposed lower layers of the seventh moss, stripped from the walls in the spring when it grows too thick and layered into stone troughs where it becomes a dark, peaty substance rich in everything except sun. What grows in it grows leggy and pale — a variety of sorrel with leaves the color of jade, a thyme that has lost its scent and gained a hardiness that borders on the absurd, a root vegetable I never saw named but which was small and white and crunched like ice and tasted like very cold celery. These are not crops. They are supplements. They are the thing that prevents scurvy, the thing that adds a dimension to the diet of skil and frog-paste and flatbread, the thing that says: we are not merely surviving. We are also gardening.

And there is the moss itself. Not eaten — the seventh moss is not edible, its fibers too tough, its decomposition products too complex — but the sixth moss, the moss that grows on bone, the moss that colonizes the refuse heaps behind the tanner's quarter, that moss is harvested and dried and brewed into a tea that is the closest thing Mossbrug has to a luxury. It is green. startlingly green, the green of spring in a place that has no spring, and it tastes like wet stone tastes if wet stone had a ghost — mineral, faintly sweet, with an undertone of something biological that reminds you that you are drinking a thing that was alive and is now part of you. The residents drink it constantly. Every dwelling has a stone pot of it simmering on the rooftop fire, and it is offered to guests the way another culture might offer wine, and to refuse it is to refuse the place itself.

I did not refuse.

---

The children are the ones who break you.

Not because they are suffering. They are not suffering, or rather, they are suffering no more than anyone else in Mossbrug, which is to say they are suffering the basic condition of life in a place that does not value warmth, and they have no frame of reference for this, having never lived anywhere else, and so they are not suffering in the way that matters, which is the way that involves comparison. They do not know they are cold. They know they are cold the way they know they are alive — as a condition, not a complaint.

But they break you because they are children, and children are children everywhere, and in Mossbrug this means they play, and the way they play is a distillation of everything impossible about the place.

They play on the rooftops. The rooftops are the only open space, and the children claim them with the territorial certainty of children everywhere, and they run across the stone slabs — wet stone slabs, slicked with the rust-colored moss that stabilizes the roof, angled slightly to shed water, separated by gaps no wider than a handspan but deep, deep enough that a foot placed wrong would drop through into an alley — they run across these slabs at a speed that made me grip the nearest wall and breathe through my nose until the dizziness passed. They do not fall. I was told they do not fall, or rather, they fall sometimes, but they fall the way cats fall — reflexively, catching themselves, rolling, absorbing the impact with the same body-wide flexibility that their elders use to grip stone. A child who falls through a gap between rooftops catches the edge of the slab below, swings down, drops to the alley, lands in the shallow water of the channel, stands up, climbs the ladder, and resumes running, all without expression, without tears, without the particular theatrical outrage that a fall produces in a child who has an audience of concerned adults.

There are no concerned adults. The adults are on the rooftops too, or in the dwellings, or in the channels, and they see the child fall and they do not react because reacting would imply that the fall was unexpected, and in Mossbrug, falling is not unexpected. Falling is what happens when you are learning the stone, and learning the stone is what childhood is.

I watched a girl of perhaps five — small, solid, her hair cropped close to her skull for practical reasons I did not ask about — I watched her cross from one rooftop to another by way of a plank no wider than my hand, laid across a gap of perhaps four feet, slicked with water, and she crossed it at a run, and her feet hit the plank twice, and she was across, and she did not look down, and I thought: she will never know how remarkable she is, because this is Tuesday.

---

The old woman in the eastern quarter who tends the fire that never goes out.

There is a fire in Mossbrug that never goes out. This is not metaphor. It is a fire built in a stone basin on the widest rooftop in the eastern quarter, a rooftop that serves as gathering space and market square and, in the deepest cold, communal sleeping platform, and the fire has been burning for — no one knows. Longer than the current buildings. Longer than the oldest resident. The stone basin has been replaced three times, I was told, because the thermal cycling of fire and cold and fire again eventually fractures even the densest bedrock, and when the basin cracks, the fire is transferred to a new basin the way an ember is carried from one fire to another, and the new basin becomes the old fire, and the fire continues.

The woman who tends it is not a priestess. She is not a keeper in any formal sense. She is old — genuinely old, perhaps eighty, perhaps more, her body bent in the particular way that cold-bodies bend when the spine compresses and the shoulders round and the head comes forward to minimize the surface area exposed to the air — and she sits beside the fire all day, every day, feeding it driftwood, adjusting the stones that ring it, skimming the ash from the basin and saving it in a stone jar that no one explained to me. She does not speak. Or rather, she speaks, but in the low, almost subvocal register that Mossbrug's people use for ordinary communication, and her voice is so deep and so quiet that it merges with the sound of the water and the fire and the air, and you cannot tell where she ends and the place begins.

The fire is not for warmth. Or rather — it is for warmth, but not the way a hearth is for warmth. It is too small to heat more than the immediate circle of people around it, and the heat it produces is swallowed by the damp air within a body-length. What it is for, I think, is light. Mossbrug is not dark — the diffused light from the overcast sky reaches every corner of the hollow — but it is monochrome, the palette of wet stone and dark moss and pale skin, and the fire is the only point of warm color in the entire town. It is orange. It is the only orange. The residents gather around it not only for the heat but for the color, for the reminder that there is a spectrum, that the world contains colors other than blue-green and gray and the rust of the roof-moss, and the old woman tends this color the way a gardener tends a flower.

She has an apprentice. A girl, perhaps twelve, who sits beside her and watches and does not speak and will, in time, become the one who tends the fire, and the fire will continue, and the old woman will be gone, and the girl will be old, and the fire will be the same fire, because the fire is not the wood or the basin or the flame but the continuation, the unbroken transfer of burning from one set of hands to the next.

---

The man who builds. The man who is always building.

His name was a sound like stone striking stone, and I will call him Kett. Kett was not young — forty, perhaps, his face already mapped with the lines that the cold carves — but he moved with the energy of a younger man, and he was always building. I encountered him three times in my stay, in different parts of the hollow, and each time he was lifting stone. Not directing others to lift stone — lifting it himself, his broad hands finding the fracture-lines in the bedrock and levering a slab upward with a sound like a gasp, the stone separating from its mother with a wet reluctance that I felt in my teeth. The slab would be taller than him, and thinner than my finger, and he would walk it — walking it, the way you walk a heavy cabinet, tilting it, shifting his grip, letting the weight find its balance — and he would walk it to wherever the next wall was going, and he would set it, and he would move on to the next slab.

Kett did not design. He did not plan. I asked him — through Veft, who served as interpreter when my grasp of the local sounds failed me — I asked him how he decided where a wall should go, and he looked at me with an expression I have been thinking about ever since. It was not confusion. It was the expression of someone confronted with a question that does not map onto their understanding of the world. He said something to Veft. Veft turned to me and said: *The stone tells him where it wants to be.*

This is not mysticism. I spent enough time in Mossbrug to understand this. The bedrock has a grain — not the geological grain of sedimentary layering, but a fracturing pattern, a network of weaknesses and strengths that determines how the stone breaks and where the pieces fit and what shape a wall wants to be when it is made from the stone that was already there. Kett reads this grain the way a carpenter reads wood. He does not impose a plan on the stone. He discovers the plan that the stone already contains, and he executes it, and the result is a wall that looks organic, accidental, inevitable — a wall that could not have been built any other way, because it was not built, it was revealed.

He works alone. This is not solitude — in Mossbrug, solitude is as impossible as privacy — but he works without the collaboration that most building requires, because the stone he lifts is not heavy in the way that stone is heavy for outsiders. It is heavy, certainly — the slabs weigh more than he does, some of them — but he has lifted stone since childhood, and his body has adapted to this the way his hands have adapted to wet surfaces, and the strength is not in his arms but in his structure, in the way his bones and tendons and the connective tissue of his back have organized themselves around the act of lifting, so that the stone's weight passes through him and into the ground and the ground takes it, and he is merely the conduit.

When a wall falls — and walls fall, always, eventually — Kett is the one who lifts the stone again. He does not mourn the wall. He does not curse the frost. He looks at the fallen slabs and he reads them again, because the falling has changed the grain, has revealed new fracture-lines, new possibilities, and the wall he builds from the fallen stone may not be the wall that stood there before. It will be a new wall. A different wall. A wall that the stone wants to be now, after the fall, and this is not loss. This is conversation. The stone spoke, and the frost answered, and the wall fell, and now Kett will speak, and the stone will answer, and the wall will rise, and the moss will grow over it, and in a year no one will remember which wall was the old wall and which was the new, because there are no old walls and no new walls. There is only Mossbrug.

The pair who tend the channels. The lovers, or the siblings, or the partners in the sense that has no equivalent in my language.

They are inseparable. They move through the channels of Mossbrug the way the water moves through the channels — together, fluid, without separation, without the momentary gaps that even the closest couples produce when they navigate a narrow space. They do not hold hands. They do not need to. They move in coordination so complete that it appears choreographed, each one's body anticipating the other's, each one's step matched to the other's step, each one's weight shift compensated by the other's weight shift, so that they cross a channel on stepping-stones as a single organism, four feet finding four stones in a rhythm that never breaks.

They are not young. Both of them have the weathered look that Mossbrug produces by thirty, and they are past thirty, and their hands are the hands of channel-tenders — scarred at the fingertips, stained with the dark green-black of the submerged moss, the nails worn to nothing by years of gripping wet stone under water. Their work is the work that no one sees and everyone depends on: keeping the channels clear. The channels are Mossbrug's arteries. The water flows through them constantly, and if the channels clog — with moss, with sediment, with the small debris that washes down from the marshplain in every rain — the water backs up, and the water has nowhere to go, and the water rises, and in a town where the floor is the bedrock and the bedrock is the water table, rising water means flooded dwellings, means saturated walls, means the moss on the walls reaching the point of saturation where it sloughs off in sheets, and a wall without moss is a wall without warmth, and *to take someone's moss is to take their winter.*

So they work. Every day. Every channel. They walk the water's path from the high edge of the hollow to the low center, and they clear what needs clearing, and they rebuild what needs rebuilding, and they redirect nothing, because the water goes where it wants. They simply ensure that where it wants to go is open, is passable, is not blocked by the accumulated detritus of a town that is constantly rebuilding itself, and this is not glamorous work, and it is not honored work — there is no honor in Mossbrug, no hierarchy, no formal recognition of labor — but it is necessary work, and the necessity is its own honor, and they do it with a completeness that I found moving in a way I cannot fully articulate.

They live in a dwelling smaller than any other I saw. A space barely six feet by four, half-submerged in the channel that runs through the eastern quarter, the floor wet at all times, the ceiling so low that even they must duck. They sleep on a stone shelf above the waterline, on a bed of dried sage moss that they replace monthly, and they sleep curled together in a way that is not only affectionate but thermal — the only efficient method of maintaining body heat in a room that is, essentially, a channel with a roof. I was invited in once. I could not stay. Not because of the cold, though the cold was remarkable, but because the space was so small that my presence displaced the air, and the air had nowhere to go, and I understood, in that moment, what density means. Not the density of the buildings or the population but the density of lives lived without margin, without slack, without the unused space that an outsider's body assumes will be available.

They did not seem to notice. They sat in their dwelling the way a fish sits in water: not occupying space but belonging to it, the space shaped around them and they shaped to the space, and the channel ran beneath the shelf and the sound of it was the sound of their home, and they were home, and I was not, and the difference was absolute.

The one who speaks to outsiders.

He was not designated for this role. No one in Mossbrug is designated for anything. But he was the one who came to the edge of the hollow when I arrived, emerging from the narrow path that descends through the mudstone, and he was the one who looked at me with an expression that was not hostility and not welcome but assessment — the assessment of a person who has learned to evaluate strangers in terms of what they will require and whether the place can provide it.

He was tall, for Mossbrug. My height, nearly. This marked him as unusual, and I learned later that his height was a source of mild discomfort among the residents — not because they disliked it but because it required accommodation, his head touching the lintels that others passed beneath, his feet too large for the narrowest paths, his body a poor fit for a place built for smaller frames. He moved through Mossbrug with a care that the others did not need, ducking, turning sideways, stepping over gaps that they stepped across without thought. He was, I realized, an outsider in his own town, not by choice but by dimensions, and this was perhaps why he met me at the edge: because he understood what it was to not fit.

He spoke my language. Not well — haltingly, with long pauses where he searched for words he had learned from the rare traders who descended to the hollow and from the few residents who had climbed to the marshplain and returned with fragments of the wider tongue — but he spoke it, and he was patient with my questions, and he answered them with a precision that I came to understand was not for my benefit but for his: he was translating Mossbrug into my language, and the translation required him to see Mossbrug from the outside, which was something he did not often do, and the effort of it was visible in his face, the concentration of a person disassembling something he had always understood whole.

He told me about the moss. He told me about the water. He told me about the rebuilding, and the fire, and the channels, and the children on the rooftops. He told me the things I have written here, and he told them in the flat, uninflected way of a person describing weather to someone who has never experienced it — not because he does not feel the weather but because the weather is so fundamental to his existence that describing it is like describing gravity, and where do you begin, and why would you need to, and isn't it simply obvious?

He walked me to the edge when I left. The same path, the same mudstone, the same transition from wet stone to wet earth to wet grass. At the point where the hollow's wall began to level into the marshplain, he stopped. He did not say goodbye. He said something in his own language — the water-over-stone syllables that I could not parse — and then he turned and descended back into the bowl, and I watched him go, and the moss closed over the path behind him like water closing over a dropped stone, and he was gone, and Mossbrug was gone, and I was standing in the wind on the marshplain with my clothes already beginning to dry, and the drying felt like loss.
