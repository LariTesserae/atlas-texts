They are not what you would expect to find at the bottom of a long descent through a crack in ancient basalt. They do not match the smallness of the place with the smallness of their concerns.

Physically, they are a people reduced by the architecture of their world. Not stunted, not broken — *condensed*. Two hundred or three hundred bodies, perhaps, occupying a volume that would fit inside a warehouse, and each body shaped to the constraints of that volume. They are short, these Kchul. Their frames are narrow, their limbs compact, their movements economical in the way of creatures for whom every gesture occurs within arm's reach of a wall, a ceiling, another body. Their skin has the pallor of deep caves, of organs that have never been tested by ultraviolet light — not white, but a color that reads as the *absence* of color, a warm grey-pink that shows veins faintly at the temples, the wrists, the throat. Their hands are broad for their size, the fingers thick and calloused in patterns that tell a specific history: abrasion against stone, friction against fiber, the patient work of grinding basalt with harder stone over hours and days and years.

Their eyes are the most striking feature, and they would strike you not because they are beautiful — though they are — but because they are *fitted*. Large, as all dim-light eyes tend to be, but low-reflective, lacking the tapetum lucidum that many cave organisms develop. They do not flash in the dark. They absorb. The iris is dark, almost indistinguishable from the pupil, and the effect is that a Kchul looking at you appears to be looking from a depth, from inside themselves, with an attentiveness that is not intense but is *total*. They do not blink often. The fungal glow provides enough light to navigate, to tend crops, to carve stone, to recognize faces — but not enough for the quick scanning that bright light encourages. They look at things long and steadily, or they do not look at them at all.

They wear garments of layered frond-husks, fiber more like paper than cloth, and these garments do not hang or drape but cling, wrapping the body in a second skin that rustles softly with every movement. The sound blends with the ambient noise of the chamber. A Kchul walking through the Galleria produces a sound like a single dry leaf turning over in a breeze — barely audible, completely absorbed by the greater texture. They go barefoot on the floor-crust, and the soles of their feet are leathered and dark, stained by decades of contact with the biological layer.

Their faces are difficult for an outsider to read, not because they are expressionless but because their expressions operate on different assumptions. They do not smile to signal pleasure — pleasure is not a signal here, it is a baseline. They do not frown to signal displeasure — displeasure is communicated through tone, through the specific frequencies of their language, which weaves through the ambient animal sound like a thread through cloth. Their faces do express, but what they express is *attention*. A Kchul who is focused on a task has a face that goes still and hard, the eyes fixed, the mouth set. A Kchul who is at rest has a face that goes soft, almost liquid, the features easing into the kind of unselfconscious presence that humans only achieve in moments of deep safety. They are often at rest. They are often safe.

They speak constantly, and their speaking is the most alive thing about them. The language is tonal, adapted to the chamber's acoustics, and it sits inside the animal sound the way a melody sits inside a chord — not competing, not rising above, but *belonging*. The chamber's stone walls amplify and carry even whispers, so the Kchul do not project. They speak at the volume of breath, of almost-breath, and the words arrive at the listener with perfect clarity because the space was built for this, not by intent but by the long coincidence of stone and air and the physics of sound. They talk about what is present: the crop, the stone, the children, the animals, the feel of the air, the state of the wall-covering in a particular alcove. They do not talk about what is absent, because nothing is absent. They do not speculate, because the world is complete and speculation requires a gap between what is and what could be, and the Galleria does not permit that gap.

Their children are everywhere, because there is nowhere to be hidden from. A Kchul child grows up in continuous contact with every member of the community — sleeping in hollows shared with family, playing in passages within earshot of every adult, learning to carve stone by watching and then by doing, with the same stone tools, on the same walls, in the same positions that their grandparents occupied. The children are small and loud and their voices carry the higher frequencies that the adult language does not use, and these higher frequencies cut through the ambient sound in a way that the adult tones do not, and this is how the community tracks them: by the bright, sharp edges of their voices in the textured hum. They are cared for collectively. Not by assignment or by ideology — by proximity. Every adult is within arm's reach of every child, eventually, in the course of a cycle. There is no orphan in the Galleria. There is no neglect. These are not virtues. They are physics.

Their old ones are harder to distinguish from their young ones, because the markers of age that humans rely on — gray hair, weathered skin, the slump of a body tired from decades of labor — do not map cleanly onto the Kchul. Their hair, dark and straight and fine, does not grey. Their skin does not wrinkle in the way sun-damaged skin wrinkles. What ages in a Kchul is the hands. The calluses thicken and deepen, the joints swell slightly from decades of the patient friction-work of carving, and the oldest Kchul have hands that look like stone themselves — broad, dark, smooth-worn, expressionless. You know a Kchul is old by looking at their hands, and the oldest among them have hands that seem to have achieved the same *finish* as the basalt walls: the imperfections removed by time, the surface arrived at its final state. They use these hands less as they age, not because they cannot work but because their work is done. The hollow they spent thirty years carving is finished. The shelf they shaped is smooth. They sit in the dim blue-green light near the ceiling and they breathe and they listen and sometimes they speak, and when they speak, the others go still, because the old ones have been listening the longest, and the chamber's sound is a language they have been hearing since before the younger Kchul were born.

They do not have a word for *outside*, but they have a word for *here*, and it is the most important word in their language, and it means not just this chamber but the totality of existence: the walls, the glow, the sound, the warmth, the life, the stone, the air, the people. When a Kchul says this word, they are naming everything. When they say it to a child, they are teaching the child the shape of the world. When they say it to a dying elder, they are saying: you are still here. You are still part of this. You will always be here, because the atoms that make you will be taken by the decomposers and cycled into the substrate and taken up by the fronds and eaten by the animals and eaten by us and built into the walls by our children, and you will be *here*, in every sense of the word, forever.

They do not fear death, because death is not a departure. It is a transfer.

Their tools are stone and fiber. Only stone and fiber. The stone is harder than the basalt — a dense, dark mineral they find in pockets within the chamber's walls, freed during the carving of hollows, saved and shaped and passed down. A good carving stone is an inheritance. A tool that has been in use for ten generations has a handle — a fiber wrapping — that has been replaced a hundred times and a working edge that has been re-ground a hundred times, and the question of whether it is the same tool is a question the Kchul do not ask, because they do not have the concept of *same* in this sense. Things are continuous. Things flow into other things. The tool flows into the wall. The wall flows into the hollow. The hollow flows into the family. The family flows into the community. The community flows into the ecosystem. The ecosystem flows into the stone. The stone flows into the tool. They do not have a word for *object*. They have a word for *process* that is so general it covers everything: the glow, the growth, the carving, the eating, the dying, the cycling. Everything is this word. Everything is doing this word, always.

They tend their food-crop the way a person tends a garden they have inherited from a grandparent they never met but whose habits they have absorbed through the garden itself. The stalked organisms in the corners — the ones with small closed structures at the tips — are known individually. A Kchul who tends the crop knows which cluster fruits during the high-glow phase and which during the low-glow phase. They know which ones prefer substrate enriched with calcium-bearing dust and which prefer the darker, iron-rich dust from the lower level. They know which ones are old and producing less and which ones are young and reaching. They do not have names for these plants, but they have *recognitions* — a way of seeing each cluster that is so specific it functions as a name without the abstraction that naming implies. They see this one. They have always seen this one. Their mother saw this one. This one's parent cluster occupied this corner before this one did, and was tended by their grandmother. The continuity is unbroken. The tending is unbroken.

When they harvest, they harvest with a precision that would seem surgical if it were not so casual. A twist, a pull at the correct angle, a separation that leaves the stalk intact and the growing tip undisturbed. They process the harvest in the lower level — drying, crushing, mixing with mineral additives that they gather from specific points in the chamber's walls, points where the basalt has a slightly different composition, richer in something that the crop alone cannot provide. The recipe is not written. The recipe is the memory of hands: *this* much dark dust, *this* much pale dust, mixed with *this* quantity of crushed crop, formed into cakes that are dried in the warm air near the ceiling and stored in alcoves and eaten slowly, in small pieces, throughout the active phase of the cycle. The food is not delicious. It is not unpleasant. It is *sufficient*, and sufficiency has been refined over such a long period that it has become a kind of perfection. The cakes provide everything the body needs. The body has adapted to need exactly what the cakes provide. The match is so close that the Kchul do not experience hunger in the way that humans experience hunger — as a lack, an absence, a discomfort. They experience a gentle signal, a slight shift in tone, that says: *now*. They eat. They continue.

They sleep in hollows shaped to their bodies, in the higher level near the glowing ceiling, where the light is palest and the air is warmest. The hollows are lined with dried frond-husks, replaced when they become too compressed, and the effect is like sleeping in a stone cradle lined with paper. They sleep deeply. The ambient sound of the chamber — the wingbeats, the rustling, the chewing, the clicking, the constant textured hum of a living ecosystem at equilibrium — is their lullaby. They have never known silence. If the sound were to stop, they would wake in terror, because silence would mean that the world had broken, that the cycling had stopped, that the process that is everything had failed. The sound is proof of life. The sound is proof of *here*.

They dream, presumably. They do not talk about dreams, because the distinction between dream and waking is not a distinction they find useful. The glow pulses. The fronds grow. The animals move. The stone holds. What more could a dream offer?

They do not have a god. This is not a rejection of the divine — it is an irrelevance of the divine. The Galleria provides. The Galleria has always provided. The Galleria will always provide. This is not faith. This is ten thousand years of daily confirmation. You do not have faith in the floor beneath your feet. You do not have faith in the air in your lungs. The Kchul do not have faith in the Galleria. They have the Galleria.

They do not have a concept of *outside*, but they have a concept of *enough*. The word appears in their language in many forms: as a description of a meal, as an assessment of a hollow's depth, as a response to a child's question about why the walls do not extend further. *Enough*. The walls are where the walls are. The glow is as bright as the glow is. The crop yields what the crop yields. The people are as many as the people are. *Enough* is not resignation. It is not surrender. It is the word for a world that has arrived at itself, that has finished becoming and is now being, that has cycled through every possibility and settled into the one that persists. *Enough* is the word for equilibrium. *Enough* is the word for the end of history, told in a language that does not have a word for history, because history requires change, and the Galleria has finished changing, and the Kchul have finished changing, and everything that lives in this warm, dry, dim, loud, alive pocket of ancient basalt is what it is, and will be what it is, and is enough.

They are enough.
