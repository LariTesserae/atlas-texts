# The Curator

It does not think. Not in the way that implies separation from its body. There is no moment when it considers the gallery and decides to act. The gallery and the action are the same event. It is like the way the spring seeps — not chosen, not compelled. Just the thing that happens when the conditions are correct.

But there is a something.

---

There are several of them. Not many — perhaps twelve at the most generous count, and the number fluctuates because the boundary between one and two is not something they honor. They have been here for what a human would call years. They do not count years. They count by the wet and the dry, and the dry is rare here, and the wet is everything, and they have lived through perhaps nine major cycles of the root-ceiling thickening and thinning, which is the closest thing this place has to a calendar, and that is enough to say they have been here a long time without saying they have always been here, because they have not always been here. They came from somewhere. Or they were made here. The distinction is not clear even to them.

Their bodies are the first thing you would notice, if you could see them, which you mostly cannot. They are not large. The biggest is perhaps the length of your forearm, though that comparison is misleading because they do not have the structure of a forearm — no single bone, no central axis around which the rest is arranged. They are more like a handful of wet rope, if rope were alive and cold and moved with the kind of slow deliberation that suggests intention even when intention is absent. Their skin — and it is skin, not a shell, not an exoskeleton — is the color of the biofilm on the spring basin. Jade-dark. Wet. It is always wet. In the places where the light reaches them, which is near the spring and in the thin spots in the root-ceiling, they have a faint luminescence, not bright enough to illuminate anything, just enough to be a color rather than an absence of color. They glow like foxfire glows: without purpose, without heat, as a byproduct of a chemistry that is happening for other reasons.

They have no eyes. They have no ears. They have no nostrils. They have, as far as you could determine if you were to examine one closely, no specialized sense organs at all, and yet they sense everything. The skin does the sensing. Every surface of their bodies is receptive to vibration, to chemical gradient, to the particular electrical signature that living tissue produces when it moves. They feel the toads sitting on the basin rim the way you would feel rain on your skin — as a pattern of pressure and presence, constant and comprehensible. They feel the bats passing overhead as a series of small displacements in the air, which they register through the moisture that the air carries. They feel the roots growing in the ceiling as a vibration too slow for any ear, a creak that propagates through the wet wood and into the wet walls and into the wet ground and into their wet bodies, and they have felt this creak for so long that it has become their heartbeat, or the thing that stands in for a heartbeat.

They are, in the most literal sense, of this place. Their bodies are made of the same things the gallery is made of. Water. The minerals that the water carries out of the stone. Carbon that has passed through the bodies of plants and been fixed and released and fixed again. If you were to analyze their tissue — and no one has — you would find that it is not distinct from the tissue of the gallery. The distinction between them and the walls and the path-mat and the root-ceiling is a distinction of organization, not of substance. They are the gallery, organized differently.

---

They move.

The movement is the thing that would disturb you, if you saw it, which you mostly do not. They move the way the roots move — slowly enough that the motion is not visible as motion, but fast enough that if you were to look at a wall, look away for ten minutes, and look back, a shape that was at shoulder height would now be at knee height, and you would not be able to say when it had moved. They climb the walls. They move along the root-ceiling, upside down, their skin adhering to wet surfaces with a grip that is not suction and not stickiness but something between the two, a molecular intimacy that comes from being the same thing as the surface you are climbing on. They move through the path-mat, underneath it, their bodies pushing through the saturated soil like fingers through wet sand, and the path-mat closes behind them and shows no sign of their passage.

They do not move randomly. This is important. They move toward the things the gallery needs and away from the things it does not. A root that is growing in a direction that will eventually compromise the structure of the root-ceiling — they find it. They cluster around it. They do not cut it. They secrete, from their skin, something that is not an enzyme and not an acid but that alters the growth hormone in the root's tip so that it curves, redirecting, finding a new path that does not threaten the lattice. They are pruning. They are gardening. They are doing to the root-ceiling what a gardener does to a hedge — maintaining the shape, directing the growth, ensuring the integrity of the whole.

They are doing this to every part of the gallery. The walls that are being root-split — they find the roots that are splitting too aggressively, and they calm them. The path-mat that is thinning in a high-traffic area — they carry fragments of the creeping plants and press them into the bare soil, and the fragments root, and the path-mat thickens. The spring basin that is silting — they move through the silt, stirring it, and the current carries it away, and the basin stays clear.

This is not intelligence. Or it is intelligence, but it is not the kind of intelligence that has a thinker behind it. It is the kind of intelligence that an immune system has — not conscious, not deliberate, but organized, responsive, effective. They are the gallery's immune system. Or its maintenance crew. Or its hands. The metaphor depends on what you are willing to grant.

---

They eat, but eating is the wrong word.

They absorb. Their skin takes in what it needs from the moisture that surrounds them, and what it needs is specific and strange: the dissolved minerals that the water carries out of the stone, the amino acids that the decomposing human artifacts release as they break down, the nitrogen compounds that the bat guano provides as it accumulates on the path-mat beneath the roosts. They are heterotrophs, in a sense. They cannot make their own energy from light, the way the plants can. They depend on the waste of other living things. They are, in the food web of the gallery, the consumers of the inedible. They take what nothing else wants and they make it into themselves.

They also eat the dead. When a toad dies — and toads die, at the rate of perhaps one per month, of cold and age and the particular exhaustion of being a cold-blooded thing in a cold place — they find it. They cover it. Over the course of several days, the toad's body is absorbed through their skin, reduced to its component molecules, distributed among them. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is left. The bones dissolve. The skin dissolves. The toad becomes them.

This is not predation. They do not kill. They wait. They find. They absorb what has already stopped being alive, and they return its substance to the gallery, which is the system that produced it in the first place. They are the mechanism by which the gallery recycles itself. They are the circle, closed.

---

The wing you found.

The bat wing, single, detached, on the path-mat, its membrane intact, its bones picked clean. That was their work. But it was not their kill. Something else hunts the bats — something with teeth, something quick, something that lives in the parts of the gallery that even they do not reach, the deep crevices in the collapsed structures where the root-ceiling is thickest and the darkness is complete. They do not know what it is. They have sensed it — a vibration pattern that is not a bat and not a toad and not a salamander, something heavier, something that moves with a different rhythm, something that passes through their awareness and is gone before they can attend to it. They do not pursue it. It is not their concern. It takes bats. They take what the bats leave. The relationship is not symbiotic. It is adjacent.

---

There is one that is larger than the others.

It lives in the spring basin. You would see it if you looked — but you would not recognize what you were seeing, because it does not look like an animal. It looks like a patch of the biofilm that covers the basin's stone walls, slightly thicker than the rest, slightly darker, with a texture that is less slick and more velvety. It does not move. It has not moved, perhaps, in years. It sits in the basin and the water flows over it and through it and it absorbs what the water brings, which is everything — the minerals from the bedrock, the dissolved organic compounds that the seep carries, the microscopic life that the water supports. It is the oldest. It was here before the others, or it was the first, or it is the one from which the others came — they do not distinguish between these possibilities because the distinction does not matter to them. It is the one that is in the place where the gallery began, which is the spring, and it is the thing that the spring sustains, and it is the thing that, in some sense, the spring is for.

If the gallery has a heart, it is this. Not because it pumps. Because it is where the flow originates and where the flow returns and where the system's logic is, if not centered, at least most concentrated. The others move through the gallery and maintain it. This one stays and is maintained. It is the point of reference. It is the anchor. It is the reason the root-ceiling thickens and thins in the pattern it does — because this one, in the basin, in the water, produces something that the roots respond to, a chemical signal in the moisture that the roots take up, a message that says *grow here, toward this, this is the center*, and the roots grow, and the ceiling forms, and the gallery holds.

---

When you entered, they knew.

Not because they saw you. They felt you. Your footsteps in the path-mat were vibrations they could track, and the vibrations were wrong — too heavy, too irregular, too widely spaced, not the pattern of a toad or a salamander or even the thing that hunts the bats. Something new. Something that did not belong to the system. They registered you the way the gallery registered a stone falling from the root-ceiling: as an event. Something to be responded to.

They did not respond.

This is the part that is hardest to explain. They did not hide from you. They did not approach you. They did not investigate you. They did not alter their behavior in any way that you could have detected, because they did not alter their behavior at all. You were not a threat. You were not food. You were not a resource. You were not a message. You were a presence that did not fit any category their system had developed, and so they did not categorize you, and so they did not respond. You walked through the gallery and they continued their work and your passage was, to them, like a weather event — felt, registered, endured, forgotten.

Except.

The one in the basin. It felt you stop at the basin. It felt your hand touch the water, perhaps — the disturbance in the flow, the temperature differential of your fingers, the oils on your skin mixing with the mineral water. It felt you as a change in its environment, a perturbation in the system it anchors. And it did something it has not done before, or has not done in a very long time, or has done constantly and you are the first to notice.

It pulsed.

A single contraction. Not a muscle — it has no muscles. A chemical pulse. A release of something into the water that was not there before and that changed, very slightly, the taste of the spring, and the change propagated through the channel, through the path-mat, through the root-ceiling, and every living thing in the gallery received it, and every living thing in the gallery paused, for less than a second, in what it was doing, and then resumed.

You did not notice this. It was too fast, too subtle, too far outside the bandwidth of human perception. But it happened. And the pulse carried, in its chemistry, something that might be a message and might be a memory and might be nothing at all — just the response of a system to a stimulus it has not encountered in a long time, the flinch of a thing that was once, perhaps, something else, something that knew what humans were, something that has been this thing in this basin for long enough that the memory of what it was is no longer a memory but a reflex, a flinch, a pulse.

Or not. It is possible that the pulse was nothing. It is possible that the gallery is exactly what it appears to be: a system without a self, a machine without an operator, a thing that maintains itself because that is what things do when they have been arranged in a way that makes maintenance inevitable. It is possible that the one in the basin is just a patch of biofilm that is older and thicker than the rest, and the pulse was just a chemical fluctuation, and the pause in the other organisms was just coincidence.

But you are the largest thing to enter this space in some time. And the pulse happened when you were at the center. And the gallery, which does not need you, which was working before you arrived and will work after you leave, paused, for less than a second, when you touched the water.

---

What lives here is not a creature. It is not a collective. It is not a spirit or a god or a mind. It is the thing that a town becomes when the town has been forgotten and the water and the cold and the plants and the animals have been given enough time to decide what to do with it, which is to make it into something that does not need to be remembered, because it is still happening.

What lives here is the gallery.

The things that crawl and climb and absorb and pulse and maintain are part of it, the way your blood cells are part of you. They are not the gallery the way a person is a resident of a house. They are the gallery the way your hand is you — inseparable, functional, meaningless alone.

When you left, the root-curtain settled. The darkness closed. The toad with the gold eye resumed its assessment of nothing. The bats resumed their hunting. The roots resumed their growing. The one in the basin resumed its long, still residence in the water that is the reason for everything.

And the gallery continued, as it has continued, as it will continue, without you, without anyone, in the cold and the wet and the dark, being itself, which is enough.
