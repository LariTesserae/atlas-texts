# What Lives Here

## The People

They are smaller than you would expect. Not stunted — never stunted — but *compressed*, the way a hand closes around something precious. Compact bodies, short limbs, deep chests built for sustained swimming in water that resists exhalation with its warmth. Their skin is dark, not from sun (there is no sun here worth naming) but from generations of living in a medium that stains. The mineral water leaves its trace. Their hands and forearms, their lower legs and the tops of their feet, carry a faint patterning — not tattooed, not painted, but *deposited*, the way a stalactite deposits itself over centuries. It deepens with age. The elders carry maps of their own long submersion.

Their fingers are long. Their toes are long. This is a people who grip stone the way their ancestors gripped soil, and the body remembers what the mind has forgotten. They can hold a catwalk edge in a rainstorm, in the dark, without looking, and they know which stones are loose and which are firm, not by sight but by the way vibration travels through their hands and into the architecture of their wrists. Children learn this before they learn to speak. *This stone holds. This stone shifts. This one sings when you press it. This one is dead.*

They do not call themselves anything in particular. There is no outside, no other people to define themselves against. There are only the ones who live on the stone and the things that live in the water. If pressed, they might say *the ones who stayed*, but this would be imprecise. They did not stay. There was nowhere to go. What they did was *continue*, and the word they use for this — a word that sounds like stone settling, like a breath released after holding it for a very long time — means something closer to *the ones who became what the place needed them to become*.

## Their Days

Theirs is a life lived in two dimensions. The rooftops are their ground. The water is their below. And the sky — what there is of it — is their ceiling, but only barely, and only sometimes, and only when the rain comes through.

They wake to the grey. It arrives slowly, the way everything arrives here, and it finds them in their upper rooms in nests of woven fiber that they suspend from ceiling hooks, above the floor, above the damp that rises even here. They sleep in family clusters. The warmth is natural. The stone holds the heat from below and the bodies hold the heat from the stone and the whole arrangement is a closed system that has been running, without interruption, for longer than anyone can count. There are no calendars. There are no years. There is the rain that comes once a month, and there is the dry that comes between, and that is enough.

The grey lightens, and they move.

The first sound of the day is always the same. It is the sound of the birds shifting — a collective resettling, a rustle of stolen cloth and bone, a brief percussion of beaks against stone. The birds wake before the people. The birds wake before the light. The birds are always first.

The people know this. They wait for it. You do not begin your day before the birds begin theirs, because the birds are the first ones to know if the water has changed in the night, and if the birds are agitated, you stay inside. You stay warm. You wait.

When the birds are calm, the people emerge onto the rooftops, and the day begins.

It begins with the swimmers.

## Catching

The swimmers come to the surface when the light changes. Not because the light calls them — they have no eyes, and the light means nothing to them — but because the warmth shifts with the light, and the warmth is their map. The surface water warms before the deep water, and the swimmers follow the warmth upward, and they congregate in the places where the old streets narrow and the current between buildings speeds up and brings food.

The people know these places. Every rooftop dweller knows them the way you know the rooms of your own house. *This canal, between this building and this one, at this warmth, there will be swimmers.* They lower their nets from the rooftop edges, working by feel, by the vibration of the net cords in the current, by the small tap-tap-tap of swimmers hitting the mesh. The nets are woven from a fiber that comes from the pale growth itself — dried, twisted, cured in the warm air until it holds its shape. The nets last perhaps a month. They make new ones constantly. This is not a burden. This is a rhythm.

Some swim. The younger ones, the ones whose lungs are deepest, slip into the water from the rooftop edges and dive to the second-floor doorways, where the swimmers shelter in the shadow of the lintels. They catch them by hand. This is a skill that takes years to learn — the swimmers feel the heat of a body in the water and they flee from it, fast and precise, and to catch one you must be colder than the water around you, which means you must hold yourself still, very still, until your body temperature drops below the threshold of their alarm, and then you move. Quick. Clean. You surface with a swimmer in each hand and you hand them up to the one waiting on the roof edge, and you go back down.

The elders do not swim. Not because they cannot — some of them swim better than the young — but because the elders have other work, and because the water taxes them more than it taxes the young. The mineral content builds in the joints over years. The warmth that is a comfort at twenty becomes an irritation at fifty, a stiffness at sixty. The elders stay above. They mend. They watch the birds. They keep the knowledge that does not require a body in the water.

## Eating

They eat the swimmers raw. Not from lack of fire — though fire is rare here, nearly impossible, the air too damp, the fuel too scarce — but because the swimmers are better raw. Their flesh is translucent, dark at the center, and it carries a faint heat, a mineral warmth, that dissipates if you cook it. The people have tried, over the generations. Drying. Salting (there is no salt, but there is the mineral crust that forms at the waterline, and it serves). None of it improves the swimmer. The swimmer is complete as it is.

They eat the pale growth, too. They harvest it from the lower walls, swimming down to the second floor, the third, prying it loose from the stone with tools of bone — a jawbone, sharpened, the teeth serving as a saw. It comes away in sheets. They bring it up and dry it on the rooftops, in the grey light, and it crisps into something translucent and papery that tastes of stone and warmth and nothing else. It is not filling. It is not pleasant. But it sustains. The body needs what it offers, and the body takes it without complaint.

The crabs are eaten when they can be caught, which is rarely, and only by the quick. The children catch them sometimes, pouncing on them at the waterline, laughing — the only laughter you hear here — as the crab's legs scramble between their fingers. The crabs are cracked open and the meat is sweet and warm and there is a moment of childish triumph that the adults watch from above with expressions that are not quite smiles but that hold the same content.

They do not eat the birds. This is not a rule. There is no rule. It is a recognition. The birds watch the water so that the people do not have to. To eat the birds would be to blind yourself.

## What They Know

They know the city. Not the city that was — the city of streets and doorways and carved decoration — but the city that is. They know every building that still stands above the waterline. They know which roofs hold and which roofs sag. They know the catwalks — every span, every stone, every join — and they know which ones need mending and which ones can wait. They know the buildings by their birds. The building with thirty nests is the one they call *many-wings*. The building with none is the one they call *empty-heart*. The building where the deep-water growth has crept up to the second floor is the one they call *old-before-old*.

They have no written language. They do not need one. What needs to be known is known in the body — in the hands, in the feet, in the lungs, in the temperature sense that tells you where the swimmers are. What needs to be passed on is passed on in the doing. You learn to catch swimmers by catching swimmers. You learn to read the birds by watching the birds. You learn the catwalks by walking the catwalks, and if you fall, you swim, and you climb back up, and you walk them again.

The elders know things that the young do not. They know the way the water moves at different temperatures. They know when the deep growth is restless — it shifts, sometimes, the patches of it expanding and contracting over weeks, and the elders can feel this in the way the water tastes and the way the birds hold their heads. They know the tone that comes from the deep canals — the resonant call that you hear on the quietest nights — and they say it is the voice of whatever lives in the open squares. They do not say what that thing is. They do not speculate. They say: it is there. It has its place. We have ours.

They know the rain. They know it before it comes — a change in the air, a thickness, a stillness in the birds that is different from their ordinary stillness. The rain is an event. When it comes, they stand on the rooftops and they let it wash them. They open their mouths. They fill every container they have. They set out the trays of tough plants and let them drink their fill, and the plants swell visibly, their small leaves opening for the first time in weeks, and for a day afterward they are green, actually green, and the color is so unfamiliar here that it seems like a memory.

The rain passes. The grey returns. The swimmers reform. The birds settle. The murmur resumes.

## What They Are

They are not sad.

This is important to understand. There is no tragedy here. There was a tragedy, perhaps, long ago, when the water came and the streets filled and the lower floors were lost. But the people who live here now do not remember that tragedy, and their parents did not remember it, and their parents' parents did not remember it, and the memory of it is as submerged as the first floors of the buildings they live in. It is down there. It exists. It does not trouble them.

They are not content, either. Contentment implies a satisfaction that requires a comparison — this is better than that, this is preferable to what was — and they have no comparison. They have never known anything else. The world is the rooftops and the water and the birds and the swimmers and the grey and the dark and the glow. The world is the warmth that rises from below and the silence that settles over everything like a second kind of light. The world is the rain that comes once a month and the dry that comes between. This is the world. It is not a good world or a bad world. It is the world.

What they are is *present*. They are here. They live in the place where they live, in the way that the place allows, and they do not strain against it. They do not dream of dry ground. They do not wish for the sun. If you asked them — and you would have to ask in their language, which has no word for *wish* in the way you mean it — they would tell you that the water is the water and the stone is the stone and the birds are the birds and they are the ones who live above the water and below the sky, and this is what they are, and it is enough.

It is enough.

## The Birds

They are the ones who were here first. Or if not first, then before. They came when the water came, or they came after the water came but before the people found their way to the rooftops, and they have been here ever since. They are not visitors. They are not migrants. This is their home in a way that the people, with their ancestral memory of dry streets, can never quite match.

They stand four feet tall. Their legs are black and their plumage is grey-brown, the color of the stone when it is wet, and they are still. They are so still that the first time you see one, you will think it is a statue. You will think someone has carved a bird from the same stone as the buildings and set it on the roof edge as a decoration. And then it will move — the head, a quarter-turn, a motion so fast and so fluid that it seems to exist outside of time, to have happened in the space between one heartbeat and the next — and you will understand that it was never still at all. It was waiting. It is always waiting.

They do not sleep. Or if they do, they sleep with one eye open, one leg locked, one part of themselves always watching the water. They are the watchmen. The people know this. When a bird on a rooftop turns its head toward the water and holds it there, the people stop what they are doing. They wait. The bird holds its pose. And if the bird relaxes — settles its feathers, shifts its weight, turns its head away — the people resume. If the bird lifts, they retreat. If the bird strikes, the people come to the edge to watch.

The strike is fast. The neck unfolds like a whip cracking, and the beak enters the water and comes out with a swimmer wriggling, and the bird swallows it whole, and it is over, and the bird is still again. The whole motion takes less time than a breath. It is the fastest thing that happens in the Drowned Quarter. It is the only fast thing.

The birds are not tame. They are not domesticated. They are not companions. They tolerate the people the way the water tolerates the stone — by necessity, by proximity, by the logic of two things occupying the same space for long enough that conflict becomes more expensive than coexistence. The people do not feed the birds. The birds do not approach the people. They exist in parallel, watching the same water, living on the same stone, and the distance between them is constant and understood.

When a bird dies — and they do die, of age, of illness, of the deep cold that sometimes creeps up from the open squares — the others gather around it. They stand in a circle. They do not move for hours. The people do not disturb them. The people know what this is. It is not grief, exactly. It is *acknowledgment*. The bird was. The bird watched. The bird is gone. The circle holds for as long as it holds, and then it breaks, and the birds return to their perches, and the watching resumes.

The people bury the dead birds. Not in the water — the water would take them. In the rooftop trays, beneath the plants, in the shallow soil that they have carried up over generations. The birds become soil. The plants grow from them. The cycle is small and complete.

## What Lives Below

The swimmers do not think. They do not need to. They are a response — a biological response to warmth and to chemical gradient and to the presence of food. They exist in the space between the wall growth and the birds, and their entire existence is a transit from one to the other. They feed on the growth. They are fed on by the birds. They are the middle of something, and they do not know it.

But they are alive. You can feel it when you hold one — the body warm in your hand, the tail flicking, the dark flesh pulsing with a heartbeat that is faster than yours and less complicated. It wants to live. It does not know that it wants to live. But it wants to live.

The growth on the walls is alive, too. It is not a plant. It is not an animal. It is something between — something that builds itself from dissolved mineral and from the warmth that feeds up through the stone, and it does not move and it does not think and it does not want. It *is*. It has been there for longer than the people have been on the rooftops. It has been there, perhaps, for longer than the buildings have been standing. It was there when the city was dry. It was there, in the volcanic cracks below, feeding on the heat and the mineral, and when the water came it rose with the water, and it found the walls, and it grew.

In the deep rooms — the third floor, the fourth, the ones you must dive to reach — it is thicker. It is darker. It has a quality that the shallow growth does not: a slight movement, a pulse, as if something within it is breathing. The people do not go there. The birds do not go there. The swimmers do not go there. It is not forbidden. It is not dangerous. It is simply *other*, and the other has its own territory, and the territory is below.

In the open squares, where the water goes dark and the current shifts and the buildings stand farther apart, something larger moves. The people have seen it. They have seen it from the rooftops on nights when the glow from below is bright enough to reveal it — a displacement, a slow turning, a shadow within the glow. It is not the growth. It is not a swimmer. It is large enough that when it passes, the swimmers scatter and the birds lift and the water itself seems to hold its breath.

The elders say it is old. Not old as the city is old. Old as the stone is old. Old as the volcanic shelf. It was here before anything was here, and it stayed when the water came, and it grew with the water, and it lives in the dark where the warmth is deepest, and it does not come to the buildings because it does not need what the buildings offer. It has the deep. It has the dark. It has the warmth that rises from the center of things.

It does not threaten. It does not call. It exists.

The resonant tone that comes on the quietest nights — the one that the elders speak of — comes from there. Or from it. Or from the deep itself. It is not a voice. It is not language. It is a vibration that passes through the water and through the stone and through the air and through the people standing on the rooftops, and for a moment, in the moment of its passing, everything in the Drowned Quarter is the same thing — the water and the stone and the bird and the swimmer and the person and the growth and the deep and the dark — and then it passes, and they are separate again, and the silence returns.

---

## What They Are Together

The Drowned Quarter is not a place that contains lives. It is a life that contains a place. The people and the birds and the swimmers and the growth and the water and the stone and the deep and the dark — they are not in the Drowned Quarter. They *are* the Drowned Quarter. They are the organs of a single body that has been breathing, slowly, for longer than anyone can remember, and the breath is the warmth that rises from below, and the heartbeat is the murmur of the water, and the voice is the tone that comes from the deep, and the eyes are the birds that watch from the rooftops, and the hands are the people who move across the stone with the careful, worn precision of those who have been doing this forever.

They do not know this. The people do not think of themselves as part of something larger. They think of themselves as people, living on stone, above water, among birds. And the birds think of themselves as birds (if they think at all), standing on stone, watching water, among people. And the swimmers think of nothing, and the growth thinks of nothing, and the deep thing in the open squares thinks — if it thinks — in a way that has no word in any language that exists here.

But together, they are the Drowned Quarter, and the Drowned Quarter is them, and it has been this way for long enough that no one remembers when it was anything else, and it will be this way for long enough that no one will remember when it changes.

The water is patient. The stone is patient. The birds are watching. The dark is full of small lives. And the people move softly across the tops of the buildings, and they do not go down, and they do not leave, and this is their home.
