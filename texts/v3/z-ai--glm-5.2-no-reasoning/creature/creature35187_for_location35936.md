# What Lives Here

---

Let me be careful, because the first answer is the wrong one.

The first answer is: nothing. Nothing *lives* here. The place is defined by absence — no fish, no invertebrates, no fauna of any kind. Just plants and stone and water in a closed loop. An ecosystem without consumers. A world without mouths.

That is the answer the place would give you if you asked it. But the place would be wrong, or at least incomplete, in the way that a body is wrong or incomplete when it says *I am only bone.* The bone is not the whole story. The bone is what remains when the rest is taken, and what remains can tell you what was there, if you know how to listen, and if you are willing to listen to things that do not speak in words.

---

## The Plants

They are alive. That is the second answer, and it is the true one, but it is true in a way that requires adjustment.

The frond-trees are the obvious life. Tall, slender, swaying. Their broad flat appendages spread like dark parasols to catch the mineral upwellings, and their edges glow that cold blue-green, the byproduct of chemosynthesis, purposeless and beautiful. They rub against each other in the current and produce the drone. They are the dominant organism by any measure — by mass, by coverage, by the sheer scale of their presence across the plateau tops and channel walls. They are what you see, or what you would see if you could see. They are what you hear.

But calling them the *life* of the place assigns them a role they did not ask for. They are not performing life. They are not standing in for the missing animals, providing the vitality that the absent fauna would have provided. They are simply doing what plants do: growing toward the nutrients, metabolizing, reproducing, dying, decomposing. The fact that they are the *only* things doing this does not elevate them. It does not make them protagonists. They are not the characters of this story. They are the medium.

The frond-trees do not know they are alone. This is important. They have no concept of *alone.* They do not experience the absence of animals as a loss. There was never a loss. There was never a before. The loop has always been closed. The frond-trees grow because the minerals rise and the water is cold and the stone is dissolving, and they die because they die, and they decompose because the water and the remaining biomass accept what they give, and the next generation grows because the minerals rise again. The frond-trees do not experience this cycle. They *are* this cycle. They are the living portion of a process that does not require experiencing.

And yet.

The frond-trees rub against each other. Millions of them, swaying, their fibrous trunks creating friction that translates into vibration that translates into the drone. This is not communication. It is not language. It is not even signaling. It is physics. But it is a sound, and the sound fills the water, and the water fills the space, and the space is not empty because the sound is in it. The frond-trees are making the place *audible.* They are making the place *present* to itself, in the only way a place can be present to itself: through resonance. The drone is the place hearing its own existence, over and over, sustaining the note, never resolving.

Is that life? Is that what lives here? A sound without a listener, a chord that is its own audience?

It is something. Let me not dismiss it. Let me hold it as one of the answers and keep looking.

---

The fibrous mat beneath the frond-trees is also alive. Also dying. Also being born. The text says this clearly: *alive and dead and being born, all at once, all in the same handful of material.* The mat is the substrate, the foundation, the ground. It is what the frond-trees root in. It is what holds the sediment. It is the primary structure of the ecosystem — not the visible structure, not the dramatic structure, but the structural structure, the thing without which nothing else would hold.

The mat does not glow. It does not sway. It does not produce sound. It is the silence beneath the drone. It is the floor of the world, and it is alive, and it is the most alive thing here, because it is the place where life and death and birth are happening simultaneously, continuously, without separation. The frond-trees are alive or dead — individuals, with stems, with edges that glow. The mat is alive *and* dead *and* being born. It is the only thing here that holds all three states at once.

If the frond-trees are the medium, the mat is the message. Not a message *about* anything. Not a message *to* anything. Just: this is what life looks like when it is not separated from death. This is what the ground is when the ground is also the growing.

---

The vine-form and the sphere-form and the crust-form — they live here too. They are variations on the theme. The vine drapes and sways. The sphere rolls and accumulates. The crust grows in mineral gradients. They are minor characters, not because they are unimportant but because the ecosystem does not have major and minor characters. It has participants. The loop is the loop, and every participant is equally necessary, which means none of them is necessary at all, which means the loop is not a system of dependence but a system of *inclusion.* Everything is included. Nothing is excluded. The loop does not choose.

This is different from an animal ecosystem, where exclusion — predation, competition, die-off — is the engine of change. Here, there is no exclusion. There is only accumulation. The biomass on the channel walls has been building for so long that the older growth has become substrate. The dead are not removed. They are built upon. This is a place where death is not an ending but a foundation, and everything that has ever lived here is still here, in some form — dissolved into the water, woven into the mat, compressed into the biomass layers on the channel walls. The place is a library of its own past, and the past is not archived, not stored, not set aside. The past is *used.* The past is the material from which the present is constructed.

---

## The Former Builders

Now. The difficult part.

There are stone structures on the highest plateau. Small, chest-height or less, built from the sandstone of the mesas, fitted without mortar. There are tools. A grinding stone, a cutting edge, a shaped weight. The technology is stone-age — literal stone-age, worked with skill but with limited materials. The builders are gone. No bones, no preserved remains. The water and the vegetation have consumed everything organic.

The question is: do they still live here?

The text says they are gone. The text is clear. But the text also says that the structures remain, and the tools remain, and the vegetation has colonized them — the fibrous mat growing over the walls, the frond-trees rooting in the corners. The stone is being slowly colonized by the crust-form. The mesas are swallowing them.

The builders are gone, but the builders are *here.* Not as ghosts. Not as memory. Not as anything spiritual or mystical. The builders are here as stone and as the shape of stone. The walls they built are stone, the same stone as the mesas, and the stone is dissolving into the water, and the water feeds the plants, and the plants grow on the structures and on the mesa surfaces alike, and the minerals from the shaped stone — the stone that was *chosen* and *moved* and *fitted* — enter the same loop as everything else.

The builders' labor has entered the loop. The shaped stone is dissolving. The minerals from the shaped stone are rising in the upwellings, or they are being absorbed by the crust-form on the walls, or they are filtering through the fibrous mat. The atoms that the builders touched, that the builders shaped, that the builders arranged into walls and tools — those atoms are now part of the vegetation. Part of the frond-trees. Part of the drone. Part of the glow.

This is not reincarnation. This is not persistence of soul. This is chemistry. The builders have been consumed by the loop, and the loop does not distinguish between the stone of the mesa and the stone of the wall. But the *shape* of the wall persists, for now. The shape is still recognizable. The shape says: *something was here that was not the loop. Something shaped the stone. Something had hands.*

The shape is fading. In a few thousand years, it will be gone. The crust-form and the mat will have erased the last evidence that anything other than plants ever lived here. The loop will be closed, fully, with no seam.

But for now — for these few thousand years, which is nothing to the loop and everything to the question — the builders live here. They live here as the shape of stone. They live here as the objects they touched still lying where they set them down. They live here as the absence that the structures describe. They live here as the only *event* in the long, unbroken *process* of the place. Everything else is cycle. The builders were *narrative.* They had a before and a during and an after. The structures are the after. The tools are the during. The before is gone.

Do they live here? They live here the way a scar lives in skin. The tissue is alive, but the scar is the record of something that happened to the tissue. The scar is not the wound. The scar is the *healing* of the wound. The structures are the healing of the builders' presence — the loop slowly closing over the interruption, the vegetation growing over the stone the way skin grows over a cut.

They live here as a healing. They live here as the place's slow recovery from the fact that something with hands once existed.

---

## The Water

The water lives here.

I know this is a strange thing to say. Water does not live. Water is the medium in which life occurs, the substance that life contains, the thing that life drinks and breathes and floats in. Water is not alive.

But this water is old. This water has been here for so long that "the flood, if it was a flood, is so far in the past that the concept of 'before the flood' has no living witness, not even in the fossil layer." This water has been circulating through the stone, rising in the upwellings, feeding the plants, absorbing the decomposition, carrying the minerals back down. The water has been the loop. Not part of the loop — *the loop itself.* The water is the medium in which the stone dissolves and the plants grow and the plants die and the minerals rise. The water is the connective tissue. The water is what makes it a loop rather than a line.

And the water moves. Not just currents — upwellings, warm fingers threading upward through the cold, carrying dissolved minerals. The water has *behavior.* It has patterns. It surges on a cycle connected to something deeper, something tectonic or thermodynamic. The surges are the closest thing this place has to weather, and the weather is the water's weather. The water is not passive. The water is not a backdrop. The water is an actor — the primary actor, the one that carries the minerals and the warmth and the chemistry, the one that touches every surface, the one that is every surface, because the water is inside the stone and inside the plants and inside the mat and inside the biomass layers. The water is the inside of everything.

The water is the place's body. Not its soul — the place does not have a soul, does not have a center, does not have a *self.* But the place has a body, and the body is the water, and the water is alive in the way that a body is alive: not conscious, not experiencing, but *functioning.* Circulating. Carrying. Delivering. Removing. Maintaining temperature. Maintaining chemical balance. The water is the physiology of the Drowned Mesas, and the physiology has been running for longer than anything can remember, because there is nothing here to remember.

The water does not live. The water is what *living* is made of, here. The water is the *of.*

---

## The Drone

I keep coming back to the sound.

The drone is not alive. The drone is a byproduct — friction between trunks, vibration through water, the acoustics of a dense medium carrying the summed frequencies of millions of independent oscillations. It is physics. It is no more alive than the hum of a power line or the rumble of a waterfall.

But the drone is the thing that the place *does.* It is the place's action, its verb. The place breathes — that is the metaphor the text uses, and it is a metaphor, because places do not breathe, and plants do not breathe in this way, and the drone is not respiration. But the drone is the place *producing.* Producing sound. Producing vibration. Producing a presence that fills the water and enters the bones and the teeth and the skull. The drone is the place making itself known. Not to anyone. Not for anyone. Just: known. Present. Audible.

The drone changes. The current shifts, the trees sway at different frequencies, the harmonics change. "It is never the same chord twice." This is the closest thing here to *experience* — not the experience of a being, but the experience of a *state.* The place is in a state, and the state changes, and the change is recorded in the sound. The drone is the record. The drone is the place's memory, not stored, not recalled, but *sounded.* Continuously. Without gap. The memory is not of the past. The memory is *of now,* and it is overwritten continuously, and it is always now, and the drone is always now.

When the upwellings surge, the drone swells. The frond-trees sway more violently, the friction increases, the glow brightens. The place *crescendos.* This is not a response. This is not a reaction. This is the place being more intensely *itself.* The surge is not an event that happens *to* the place. The surge is the place, in a different state. The drone in surge is the place at its loudest, its brightest, its most active. It is the place *present* to itself at maximum intensity.

Is that life? Is that what lives here?

I think it is. I think the drone is the life of the place, not because it is produced by living things — though it is — and not because it serves a purpose — it does not — but because it is the place's *presence.* The drone is the place existing. The drone is the place saying *I am here,* except there is no *I* and there is no *here* and there is no *saying.* There is just the sound, and the sound is the place, and the place is the sound, and it has been this way for so long that the sound and the place are the same thing.

---

## What Lives Here

What lives here is the loop.

Not the plants. Not the water. Not the stone. Not the absent builders. Not the drone. The *loop* — stone to water to plant to stone, the cycle that has been running since before the concept of animal existed, the closed system that does not need anything from outside, that does not want anything, that does not miss anything.

The loop lives here the way a heartbeat lives in a chest. The heartbeat is not the heart. The heartbeat is not the blood. The heartbeat is the *event* — the recurring, rhythmic, sustaining event that means the system is running. The loop is the heartbeat of the Drowned Mesas. It has been beating for longer than anything can measure. It does not know it is beating. It does not know anything. It is simply running, the way a heart runs, the way a river runs, the way time runs.

And what lives here, truly, is the *consequence* of the loop. The frond-trees are the consequence. The mat is the consequence. The biomass on the channel walls is the consequence. The glow is the consequence. The drone is the consequence. The old stone structures being slowly consumed are the consequence. The cold, the dark, the mineral upwellings — consequences. The place is the consequence of a loop that has been running for so long that the consequences have become the landscape.

What lives here is a process that has become a place.

What lives here is a place that has become a sound.

What lives here is a sound that has become a light — cold, blue-green, purposeless, pulsing on a cycle that might be minutes long, might be longer — and the light traces the edges of the frond-trees against the black, and the patterns shift and recombine, and it is beautiful, and nothing sees it, and it does not matter that nothing sees it, because the beauty is not for seeing. The beauty is the byproduct. The beauty is what happens when a loop runs long enough that its consequences begin to rhyme.

What lives here is the rhyme.

---

The builders, if they could see it now — if they could descend into the cold and the dark and stand on the highest plateau beside their colonized structures and look out across the expanse of frond-trees and their glowing edges — the builders would say: *this is not what we made.* And they would be right. This is not what they made. This is what was already here. They were the interruption. The loop was the whole story before them, and the loop is the whole story after them, and the loop will be the whole story when the last wall has been consumed and the last tool has been colonized and the last trace of shaping has been erased by the crust-form and the mat.

But the builders *were* here. The loop includes them, the way the loop includes the stone and the water and the plants. The builders are in the loop now. Their minerals are rising in the upwellings. Their labor is dissolving. Their hands are in the water, in the frond-trees, in the drone, in the glow. They are part of the consequence. They have been absorbed.

What lives here includes what has died here. What lives here includes what was shaped and what was built and what was set down on a shelf inside a structure and left. What lives here includes the absence of the builders as a shape — the shape of the walls, the shape of the doorways, the shape of the tools. The absence has a shape because the builders had hands, and the hands made forms, and the forms are still here, and the forms describe the builders the way a mold describes the object that was pressed into it.

What lives here is a mold with nothing pressed into it anymore. What lives here is a negative space. What lives here is the shape of *was.*

And what lives here is the slow, patient, indifferent filling-in of that shape by the fibrous mat, by the crust-form, by the roots of the frond-trees. What lives here is the healing of the only wound this place ever received — the wound of being touched by something that was not the loop.

---

So. What lives in the Drowned Mesas?

The plants. The water. The drone. The loop. The stone, dissolving. The builders, as shape and as mineral and as absence. The cold, which is not alive but which is the condition of life here. The dark, which is not alive but which is the medium of the glow. The glow, which is the byproduct of the process that is the place.

And the *hum.* The hum that has been here longer than the builders, longer than the structures, longer than the current arrangement of frond-trees and mats and biomass layers. The hum that was here before the loop was a loop, when it was just a process, before the process had run enough times to wear a groove, before the groove became a channel, before the channel became a circuit, before the circuit became a *loop.* The hum that is the sound of the loop running, the sound of the place being, the sound of the oldest thing here.

The hum lives here. The hum is what lives here. Everything else is the hum's body — its frond-trees, its water, its stone, its cold, its slow and beautiful and purposeless light.

The hum lives here, and it does not know it lives, and it does not need to know, and it will live here until the loop breaks, and there is nothing here that can break it, and so it will live here until the world changes, and the world does not change here, and so it will live here forever, in the dark, in the cold, humming the only song it knows, a song that is never the same twice, a song that no one hears, a song that fills the water like a choir sustaining a single chord that never resolves.
