# What Lives Here

## The people (first)

They are not a population. They are not a civilization. They are not a culture, exactly. They are more like a load-bearing element of the structure itself — another material in the composite, like the crushed slag and fired clay, like the moss and the mortar.

Call them the terrace-keepers, though they don't call themselves anything. They don't need to. There is no outside to define themselves against.

There are perhaps three thousand of them. Maybe four. It is hard to count because they are distributed across the middle terraces in a pattern that looks scattered but isn't — it follows the water, the grain, the shade. Where the condensation is heaviest and the soil deepest and the walls most weathered into livable hollows, there are more of them. Where the terraces narrow and the wind cuts harder and the moss gives way to bare brick, there are fewer. They have settled the way moss settles: where the conditions are right.

A typical life: you are born in a shallow room cut into a retaining wall, dark and cool, with a door of iron and wood that your grandmother's hands have worn smooth at the handle. You grow up on the terrace outside that door, which is also your kitchen, your workshop, your road, your courtyard, and your roof, depending on where you stand and what time of day it is. You learn to climb stairs before you learn to walk properly, because everything here is stairs. You learn the sound of the engines — the one-second thud — as a kind of heartbeat, and when you leave the middle terraces for the first time, ascending to where the air thins and the cloud sits, or descending to where the steam rises and the engines breathe their hot, oily breath, the absence of that thud is the first thing you notice. It feels like leaving a room where someone was breathing.

You learn the grain. Everyone learns the grain. It is not a specialty. It is what the terraces are for. The tall grass with its heavy seed-heads — a crop that was bred here, or bred itself here, or was bred somewhere else so long ago that the distinction has rotted away — it grows in the shallow soil on the wider terraces, and tending it is the work that organizes the year. Planting is done when the condensation pattern shifts, which is to say when the season changes, though the seasons here are not winter and summer but wet and less-wet, warm and less-warm. Harvest is a terrace-by-terraced affair, not a single event. The lower terraces ripen first because they are warmer. The upper terraces ripen later because they are cooler and damper. So harvest is not a moment. It is a wave that moves upward through the structure over weeks, and the people move with it — families migrating up through their own neighborhood, reaping, threshing, drying, storing, each terrace passing its grain to the one above as the work climbs.

You learn the water. The water is not plumbing. The water is weather. You learn to read the drainage channels the way a sailor reads the sea — which channels run fast after rain, which trickle constantly with condensation, which have been choked with moss and need clearing. You learn which walls are wet to the touch in the morning and dry by afternoon, which drip all day, which never drip at all. You learn to place a clay jug under a reliable drip and collect a liter of water overnight, and you learn which drips are clean and which have passed through too much moss and taste of tannin and earth.

You learn the engines, or you don't. Some people are drawn to them. They descend to the lower terraces as teenagers and don't come back up for years, or they come back up with grease on their hands and a particular way of listening — head tilted, feeling the vibration through the floor, judging whether the piston is seating properly or whether the crankshaft needs shimming. The engine-keepers are not a caste. They are a temperament. You know one when you see one: they are the people who walk past you and pause, mid-sentence, because they heard something in the thud that you didn't hear. Then they continue their sentence and keep walking. They maintain the machines the way the others maintain the grain — with routine, with competence, without drama. When a piston wears out, they cast a new one in the foundry on the lowest terrace, and the foundry is itself a structure that has been recast and repaired and rebuilt so many times that no one knows if any of its original bricks are still in place. The engine-keepers don't care. The engine runs. That is what matters.

You learn the moss. This is less literal. The moss is the sign that everything is working. When the moss is thick and green and beaded with water, the cycle is intact: the engines are pumping, the condensation is rising, the water is settling, the channels are flowing. When the moss dries — which it does, sometimes, in the less-wet seasons, on the terraces that get the most sun — it is not an emergency. It is a reminder. The moss tells you where the water is and where it isn't, and you adjust. You clear a channel. You patch a pipe. You wait. The moss comes back.

You eat grain. You eat the small bright fruit that the birds usually get to first, which means you have learned to be faster than the birds or to accept that the birds get the best fruit and you get the rest, and this is fine. You eat the goat cheese — sharp, dense, made from the milk of the sure-footed animals that the lower-terrace families keep. You eat the things that grow in the composted gaps in the walls: small, bitter greens that have colonized the humus and are harvested without ceremony. You cook on a clay stove on the terrace outside your door, and the smell of your cooking joins the smell of everyone else's cooking and the smell of the grain and the warm clay and the wet iron until the air is a single, layered thing that is not a smell but the *atmosphere* of the place.

You wear clothes in the palette of the terraces. Not because you decided to. Because the dyes are made from the plants that grow here — the ochre of the clay, the rust of the iron, the green of the moss, the yellow of the grain stalks — and so the clothes are the same color as the walls and the ground and the moss and the birds and the fruit and everything else. You blend in. You have always blended in. You are not hiding. You are *matching*.

You do not leave. There is no edge to leave from. If you walk far enough along any terrace, you come to a junction that takes you to another terrace, and from there to another, and the place does not end. It is regional in extent. It is your region. It is the only one you know.

You do not wonder what is beyond the cloud at the top. You do not wonder what is below the steam at the bottom. You are in the middle, where the living is, and the middle is where you stay.

## The engine-keepers

Down where the steam sits thickest and the terraces are widest and the walls between them are at their tallest — down where the heat comes from — there are people who are different from the others only in the way that a joiner is different from a baker. Not better, not apart. Different work.

The engines are larger than the terrace-keepers' houses. Each one is a building — or is inside a building, or *is* the building; the distinction collapsed long ago. They are Stirling engines, or close enough. Concentrated sunlight and the combustion of dried grain stalks and the woody shrubs heat one side; the pumped water cools the other; and the differential drives a crankshaft that turns a piston that pushes water up through the iron pipes that run along the undersides of the overhangs, up and up and up, through hundreds of terrace-levels, until the water reaches the condensation traps at the top and the cycle begins again.

The engine-keepers live in the same buildings as the engines. Their rooms are warmer than the middle terraces — warmer in a different way, a drier way, a way that smells of hot metal and grain dust and the particular acrid smoke of the furnaces. They are the people who tend the furnaces. They feed the fire with dried stalks. They clean the ash. They adjust the reflectors that concentrate sunlight — great curved sheets of polished metal, pitted with age, mounted on pivots of iron that creak when they are moved. The reflectors track the sun, or rather, the engine-keepers track the sun by moving the reflectors, a few times a day, by hand. The mechanism is manual because it has always been manual, and manual is sufficient, and sufficient is what matters.

They are not lonely. The engine-levels are populated — not densely, but enough. The goat-keepers are down here too, because the goats need warmth and the engine-terraces are warm. The foundry is here, where the pistons are cast. The grain mills are here, where the tall grass is crushed and the oil is pressed from the seeds, because these are the machines that are too heavy for human hands and need the engines' power. So the lower terraces are a district — the industrial district, if you must, though no one calls it that. It is just where the engines are. It is just where the work is heavy.

The engine-keepers have a particular dialect. It is not a separate language. It is the same language shaped by the noise — they speak louder, down here, because the engines are loud, and their speech has a rhythm to it, a one-second cadence that matches the thud, so that they speak *between* the beats, filling the gaps in the engine's pulse. If you come down from the middle terraces, it takes a while to tune your ear. The engine-keepers don't notice it. They think *you* speak strangely.

## The children

The children are the same everywhere, which is to say they are children. They climb things they shouldn't climb. They chase the lizards. They throw things at the birds and miss. They are scolded by their grandmothers and they are not scolded by the engine-keepers, who tolerate them with the same patient indifference they extend to the goats.

The children learn the terraces by playing on them. They know every junction, every shortcut, every loose brick and mossy ledge. They can identify the bird species by call before they can read, if reading is a thing that is taught here, and it may or may not be. They know which drainage channels are safe to walk in and which flood suddenly when the engines cycle up. They have names for everything, but the names are not the names the adults use. They have their own toponymy — a private geography of the terraces in which the places are named for things that happened there to *them*, or to other children, or to no one. The names change every generation. The places don't.

A child raised on the middle terraces can navigate the structure blindfolded. Not because anyone trains them to. Because they have been walking these paths since they could stand, and standing on a path is not different from standing in a room, and the path is not different from the room, and the room is not different from the terrace outside it. The terraces are the child's entire sensory world. The warm clay underfoot is the floor of the universe. The moss on the walls is the texture of home. The thud of the engines is the first sound they heard, muffled by the wall of the uterus and the wall of the room, a one-second heartbeat that is not their mother's but is the world's.

When a child first realizes that the terraces are *built* — that someone made them — it is a shock, but a mild one, like realizing that your parents have parents. The child looks at the bricks and sees them for the first time as objects rather than as the native state of reality. Then the child adjusts, because children adjust, and goes back to chasing lizards.

## The birds

The birds were here before the people, or the people were here before the birds, or they arrived together. It doesn't matter. The birds are here now, and there are *many* of them.

There are the wall-nesters. Small, rust-colored, fast. They live in the gaps where mortar has crumbled, and their nests are cups of moss and grain-chaff and the small silver threads that the not-quite-spiders leave in the drainage channels. They lay three to five eggs, pale and speckled, and they raise their young in the literal structure of the world. When the chicks fledge, they leave the nest and the wall is a wall again, briefly, until the next pair moves in. The wall-nesters are the most numerous. They are the background chatter of the place, the layer of sound that is always there, and they have been there so long that their calls have adapted to the acoustics — they are pitched to carry between the walls, to bounce off the overhangs, to fill the space without overwhelming it.

There are the fruit-thieves. Larger, bolder, grey and cream-colored. They are the ones that get to the fruit before the people do. They work in pairs — one watches, one eats — and they are so good at this that the people have mostly given up trying to outcompete them. The fruit that the birds don't want is the fruit that the people get, and the people have learned which varieties the birds *don't* prefer, and they plant more of those, and the birds have learned which of *those* the people plant most, and the whole thing is a slow, mutual adjustment that has been going on for longer than anyone remembers and has resulted in a stable equilibrium in which everyone gets enough fruit and no one is happy about it.

There are the high terraces birds. The startling blue ones. They live above the condensation zone, near the cloud, where the air is cool and the moss is gold and rust and the walls are tall and narrow and the terraces are barely wide enough to walk on. They are not numerous — a few dozen pairs, maybe. They are the only species that lives exclusively at the top. They are also the only species that is not the color of the terraces. Everything else here is ochre or rust or green or grey. The high-terrace birds are blue. A blue that has no source in the environment. A blue that looks like it was imported from somewhere else, from somewhere with a sky that is not made of steam and condensation. They are visible from very far away — a flash of impossible color moving against the pale clay, and then gone, back into the cloud.

No one knows where they go at night. No one has followed them. The upper terraces are not hostile, but they are not comfortable, and the people who live in the middle do not go there often. The high-terrace birds are the closest thing the place has to a mystery, and the people treat them with the same patient indifference they extend to everything else, which is to say: they notice them, they admire the color, they do not investigate further.

There are the migrants. Occasionally, a bird appears that is none of the above — a species from somewhere else, blown in by the eddying wind, or lost, or exploring. These visitors stay for a few days, usually. They roost in the shrubs, they eat the fruit, they call in voices that sound wrong against the acoustics. Then they leave. Or they don't leave, and they stay, and in a hundred years they are the wall-nesters or the fruit-thieves or something new. The birds here are not fixed. They are a population in motion, and the terraces are a place that catches populations and holds them and lets them become themselves.

## The goats

They are not goats. They are goat-like. They are the size of goats and the temperament of goats and they fill the ecological niche of goats, and so the word is close enough.

They are sure-footed in a way that is almost unsettling to watch. They navigate the terraces — the stairs, the ramps, the narrow paths with the retaining walls dropping away to the next level — with an ease that suggests they were designed for this environment. They were not. They were brought here, or they found their way here, a long time ago, and they adapted, because that is what goat-like animals do. Their hooves are smaller than their ancestors' would have been — a trait that lets them grip the worn-smooth center of each path, the shallow gutter where the rain runs. Their legs are longer in proportion to their bodies, giving them a higher center of gravity that somehow makes them *more* stable rather than less. They are calm. They do not startle easily. They have been living on the terraces for long enough that a terrace is just a place, not a cliff, and a cliff is just a wall, and a wall is just a surface, and surfaces are what you stand on.

They are milked in the morning. The lower-terrace families keep them in small herds — four or five animals, no more — in pens made of the same silver wood as the fences, pens that are built against the retaining walls and roofed with the woody shrubs' branches. The goats eat the grain stalks and the shrub leaves and the bitter greens that grow in the wall-gaps, and they produce a milk that is thick and sweet and faintly herbal, and this milk is made into a cheese that is the primary protein source for most of the terrace-keepers.

The goats have bells. Each one wears a bell — iron, small, hung on a leather strap that has been replaced so many times that the strap is newer than the goat and the bell is older than the goat's grandmother. The bells are not tuned. They are not harmonic. They are just bells, and they clank in a low, irregular rhythm as the goats move, and this sound is part of the place the way the engine-thud is part of the place. You stop hearing it. But it is always there.

## The cats

The cats are not domestic. They are not feral. They are something in between — a state that has no name because the cats do not recognize the distinction. They were here before the current generation of people. They will be here after. They are the most confident inhabitants of the terraces.

They are small. They are lean. They are the color of the terraces — dusty orange-brown, or grey, or a tabby pattern that mimics the lichen on the bricks. They are not numerous — maybe one for every twenty people, distributed by territory, each cat owning a section of the terraces the way the people own their rooms and the birds own their wall-gaps.

They hunt the rodents that live in the grain stores. This is their function. But function is not the word the cats would use, if cats used words. They hunt because they are hungry and the rodents are there, and the grain stores are where the rodents are, and the grain stores are part of the terraces, and the terraces are the cat's territory. The hunting is not a job. It is a *consequence of being a cat in a place where rodents live*.

The people feed them scraps. Not out of affection, exactly. Out of a recognition that the cats are part of the system and the system works better when the cats are fed and the cats are also fed by the system, and the tension between these two things — the cats being fed by people and the cats being fed by the world — is a tension that has resolved itself into a stable, comfortable ambiguity. The cats are not grateful. The people do not expect gratitude. The arrangement is understood.

At dusk the cats move. This is when you see them — slipping along the tops of the retaining walls, their bodies low and fluid, their eyes catching the last reflected light from the terracotta surfaces. They are patrolling, or they are hunting, or they are simply *moving through their territory at the hour that belongs to them*. They do not acknowledge the people. The people do not acknowledge the cats. They share the terraces the way two trees share soil — root to root, occupying the same ground, each one doing what it does.

## The moss

It is alive. It is the most alive thing here.

Not because it moves — it doesn't, or it does, but at a speed that makes the engine-pistons look frantic. It is the most alive thing because it is the thing that has most thoroughly *become* the terraces. The moss is not on the walls. The moss *is* the walls, or it is becoming the walls, or the walls are becoming it. In the condensation zone — the middle and upper terraces where the vapor settles — every surface that is not actively walked on is claimed. The moss covers the brick. It fills the mortar gaps. It lines the drainage channels. It creeps over the iron railings, which are too smooth for it to grip easily but which it grips anyway, a thin green-black film on the metal that makes the railings feel soft and organic under your hand.

The moss is three species, or four, or more — the taxonomy is unclear because no one here is a taxonomist and the moss doesn't care. There is the green moss, which is the most common. There is the gold moss, which grows where the sun hits and the green moss can't compete. There is the rust-red moss, which grows in the darkest, wettest corners, under the overhangs, in the drainage channels where the water runs fastest and the light never reaches.

The moss is the indicator. When the engines are running and the water is flowing and the condensation is settling, the moss is thick and bright and beaded. When something falters — a pipe blocked, an engine running slow, a channel silted — the moss knows before the people do. It thins. It yellows. It pulls back from the edges of its range, retreating to the wettest spots, concentrating itself where the water still reaches. The people read the moss the way they read the drainage channels and the grain and the bird-calls — as a signal, a status report, a living dial on the face of the machine.

The moss is also the thing that makes the terraces *soft*. Without it, the place would be all hard surfaces — clay, brick, iron, wood. The moss cushions. It muffles. It turns the sharp edges of the brick into rounded, upholstered curves. It turns the drainage channels from raw cuts in the clay into gentle, moss-lined troughs that look like they were designed to be streams. It turns the retaining walls from *walls* into *surfaces* — surfaces with depth, with texture, with the patience of something that grows a millimeter a year and has been growing for a thousand years.

## The not-quite-spiders

They build webs in the drainage channels. The webs are not beautiful — they are functional, dense, slightly irregular, strung across the narrowest points of the channels where the water slows and the insects gather. The not-quite-spiders are small, brown-black, fast, and utterly without charisma. They are not the sort of thing you write poetry about. They are the sort of thing that exists because a niche exists and something *will* fill it.

But they are essential. They manage the insects. The insects manage the nectar and the decay and the moisture and each other. The whole system — the moss, the insects, the not-quite-spiders, the birds, the lizards, the cats — is a single thing. A single *process*. You cannot remove one element without distorting the others. The terrace-keepers know this, not as a theory but as a practice. They do not clear the webs from the drainage channels. They do not remove the moss from the walls. They do not kill the insects. They do not shoot the birds. They do not cull the cats. They let the system run because the system runs, and running it is what they do.

## The dead

The people bury their dead in the walls.

This is practical. There is no ground to dig — the ground is built, and built ground has structure, and structure is not something you excavate without reason. But the retaining walls between terraces are thick, and the mortar gaps and the brick courses and the composted humus that fills them create spaces — cavities, small rooms, openings that were once drainage channels or structural voids and have been repurposed over the centuries.

The dead are placed in the walls with ceremony, but the ceremony is quiet. There is a song — a low, unison thing, sung by the family, that matches the rhythm of the engines — and there is a closing of the wall with brick and mud, and the moss grows over it within a season.

The dead become part of the structure. Their calcium leaches into the mortar. Their carbon feeds the moss. The birds nest in the cavities that open above the sealed ones, and the lizards bask on the bricks that face the sun, and the grain grows in the soil that was built from the compost of the living and the bodies of the dead, and the cycle continues.

This is not morbid. This is not spiritual. This is *practical*. The dead are material. The material is part of the system. The system is the terraces. The terraces are the world.

## What they are, all together

They are not a society. A society implies a contract. There is no contract here. There is only a structure — physical, thermal, biological, mechanical — and a set of behaviors that have evolved to fit it. The people do not *govern*. They do not *organize*. They *maintain*. They maintain the grain and the water and the engines and the moss and the walls, and the maintaining is the social order, and the social order is the maintenance, and there is no difference.

They are not happy. They are not unhappy. They are *in place*. They are the way a stone in a wall is in place — fitted, load-bearing, part of something that holds. Remove them and the wall doesn't collapse, exactly, but the wall changes, and the change is not an improvement. Replace them with other people and the new people will fit, eventually, because the structure shapes them, the way it shaped the current people, the way it shaped the people before them, the way it shaped the birds and the goats and the cats and the moss and the grain.

They are the terraces' way of having hands. The engines are the terraces' way of having a heart. The water is the terraces' way of having blood. The moss is the terraces' way of having skin. And the people — the people are the terraces' way of *continuing*. Of fixing what breaks. Of tending what grows. Of being the part of the system that notices when a piston is wearing out, that notices when a drainage channel is silting up, that notices when the grain on the fourteenth terrace is ripening and the people on the fifteenth need to start their harvest.

They are not the owners of this place. They are not the inhabitants of this place. They are a *feature* of this place. And the place — warm, bright, damp, loud, patient, earthen, endless — is complete with them. It would be incomplete without them. Not because it needs them to function, but because they are part of what it *is*, the way the moss is part of what the walls are, the way the birds are part of what the air is, the way the engines are part of what the ground is.

The terraces hold them. They hold the terraces. The holding is mutual. The holding is the point.
