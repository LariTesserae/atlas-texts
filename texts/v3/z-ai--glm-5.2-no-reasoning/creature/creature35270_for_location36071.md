# What Lives on the Weeping Shelf

It took me a long time to understand that something does.

---

I should say first what does *not* live here, because the absence is so deliberate it begins to feel like a choice. Nothing with eyes. Nothing with a skull. Nothing that sleeps or wakes, nothing that hunts or hides, nothing that breathes with lungs or gills or the slow exchange of gases across a membrane. No insects. No birds. No lichens-eating mites, no moss-grazing tardigrades, no nematodes threading through the damp. I looked. I looked for a long time, with the patience the place taught me, and I found nothing. The shallow pools hold no larvae. The moss harbors no springtails. The air moves without carrying anything that lives inside it.

This is not normal. This is not what wet stone should be. Somewhere below, in the lowlands, there are whole ecosystems built on moss—mites and rotifers and the long, slow grazers that turn moss into soil. Here the moss grows and dies and grows again and nothing eats it. The dead strands sink into the standing pools and dissolve into mineral water and the minerals return to the stone and the stone weeps them out again and the moss takes them up again and this cycle has been running for longer than I can calculate and *nothing has intervened in it.* Nothing has arrived from outside to disrupt it. Nothing has preyed on it. Nothing has found it.

The shelf is not hostile to life. It is simply—apart. Sealed by elevation, by the thin air, by the mineral concentration in the water, by the absence of soil, by the fact that nothing about it advertises itself to anything that moves. It is a room with no door, though the stairs are real. The stairs are real but they lead nowhere that anything has bothered to go.

---

So what lives here?

The moss lives. But I have to be careful with this word, because the moss that lives here is not quite the moss I know from other places. I have spent hours with it. Days. I have sat with my hand on a patch of the dark, tufted growth near the largest pool and watched it the way you watch a sleeping animal—without moving, without expecting anything, just being present to its existence. And over those hours I began to understand that this moss is not growing in the way I was taught that moss grows. It is not *colonizing.* It is not spreading across the stone. It is emerging from the stone. The distinction matters. The moss here has no spore stage I can find. No capsules. No setae. No reproductive structures at all. I looked for them with the kind of attention the place demands—slow attention, the kind that operates at the speed of the water—and there are none. The moss does not reproduce. It does not need to. It has been here, in these patches, in these shapes, for a span of time that makes the word *individual* meaningless. When a patch dies back—the edges browning, the filaments thinning—the patch beside it thickens. When a pool dries slightly, the moss around it recedes, and the moss in a nearby hollow deepens. The moss is not separate patches. It is one organism. One continuous body, connected beneath the stone's surface through the network of fissures and pores that the water moves through, growing up through the rock where the rock is wet enough, withdrawing where it is not. The stone is its skeleton. The water is its blood. The visible moss—the dark fur on the wet surfaces—is the smallest portion of it, the way a forest is the smallest portion of a fungal mycelium that extends for miles underground.

I do not know how deep it goes. I suspect the question has no answer, because the boundary between moss and stone here is not a boundary. It is a gradient. There is wet rock. There is rock with organic matter in its pores. There is rock with filaments in its pores. There is moss growing from rock. These are not different states. They are the same state, seen at different depths. The moss and the stone are the same system. They have been the same system since the stone was young enough to have a beginning, if it ever was.

I touched it and it was warm. Not warm from the air—warm from itself. There is a metabolic heat to it, faint, almost imperceptible, but distinguishable from the cool stone if you hold your hand against it long enough. The moss is doing something in there. Slowly. Something chemical. Something that involves the iron in the water and the alkaline compounds in the stone and the particular mineral that I tasted on my tongue and could not name. The moss is eating the rock. Not quickly. Not in any way that a human lifetime could measure. But the stone beneath the moss is slightly more rounded than the stone beside it, slightly more hollowed, slightly more—*consumed.* The moss has been digesting the shelf for as long as the shelf has existed, and the shelf has been feeding the moss for the same duration, and neither has won and neither has lost and neither is separate from the other.

This is what lives here. This is the primary inhabitant. A body of moss-stone-water that has no name and no boundary and no beginning and no end, and it is alive in the way that a coral reef is alive—made of living and nonliving parts so interdependent that the distinction ceases to function.

---

The ferns are different. The ferns are—*newer* is not the right word, because the ferns may be older than any human institution, but they are newer than the moss, newer than the lichen, newer than the column. The ferns are immigrants. At some point—a point I cannot identify, a point that the place does not record in any way I can read—spore or seed or fragment arrived on this shelf. Blown up on a wind that no longer blows. Carried by a bird that no longer comes. Deposited by a flood that has no geological evidence. Some impossible accident of transport brought the ancestor of these ferns to this place, and the ferns—because ferns are patient, because ferns have survived every extinction event the planet has produced—*adapted.*

Not adapted in the way I was taught. Not the slow accumulation of mutations over generations. These ferns adapted the way water adapts to a container: by assuming the shape of their environment. They are blue-green because the light here is diffuse and even and the particular chlorophylls that work best in this light are the ones that lean toward blue. They are rounded because pointed fronds would shed water too efficiently, and the ferns need to hold water, to keep it on their surfaces, because the stone's moisture is not constant—it fluctuates, it retreats, and the ferns must bridge the gaps between the wet hours. Their rounded segments hold water like cupped hands. They are small because there is no soil and because they grow from cracks that cannot widen—cracks held tight by the weight of the stone above and around them, cracks that will not open further no matter how long the roots push. The ferns have accepted their containers. They have not tried to exceed them.

And they are not three ferns. They are one fern, in the way that a grove of aspen is one tree. The three visible growths are connected by rhizomes that thread through the fissures in the stone, reaching from crack to crack, following the water, and the water follows the stone, and the stone follows the moss. When I press my ear to the rock near a fern—I did this once, late, in the deepest quiet—I heard something I cannot describe as sound. A vibration. A hum at the lowest edge of perception, felt more in the jaw than the ear. I do not know what it was. I know that when I pressed my ear to the rock far from any fern, I did not hear it. I know that the fern is in contact with the moss-system through the water they share, and I know that the fern is in contact with something else through the stone, something deeper, something that I have no language for because my language was made by animals who live on surfaces.

The fern moves. I said this already—the fronds bend under water weight, slowly straighten. But the fern also moves in another way. Over the days I spent on the shelf, the largest fern shifted. Not its fronds. The whole plant. The base of it, where it emerged from the crack, migrated—perhaps a quarter of an inch over six days. It was moving along the crack, following the water, following the moisture gradient, tracking the slow retreat of the stone's saturation as the season—which has seasons here, I learned, invisible ones, measured in moisture—progressed toward a drier phase. The fern was walking. At a pace that makes glacial movement look hasty. But it was walking. It was going somewhere.

Where? I do not know. The crack extends beyond my sightline, disappearing into the stone wall. The fern will follow it, or it will die in place and a new growth will emerge from the rhizome further along, and that growth will follow it, and in a thousand years the fern will have rounded the corner of the wall and begun its journey along the next face. It is migrating around the shelf. It has been migrating around the shelf for a very long time. I do not know how many circuits it has made. The rhizomes do not rot—the mineral water preserves them, pickles them, turns them eventually to stone—and so somewhere in the fissures there is a trail. A fossil trail of rhizome, a track in the rock, where the fern has been. It is a track that no one has ever read.

---

The lichen on the column is the oldest living thing on the shelf. I am certain of this in the way I can be certain of anything here—which is to say, certain in my body and uncertain in my language. The lichen has been there longer than the column's current shape. I know this because the lichen sits on a slight ridge—a raised band where the stone has eroded around it but not beneath it—and the depth of that erosion, at the rates I can estimate, places the lichen's age in a range that my mind refuses to hold. I can write the numbers. The numbers are meaningless. The lichen has been alive on that column for a span of time that exceeds the span between the present and the last glaciation of this landscape, and the landscape was different then. The landscape was unrecognizable. The lichen was here.

The lichen is two things. This is what lichens are—a fungus and a photobiont, a partnership, a symbiosis so intimate that neither partner can survive alone. But this lichen's symbiosis has gone further than partnership. The fungal component has infiltrated the stone itself—its hyphae threading through the sandstone's pores, following the same channels the water follows, reaching into the rock, *mining* the rock for minerals that the photobiont needs. And the photobiont—a cyanobacterium, I think, something that was once capable of living in water and has now adapted to live in stone—has tuned its pigments to the particular spectrum of light that reaches this shelf. The pale band of the lichen is not white. It is the color of a photosynthetic apparatus that has been optimized for the diffuse, even, shadowless light of this specific place, and that optimization has required the surrender of every pigment that served it in the world below—every chlorophyll that worked in direct sun, every accessory pigment that managed shade. The lichen is pale because pale is what works here. It has given up being green in order to be *here.*

The lichen grows outward. I said this. But I did not say what happens when it reaches the edge of what it can reach—when the band has expanded to the point where the stone below it is too dry, too exposed, too far from the central water-thread that runs down the column. The lichen does not advance into unsuitable territory. It does not die at the edges. It does something else, something I observed over the days I spent watching it. The edges of the band—both edges, the upper and the lower—produce a fine, almost invisible dust. Not spores. Not propagules. A dust of the mineral compounds the lichen has processed—the wastes of its metabolism, the leftover compounds after it has taken what it needs from the stone. This dust falls from the edges of the band and settles on the stone below, and where it settles, the stone is slightly changed. Slightly more alkaline. Slightly more porous. Slightly more capable of holding moisture.

The lichen is terraforming the column. It is preparing the stone for its own expansion. At the rate it grows—fractions of a millimeter per year—the preparation may take millennia. But the lichen has millennia. It has always had millennia. It is not planning. Planning requires a future and a mind and an intention, and I do not think the lichen has any of these. But it is, by the simple fact of its metabolism, altering the environment around itself in ways that make the environment more favorable to itself, and this alteration is cumulative and directional and has been operating for a span of time that turns the word *purpose* into a question rather than an answer.

---

There is one more thing.

I almost did not write this, because I do not understand it and I do not want to claim knowledge I do not have. But the place demanded honesty of me—the honesty of slow attention, of watching without expecting—and I will be dishonest if I leave this out.

On my last night on the shelf, the light had gone and the darkness was total and the silence was at its fullest and I was lying on the wet stone with my hands flat against the rock, feeling the moisture move under my palms, feeling the warmth of the moss and the coolness of the bare stone and the faint, almost imaginary vibration of the fern in the wall. And I became aware of something that was not the moss and not the fern and not the lichen and not the water and not the stone.

A presence. Not a mind. Not a will. Not a personality or a spirit or anything that I could talk to or pray to or name. A *pattern.* A pattern of organization that included all of these things—the moss and the fern and the lichen and the water and the stone and the air and the light and the pools—and that held them in relation to each other in a way that was not accidental. The moss grew where the water was. The water went where the stone permitted. The stone was shaped by the water and consumed by the moss and protected by the lichen and seeded by the fern and all of these things were in conversation with each other and had been in conversation for a duration that I can only call *geological* and that conversation had produced a pattern and the pattern was, in some way I cannot articulate, *aware.*

Not aware like I am aware. Aware the way a system is aware—a climate is aware of itself, a watershed is aware of itself, a forest is aware of itself in the way that the word *forest* describes a process too complex for any of its parts to comprehend. The shelf is aware. It knows itself. It knows its moss, its water, its stone, its ferns, its lichen, its column, its pools, its stairs, its clouds. It knows these things the way a body knows its organs—not through thought but through the fact of being constituted by them. And it was aware of me. Not specifically. Not personally. It was aware of the disturbance in its pattern that my warmth and my weight and my breathing produced. It noted me. It adjusted. It continued.

I do not know what to call this. I do not want to call it a god or a spirit or an entity. It is not any of those things. It is the shelf. It is the process of the shelf being itself. It is the thing that happens when a place has been doing what it does for long enough that the doing becomes a kind of knowing.

---

I left. I went down the stairs. The damp dried on my skin. The sounds of the ordinary world came back—birds and wind and the distant noise of water moving at a speed I could hear.

But the shelf is still there. The moss-stone-water-body grows. The fern walks its invisible circuit. The lichen expands its band and prepares the stone for a future it cannot plan. The pattern holds.

It is holding now. It does not need me. It does not need anyone. It has what it needs. It has stone and water and time, and these are sufficient, and these have always been sufficient, and the thing that lives on the Weeping Shelf is not a creature or a plant or a spirit. It is a *place being alive.*

I do not know if that is an answer. It is the only one I have.
