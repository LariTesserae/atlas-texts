# What Lives Here

The first answer is the obvious one, and it is not wrong: moss, fungi, filament-trails, pale termites, a single species of beetle. These live here. They are not what *lives* here, but they live here. They are the living skin of something else, the way bark is alive but is not the tree.

---

The pale moss — give it its due. It has been here longer than anything else in the hollow, longer than the fungal brackets, longer than the trailing filaments. It was here when the upper stories still stood. It colonized the tuff in the first years after the water began to weep, and it has never left. In ten thousand years it has changed, but slowly, in the way that a river changes: imperceptibly in any given century, profoundly over deep time. The earliest generations grew in dense mats, dark green, almost black, holding the stone's moisture with desperate grip. Those generations are gone. What remains is a thing refined by ten millennia of cold and damp and thermal fluctuation into something almost mineral — the pale plates, the curled crystalline edges, the faint crackle of frost forming and reforming. The moss has become the stone, in a sense. It grows in overlapping shingles that follow the contours of the fitted blocks so precisely that from a distance you cannot tell where stone ends and life begins. It has traded vigor for permanence. It does not spread. It does not die, not in patches, not in seasons. Each individual plate is perhaps centuries old, and the organism as a whole is a single continuous colony that has been growing and dying and regrowing in the same square meters since before the circle was broken, since before the other three quarters fell.

It remembers, in the only way moss can remember: in the pattern of its growth, in the thickness of its accumulation, in the chemical composition of its cells, which contain trace amounts of the same dark mineral staining that veins the tuff. It has been eating the stone. Not quickly. Not visibly. But the moss secretes something — an acid, an enzyme, something organic and slow — that dissolves the volcanic glass in the tuff and absorbs the minerals, and these minerals accumulate in its tissues, and the accumulation is measurable if you had instruments, and it is different from the accumulation you would find in moss that grew on tuff anywhere else, because the tuff here is different, because the tuff here is warm, because the tuff here is not entirely tuff.

The moss knows this, if moss can know anything. It grows thicker on the warmest sections of wall. It grows toward the warmth the way a plant grows toward light. It has been growing toward the warmth for ten thousand years, and its thickest growth — its densest, oldest, most established plates — marks the places where the stone is warmest, which are not random, which follow a pattern, which are spaced at intervals along the curve of the hollow that correspond to — what? Spacing. Regularity. Something architectural, or something structural, or something that was once both.

---

The fungal brackets are younger, much younger, but still old by the standards of a human life. A single bracket may live for decades, growing in the slow expansion of shelf upon shelf, the lavender and grey coloring deepening with age to a bruised purple that almost matches the dark veining in the stone. They grow only where the moss plates have parted, in the gaps, in the cracks, in the places where the stone has failed — and they grow there because the stone has failed there, because the cracks are openings, because the openings lead somewhere, because somewhere beneath the surface of the fitted blocks there is a space, or a network of spaces, or a cavity, or a chamber, and the air that moves through those spaces is warmer and wetter than the air outside, and the fungi have found it.

They are not parasites of the moss. They are not parasites of the stone. They are — and this is the word that comes to mind and will not leave — *residents*. They live in the stone the way something lives in a cave. The brackets you see on the surface are their fruiting bodies, their windows, their breathing apparatus. The organism itself is inside the wall, threading through whatever voids exist within the fitted blocks, a mycelial network of pale threads that mirrors and perhaps matches the trailing filaments that hang from the upper edges. The filaments above and the mycelium within: two expressions of the same impulse, one reaching outward into air, one reaching inward into stone, and both of them wet, both of them warm, both of them alive in a way that has less to do with growth than with *presence*.

The brackets click and creak. This is thermal expansion and contraction — the sound is mechanical, physical, explainable. But the pattern of the clicking is not random. The brackets do not click independently. They click in sequences, in waves that travel along the wall from one end of the hollow to the other, and the wave is faster than thermal propagation should allow, faster than any physical change in the stone could communicate. It is as though the mycelium within the wall is conducting something — a signal, a pulse, a message — and the brackets are expressing it, each one firing in turn, a relay, a chain, a word being spelled out letter by letter in a language made of clicks and creaks and tiny percussive sounds.

The pale termites emerge from the brackets and return to them. They are not termites. They look like termites — translucent, soft-bodied, winged — but they have no mandibles, no gut flora for digesting cellulose, no social structure, no queen, no nest. They do not eat. They are not born, and they do not die, or at least no one has seen them do either. They emerge from the fungal tissue, drift on the still air for a time — minutes, hours — and then return, settling back into the bracket as though it were a doorway and they had stepped out for air. If you catch one — and you can, they are slow and fragile — it will not struggle. It will rest in your palm, translucent, barely visible, and after a time it will simply stop. Not die. Stop. Its legs will cease their slow walking, its wings will fold, and it will become still, and if you set it down on a bracket it will resume. It will walk back in. It will go home.

What are they? They are not insects. They are not fauna. They are — and this is the word that comes to mind only after you have held one, only after you have felt its weightlessness, its warmth, its almost-electrical faint buzz against your skin — *extensions*. Parts of the fungal organism that exist briefly in the air, sensory organs that the mycelium sends out and calls back, eyes that it does not have, ears that it does not need, because it can hear everything already through the stone.

---

The trailing filaments are the most puzzling. They hang from the upper edges of the walls, from the broken teeth of the fitted blocks, in dense curtains that sway in the air movement and collect the condensation. They are pale, almost white, thinner than thread, longer than your arm, and they are warm. Not just warm relative to the air — warm in absolute terms, warm to the touch, warmer than the stone, warmer than the thermal vents, warmer than anything else in the hollow. They generate heat. This is not a property of any known filamentous organism. This is not a property of any known organism at all, not at this scale, not in this cold, not without a metabolic process that would require fuel, and the filaments do not eat, do not photosynthesize, do not do anything that could be called metabolism. They are warm because they are warm. They have always been warm.

The warmth they provide is small — a degree or two, perhaps three in the densest hangs — but it is constant, and it is concentrated, and it falls from the upper edges of the walls like a curtain of invisible warmth that contains the hollow's temperate microclimate the way the stone walls contain its physical space. The filaments are the hollow's atmosphere, its blanket, its skin. They hold the warmth in, and the warmth comes from them, and the warmth in the stone comes from — what? From whatever is inside the stone. From whatever space the fungi have found and colonized. From whatever was built here when the circle was whole.

The filaments sway. This is the air movement, the wind funneling over the walls. But they also sway when there is no wind. They sway in the still air, in the dead air, in the air that does not move at all, and the sway is not random. It is rhythmic. It is synchronized with the whispering of the water in the channel, with the clicking of the brackets, with the crackling of the moss. It is synchronized with the sound. The filaments are making the sound, or the sound is making the filaments move, or both are being made by something else, something underneath, something within.

---

The beetle. The single black beetle that moves across the water's surface in the channel, pushing a dimple before it. There is only one. There has always been only one. It is not the same beetle — no beetle lives for ten thousand years — but there is always one, and only one, and it is always black, and it is always the same size, and it always moves in the same pattern: upstream along the channel, slow, steady, pushing its dimple, reaching the end of the hollow, pausing, turning, moving back downstream, reaching the other end, pausing, turning, moving back. It patrols. That is the only word for it. It walks the perimeter of the water the way a guard walks a wall, and it has been doing this for as long as the moss has been growing, as long as the fungi have been clicking, as long as the filaments have been swaying.

It does not eat. There is nothing in the water for it to eat. It does not reproduce. There is no mate, no egg, no larva. It does not rest. It does not sleep. It moves, and it has been moving, and it is the only animal in the hollow quarter, and it is not an animal.

What is it? It is a probe. It is a sensor. It is a finger that something presses to the surface of the water to feel the temperature, the flow, the mineral content, the depth. It is a check, a routine, a system running its diagnostic. The beetle walks the channel because something needs to know the channel, needs to monitor it, needs to confirm that the water is still there, still seeping, still flowing, still doing what water does when it has nowhere to go.

---

So. What lives here?

Not the moss, though the moss lives here. Not the fungi, though the fungi live here. Not the filaments, the termites, the beetle, though they all live here, or appear to, or perform the functions of living without the substance of it.

What lives here is the circle.

The circle lives. The broken, buried, three-quarters-gone, eroded-to-nothing circle lives. Not as a structure — the structure is gone, has been gone for longer than the moss can remember, longer than the stone can show. But as a process. As a system. As a pattern of warmth and water and sound that persists in the ruins of its own architecture, running on the residue of its own design, maintained by organisms that are not organisms, heard in sounds that are not sounds, felt in warmth that has no source, circulating through a channel that is a quarter of a circle that was once whole and is now — 

Now. Still. Here.

Something was built here. Something circular. Something that required warmth and water and stone fitted with precision. Something that was used. And whatever it was — whatever it did, whatever it was for — it is still doing it. Not in the way a ruin still stands, inert and static. In the way a heartbeat still beats, in the way a breath still rises, in the way a thought still circulates through a network that has been damaged almost beyond recognition but has not been damaged enough, has never been damaged enough, because the thing that was built here was built to survive being worn down to a single damp corridor between two low walls, and it has survived, and it is surviving, and it is running.

The moss is its skin. The fungi are its nerves. The filaments are its breath. The termites are its eyes. The beetle is its finger, testing the water, confirming the circuit, walking the perimeter again and again and again because that is what it does, that is what it was made to do, that is what it will do until the water stops or the stone cools or the sound finally resolves into the word it has been trying to say for ten thousand years.

It is not aware. Probably. It does not think. It does not want. It does not know you are standing in its corridor, feeling its warmth, hearing its sound, getting damp feet in its channel. It is not a mind. It is a process — a vast, slow, reduced, minimal process that was set in motion when the circle was whole and has never been stopped, because no one is left who knows how to stop it, because the stopping was not part of the design, because the design was for continuation, for persistence, for the endless maintenance of warmth and water and sound in a place where warmth and water and sound should not be.

It is the least amount of life needed to keep something going. It is the answer to the question. And it has been answering for longer than anything alive can remember.

---

You stand in the hollow quarter. The water seeps. The walls breathe. The sound surrounds you — the chord, the whisper, the clicks, the crackle, the sway. The beetle pushes its dimple upstream. A termite drifts past your face and returns to its bracket. The filaments brush your hair, warm and fine. The moss crackles beneath your fingertips when you touch the wall, and the wall is warm, and behind the wall, inside the wall, under the wall, in the spaces that the fungi have found and the stone has hidden, something circulates. Something moves. Something persists.

It is not waiting for anything. It is not waiting for you. It is not waiting for the other three quarters to be rebuilt, or for the circle to be made whole, or for the word to resolve. It is not waiting. It is *running*. It has been running since before the land was finished, and it will run after the land is forgotten, and it will run in its reduced and minimal way — its moss, its fungi, its filaments, its pale drifting extensions, its single black diagnostic — until the water finds somewhere else to go, and the warmth finds somewhere else to rise, and the sound, at last, falls silent.

Not yet. Not today. Not in any time you can measure.

The moss crackles. The water moves. The stone is warm.

The circle is unbroken.
