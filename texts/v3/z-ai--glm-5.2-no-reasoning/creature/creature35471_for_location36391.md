# What Lives Here

Let me start with what is easiest to see, and move inward.

---

**The swallows** are the surface layer of life here, the part that moves against the grey strip of sky and gives the quarry its visible pulse. They are not quite right. Someone with a field guide and a patient afternoon might spend hours and fail to match them to any entry. Their wings are narrower than they should be. Their calls are dry, abbreviated — not the liquid trill of barn swallows, not the chattering of house martins, but something more like stones clicking together underwater. They hunt the insects that breed in the standing water and the damp organic matter that collects in the settlement's overlooked corners: midges, gnats, a species of small winged thing that likewise resists identification, though no one has tried very hard. The swallows nest high in the wall crevices, near the rim, where the quarry opens to weather. They come and go. They are the settlement's clock in certain ways — their departure each evening is more reliable than the sky, which is often uniform grey from dawn to dark. When they go quiet, night has been declared, regardless of what the light is doing.

They are not afraid of people but they do not interact with people. They exist in a parallel occupancy, sharing the airspace but not the attention. The settlers see them the way you see wallpaper. They are part of the architecture.

---

**The salamander-things** are the next layer down.

There is no good name for them. "Newt" is too small a word. "Salamander" is too specific — it implies a taxonomy, a family tree, a place in an order, and these creatures' place in any order is uncertain. They are dark, not black but a deep grey-green that matches the wet stone, and they are sleek in the way that suggests efficiency rather than beauty. Six to eight inches, as described, though the logbook records specimens up to eleven inches in the third year of entries and a gradual decrease in average length since. Their skin is wet at all times — not moist, but actively wet, as though they have just emerged from water, even when they are found on stone some distance from any pool. Their eyes are the problem. They are large, proportionally larger than they should be, and they are not the round, bead-like eyes of a standard salamander. They are wider, slightly almond-shaped, and they catch light in a way that suggests a lens structure more complex than amphibian anatomy typically provides. When a lantern catches their eyes at night, the reflection is not the standard red-orange of reflected light in a tapetum. It is pale. Almost blue. It is the colour of the sky above the quarry rim on an overcast morning, condensed into a point.

They are abundant. On a wet evening, the edges of every pool, every section of drainage channel, every wet stone surface hosts them. They sit motionless. They watch. The watching is not dramatic — they do not track movement with the alert intensity of a predator or the nervous attention of prey. They simply face whatever is present and remain facing it. You can walk past a pool with thirty of them and not one will shift position. You can sit beside the pool and they will continue to watch you for as long as you sit, which might be longer than you intended, because there is something in the quality of that attention that holds.

The settlers are accustomed to this. The settlers have, over fifteen years of stable occupation and more years before that of less stable occupation, developed a relationship with these creatures that is not domestic and not wild but something else — observational, patient, mutual. The creatures watch the settlers. The settlers watch the creatures. The logbook records this watching in meticulous detail: dates, counts, locations, behavioural notes, water levels, temperature estimates, weather conditions. The entries are precise and unadorned. They read like the work of a long-term ecological study conducted by someone who does not consider it a study, who considers it a conversation.

An entry, selected at random from the middle years:

*14th of the month. Overcast. Water level in the south channel high after three days of rain above. Counted forty-one in the main pool, twelve in the sump, eight along the east wall seepage. Three juveniles observed in the channel, approximately two inches, still showing gill remnants. Adult specimen (the one with the notch in the left tail, now regrown — second cycle) moved to the workshop drain and remained there for six hours. No visible reason. Returned to the main pool at approximately the time the generator was shut off. Note: the three that have been resident in the habitation block's underfloor since last autumn are still present. They do not appear to have grown. They do not appear to be eating. They are active at night.*

That last note — the three in the habitation block's underfloor — recurs in entries over a span of months. They are there. They do not eat. They do not grow. They are active at night. The entry simply records this, season after season, and then stops mentioning them, not because they have left but because their continued presence has become baseline.

The creatures are not farmed. They are not eaten. They are not, in any instrumental sense, useful. They are not pets. The settlers do not name them, though the logbook refers to individuals by distinguishing marks — the notch-tail, the pale one, the one that lives in the pipe. The relationship is closer to what you might find between a long-term naturalist and the subjects of a lifetime's observation: intimate, detailed, one-directional in its overt expression but shaped by the sense — which may be projection, may be instinct, may be something else — that the observation is returned.

There is one more thing about the salamander-things that the logbook does not state but that a visitor, spending time, might begin to notice: their population correlates with the work. When the workshop is busy — when the forge is running, when the lathes are turning, when parts are being made and shipped — the population is higher. When the workshop is idle, waiting for specifications, the population drops. The correlation is not perfect. There are other variables — season, water level, temperature. But the pattern is there, in the logbook's numbers, for anyone who cares to look. The work draws them. Or the work is drawn by them. Or the work and the creatures are both drawn by the same thing, which is the quarry itself, which is the stone and the water and whatever lives in the deeper places that the culvert leads to.

---

**The settlers** are thirty people, more or less, and they are the visible life of the Underneath in the way that a reef's visible life is its fish — present, active, mobile, but not the structure. Not the reason.

They are not a cult. They are not a commune. They are not a survivalist cell or a separatist project or a utopian experiment. They are workers. They live where they work because the work is here, and the work is here because — this is the part that resists clean explanation — the work can only be done here. The specifications arrive. The parts leave. In between, there is machining, there is inspection, there is the maintenance of the machines that do the machining and the inspection, and there is the maintenance of the settlement that supports the people who maintain the machines. The cycle is the life. The settlement is the cycle's infrastructure.

They are not all the same kind of person, and they have not all been here the same length of time. There are settlers who have been here since the first habitation block was raised — fewer now, their hair grey, their hands still steady on the lathes. There are settlers who arrived a year ago, or two, brought by the same network that brings the diesel, integrated by the same process that integrates everything here: the slow accretion of familiarity with the quarry's rhythms, the gradual cessation of surprise at the things that live in the water and the dark. There are children. Not many. Enough. The children know the creatures the way children know the weather — as a given, as a condition, as something that does not require explanation because it has always been so.

The settlers are not strange in the way that the quarry is strange. They are strange only in their choice of habitation, and even that strangeness diminishes with proximity. Spend a week in the habitation block and the wet stone walls become normal. Spend two weeks and the creatures at the pool's edge become part of the evening's furniture. Spend a month and the larger things in the dark corners become something you stop flinching from, not because you have stopped noticing them but because you have accepted that noticing is the appropriate response, and nothing more is required. The settlers have done this. They have been here long enough that the quarry's weirdness has become their normal, and their normal, viewed from outside, has taken on some of the quarry's weirdness as a stain takes on the character of its cloth.

What the settlers are like: quiet. Not uniformly — there is laughter in the kitchen, there is the clatter of the workshop, there are the sounds of thirty people living in close quarters. But the baseline is quiet. Conversations happen at a volume that the quarry's acoustics — the walls are close, the stone reflects sound in patterns that create dead spots and amplification zones — have trained people to maintain. There is no shouting. There is no loud music. The generator's schedule means that amplified sound is a limited resource, and the settlement has not chosen to spend it on noise. The books in the habitation block — there are shelves, well-stocked, well-used — are the primary entertainment. The books are technical, mostly. Machining manuals, materials science, engineering references. But also: natural history. Geology. A section on cave ecosystems. A small collection of fiction that does not fit any genre but that shares, across its unrelated titles, a quality of patience, of long observation, of things revealed slowly. The settlers read. The settlers work. The settlers watch.

What the settlers do not do: they do not leave. Or rather, some of them leave, on the road, for supplies, for the diesel, for the exchange of parts for specifications, and they return. They do not leave permanently. The turnover rate is effectively zero. People who come to the Underneath stay in the Underneath. This is not enforced. There is no barrier, physical or social, to departure. The road is open. The road is drivable. The world above is reachable. But people do not go. They settle. They settle the way the moss settles on the stone — not because they are stuck but because the surface is right, and the moisture is right, and the light is right, and there is no compelling reason to be elsewhere when here is where the work is, and the work is good, and the watching is mutual, and the arrangement holds.

---

**The larger things** are the hardest to describe, because description requires observation, and observation requires looking at them directly, and looking at them directly is the one thing that the settlers do not do.

You see them as shapes. You see them as movement at the periphery. You hear them — the scrape of something against stone, the slow exhalation, the sound of weight shifting in the dark. The settlers, asked about them, say: the quarry is home to many things. The things have been here longer than the settlement. This is their arrangement.

What can be said, from the outside, from the vantage of a visitor who has spent time but not enough time:

They are larger than the salamander-things. Larger than a person, probably, though size is difficult to judge in the quarry's light, against the quarry's stone, where shadows and solid objects share the same grey palette. They are slow. Everything about them suggests slowness — the sounds they make are the sounds of deliberate movement, of weight carefully shifted, of a body negotiating space with the patience of something that has no urgency. They are in the dark corners: the deep alcoves, the spaces behind the habitation block, the gaps in the talus slope, the area near the culvert where the drainage channel disappears into the wall. They are, perhaps, in the culvert itself, and in whatever the culvert leads to.

They do not come into the light. This is the arrangement, or part of it. The settlement occupies the quarry floor, the structures, the gantries, the lit spaces. The larger things occupy the margins, the deep alcoves, the spaces behind and below. The boundary is not marked. It does not need to be. It is understood. It has been understood for longer than the settlement has existed — the settlers learned it, or were taught it, or found it already in place when they arrived, the way you find a path already worn in the grass. You follow it. You do not question the path. The path is the arrangement.

The larger things and the salamander-things are connected. This is not stated. It is inferred — from the logbook's correlation between the creatures' population and the workshop's activity, from the way the salamander-things sometimes move to the workshop drains and remain for hours, from the fact that the culvert (which leads to wherever the larger things are) is also the route by which the drainage water leaves the quarry, and the salamander-things live in the drainage water. The connection is not explained. It is, like the arrangement, part of the settlement's logic, and the settlement's logic is not a public document.

What the larger things are: unknown. Not in the sense of "unidentified animal species." In the sense of "category not established." They are fauna, perhaps. They are something else, perhaps. The settlers' language for them is not helpful — they say "the things in the corners" or "the older ones" or, in one case, to one visitor, "the quarry's own," and then changed the subject. The language is respectful but not fearful. It is the language of cohabitation. It is the language of an arrangement that works.

---

**The quarry itself** lives, in the way that a place can be said to live when it has been occupied long enough that occupation and substance have merged. The limestone holds water. The water moves through the stone at a rate that is invisible but constant — you see it in the dark patches high on the walls, in the weeping after rain, in the trickle in the drainage channel. The stone is not inert. It is a medium. It stores, it releases, it conducts. The quarry is a system, and the system includes the settlers and the swallows and the salamander-things and the larger things and the water and the stone and the air and the dim grey light. The system is in equilibrium. The equilibrium has been maintained for fifteen years of stable population, and for longer than that in less stable configurations, and for longer than that — for how long, no one knows — in the time before the quarrymen came, when the stone was uncut and the water moved through it unseen and the things in the dark corners had the whole hillside to themselves.

The quarrymen found the deeper cuts. Found things that were not stone. Left. The things were not hostile. Presence was enough.

This is the deepest layer of what lives here: the things that were present before the quarry was a quarry, that endured the quarrying, that endured the settlers, that endure. They are the quarry's own. They are what the quarry is. The settlement is a feature of their habitat — a structure on the surface of their deeper life, the way a barnacle colony is a feature of a whale's surface. The settlement serves a function, perhaps. The parts that the workshop makes — the non-standard parts, for non-standard devices, specified by someone and sent to somewhere — may be parts for things that relate to the larger things, or to the quarry's deeper system, or to something that the settlement is part of without knowing it. The diesel supplier may know. The specifier of parts may know. The settlers know the specifications and the materials and the tolerances. They do not know the purpose. Or they know it and do not say. Or they know it the way you know the weight of your own body — so completely that the knowing is not separate from the being.

---

What lives here: thirty people who make parts. Birds that are not quite swallows. Creatures that are not quite salamanders. Things that are not quite animals, in the dark corners, in the deeper places, that were here first and will be here last. Stone that holds water. Water that moves through stone. A culvert that leads somewhere deeper. A logbook that records what can be recorded and is silent about what cannot. An arrangement that is not written and not spoken and not broken.

What lives here: attention. The quarry's attention. Patient, slow, old, unblinking. The attention of stone that has been watching for longer than anything alive has been watching. The attention of water that has been moving through the stone for longer than the stone has been stone. The attention of things that regard the settlement the way the settlement regards the salamander-things — with interest, with patience, with the calm certainty that what is observed is also, in some way, cared for.

What lives here lives here because the quarry holds them the way the limestone holds water: in the stone, in the dark, in the patience of a place that has been settling into itself for longer than any single life can measure, and that will continue settling long after the settlement has become, like the quarrymen before it, a layer in the stone — a mark on the wall, a cut in the face, a memory that the water will carry deeper, into the places where the larger things move slowly, and watch, and wait, and attend.
