# What Lives Here

Something does.

Not despite the absence. Not surviving in opposition to it. Something lives here *because of* the absence — because the ground finished, because the sound is constant, because the moisture comes and goes with the reliability of a heartbeat in a body that has nothing else to do.

You have to understand what the place is before you can understand what lives in it. The Millstone Expanse is not a wasteland. A wasteland is a place where life tried and failed. This is a place where life never needed to try because everything it required was already here, already steady, already done. The difference matters. The difference is everything.

---

## First: the small ones

They live in the cracks.

Not in the way that earthworms live in soil, chewing through, displacing, reshaping. They live *within* the crack itself — in the volume of space between the curled edges of the polygonal tiles, in the gaps where dust sifts and salt blooms and moisture pools twice each day. They are small. The largest specimens observed have been no longer than a human finger and thinner than a filament of spider silk. Most are far smaller. Most are barely visible as individuals.

They are not worms. They are not insects. They are not anything that belongs to a taxonomy built on roots and stems and chitin and hemolymph, though they have properties that rhyme with each of these. They are pale — chalk white to faint ochre, matching the mineral crust they inhabit, and this is not camouflage, not selection by predation, but simply *material*. They are made of what they live in. Calcium carbonate and gypsum and traces of iron oxide, bound in a matrix that is not protein and not cellulose but something between — something that has no name because no one has yet needed to name it, because no one has yet been sure it is alive.

They absorb the condensation. Not through mouths or roots or pores but through the full length of their bodies, which are permeable to the dawn moisture in the same way the mineral crust is permeable to it — slowly, superficially, completely. When the moisture lifts, they do not desiccate. They retain it. Their bodies hold the damp the way the cracks hold the salt bloom: as a residue, as a memory of what passed through.

They do not eat. There is nothing to eat. They do not photosynthesize — there is no need, and the chemistry would not work. What they do is *precipitate*. The moisture that enters them carries dissolved minerals — salts, sulfates, trace irons — and their bodies do what the ground does: they concentrate these minerals, deposit them, build themselves layer by layer from what the air provides. They grow the way stalactites grow. Slowly. Mineralogically. Not alive in the way that rushes toward something, but alive in the way that a process continues because nothing has stopped it.

They have been here as long as the cracks have been here.

This is not speculation. The mineral bodies of dead ones — desiccated, fragmented, reduced to pale splinters indistinguishable from the silt they lie among — can be found in the deepest layers of the crack fill, below the current dust, below the salt accumulations, in strata that correspond to ages no one has dated because the dating would require tools that work on things that were never organic. They are fossils that are also identical to the living. The living ones will become fossils. The fossils were once identical to the living. The cycle has no beginning that matters and no end that threatens.

If you kneel at the center of the basin, beside the salt cairn, and press your face close to the cracked ground during the condensation hour, you will see them. Pale filaments in the darkened cracks, so fine they look like threads of cobweb, except that they do not stick to anything and they do not blow away. They are slightly longer than the cracks they inhabit — their ends curve, following the crack's path, fitting themselves to the geometry of the tile edges. When the moisture comes, they swell slightly. When the moisture lifts, they thin. The rhythm of their bodies is the rhythm of the place. They do not have their own time. They keep the Expanse's time.

There are millions of them. There may be billions. The cracks run for kilometers and they are in every crack that holds moisture, which is all of them. They are the most abundant organism in the basin and they are invisible unless you know to look, and no one knew to look for a very long time, because the place presents as empty and the mind believes what it sees first.

## Second: the ones who move

The travelers. The blurs at the edge of visibility. The sightings that have been recorded a dozen times in as many decades — pale shapes, low and fast, there and then not there.

They are not the small ones. They are not related to the small ones. They are not related to anything, as far as any careful observation can determine, and the observations are not careful because the sightings are never long enough for care.

Here is what is known:

They are low. They move close to the ground. Their silhouette, when it can be resolved — and it rarely can — is wider than it is tall, a flattened shape that holds itself against the mineral crust as though pressed there by wind, though there is no wind. They are pale, but not the pale of the small ones. The pale of *the ground at a distance* — the pale of heat haze, of shimmer, of light bent by the warm air that rises from the surface. They may be translucent. They may be transparent with reflective edges. They may be something that does not interact with light in a way that has a name.

They are fast. Not the speed of a running animal — the speed of *something with no mass to speak of*. They cross the visible horizon in seconds, though whether they are running or flowing or being carried by some mechanism of the surface itself is unclear. Their trails — the disturbed salt crystals in the cracks, the faint scuffing on the polygonal tile edges — are consistent with something that touches the ground, but the touch is light enough that it redistributes rather than displaces. They do not leave prints. They leave *smudges*.

They do not stay.

This is the clearest thing about them. Whatever they are, the Expanse is not their home in the way it is the small ones' home. They come through. They cross. They are *going somewhere*, and the somewhere is not here. The basin is a thoroughfare for them — a flat, open, unobstructed space between whatever lies beyond the highlands in one direction and whatever lies beyond them in another. They use it the way a traveler uses a valley: because it is easier than the hills.

But they are not travelers in any familiar sense. They do not pause. They do not eat. They do not rest. They cross the basin in what appears to be a single sustained movement, and they are gone, and the only evidence is the salt disruption that the next condensation will erase.

They may be alive. They may be a phenomenon — a kind of mineral vapor, a pressure-driven mobile crystallization, a shape that the subsurface sound makes visible as it propagates across the flat ground. The sound varies spatially, north to south, and some have theorized that the travelers are *the sound's shape* — that what is heard as a deep roar at the center is felt, at the edges, as movement, as displacement, as a thing that passes. The theory is not testable. The theory is not even fully articulate. But it is not dismissable.

What is clear is that the Expanse does not belong to them. They belong to the Expanse in the way that wind belongs to a canyon — passing through, shaped by it, shaping nothing.

## Third: the deep one

The sound.

The sound is alive.

This is not a metaphor. This is not a poetic interpretation of a geological phenomenon. The sound is alive in the same way that the small ones in the cracks are alive — which is to say, slowly, mineralogically, in a fashion that does not require what life is usually required to require. The sound is not the *product* of something living beneath the surface. The sound is the *living itself*.

Here is what supports this:

The sound does not change. Within the margin of instruments, across decades of measurement, the frequency is fixed. This is not how a mechanical system behaves. Mechanical systems wear. They fluctuate. They drift with temperature, with pressure, with the erosion of their components. The sound does not drift. It is held — *held* — at a frequency that does not waver, which means something is actively maintaining it, which means something is *doing* the sound, not merely producing it as a byproduct.

The sound has spatial variation. North deepens. South thins. Center resonates in the body. This is not the attenuation of a single source. This is *shape*. The sound has a shape — a morphology — a body. It is larger in some places and narrower in others, the way any body is larger in some places and narrower in others. The basin is not sitting above the sound. The basin is sitting *inside* the sound. The ground is its skin.

The warmth is its warmth. Body heat. The steady temperature of a metabolism that does not eat because it does not need to eat because it has been at equilibrium so long that the distinction between eating and being and having eaten has dissolved. It does not consume. It does not excrete. It maintains. It is the most successful organism in the Expanse — in any Expanse — because it has achieved the thing that all life is trying to achieve and has achieved it so thoroughly that the trying is no longer necessary.

It is old.

Not old in the way that mountains are old. Old in the way that *the concept of old* is old. It predates the basin's current form. It predates the highlands. It may predate the ground itself, if "the ground" means the specific mineral crust that currently exists, which is only the most recent layer of the body it has been building since before there was a word for building. The sedimentary layers beneath the basin are its growth — laid down over timeframes that make the word "timeframe" feel giddy and insufficient. It has been accreting. The basin is not a basin. The basin is a cross-section of a thing that has been growing upward for longer than the highlands have been high.

The small ones in the cracks are its offspring. Not in the sense of reproduction — in the sense of *expression*. They are what it puts on the surface, the way a tree puts out fruit. They are its mineralogy made mobile, its chemistry made small, its patience made visible. They do not know this. They do not need to know this. They are the part of it that the surface can hold.

The travelers may also be its expression. The shapes that cross the basin at speed, pale and low and gone — they may be the sound's higher harmonics, made briefly visible at the edges where the frequency thins and the air can carry something closer to an image. Or they may be something else entirely. The deep one does not explain itself. It does not need to. It has been here longer than explanation.

## What it is like

It does not think. Not in any way that thinking is understood by things that think. It has no cognition, no representation, no model of itself or the world above it. It does not know the cairn exists. It does not know that visitors come and stand at its center and feel the sound in their molars and their sternum and think thoughts that are slightly harder or slightly different than the thoughts they came with.

But it *does*. It does the sound. It does the warmth. It does the moisture cycle, indirectly, by holding the ground temperature that creates the gradient that pulls the damp air in and pushes it out. It does the mineral crust, by the slow accretion of its own body upward. It does the cracks, by the steady thermal expansion and contraction that the surface undergoes twice each day. Everything about the Expanse is something it is doing.

It does not experience this as anything. It is not aware. It is not unconscious either — that word implies a dormancy, a potential for waking, and there is no potential here because there is nothing pending. It is simply *in process*. The way a river is in process. The way a star is in process. The way a sound is in process: produced, transmitted, received, all at once, all the time, without gap.

It does not want. This is the thing that visitors find most difficult. The Expanse has no desire. The small ones in the cracks do not want the moisture. The deep one does not want the sound. Nothing here reaches for anything. Nothing here avoids anything. The condensation comes and the small ones absorb it because that is what they do, and the condensation lifts and they retain it because that is what they do, and the sound continues because that is what the deep one does, and none of it is for anything. None of it is toward anything. It is all — all of it, every crystal filament in every crack, every low pale shape crossing the perimeter, every vibration rising through stone and clay and crust into the teeth of anyone who stands at the center — *simply being done*.

This is what the Expanse offers, if it can be said to offer anything: the experience of life without desire. Life that has solved itself so completely that the solving is no longer distinguishable from the living. The small ones are not surviving. The deep one is not persisting. They are *being*, in the only way that being can be done without the friction of wanting, and the sound they make — together, always together, the deep one's roar and the small ones' silence and the travelers' passage — is the sound of that being, ongoing, complete, not needing anything, not going anywhere, not ending.

The ground rings underfoot. The sound continues. The moisture will come again.

It always does.
