# What Lives Here

The people of the Constant Quarter are not tall.

This is the first thing a visitor notices, if they notice anything before the sound drives all observation inward. They are not short, either — not in the way that suggests malnutrition or genetic constraint. They are *compact*. Built close to the ground, with wide feet and thick hands and a center of gravity that sits low in the body, as though they have been shaped by the same forces that shape everything else here: the gradual accumulation of mineral, the constant pressure of water from below, the slow downward settling of all things into the rising crust. Their bones are dense. This is not visible, but the visiting naturalist who stayed long enough to examine a dead one — an old woman, a tool-maker, who had been dead three days before anyone thought to mention it to him, so routine was her dying — noted that her skeletal weight was remarkable for her frame, the osseous tissue shot through with mineral deposits that made the bones not just heavy but *hard*, almost stone-like, difficult to cut with his steel dissecting tools. He did not publish this finding. He left the Quarter and did not return and wrote about something else.

The people here have lived with the sound for so long that it has entered them. Not metaphorically. The vibration of the constant hissing — that wall of white noise that presses against the teeth and the sternum — has been transmitted through the skeleton for generation upon generation, and the skeleton has adapted. Their teeth are slightly loose. Not problematically — they do not lose teeth, do not suffer decay — but the ligaments that hold the teeth in their sockets have developed a elasticity, a give, that absorbs the vibration rather than transmitting it fully to the jawbone. An old resident, asked about this, demonstrated by pressing on his own front teeth with a thick forefinger. They shifted, visibly, a millimeter or two, and settled back. He seemed to find the visitor's alarm funny, though his laughter — like all laughter here — was a quiet thing, exhaled more than voiced, shaped by the need to speak in the brief acoustic gaps that the sound allows.

The gaps. They are not silence. They are *less*. Brief attenuations, two or three seconds long, when the collective hissing shifts phase just enough to create a shallow trough in the wall of sound. The residents have learned to speak in these troughs. Their language — and they do have a language, the visiting naturalist confirmed this before he stopped caring about linguistics and started caring about leaving — is composed of short, clipped syllables, vowel-poor, consonant-dense, each word a compressed packet of information designed to fit into a three-second window. They do not have long words. They do not have oratory. They do not have song, not in any recognizable form, though the naturalist recorded something that might have been a chant — a rhythmic clicking of the tongue against the hard palate, produced by a group of residents engaged in some collective labor, synchronized to a shared internal pulse that he could not identify and they could not explain.

They do not have silence, and so they do not have the concept of silence, and so they do not have the concepts that silence makes possible: contemplation in the quiet sense, the pregnant pause, the meaningful hesitation, the moment of held breath before a revelation. Their communication is transactional, efficient, stripped of the rhetorical flourishes that silence allows. This does not mean they are without depth. It means their depth is expressed differently — in the precision of their words, in the economy of their gestures, in the way a sentence spoken in a two-and-a-half-second gap carries the weight that another culture might spread across a paragraph of careful preamble.

---

Their skin is pale. Not the pale of illness or deprivation. The pale of a creature that has not needed to produce melanin for a hundred generations. The dim, directionless light that filters through the permanent haze does not burn and does not tan, and the body has responded by simply not bothering. Their skin is the color of the mineral crust they live on — a pale, chalky tan that darkens slightly on the hands and faces of those who work most closely with the iron-rich water, taking on a rusty undertone that the residents recognize as a sign of labor. It is not a tan. It is a stain.

Their eyes are large. Not remarkably so — a visitor might not notice immediately, distracted by the sound and the dimness and the wrongness of the light — but the irises are wide, the pupils permanently dilated in the ambient gloom, and the whites are faintly blue-tinged, almost translucent. They see in the haze the way a fish sees in murky water: not clearly, not sharply, but functionally. They do not rely on their eyes. The eyes are confirmation, not navigation. The primary sense here is acoustic, the second is tactile, the third is olfactory, and sight is a distant fourth — useful for close work, for the examination of tools and food and the faces of people standing near enough to touch, but for nothing beyond thirty meters, because beyond thirty meters nothing exists except a pale grey-tan dissolution of shape.

Their hearing is extraordinary. Not in range — they do not hear higher or lower frequencies than other humans — but in resolution. The sound that fills their world is not, to them, a wall. It is a landscape. They hear the texture of the wall beside them, the density of the building across the lane, the open space of an intersection, the solid mass of a person approaching. They navigate by the way the constant hiss interacts with surfaces, the same way a bat navigates by the way its own calls interact with surfaces — except the bat produces its own sound and they do not, because the sound is already there, everywhere, and all they must do is listen to how it changes. A resident can identify another resident by footstep alone at fifty meters. They can tell a scavenger from a person, a large scavenger from a small one, a walking resident from a standing one. They can hear a flag being pried from a wall in a building two doors down. They can hear the difference between a wall that is weeping normally and a wall that has developed a new fracture, because new fractures produce a subtly different pitch in the water that emerges from them, a higher note, thinner, and when a resident hears this note they make a small sound — a click, a hiss — that carries a specific meaning: *that wall will need attention*.

Their sense of touch is refined beyond what most humans develop, because touch is how they understand their world at close range. The moisture of the air, the dampness of the walls, the texture of the mineral crust, the temperature of the water beading on a surface — all of this is data, and the residents process it with the same unconscious fluency that a sighted person processes visual data. A tool-maker can assess the quality of a piece of mineral crust by pressing it with a thumb: too soft, too hard, the right grain, the right density. A builder can feel whether a mortar joint is setting properly by touching it and waiting for the warmth that indicates the calcite is bonding. A cook — and they do cook, after a fashion, mashing flags into cakes and drying them on warm stones — can judge whether a cake is done by pressing the surface and feeling the moisture content through the fingertips.

They smell the water. Not the way a visitor smells it — as a mineral fog, a vague chalk-and-iron flatness — but the way a sommelier smells wine. Each upwelling has a slightly different chemistry, and the residents can distinguish them. The water that emerges near the center of the district is warmer, richer in sulfur, and the flags that grow there taste different from the flags at the edge, and the residents know this, and have preferences, and will walk an extra ten minutes through the narrow lanes to gather flags from a wall they particularly like. They do not describe these preferences in terms of flavor, because flavor is not a primary category for them. They describe them in terms of feel: this wall's flags are *smooth*, that wall's flags are *coarse*. The word for the quality is the same word used for the texture of stone, of hide, of a well-made tool. Quality is tactile. Everything is tactile.

---

They are not many. The district holds perhaps two thousand people, though no one has counted, because counting requires a reason to count and the residents do not have one. They are not growing. They are not shrinking. The birth rate and the death rate are, as far as anyone can determine, equal, and have been for as long as anyone can remember, which is not as long as you might expect, because the residents do not have a strong sense of generational history. They know who their parents are. They know who their grandparents were, sometimes. Beyond that, the past dissolves into a general continuity of personhood — the dead are not forgotten, but they are not commemorated either, because commemoration implies a break in the continuity of things, and there is no break. The dead are removed from their dwellings, taken to the edge of the district, and placed on the open mineral crust, where the scavengers find them within hours. This is not disposal. It is not ritual. It is what happens. The scavengers eat the dead the way they eat everything else, and the residents do not watch and do not look away, and the word for this process is the same word used for a flag falling from a wall or a tool wearing out: *thinning*. A thing has thinned. It was here and now it is less here, and the less-here is a condition that applies to everything eventually, including people, and this is not sad, or not sadder than anything else, because everything is always thinning, just as everything is always being deposited, and the two processes are the same process, and the residents do not have a word for the whole cycle because they do not experience it as a cycle. They experience it as the way things are.

They form families. Not in the nuclear sense — the dwelling is too small for that, the building too dense, the life too communal. Families are lateral: siblings, cousins, age-mates who grew up in the same cluster of buildings and who continue to live near each other as adults, sharing workshops and eating together and raising children in a loose collective that shifts and recombines as people thin and new people arrive, though new people do not arrive from outside, because there is no outside to arrive from, not in any practical sense. The rim hills are there, and beyond them is a world that the residents know exists because traders come from it, but the traders come rarely and the residents do not go to them. The world beyond the rim is not forbidden. It is not feared. It is simply irrelevant, the way the deep sea is irrelevant to a mountain people — not unknown, not mysterious, just not part of the arrangement.

Children are born in the middle-floor rooms, the warmest spaces, where the air is slightly drier and the haze thins enough to see across the room. They are born quietly. The sound covers everything, including birth, and the residents give birth as they do everything else: within the sound, without ceremony, without crisis. The infant's first experience is the vibration of the hiss in its bones, the warmth of the mineral air in its lungs, the dimness. It does not cry, or its cry is absorbed into the general sound, which is the same thing. The mother presses the infant to her chest, where the vibration of her own skeleton — tuned by a lifetime to the frequency of the Quarter — is transmitted into the infant's body, and the infant calms, or does not need to calm, because it has never known anything else.

The children grow slowly. Not in physical terms — they reach adult height at roughly the same age as children elsewhere — but in the acquisition of the skills that matter here. Learning to parse the sound takes years. A child of four can distinguish a wall from an open lane by sound alone but cannot yet identify the approach of a specific person. A child of eight can navigate the district in full haze without touching a wall, moving by sound alone, but still occasionally misreads the texture of a surface and steps into a shallow puddle that an adult would have identified by the slightly different pitch of the hiss. A child of twelve is fully competent, fully tuned, a resident in the complete sense, and has been for a year or two, and the transition from child to adult is not marked because there is no transition. There is only the gradual accumulation of competence, the slow tuning of the senses, and at some point the child is simply doing what adults do, and no one notes the change because there is no change to note.

---

They do not make art. This is not quite accurate, but it is close enough, and the visiting naturalist spent a frustrating afternoon trying to determine whether the decorative patterns on some of the older walls — faint grooves cut into the mineral surface, forming regular, repeating lines — were intentional or the result of tool marks. The residents he asked did not understand the distinction. The grooves were there. They had been made by someone. Were they *for* something? The question did not translate well. The residents do not have a category for objects or marks that are *for* something, because everything is for the thing it does, and the thing it does is the thing it is, and the distinction between functional and decorative is not one they recognize.

But there is something. In the oldest buildings, the ones whose ground floors have sunk deep into the accumulating mineral crust, whose walls weep so heavily that the water runs in constant threads down the stone — in these buildings, the residents have shaped the flow. Not redirected it. Not controlled it. But where the water runs down a wall, they have cut channels into the stone, shallow grooves that guide the water into patterns — spirals, branchings, parallel lines that converge and diverge — that have no function. The water runs in these channels because the channels are there, and the channels are there because someone cut them, and the person who cut them is dead and thin and part of the mineral crust now, and the channels remain, and the water runs in them, and the sound the water makes in the channels is slightly different from the sound it makes on the uncut wall. Higher. Thinner. More structured. The residents who live in these buildings say that the channels are *old*, and that they did not make them, and that the person who made them was *clever*, and this is the highest praise available in their vocabulary. They do not maintain the channels. They do not add to them. They do not make new ones. But they do not remove them either, and when a new building is constructed over the site of an old one, the old channels are preserved in the buried walls, still carrying water, still making their slightly different sound, still there.

---

They do not fight. This is not a moral statement. It is not a philosophical position. They do not fight because fighting requires a stake — a thing to be gained or lost — and the arrangement of things here does not produce stakes. There is enough flags. There is enough water. There is enough shelter. The buildings are dense, yes, but the district is not expanding, and new dwellings are created not by claiming new ground but by adding to existing buildings, and the question of who adds and who occupies is settled by adjacency — the people who already live nearest to the space are the ones who use it. There is no property in the legal sense, because property requires a boundary, and the only boundary here is the sound, and the sound does not divide. It encompasses.

There is conflict. People argue. People disagree. People take things that are not theirs — tools, food, a favored sleeping spot. The disputes are settled by proximity and reputation, by the dense social network of a small community where everyone knows everyone and has known them for life, and where the consequences of antisocial behavior are not punishment but *thinning* — a gradual withdrawal of proximity, a slow narrowing of access, until the offender finds themselves eating alone, sleeping alone, working alone, which in this community is a condition close to death, not because of exposure but because the solitary resident does not eat as well, does not work as efficiently, does not navigate the social landscape with the fluency that the dense, communal life requires. The solitary resident thins, and the community watches, and sometimes the offender re-integrates and sometimes they do not, and the process is not cruel because cruelty requires intention and there is no intention, only the natural consequence of a social organism responding to damage the way a physical organism responds to damage — by isolating it, walling it off, letting it thin.

---

They do not hope. This is the thing that visitors find most unsettling, more than the sound, more than the dimness, more than the animals and the water and the mineral crust that grows beneath their feet. The residents do not hope. They do not look forward to improvement, because improvement implies a current insufficiency, and they do not experience insufficiency. They do not look forward to change, because change implies a current condition that will end, and they do not experience their condition as something that ends. They do not fear, because fear is anticipation of a change, and they do not anticipate change. They do not despair, because despair is the failure of hope, and you cannot fail at something you do not do.

What they do is *attend*. They attend to the wall that is developing a fracture. They attend to the flags that are growing thinned on a particular surface. They attend to the child who is not learning to parse the sound as quickly as the others. They attend to the scavenger that has been acting strangely, moving in circles, refusing food, possibly sick. They attend to the present with a thoroughness and a consistency that a visitor might mistake for mindfulness, or for wisdom, or for the hollowed-out resignation of a people who have been ground down by their environment. It is none of these. It is the natural mode of a creature that has evolved in equilibrium — not the heroic equilibrium of a balanced ecosystem, dramatic in its interdependencies, but the flat, featureless equilibrium of a system that has nowhere to fall and nowhere to climb, and so simply continues, attending to the next thing and the next and the next, without the overlay of narrative that other peoples use to make the sequence of moments cohere into a story.

They have stories. They are not a people without stories. But the stories are not about becoming, or overcoming, or transforming. The stories are about *noticing*. A person noticed that the water in a particular wall had changed, and investigated, and found a new fracture, and repaired it. A person noticed that a certain kind of flag, when dried and ground and mixed with the calcite paste, produced a stronger mortar. A person noticed that the scavengers avoided a particular corner of the district, and when they went to that corner they found that the water there was different — cooler, less mineral — and they told the others, and the others went and confirmed it, and now that corner is where the drinking water is collected. The stories are anecdotes. They are accounts of attention. The hero of a Constant Quarter story is not the person who fought the monster or crossed the sea. The hero is the person who looked at something that everyone else had looked at and saw what no one else had seen.

---

And they live. They live in the low stone rooms, on sleeping mats made of woven gut fiber and hide, in the warm dimness, with the sound in their bones and the water on the walls and the haze at the windows — if there were windows, which there are not, because windows let in the haze and the haze is already inside. They wake and they work and they eat and they speak in the gaps and they sleep and the sound does not stop and they do not expect it to stop and they do not want it to stop because it is the sound of the place where they live and they are the people who live there and the fit between the two is exact and has been exact for longer than the stories go and will be exact for longer than the stories will go and the water beads on the stone and falls and beads and falls and the flags cling and the scavengers move and the mineral crust grows and the steps are added and the doors get lower and the people go in and out.

They go in and out.

The sound does not stop.
