# What Lives Here

---

The first thing to understand about the gravi is that they are not incomplete. There is a temptation, standing on the causeway with the file in your pack and the assumptions of your taxonomy also in your pack, to see them as transitional — a species on its way to becoming something else, a draft, a first sketch of life on land that has not yet decided what it wants to be. The Drumplain itself invites this reading. New ground, new water, new air. Everything here is provisional. The moss has been growing for twenty years and covers a square meter. The grass stops at fifteen centimeters as though it has reached the limit of what is reasonable. The lichen is winning a war no one is fighting anymore. Everything here is in medias res, and it is easy to extend that condition to the animals and call them unfinished.

They are not unfinished. They are *adequate.*

Which is a different thing, and a harder one to recognize if you come from a world where adequacy is not considered a terminal state.

---

## Body

Consider the gravi as a solution to a problem. The problem is this: how do you live on a surface that is wet, that is unstable, that is slowly rising, that cracks without warning, that is colder than the air around it, and that breathes?

The gravi answer this with mass.

An adult gravi weighs between four and six hundred kilograms — the survey file estimates, but the estimates are imprecise because no one has successfully weighed one. They do not tolerate close approach by human investigators, and the few attempts to tranquilize individuals for study resulted in what the file describes, with characteristic restraint, as "structural damage to equipment." They are not aggressive. They are *committed* — to their territory, their rhythm, their routine — and a tranquilized gravi does not fall down. It simply continues what it was doing with diminished coordination, and what it was doing, in the survey team's case, was walking through a measurement apparatus. Through, not around.

The mass is low-slung. The body is a long oval — perhaps two and a half meters from the front of the head to the base of the hindmost legs — and it sits close to the ground, the highest point of the spine rising no more than sixty centimeters above the basalt. The legs are the most immediately striking feature: six of them, arranged in three pairs along the body's length, each one short, thick, and splayed at angles that would look pathological on any other animal and that on the gravi look *engineered*. The angles are not random. Each pair is offset slightly from the pair ahead and behind it, so that the footfalls never align in a single track, and the weight of the animal is distributed across six points of contact at all times, even when one leg is in mid-stride.

This matters because of the basalt. The slabs of the Drumplain are layered, thin, and lubricated by the water that weeps between them. A heavy animal with concentrated weight — two or four legs, narrow stance — would crack the slabs beneath it. The gravi's weight is spread so thin that the pressure under any single foot is less than the pressure of a human boot heel. They can walk anywhere on the plain. They do walk anywhere on the plain. They go where the grass is and the grass is where the water pools and the water pools where the basalt has settled, and the gravi cross the settled ground without disturbing it, their pad-feet resting on stone that would crack under a lighter animal moving faster.

The pads themselves are worth a paragraph. They are wide — fifteen centimeters across, roughly circular, slightly convex — and they are not hard. They are a dense, fibrous tissue, almost cartilaginous, covered in a surface that is rough but not rigid, something like the feel of a wet callus, something like the feel of unwaxed leather. When a gravi steps, the pad compresses, spreading its contact area by perhaps thirty percent, absorbing the impact of the stride without transmitting vibration to the stone beneath. The animals are silent walkers. On the causeway, you can hear your own footsteps. You cannot hear theirs. You feel them — a soft percussive displacement in the carbon-laminate, transmitted up through the ribbon's material into your own feet — but the sound is so low, so distributed, that it registers as sensation rather than noise.

They are, in this sense, the opposite of drums. The Drumplain is a drum — a vast taut membrane of stone, resonant, waiting to be struck. The gravi are the thing that lives on a drum without striking it. They are muffled. They are dampened. Their entire morphology is a sustained act of quiet.

The pelage is dense and short — a pelt of coarse hairs, each one perhaps two centimeters long, lying flat against the body in a direction that channels water downward and backward, away from the skin. The color is the color of the basalt: blue-black, slate-dark, deepening to near-purple when wet, which it always is. The condensation that forms on the gravi's body does not bead and roll off; it is absorbed into the grain of the fur and held there, a permanent sheen of moisture that the animal carries with it like a second skin. In the reflected light of the pools, when a gravi passes between you and the water, its body catches the light from below and carries it, and for a moment the animal is outlined — rimmed in silver, luminous, a dark shape lit by the ground it walks on.

The head is broad and flat, barely distinguished from the body in profile — a widening of the anterior end, not a raised cranium, giving the gravi the silhouette of something designed by wind rather than by vertebrae. The eyes are low, wide-set, half-shaded by the bony shelf of the brow, and they are dark — not the dark of pigment but the dark of depth, deep-set, recessed, protected from the horizontal wind and the reflected glare of the wet stone. The gravi do not look up. There is nothing above them that matters. Their world is the ground, the water, the grass, the stone, and their visual field is a wide horizontal band, perhaps thirty degrees above and below the plane of their head, extending in an almost complete circle around them. They see everything that is their height or lower. They see almost nothing that is not.

The mouth is the other remarkable feature. It is wide — nearly the full width of the head — and it is forward-facing in a way that most grazing mouths are not. Most grazers have lateral mouths, broad and side-mounted, designed to crop grass as the head swings from side to side. The gravi's mouth is anterior. It bites forward, like a turtle's, like a fish's, and it is designed for two actions: cropping the pale grass at the pool edges, which it does with a broad, scissor-like motion of the lower jaw, and scraping the mineral film from the surface of the interstitial pools, which it does by extending a rough, almost geologic tongue across the water's surface, lifting the iridescent layer without breaking the water beneath it.

This second action is the one the survey team found most difficult to describe. The tongue is dark, wide, flat, and it has a texture that is not quite biological — a surface like fine-grained sandstone, like pumice, rough enough to lift the mineral film without dispersing it, smooth enough not to disturb the water below. The gravi's tongue is, in effect, a geological instrument. It scrapes the earth's breath from the earth's surface and metabolizes it. The compounds it harvests — silicates, transition metals, the dissolved vocabulary of the deep rock — are not available from any plant on the plain. The gravi eat the stone's secretion the way a ruminant eats salt: not as food but as *necessity*, as the thing the body cannot build itself and must therefore harvest from the world.

And the waste — the chemically altered residue of the gravi's digestion — is deposited across the basalt as the animals move. It is dark, it is thin, it dries to a film almost identical in color to the stone itself, and it is the beginning of soil. The moss grows in it. The grass extends its runners through it. The mineral film in the pools is modified by it, its chemical composition shifted, its iridescence subtly altered. The gravi are not grazing on the plain. They are *manufacturing* it. They are eating the landscape and excreting its next version, and they have been doing this for long enough — how long? the file does not say; the file does not know — that the city sits on a layer of their making as much as on the basalt's.

## Behavior

The gravi do not have leaders.

This needs to be said plainly because it is the thing that is hardest to see when you watch them. A column of sixty-wide gravi moving across the basalt in a single direction, their pace uniform, their heading unwavering, looks like a military formation. It looks like a migration. It looks like a procession with a purpose and a destination and someone at the front who knows where they are going.

There is no one at the front. The front is not the front. It is the *edge* — the leading edge of a movement that is happening everywhere in the column at once, each animal responding to the same signal at the same moment, the signal arriving not from ahead but from *below*, through the basalt, through the breathing, through the subsonic frequency that every gravi is emitting and every gravi is receiving at all times.

The signal is the ground.

The Drumplain breathes four minutes up, four minutes down, and the gravi breathe with it. Not literally — their respiration is faster, shallower, a steady bellows-action in the low chest. But their *communication* breathes with the ground. The subsonic rumbles they produce — deep, below the threshold of human hearing, felt in the sternum and the basalt rather than heard in the air — are timed to the geological rhythm. When the ground rises, their signal shifts in frequency. When the ground falls, it shifts again. The entire population is synchronized to a metronome that no one set and no one maintains, a clock that is the earth's own diastole, and the gravi are its congregation.

What they say with this signal — if *say* is the right word for a communication that is more chemical than linguistic, more weather than language — is not known. The file speculates: direction, proximity, identity, reproductive status. But the file is clear that these are guesses. What is observed is this: the gravi move together, they graze together, they rest together, they build together, and they do all of it without any visible signal, any hierarchical structure, any individual that can be identified as dominant or directive. The column does not have a leader because the column does not need a leader. The column is a single thing expressed in thousands of bodies, the way a wave is a single thing expressed in thousands of water molecules, and the wave does not vote.

This is not instinct, exactly. Instinct is the body's memory of what worked before. The gravi's collective behavior is closer to *computation* — a distributed processing of environmental data (where is the water, where is the grass, where is the stable stone, where are the young, where is the city) that takes place across thousands of nervous systems simultaneously and resolves into action without any single system containing the whole solution. Each gravi knows a little. Together, they know enough.

The city is the output of this knowing.

## The City, Again

Watch a gravi push a slab.

The slab is perhaps a meter and a half long, a meter wide, and ten centimeters thick — a page from the basalt book, separated by water and stress from its neighbors, lying flat on the surface of the plain. A gravi approaches it, aligns its body against the slab's edge, and leans. Not pushes — *leans*. The way a tree leans in wind. The way water leans against a dam. A slow, patient, continuous application of pressure, the gravi's weight shifting forward onto the slab, the pad-feet gripping the wet basalt beneath, the muscles in the low chest and the broad shoulders working with a steady, unhurried force that has no sharp edges in it.

Nothing happens. For a long time, nothing happens. The slab does not move.

Then another gravi arrives. It aligns itself beside the first. It leans.

Then another. And another.

The file describes what it calls "accumulation events" — groups of gravi assembling at a slab edge, one by one, over hours or days, each one adding its weight to the push without any visible signal passing between them. They do not face each other. They do not touch. They simply arrive, align, and lean, as though the slab itself were calling them, as though the stress in the stone were a sound they could hear through their feet.

And then the slab moves. Not dramatically. Not suddenly. It shifts — perhaps five centimeters, perhaps ten — in the direction the gravi are leaning, and the sound it makes is a deep, resonant *tone*, a note struck on the Drumplain's drum, and the note travels through the basalt at a frequency the gravi can hear and the survey team's instruments can measure, and somewhere — perhaps kilometers away — other gravi feel the note and begin to move.

The slab settles into its new position. The gravi leave. The moss will grow in the gap. The water will find the crack. The mineral film will form. And in a year, or three, or ten, the slab will be part of a wall, part of a shelter, part of the city's slow expansion, and no gravi alive will remember the day it was moved, and no gravi needs to.

This is how the city is built. Not by architects but by *leaning*. The same behavior that moves a gravi from one pool to another — the same instinct, the same subsonic synchronization, the same response to the ground's breathing — moves the stone. The city is not a project. It is a habit. It is what the gravi do the way a river does what a river does: because the ground tells it to, because the water finds the path, because the weight of the thing, accumulated, is enough.

The structures are not arbitrary. The interlocking patterns — the slabs leaned against each other, the walls that emerge from the plain's own geometry — are not designed but *solved*. The gravi push the stone until the stone finds its lowest-energy configuration, the arrangement that the basalt's own layering and the plain's own contours make most stable. The buildings are what the stone wants to be when pushed. The gravi are the push, and the stone is the shape, and the city is the conversation between them.

And the covered walkways — the carbon-laminate roofs over the narrow passages — these are different. These are *found*. The gravi do not manufacture carbon-laminate. They do not grow it. They encounter it — fragments of the causeway's material, pieces that have cracked free over time, or perhaps remnants of the original seeding process, scattered across the plain's surface by whatever deposited the causeway in the first place — and they push it, too. They push it the way they push the basalt. They lean against it and it moves, and it ends up over a passage, and the passage becomes a walkway, and the walkway becomes a route, and the route becomes part of the city's *song* — because the walkways are the instruments, and the wind is the player, and the notes that sound when the air moves through the passages are part of the gravi's acoustic environment, part of the frequency map they navigate by.

Whether they compose these notes intentionally — whether they alter the walkways to change the tones, whether they are *making music* — is the question that the survey team's ethologist circled in red ink in the margin of her report and then, apparently, stopped writing. The report ends mid-sentence. The file does not indicate whether she returned to the Drumplain. The file does not indicate whether anyone has returned.

## The Young

The young are small.

This sounds obvious, but on the Drumplain, where the adult gravi are low and wide and substantial, the young are *noticeably* small — almost disarmingly so, in the way that anything newborn is disarming when its context is geological. An adult gravi fits the plain the way a boulder fits a talus slope: it belongs there, its weight is the ground's weight extended. A young gravi — perhaps thirty kilograms, perhaps forty, standing barely forty centimeters at the shoulder — looks like a mistake. Like something that has wandered out of scale. Like a pebble in a field of flagstone.

They stay in the city. This is the function the city serves most clearly: the young are sheltered in the spaces between the basalt structures, in the covered walkways, in the narrow passages where the wind cannot reach and the condensation is thickest and the stone is warmest from the ground's breathing. The young do not graze. They nurse — and here the file becomes uncertain again, because the survey team was unable to determine the mechanism of nursing. There are mammary structures on the female gravi — four of them, ventral, arranged in two pairs along the body's undersides — but the young do not appear to attach in the manner of most mammalian nursing. Instead, they stand close to the mother's underside, in the warm space between her legs and the ground, and they drink from the condensation that forms on her body — the moisture that beads on her pelage and runs down her fur in thin streams, mineral-rich, chemically altered by her metabolism, a fluid that is not milk but that serves the function milk serves.

The young grow slowly. The file estimates — and the estimates are preliminary, based on a single observation cycle — that a gravi reaches adult size in approximately eight years. Eight years of standing in the city, in the shelter of the walls, drinking the condensed sweat of the adults around them, listening to the ground breathe. Eight years of learning the frequency map. Eight years of absorbing the city's geometry, the routes, the rhythms, the way the wind sounds in the walkways and the way the stone sounds when it is pushed.

If the gravi have culture — and the survey team's ethologist believed they might, and circled this question in red ink as well — then the young are where it lives. Not in the adults, who are complete, who are adequate, who are the finished expression of a system that does not need finishing. In the young, who are unfinished, who are still becoming, who stand in the warm spaces between the walls and listen to the city's song and learn, somehow, without language, without instruction, without anything that a human would recognize as pedagogy, the shape of the place they will spend their lives building.

## What They Are Not

They are not domesticated. The causeway was not built for them — it was built for something else, something that has not arrived or has departed, and the gravi use it only incidentally, and rarely. They do not approach it. The lichen grows on it because the gravi do not.

They are not sacred, though standing among them, feeling their subsonic calls in your sternum, watching the column move across the basalt in the reflected light of the pools, it is easy to understand why the word comes to mind. They are animals. They eat and excrete and mate and die. Their waste becomes soil. Their bodies, when they die — which they do, though the file contains no estimate of lifespan — their bodies become part of the basalt's chemistry, the minerals returning to the stone they spent their lives pushing, the cycle closing the way cycles close in a place where everything is made of the same few materials and nothing is wasted because nothing has yet learned how to be wasted.

They are not the end of the story. The Drumplain is rising. The file notes this with a precision that feels, in context, like grief: the altitude increases by roughly the height of a human knee each year. The air is getting thinner. The condensation layer is getting colder. The pools freeze harder each winter. The mineral film is changing composition as the pressure beneath the dome shifts and different strata are exposed. The grass is not expanding its range. The moss is not accelerating. Everything here is living at the edge of what is possible, and the edge is moving — upward, where the air is thinner and the light is sharper and the ground is colder — and the gravi are moving with it, or they are not.

The file does not predict. The file observes. But standing on the causeway, with the condensation soaking your sleeves and the ground breathing under your feet and the gravi's subsonic calls tightening your chest, you can feel the trajectory. The plain is rising. The air is thinning. The city is growing. The gravi are adequate, and they are building, and the ground is breathing, and the stone is still arriving.

And somewhere — in the file, in the margins of the report, in the red ink that the ethologist stopped writing — there is a question that no one has answered, and the question is this:

When the plateau rises high enough that the air is too thin and the water freezes solid and the grass stops growing and the mineral film stops forming and the basalt stops breathing —

*what will the gravi build then?*
