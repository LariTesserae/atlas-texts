# What Lives Here

## The People

They do not stand straight. That is the first thing. Not from deformity, not from deficiency — from *habit*. The ceilings are low where the upper floors lean inward, and the stairways are narrow where the walls have shifted, and the doorframes tilt at angles that would be comical if they were not so constant. The people of Vossholm have learned to move through their city the way the city moves through its ground — slightly crooked, slightly accommodating, always ready to adjust. Their posture is not broken. It is *negotiated*. You can spot an outsider by the way they hold their shoulders — squared, upright, expecting a plumb line that does not exist here. The locals carry themselves like reeds. Like something that bends because bending is how you stay.

They are not tall. This is generational, and it is recent — only a century and a half of Vossholm, only a few generations, but the body responds to its environment faster than the old texts would have predicted. The women average just above five feet. The men a few inches more. Broad hands. Thick wrists. The kind of build that comes not from labor alone but from labor in cold — the body insulating itself, packing on mass against a dampness that pulls heat from the flesh. Their frames are compact, dense, economical. Nothing wasted. Nothing decorative. They look, from a distance, like a people designed by a committee that had heard about humans but had never met one — close enough to be familiar, far enough to be *wrong* in ways you cannot immediately name.

The skin is the second thing. It is pale, but it is a specific pale — not the delicate porcelain of the aristocratic north but a thick, weathered pallor, mottled in places, rough-textured, with a quality that suggests not so much fairness as *absence of sun*. The basin sees the sky, but the sky sees little of the basin. The smoke and the damp filter what light arrives, and the light that penetrates is weak, indirect, green-gray. The people carry the color of their weather in their faces. A ruddiness at the cheeks and noses, not from health but from the constant low irritation of cold and wet. Their lips are often cracked. They use a salve — rendered fat, beeswax, something herbal that smells of camphor — and they apply it without self-consciousness, in conversation, at market, during transactions, the way another people might gesture.

Their eyes are adapted. You realize this slowly. Vossholm is dark — not the darkness of night but the permanent, ambient, twilight dimness of streets that are covered and buildings that lean and light that must pass through moss and condensation before it arrives. The pupils of the locals are larger than yours. Not dramatically, not alarmingly, but measurably, if you were to measure, which you are not inclined to do because looking into their eyes closely enough to notice is an act of intimacy they have not invited. The irises are pale — gray, pale blue, pale green, sometimes a muddy amber that seems to hold the color of the tannin-stained water. The pale eyes open wider in the dimness. They see what you cannot. You stumble over loose cobblestones and they do not, and it is not only because they know the streets. It is because the streets are *visible* to them in a way they are not to you.

Their hair is straight or wavy, never curly. Dark in the older districts — a brown so deep it reads as black in the dim light — and lighter in the higher districts, where a bit more light reaches and the genetics have wandered, as genetics will. The women wear it long, braided, pinned up in styles that are practical first and ornamental second. The men wear it shorter, sometimes oiled with something that smells of rendered fat and resin. Beards are common among the men, and they are not groomed — they are *managed*, trimmed where they interfere with work, left where they do not. The grønnfolk — the green-people, the vegetation engineers — often have lichen in their beards. They do not remove it. It is a mark of their trade, like the calluses on a builder's hands or the chemical stains on a tanner's forearms.

## The Dialect

They speak low. This is the thing you notice first about their voices and the thing you understand last. The volume is not shyness. It is not secrecy. It is *architecture*. The narrow streets carry sound in ways that make normal speech feel invasive — a word spoken at ordinary volume bounces between close walls, arrives at ears that did not ask for it, becomes a trespass. So the people of Vossholm have developed a register of speech that is almost subvocal, a murmuring drone that carries within the three-meter radius of a conversation and no further. You learn to lean in. You learn to listen with your whole body, because the words are sometimes more felt than heard, vibrations in the damp air more than articulated sounds.

The language is a descendant of something coastal — you catch fragments of the old tongue in the suffixes and the consonant clusters — but it has diverged, the way the basin has diverged from the landscape around it. The vowels have flattened. The consonants have softened where the architecture requires it and hardened where the water requires it. There are words that exist only in Vossholm, words for conditions that do not occur elsewhere. *Grunnvilkår* — the ground-conditions, the terms under which existence is being negotiated. *Loose ground* — the soft patches, the undecided substrate. *Bunnfolk* — the things in the water. *Grønnfolk* — the vegetation engineers. *Byggfolk* — the builders, the largest trade, the immune system. These are not dialect words, exactly. They are *ecological* words — words for things that exist only in this place, because the place itself is the condition that makes them necessary.

The grammar is unusual. It has a tense — or rather, a mood — that you have not encountered before. It is used for things that are true now but may not be true later, not because they will change but because the *ground* beneath them may change. *The wall is straight* becomes *the wall is straight-for-now*, or more precisely, *the wall holds its straightness under current grunnvilkår*. This is not pessimism. It is precision. The people of Vossholm do not say things are stable when they are not. They do not say things are permanent when the ground beneath them is still deciding. The mood is used for buildings, for streets, for alliances, for agreements, for health, for the weather, for the political situation, for the continued existence of the market stall you are standing in front of. It is the grammar of a people who live in the provisional, and it is so deeply embedded in the language that they do not know they are using it, and when you ask about it they give you the look — *yes, and?*

They do not name their children at birth. This is the thing that takes you longest to learn, and when you learn it, it rearranges everything you thought you understood. A child is born, and the child is called by a diminutive — *little one*, *this one*, *the new one* — and the diminutive holds until the child's character has *settled*, which is to say until the child has demonstrated, over several years, what kind of person they are becoming in the context of the grunnvilkår they have been born into. Then a name is chosen, and the naming is done by consensus — the family, the neighbors, the district — and the name is not a label but a *description*, a word that captures how the child relates to the ground it stands on. The names are not translatable. They are not *River* or *Stone* or *Dawn*. They are more like *the one who builds where the ground is soft* or *the one who listens to the water before deciding* or *the one who grows where nothing else will*. These are not metaphorical. They are literal. They are job descriptions, ecological assessments, predictions of function.

When a person changes — and people change, because the ground changes and the person must change with it — the name can change. This is not common, but it is not rare. A builder who becomes a green-person takes a new name. A market vendor who becomes a well-keeper takes a new name. The old name is not forgotten. It is retained, like the buried streets beneath the current streets, a layer in the stratigraphy of a person's life. People will say, *She was called X before the shift in Lekkve, and now she is called Y.* The old name is a coordinate. It tells you where the person *was*, in the geography of the city's constant becoming.

## The Trades

The byggfolk are the largest and most visible trade. They are the ones you see everywhere — on the scaffolding that is permanent in some districts, in the basements where foundations are being reinforced, in the streets where cobblestones are being re-laid after a subsidence. They work in crews of four or five, and they work in a silence that is almost ritual. One holds, one braces, one mortars, one checks. The checking is constant — they have instruments, spirit levels and plumb bobs, but they also have a sense, a feel, a reading of the ground through their boots that tells them when the substrate is shifting and when it is holding. The byggfolk are respected in a way that is almost reverential. They are the ones who keep the city upright, and the city knows it. A byggfolk crew passing through a market is given right of way without being asked.

The grønnfolk are the ones who work with the vegetation. They are harder to spot — they work on walls and rooftops and in the drainage channels, and their work is less visible because it is less structural. But it is no less essential. The moss and the ferns and the alders and the willows are not ornamental. They are *erosion control*. Their roots hold the substrate. Their growth fills the cracks that would otherwise widen. Their presence is a *stabilization*, and the grønnfolk are the engineers of that stabilization. They know which plants to encourage where, which to remove, which to redirect. They know the language of roots — they can read the cracks in a wall and know which ones are being held together by root systems and which ones are being pulled apart. They carry tools that are not like other tools — small blades, trowels, but also pots of *seed-slurry*, a mixture of moss spores and silt and something organic that they spread on bare surfaces to encourage growth. The grønnfolk are slightly feared because their work is with the living system that is *becoming* the city, and becoming is not a force you treat casually.

The well-keepers are the most mysterious. They are a guild — the only true guild in Vossholm, the only trade with formal organization and internal hierarchy. They maintain the deep wells that draw clean water from the substrate, and the wells are the only reliable source of potable water in the city. The river water is tannin-stained and metallic. The drainage water is not to be trusted. The well-keepers know the substrate — they know where the water is, how deep, what it has passed through, what it has dissolved. They guard this knowledge. They are not unfriendly, but they are not forthcoming. They speak to the ground, they say — they listen to the substrate, and the substrate tells them where the water is. Whether this is metaphor or literal, they do not clarify, and the look they give you when you ask is the look that says the question is the wrong kind of question.

There are other trades. The punt-operators who navigate the flooded channels, polying their flat-bottomed boats through the dark water with a skill that is not taught but *absorbed*, learned from childhood by the children who grow up in the lower districts where the ground floors are waterways. The foundry-workers, the tanners, the weavers, the ceramicists — the industrial trades that produce the goods the rail causeway carries away. The market vendors, who are not a trade so much as a *condition* — everyone in Vossholm trades, at some point, because the density and the turbulence mean that no one has everything they need all the time and the market is the mechanism by which the city redistributes itself constantly. The children who run messages between districts, slipping through gaps in the walls that adults cannot fit through, learning the hidden geography of a city that is all hidden geography.

## The Daily Life

They eat simply, or rather, they eat *compactly*. The diet is dense — fish from the coast, cured and salted, and fish from the channels, fresh and small and bony. Meat from the interior, mostly pork and mutton, preserved in salt or smoked in the boiler-house chimneys. Vegetables from the rooftop gardens and the courtyard plots — root vegetables, mostly, potatoes and turnips and a pale cabbage that grows well in the damp. Mushrooms, cultivated in the basements where the substrate is too loose for habitation. Herbs from the moss-ecosystems — the grønnfolk harvest them, and they are traded in the markets, and they flavor everything. The food is not spicy. It is not bright. It is *warm* — thick soups, stews, porridges, breads that are dark and dense and slightly sour from the damp that gets into the flour. The food is the opposite of the city: where the city is provisional, the food is *certain*. It is the one thing that is reliably what it is. A bowl of fish stew in Vossholm is a bowl of fish stew. It will not shift. It will not subside. It will not develop a crack in its left wall. It is what it is, and the people eat it with a deliberateness that you come to recognize as gratitude.

They sleep in shifts. This is not a cultural choice but a *spatial* one — the buildings are crowded, the rooms are few, and the beds are shared by necessity. A family of six might have two beds, and the family sleeps in rotations, the ones who work the day shift sleeping at night, the ones who work the night shift sleeping by day. The sleeping is not private. It is not quiet, exactly, but it is *contained* — the murmured sounds of a household that is always partly awake, the creak of a bedframe, the drip of condensation, the low conversation of the ones who are not sleeping and the ones who are waking. The concept of a private bedroom does not exist in Vossholm. The concept of privacy, as the outside world understands it, does not exist. The density makes privacy a luxury that no one can afford and no one, particularly, wants. They live among each other. They hear each other. They know each other's sounds, each other's habits, each other's bodies. This is not intimacy in the romantic sense. It is *proximity*, and proximity is the condition of life here, and the people have made of it something that is not quite community and not quite family but is *functional* in the way that everything in Vossholm is functional — provisionally, turbulently, but reliably enough.

They wash in the well-water, heated in boilers, in communal bathhouses that serve a district. The bathhouses are among the oldest structures in the city — some of them predate the rail causeway, built by the fishing villages that were here before the city accumulated. They are stone, thick-walled, with deep stone tubs that hold the heated water, and the people sit in them together, and the washing is not separate from the social, and the social is not separate from the practical. News is exchanged in the bathhouse. Agreements are made. Disputes are aired. The bathhouse is the closest thing Vossholm has to a civic institution, and the bathhouse-keepers are respected in a way that approaches the respect given to the byggfolk.

## The Relationship to the Strange

They do not talk about the bunnfolk. This is not taboo — taboo implies a rule, and there are few rules in Vossholm. It is simply that there is nothing to say. The things in the water are there. The water is theirs, as much as the ground is the city's. The people do not enter the water. They do not touch the water. They do not draw water from the channels or the pools, only from the deep wells. They do not explain this because it is not a decision they made — it is a *condition* they inherited, a term of the grunnvilkår. You ask a woman in Vanngate about the bunnfolk and she pauses, and the pause is not discomfort but something more like the pause you make when someone asks you why you do not eat stones. The question does not compute. The bunnfolk are not a topic. They are a *fact*, like the damp, like the moss, like the silence that is not silence.

They do not talk about the silence either, but they *use* it. You notice this in the markets, in the workshops, in the narrow streets. The people navigate by sound as much as by sight — the drip of a gutter, the creak of a wall, the low murmur of a boiler, the particular absence of sound that marks a patch of loose ground. They hear the city the way a sailor hears the sea. They know its moods, its textures, its *conditions*. A change in the acoustic landscape is a warning — a shift in the substrate, a new crack in a wall, a blockage in a drain. They respond to these changes with a speed that seems instinctive but is *learned*, learned from childhood, learned from the moment they could walk and listen at the same time.

The crows. They have a relationship with the crows that is... *diplomatic*. The crows are intelligent, and the people know this, and the people treat the crows as entities to be *dealt with* rather than pests to be eliminated. There are people — not a trade, not a guild, but a *type* — who leave food for the crows on the rooftops. Scraps, offal, the trimmings from the fish-curing. The food is not charity. It is *tribute*. The crows that are fed do not steal. The crows that are fed do not damage. The crows that are fed watch, and what they watch for is not clear, but the people who feed them say — in their low, precise, for-now manner — that the crows see things that the people do not. The crows see the city from above. The crows see the patterns of shift and subsidence that the people on the ground can only feel. And sometimes, the people say, the crows *tell* — they gather on a rooftop and they call, and the calling is not random, and the byggfolk go to the building the crows have called on and they check, and they find a crack that was not there yesterday.

Whether this is true, or whether this is the kind of story a people tells itself to make sense of the place it lives in, you cannot determine. The people who tell it believe it. The byggfolk who respond to the crows' calling believe it. And the crows, for their part, watch you with an intelligence that is not animal and not human and not anything you have a word for.

## The Children

The children of Vossholm are not like other children. They are not louder or quieter, not happier or sadder, not healthier or sicker. They are *different* in a way that is hard to pin down and that you eventually realize is about *expectation*.

The children here do not expect the world to be stable. They do not expect the floor to hold, the wall to stay, the street to go where it went yesterday. They are born into the turbulence and they absorb it the way they absorb the language — not as a lesson but as a *condition*. A child in Vossholm learns to walk on surfaces that move, and the walking is different — more fluid, more attentive, the weight distributed differently, the balance always *provisional*. The children fall, and the falling is not a failure but a *data point* — the ground shifted, the child adjusts, the child walks. They learn the loose ground the way a child elsewhere learns traffic — as a danger that is constant and that must be understood, not avoided.

They play in the ruins. There are always ruins in Vossholm — buildings that have shifted beyond correction, walls that have cracked beyond repair, streets that have subsided beyond re-laying. The ruins are not abandoned. They are *playgrounds*, and the children climb on them and through them and under them with a sureness that terrifies you and that the parents do not share, because the parents know what the children know: the ruins are more stable than they look, because the ruins have already *finished* their shifting, and what is finished is, for now, reliable.

The children know the cats by name. The cats are their first education in the ecology of the city — which cats are friendly, which cats are not, which cats hunt in which districts, which cats will accept food and which will not. The children learn the boundaries of the cats' territories the way they learn the boundaries of the districts, and the two geographies overlap but do not coincide, and the children know both.

The children do not fear the bunnfolk. This is the thing that unsettles you most. You ask a child — a girl of perhaps eight, small and dark-eyed and sure-footed in a way that is almost uncanny — about the things in the water, and she looks at you with an expression that is not the adult expression, not the *yes, and?* but something more open, more curious, more willing to engage. *They are there,* she says. *They are always there. They were there before we were.* She says this the way a child elsewhere might say *the sun comes up in the morning* — as a fact of the world, not a strange one. And you realize, with a feeling that is not quite fear and not quite wonder, that the children of Vossholm do not experience the strangeness of their city as strangeness. They experience it as *home*. The bunnfolk are part of the home. The silence is part of the home. The shifting ground, the aggressive moss, the intelligent crows, the dark water, the provisional everything — this is the world, and it is *theirs*, and they are becoming in it, with it, alongside it.

## What They Are

They are becoming. This is the only adequate description, and it is the one they would not use, because they do not think of themselves in these terms. They think of themselves as *living*, as *working*, as *managing*. But what they are, from the outside, is a people in the process of adapting to a place that is itself in process, and the adaptation is not reactive but *collaborative*. They are not surviving the ground. They are *with* the ground, co-becoming, their buildings and their streets and their names and their grammar and their posture and their pale eyes and their compact bodies all *participating* in the formation of a place that has not yet decided what it is.

They are not old. A century and a half is nothing. They are a young people in a young place, and they have the qualities of young things — the flexibility, the pragmatism, the lack of reverence for what was because what was is still being determined. They have no ancient traditions because they have no antiquity. They have no sacred sites because nothing here is old enough to be sacred. What they have is *practice* — the accumulated, compacted, layered practice of a hundred and fifty years of living where the ground is soft and the water is dark and the silence has a quality that is not silence but is something else, something that holds the space where noise would be and gives it a different weight.

They are not happy, in the way that the word is used outside. They are not unhappy. They are *engaged* — in the daily, hourly, minute-by-minute work of being in a place that is still being itself, and being themselves in it. They are *present* in a way that you have not encountered before, because presence, for them, is not a virtue or a practice. It is a *necessity*. The ground shifts and you must be present to feel it. The wall cracks and you must be present to catch it. The water rises and you must be present to move up a floor. The market rearranges itself and you must be present to find what you need. The *now* is not a concept here. It is the only tense that is fully reliable, and even the now has the provisional mood, the *for-now*, the grunnvilkår, the acknowledgment that what is true in this moment may not be true in the next, and that this is not a problem but a *condition*.

They do not want to leave. You realize this eventually, and it surprises you, because the city is hard — the damp, the cold, the dark, the constant correction, the turbulence, the strangeness that is not strangeness to them but is *the world*. But they do not want to leave. They do not want to go somewhere easier, somewhere stable, somewhere where the ground has decided what it is and the buildings stand straight and the water is clear and the silence is just silence. They want *this*. This place, this ground, this air, this damp, this provisional, turbulent, moss-green, dark-watered, crow-watched, cat-inhabited, bunnfolk-haunted, for-now, still-becoming *this*.

They want it because it is theirs, and they are its, and the grunnvilkår are the terms under which existence is being negotiated here, and the negotiation is not finished, and they do not want it to be finished, because when it is finished they will be something else, and they are not ready to be something else, and the ground is not ready, and the bunnfolk are not ready, and the moss is not ready, and the crows are not ready, and the city is not ready.

It is not ready. They are not ready. And they live in the not-ready with a commitment and a density and a warmth — the warmth of bodies in close rooms, of boilers in basements, of stew on the fire, of voices murmuring in the dark — that is, from the outside, from the rail causeway, from the place where the ground is old and the world is what it is, almost unbearably moving.
