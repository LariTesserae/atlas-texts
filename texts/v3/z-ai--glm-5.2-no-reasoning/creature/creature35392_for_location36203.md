# What Lives Here

## The Consortia

The first things you notice are the things that have given up on being any one thing at all.

They live on the trunks of the great columnar trees, the ones whose roots thread across the basin floor like the fingers of pressed hands. They are not moss. They are not lichen. They are not fungus. They are what happens when those categories stop being useful — when several lineages of life decide that the boundary between *I* and *we* is an unnecessary expense in a place this dark, this warm, this generous with moisture.

Each consortium is a patch. Some are the size of a thumbnail. Some spread across a meter or more of bark, thick as carpet, dark as the wet wood they cover. They are felted, textured, slightly raised. When condensation builds on them — and condensation builds on everything — the water doesn't bead. It absorbs. The consortium drinks through every surface it has, which is every surface it is. It has no inside and no outside. It is all contact.

What lives in a consortium? Fungal hyphae, certainly — threads of them, branching and anastomosing, forming the structural scaffolding. Algal cells nested among the threads, green even in this darkness, green in the way that a deep bruise is purple, holding their color out of stubbornness or memory. Bacterial mats in the deeper layers, anaerobic and content, breaking down whatever the upper layers pass down to them. Nematodes — microscopic worms — threading through the matrix, eating, being eaten, cycling nitrogen in their tiny bodies. Mites. Tardigrades. Things that have no common name because they have never needed to be spoken to.

The consortium is a city. Not metaphorically. It has infrastructure. The fungal threads are roads. The algal cells are farms. The bacterial layers are refineries. The mites and nematodes are the population that keeps the economy moving. There is waste management. There is resource distribution. There are layers of specialization, organisms that do one thing and one thing only, and do it in a particular layer of the consortium at a particular depth of moisture.

They reproduce, or rather they *disperse*, when a fragment breaks free. This happens during the wet cycles, when the pools rise and the water touches the bark higher than it normally touches. A patch of consortium detaches and floats — a dark, saturated flake of life — and if it lands on a new surface, a new trunk, a new root, a stone that has been bare for too long, it begins. Slowly. A thread. A cell. A mite carried along by accident. The beginning of a new city.

You would not notice them. They are the texture of the place, not the content. They are what makes the bark soft under your fingers, what makes the stone feel furred, what makes the root you sit on feel less like wood and more like the arm of a very old chair. But they are the base of everything. Everything else here eats them, or eats something that ate them, or eats something that ate something that ate them. They are the reason the bark is not bark but habitat. They are the reason a fallen log doesn't stay a log but becomes, over the patience of decades, a ridge of soil.

They do not know you are there. They do not know anything. But they will be the first to investigate your boots, your dropped food wrappers, your sweat-stained collar. They are the frontier. The first contact. They will begin to incorporate you before you have decided whether to stay.

## The Ferals

You hear them before you see them. You hear them and you are never sure you have heard them, because the sounds they make are so close to the sounds the basin already makes that they vanish into the general hum.

They are small. Smaller than your hand. Some are smaller than your thumb. They are warm-blooded, or close enough — their metabolism runs hot in a place that runs hot anyway, and the difference between their body heat and the ambient air is a matter of two or three degrees. They are furred, but the fur is not the fur you would recognize from a temperate mammal. It is flat, slick, dense. It repels water the way a seal's coat repels water. When they are dry, they look like they've been oiled. When they are wet — and they are usually wet — they look like they've been sculpted from the moss itself.

They are the things that rustle in the litter. The eyes you see at the base of a root, low to the ground, that blink and are gone. They live in the gaps. The space between a root and the soil above it. The hollow where a log has decomposed and left a tunnel. The tangled architecture of epiphyte roots where they meet a trunk. They build nothing. They occupy.

There are dozens of species. Maybe more. You cannot tell them apart because the darkness makes them all the same color — the color of wet bark, the color of dark moss, the color of shadow on shadow. Some are long-bodied and low, moving like ferrets or like water through cracks. Some are rounder, slower, sitting on leaves with their feet tucked under them, breathing in a rhythm so close to the dripping that you mistake one for the other. Some have almost no eyes at all — flat dark patches on their faces, unbothered by the dark because they have lived in it for longer than eyes have been worth the cost of growing them.

They eat the insects. They eat the frogs. They eat each other, occasionally, when the opportunity presents itself and the size differential allows. They do not eat the consortium, but they eat the mites that live in the consortium, and the nematodes that live in the mites' digestive tracts, so the consortium feeds them anyway, three steps removed.

They have a social life, but it is not one you would recognize. They do not gather. They do not call to each other across distance. They communicate by scent — by the deposits they leave on the roots they travel, on the bark they brush against, on the broad leaves they cross. Each individual leaves a chemical diary as it moves through its night, and each individual reads the diaries of every other individual it encounters. The root system of a single tree is, to them, a library. They know who passed this way. They know when. They know what they ate. They know, in some chemical approximation of knowing, how they felt about it.

They are not afraid of you because they do not have a category for you. You are a scent they have not encountered. You are a warmth they can feel but cannot place. You are a vibration in the litter that does not match the pattern of any of their predators — and they have predators. The snakes take them. The things in the canopy occasionally drop down and take them. But you are not those things, and they are curious in the way that small, warm things are curious: cautiously, briefly, without commitment. One will approach your boot. It will press its face against the leather. Its whiskers — if they are whiskers, if that word still applies to something so small and so dark — will brush the surface. It will decide. Then it will go.

They are the mammalian theme in the larger composition. A minor voice. But persistent. Present in every square meter of the basin floor, in every gap and hollow and tunnel. They are the things that make the litter move when the wind doesn't.

## The Listeners

The frogs are not what you expect.

You expect frogs. You expect the loud, declarative, single-minded frog of a temperate pond — the one that sits on a lily pad and announces its existence to the world. That frog is a soloist. That frog has a stage.

These frogs have no stage. These frogs have an audience they cannot see and a theater that extends to every surface in the basin. They are not soloists. They are an orchestra. And they are not performing for you.

They are everywhere. Every leaf. Every root. Every stone. Every surface that holds moisture, which is every surface, holds a frog. Some are the size of your thumbnail — translucent, almost, their dark organs visible through the skin of their bellies, like a watch face through glass. Some are the size of your fist — thick, warty, their skin folding into creases that harbor mites and consortium fragments and the beginnings of a small ecosystem of their own. Some are in between. The gradient of sizes is continuous. There is no gap in the size distribution. If you plotted them on a graph, the line would be smooth, unbroken, as if every possible size of frog is represented by at least one species, as if the basin has filled every available body-size niche with a dedicated occupant.

Their skin is the thing. It is not a boundary. It is a membrane. It is a meeting place. Water passes through it — in and out, constantly. Oxygen passes through it. They breathe through their skin as much as through their lungs, and their lungs are reduced, simplified, a backup system in a place where the air is so thick with moisture that skin-breathing is more efficient. Their skin is dark. All of them. Even the ones whose cousins, in other parts of the world, would be bright — poison-dart red, banana yellow, warning-sign orange. Here, color is wasted. Color is an expense that buys nothing in a world without light. So they are the color of the place. Dark green. Dark brown. Black. The color of wet bark, of shadow, of water that has no light to reflect.

They listen.

This is the thing you notice, if you are still enough to notice it. The frogs are not just calling. They are *listening*. Their calls — the clicks, the trills, the long low hums you feel in your chest — are not declarations. They are questions. Each call is a probe. *Who is near? What species? What size? Male or female? Ready or not?* And the answer comes not as a single response but as a shift in the chorus. A nearby frog adjusts its call. A distant frog drops out. A third, somewhere in between, changes its rhythm. The entire soundscape is a continuous negotiation. A continuous census. The frogs know, at any given moment, the composition of their community within earshot. They know the demographics. They know the competition. They know the opportunities.

And they know when something foreign enters the soundscape. Your footsteps. Your breathing. Your coughing. These sounds are not in the library. They are not in the pattern. The frogs nearest to you go quiet — not silent, but quieter, their calls dropping to a sub-frequency you feel rather than hear. The frogs farther away continue. The silence ripples outward, a shrinking circle of attention, and then, when you have been still long enough, the circle fills back in. The nearest frogs resume. The chorus reconstitutes itself. You have been incorporated into the census as an anomaly, noted, and — because you don't do anything — filed. Ignored. Tolerated. The frogs return to their business, which is the business of knowing where every other frog is at all times.

They lay their eggs in the film of water that covers everything during the wet cycles. Not in the pools — the pools are too deep, too dark, too full of other things. In the film. The thin, warm layer of water that sits on a leaf, that fills the cup of a bromeliad, that pools in the hollow of a root. The eggs are small. They are numerous. They hatch into tadpoles that are smaller still and that live their entire aquatic lives in a volume of water that would fill a thimble. They metamorphose in days, not weeks. They emerge as froglets the size of a grain of rice and they climb — up the root, up the trunk, into the tangle of the mid-story — and they begin to call.

The calls change over the day. Not over a day, the way a temperate forest changes — morning chorus, evening chorus, silence at noon. Here there is no morning and no evening. The light doesn't change. But the calls change. There are cycles. Slow ones. The chorus shifts over the course of hours, different species rising to dominance and then fading, replaced by others, in a pattern that might reflect the cycles of the insects they eat, or the temperature of the water they live in, or something else entirely — some rhythm that the basin keeps that you cannot perceive because you are not tuned to it. The frogs know the rhythm. They are the rhythm.

There is one that is different. Larger than the others — nearly the size of your spread hand. It does not call. It sits, motionless, on a root that crosses a shallow pool, and it waits. Its skin is perfectly black. Its eyes are the only bright thing about it — a faint, warm amber, the color of the water when light somehow touches it. It does not call because it does not need to. It listens. It listens to the entire basin. And when something — a frog, an insect, a feral — comes within range of its mouth, it moves with a speed that has no precedent in this slow, patient place. A single, explosive motion. Then stillness again. The thing that was alive is now inside it.

This frog is old. You can tell because the consortium has begun to grow on its back. Not much. A patch. A dark, felted patch on its left shoulder, where it has been sitting in the same position for long enough that the consortium has found it and decided it was a surface worth colonizing. The frog does not seem to mind. The frog has been colonized before. The frog will be colonized again. The frog is a surface. The frog is a habitat.

## The Patient Ones

The snakes do not move.

You could watch one for an hour — and you will, because you will not know it is there until you have been watching it for an hour, and by then you are committed to the watching — and you will not see it move. It lies along a root. Its body follows the contour of the bark, the curve of the wood, the dip where one root crosses another. It is the same temperature as the root. It is the same color as the root. It is the same texture as the root, because the consortium has grown on its scales the way it has grown on the bark, and the snake is, from a distance of more than a meter, indistinguishable from the surface it rests on.

It has been there for days. Maybe longer. You infer this from the position of the droplets on its skin — the condensation has formed a pattern that suggests the snake has not shifted, even slightly, in the time it took for the water to build up and begin to drip. It is waiting. It will wait. It is not waiting for anything in particular. It is waiting for the world to bring something within range.

The snakes here are not hunters. They are filters. They position themselves in the flow of life — along a root that the ferals use as a path, beside a pool that the frogs breed in, at the base of a trunk that the insects climb — and they let the world pass through them, the way a filter lets water pass through and catches what it catches. They are not aggressive. They are not venomous, most of them — venom is an expense, and the things they eat are small enough that constriction or simple seizure is sufficient. They are slow because everything they eat is slow. They are patient because patience is not a virtue here; it is a metabolic strategy. A snake that moved would burn calories it does not need to burn. A snake that waited would eat eventually. The basin provides. The basin always provides.

There are several species. You will see three, maybe four, if you are lucky. The thick-bodied ones on the roots, the ones you mistake for bark until they breathe. The thinner ones in the mid-story, draped over branches like vines, like lianas, like the structural elements of the vegetation itself, waiting for a bird or a large insect to settle on what it thinks is a solid perch. The aquatic ones — the ones that live in the pools, barely submerged, their nostrils the only part of them above the surface, their bodies as still as the water they are in, which is very, very still. And the small, quick ones — the only fast snakes in the basin — that live in the litter and hunt the insects and that move with a fluidity that seems impossible in a place where everything else is so committed to slowness.

They do not care about you. You step over one. It does not react. Its eyes — if it has eyes, if the eyes are not just dark spots on a dark head — do not track you. You are too large. You are not in its flow. You pass through its world the way a cloud passes through the sky: noted, irrelevant, gone.

But there is one that watches you.

It is on a root, at the level of your waist, in a position you pass near as you walk. You do not see it the first time you pass. You do not see it the second time. The third time, you see it because it has moved — not much, just a repositioning of the head, a slight angling toward you — and the movement, in a place where nothing moves, is the most visible thing in the world.

It is not large. A meter, maybe. Its body is the color of wet bark with a faint, regular pattern that you can only see because the consortium has not yet grown over this part of its skin — a banding, a subtle striping, the kind of pattern that dissolves at a distance into the general texture of the root. Its eyes are visible. They are not the flat, dark patches of its cousins. They are directed. They are looking at you.

It does not do anything. It watches. It is not a threat. It is not a predator of things your size. It is curious — or if not curious, then attentive, in the way that a thing that has been still for a long time becomes attentive to the one thing in its environment that moves with a pattern it does not recognize. You are a novelty. The basin does not produce novelties. The basin is the oldest thing in the region. Everything in it has been in it for a very long time, and everything in it has seen everything else a thousand times and a thousand times again. You are new. You are the only new thing.

The snake watches you until you move out of range. Then it settles. It returns to the patience that is its strategy and its method and its life. You are filed. Not as prey. Not as threat. As weather. As a temporary condition. Something that will pass. Everything passes. The snake knows this. The snake has been here longer than the root it rests on, and the root has been here for decades.

## The Canopy Dwellers

You almost never see them. You hear them — sharp, single notes that punctuate the frog chorus, and the creaking of branches as something heavy moves from one perch to another. You see their evidence — the droppings on the leaves below, the occasional feather that spirals down through the gloom. But the canopy is seventy meters up, and the light, such as it is, is up there, and the life that lives up there lives in a different world from the one on the floor.

But things fall.

A bird comes down. Not by choice. Something has happened — a miscalculation, a collision, a predator — and a bird is falling through the mid-story, through the tangle, through the gloom, and it lands on a root near you with a sound that is, in the silence of the basin, shocking. A thump. A fluttering. A sharp, anguished call that is cut short by the dark.

The bird is not like a bird you know. It is not a shape you would recognize as a bird, at first. It is large — the size of a crow, maybe, or larger — and it is the color of the canopy: dark green, dark brown, with a sheen that suggests the same oiled, water-repellent fur of the ferals but that is, in fact, feathers. Feathers that are built for a world of constant moisture, that shed water the way a duck's back sheds water, that are as much about insulation as flight. Its eyes are large. They must be large, because the light up there is only marginally better than the light down here, and a bird that flies through a dark canopy at speed needs every photon it can get. Its beak is hooked. It is a predator. Up there, it eats the things that live in the canopy — the insects, the smaller birds, the things that crawl along the branches.

It is injured. Or exhausted. Or both. It sits on the root and its chest heaves and the basin responds. The frogs nearest to it go quiet. The insects on the root scatter. A feral, somewhere in the litter, gives a chemical alarm — a scent deposit that will be read, in the coming hours, by every feral that passes this way, a note in the diary that says *something large has fallen here, something new, something that is not us*.

The snake — the one that watched you — moves. For the first time, you see it move with purpose. It is not fast. It does not need to be fast. The bird is not going anywhere. The bird is sitting on a root in a world it does not know, in a darkness it is not adapted to, and it is making the sounds that a thing makes when it has fallen out of its world and into another one. The snake moves toward it. Slowly. Along the root. The consortium on its back glistening with condensation.

This is the economy of the basin. This is the rule. Everything that falls is incorporated. Everything that dies is processed. Everything that enters the system is taken apart and used. The bird will not be wasted. The snake will eat what it can. The insects will eat what the snake leaves. The consortium will grow over the bones. The bones will become soil. The soil will become root. The root will become tree. The tree will become canopy. The canopy will become the world that produces birds, and a bird will fall, and the snake will be waiting.

The bird does not know this. The bird knows only that it is in a place it does not understand. The bird is afraid. The bird's fear is the first truly urgent emotion you have encountered here. Everything else — the patience of the snakes, the curiosity of the ferals, the industriousness of the insects — is an emotion tuned to the pace of the basin. The bird's fear is tuned to a faster world. A world of light and air and flight. It does not belong here. It is not unwelcome, but it does not belong.

It will be included, though. That is the rule. Everything is included.

## The Builders

There is something in the pools that you will not see unless you sit beside a pool for a very long time and watch the surface with the patience that this place demands.

They build. They are the only things in the basin that build, in the sense of constructing something that was not there before. The consortium grows. The ferals occupy. The frogs listen. The snakes wait. But the builders make.

They are small. The size of a fingernail, or smaller. They are not insects. They are not crustaceans. They are something in between, or something beside — a lineage of freshwater life that has adapted to the basin's particular chemistry, to the warmth of the water, to the organic richness, to the darkness. They live in the pools, on the surfaces of the submerged roots, on the underside of floating leaves. They are nearly transparent. In the darkness of the water, they are invisible.

What they build: tubes. Small tubes, attached to surfaces, made from the material of the basin — fragments of leaf, particles of organic matter, grains of the dark, wet soil. The tubes are the size of a grain of rice. Each one is a home. A single builder lives inside, extending the tube as it grows, adding new material at the opening, sealing the back. The tubes are not random. They are oriented — the openings face the current, or what passes for current in a pool that doesn't flow, the gentle, molecular drift of water that is warmed by decomposition and cooled by condensation. The builders orient to this drift. They position their openings to catch what the water brings, which is food — bacteria, organic particles, the microscopic detritus of a world that is always breaking down.

A colony of builders covers a root in a pool. Hundreds of tubes. Thousands. They look like a rough fabric — a dark, textured mat on the surface of the wood. They are, in their way, the aquatic equivalent of the consortium: a community of small things that, in aggregate, form a larger thing. The tubes are the infrastructure. The builders are the population. The food that drifts in is the economy. The waste that drifts out is the contribution to the larger system — nitrogen, phosphorus, the broken-down molecules that the plants in the pool will take up through their roots.

During the wet cycles, when the pools rise and merge, the builders go with the water. New surfaces become available. New roots. New undersides of leaves. They colonize. They build. The tubes appear on surfaces that were bare a day ago. The colony expands. During the dry cycles, when the water recedes, the builders on the surfaces that are exposed die. Their tubes remain — small, dark, empty structures on the bark, on the stone, on the leaf — and these structures become habitat for other things. For mites. For nematodes. For the consortium, which grows over them and incorporates them into its city.

The builders do not know they are builders. They do not know they are part of a colony. They do not know that the wet cycle is coming or that the dry cycle will follow. They build because building is what they do. The tube is the behavior. The behavior is the tube. There is no separation between the organism and its work. This is true of everything in the basin, but it is most visible in the builders, because their work is the most visible. The tubes are there. You can see them. You can touch them. You can feel the difference between a root that has been colonized and a root that hasn't — the texture, the complexity, the sense that this surface has been *shaped* by something that was here.

## The Giant

You will not see it. Almost certainly. You will see evidence of it. You will feel it.

It moves through the canopy. You hear the creaking — not the slow, growth-driven creaking of wood expanding, but a different creaking. A heavier creaking. The creaking of a branch bearing a weight it was not expecting. A pause. A shift. Another creaking. Then silence, and then, somewhere else, another creaking. It is moving. It is slow. It is large.

It is a mammal. The largest in the basin. Perhaps the largest anywhere in the region. You know this because the sounds it makes are the sounds of weight, of mass, of something that takes up space in the canopy the way the trees take up space on the floor. It does not call. It does not roar. It does not announce itself. It simply moves, and the canopy accommodates it, and the canopy objects, and the objection is the creaking.

What does it eat? The canopy. It is a browser. It eats the leaves, the epiphytes, the mosses, the lichens, the consortium that grows on the upper branches. It eats the way everything here eats — slowly, continuously, without urgency. It moves from tree to tree — not quickly, not dramatically, but with a deliberateness that suggests it knows where it is going, that it has a route, that it has been this way before, that the canopy is its home in the way that the floor is the snake's home: known, mapped, familiar.

You find its droppings on the floor. They are large. The size of your fist. Dark, fibrous, full of the undigested remains of leaves and bark and consortium. They are warm when they are fresh — warmer than the air, warmer than the ground — and they are immediately colonized. Within an hour, the insects find them. Within a day, the consortium begins to grow on them. Within a week, they are part of the floor, indistinguishable from the rest of the organic matter, incorporated, processed, gone.

The giant is not the only one of its kind. You find evidence of others — different droppings in different parts of the basin, of different sizes, suggesting at least two or three individuals. They do not gather. They do not interact, as far as you can tell. They are solitary. They are large enough that the canopy can only support so many of them, and the spacing between their droppings suggests that each has a territory, a section of the canopy that is theirs, that they know, that they move through on a cycle that takes days or weeks to complete.

You will not see it. But one night — and night here is indistinguishable from day, but your body knows the difference, your body has its own clock, and at the hour your body calls night — you are sitting on a root, and the basin is humming, and the dripping is dripping, and something above you creaks, and a branch shifts, and for a moment — a moment so brief you might have imagined it — a shaft of the faintest, most attenuated light, light that has passed through seventy meters of canopy and emerged as a single photon, lands on something. A shape. Large. Dark. Moving. An arm, or a leg, or something that serves the same purpose. For that moment, you see it, or you see a part of it, and it is there — it is real, it exists, it is alive — and then the moment is over and the shape is gone and the darkness is total again and you are left with the creaking, moving away, and the dripping, and the frogs, and the knowledge that something very large is sharing this darkness with you and is not interested in you and will not be seen again.

## The Ones Who Were Always Here

There is something else. Something you cannot see, cannot hear, cannot touch. Something that is not an animal and not a plant and not a consortium but that is alive, or was alive, or is in the process of being alive in a way that does not match the categories you have.

It is in the soil. In the organic matter. In the dark, wet, warm layer of decomposition that covers the rock below. It is the thing that does the decomposing. Not the insects — the insects break things into pieces. Not the consortium — the consortium colonizes surfaces. This is the thing that dissolves. That takes the solid and makes it soluble. That takes the complex and makes it simple. That takes the dead and makes it available to the living.

It is everywhere in the floor of the basin. It is the most widespread organism here. It is the most widespread organism anywhere. It is the connective tissue of the living world, and in this place, in this hot, dark, wet, saturated place, it is at its most dense, its most active, its most complete.

It does not have a shape. It does not have a body. It is a network — threads so fine they are invisible, threading through every particle of organic matter on the floor, connecting root to root, connecting tree to tree, connecting the dead to the living and the living to the dead. It is the internet of the basin. It is the substrate on which all other life depends.

You cannot see it, but you can feel its effects. The warmth of the ground — that is its metabolism. The smell of decomposition — that is its chemical signature. The softness of the soil — that is its work. The fact that a fallen leaf disappears in a week, that a dead tree is reduced to a ridge of soil in a decade, that the floor of the basin does not rise indefinitely despite the constant rain of organic matter from above — that is its doing. It is the recycling system. It is the thing that makes the basin a closed loop, a machine that produces nothing wasted, a system that runs on its own output.

It does not care about you. But it is interested in you, in the way that it is interested in everything that enters the soil. Your dead skin cells. Your hair. The fibers of your clothing. The sweat that drips from your body and soaks into the moss. These things are entering its domain, and it is taking them apart, molecule by molecule, and passing the components into the network, where they will be taken up by roots and sent to the canopy and built into leaves and eaten by the giant and dropped to the floor and taken apart again.

It is the oldest thing in the basin. It was here before the trees. It was here before the canopy. It was here when the basin was a bare depression in the rock, collecting water, collecting the first organic matter blown in from outside. It colonized the rock. It broke the rock down. It made the first soil. It allowed the first plants to root. The plants grew and died and it broke them down and made more soil and allowed more plants to root and the process compounded, century by century, millennium by millennium, until the basin was what it is now: a living machine built on the back of the thing that lives in the soil and that does not stop.

You are standing on it. You have been standing on it the entire time. It is beneath your boots, beneath the moss, beneath the roots, beneath the organic matter. It is the floor of the world, and it is alive, and it does not care, and it will be here long after the trees are gone and the canopy has opened and the light has come in and the basin has changed into something else. It will adapt. It has adapted before. It will be here, in the dark, in the wet, in the warm, dissolving, breaking down, making available. It is the patient one. The patient one that makes all other patience possible.

## What Lives Here

Everything. Everything lives here. That is the answer, and it is not a glib answer, and it is not a mystical answer. It is a literal one.

The basin is not a place that contains life. It is life. It is the densest, most complete, most self-sufficient expression of the living process that you have encountered. What lives here is not a list of species — though the list is long, and the species are many, and each one is a thread in a fabric so tightly woven that you cannot pull a single thread without unravelling the whole. What lives here is the fabric itself. The weaving. The process of weaving. The thing that happens when you put heat and water and darkness and time in the same place and let them work.

The ferals live here, and the frogs, and the snakes, and the builders, and the giant, and the consortium, and the thing in the soil, and ten thousand other things you have not seen and will not see and that do not need to be seen to be real. They live here the way the cells of your body live in you: not as guests, not as occupants, but as components. As parts of a whole that is itself a part of a larger whole that is itself a part of a larger whole, up and up, until the whole is the basin, and the basin is the world, and the world is the floor of the world, and the floor is alive, and it is patient, and it is dark, and it is wet, and it is warm, and it does not need you, and it includes you, and it will go on.
