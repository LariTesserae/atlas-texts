# What Lives in the Chorus Hollow

---

## The Singer at the Center

She was a woman once. Or he was a man. Or they were something between or beyond or other — the root-knot has made the question irrelevant, has composted gender and name and memory into the same fertile medium that feeds the flowers. What can be said with certainty is only this: they were *a person*. They walked on two legs. They had a language, and the language had sounds in it, and the sounds meant things, and this — this perhaps more than anything else — is what they could not let go of.

The sound is not their voice. The sound is what their voice *became* when it was fed through centuries of root and rhizome, through the acoustic architecture of a body that is also a city. They did not design the hollow. They * consented* to it. There is a difference. Something came to them — something patient, something vegetable, something that thinks in centuries the way a human thinks in afternoons — and offered them a choice. You can die, it said, in the way that all your kind die, your sounds fading into silence, your language dissolving into the earth like rainwater. Or you can become a place. You can keep speaking. Not with a mouth. With a *geography*. With streets made of your arteries and arches made of your ribs and a voice that fills a bowl in the earth like water fills a cup, and the voice will never stop, will never tire, will never thin to a whisper. You will be loud forever. You will be the loudest thing for miles. You will be the ground people walk on and the air they breathe and the sound that lives in their bones long after they leave.

They chose.

What lives in the center of the Chorus Hollow is not a ghost. A ghost is a residue, a recording, a repetition. What lives here is *ongoing*. The root-knot pulses because the person is still thinking, still speaking, still *composing* — and the composition is the four-minute cycle, the swell and peak and subside that is not a heartbeat but a breath, or a phrase, or a sentence in a language that has only one sentence, endlessly revised. The sentence is long. The sentence has been in progress for perhaps three hundred years. The sentence is not finished. The sentence may never be finished, because the person at the center has discovered, in the slow vegetable expansion of their consciousness into the root system, that they have more to say than they ever imagined, that the silence they feared at the end of their life was not a wall but a door, and the door opened onto a room with no walls, a room that was a hollow, a room that keeps growing.

They do not remember their name. They remember *having had* a name, the way an amputee remembers having had a hand — the shape of the absence, the ghost of the function, but not the thing itself. They remember the feel of speaking with a mouth, the way the tongue shaped air into vowels, the way the teeth touched to make consonants, the way the diaphragm pushed and the sound came out and traveled a few feet and was gone. This memory is precious to them. It is the memory of *smallness*. They keep it the way a wealthy person keeps a coin from a time when they were poor — not for value, but for perspective.

They are not suffering. This is important to understand, though you will understand it differently after you have stood at the center and felt the sound replace your hearing. Suffering requires a boundary between the self and what acts upon the self, and the person at the center has no such boundary. The roots that grow through what was their skull are not intrusions; they are *extensions*. The flowers that pulse with their rhythm are not parasites; they are *organs*. The tympanic-eyed creatures that walk their root-streets are not visitors; they are *citizens*, or perhaps *children*, or perhaps *words* — living words in a language made of living things, each one a phoneme in a sentence three centuries long.

The person at the center is *composing* the hollow the way a composer writes a symphony, but the composing is not conscious in the way a human composing is conscious. It is not a matter of choosing notes and writing them down. It is a matter of *growing* — of directing the slow upward push of roots and the patient weaving of vines and the gradual thickening of arches into shapes that produce the right resonances, the right standing waves, the right interference patterns in the mist. The city is being *tuned*. It has been being tuned for centuries. It will continue to be tuned for centuries more, because the person at the center is not trying to produce a finished work. They are trying to produce a *living* work — a thing that changes as it is experienced, that responds to the creatures that move through it, that incorporates the dance of the resonant insects and the drinking of the tympanic-eyed creatures and the condensation that forms and falls and forms again into its composition. The city is not a performance. It is a *conversation*. The person at the center speaks through the sound, and the creatures and the plants and the mist speak back through their movements and their growth and their patterns of condensation, and the person listens — through the roots, through the wood, through the flowers that cup the air in their petals — and the conversation continues.

They know you were there. They know you left. They know you carry the four-minute pulse in your ears now, a faint trace of their voice, a seed of their sound planted in the medium of your body. They did not intend this. Or perhaps they did. Perhaps the hollow is not only a composition but a *broadcast*, and the broadcast is not meant for the creatures that live there — those creatures are already fluent — but for the ones who pass through. The visitors. The descendees. The ones who come down into the bowl and are changed by what they hear.

The person at the center is *planting* something in you. Not a memory. Not a compulsion. A *frequency*. A four-minute pulse that will live in your auditory system for the rest of your life, growing fainter but never quite silent, and in certain conditions — in mist, in rain, in the condensation on a glass at a café — it will briefly clarify, and you will hear, in the language of water and air and vibration, a fragment of a sentence that has been in progress for three hundred years and will continue for three hundred more, and the fragment will mean something to you, though you will never be able to translate it, and you will carry it with you, a small wet seed of sound from a place where a person became a city and a voice became a place and the distinction between speaking and *being* was dissolved like salt in warm water.

## The Listeners

The tympanic-eyed creatures do not have a name for themselves. Names are a technology of language, and language is a technology of sound, and sound is the medium in which they are already so thoroughly immersed that to name a thing would be like a fish naming water. They are in it. They are of it. The naming would be redundant.

There are eleven of them. There have always been eleven — or rather, there have been eleven for as long as the current cycle of the composition has been running, which is perhaps forty years, which is perhaps a generation, which is perhaps a *phrase* in the long sentence of the person at the center. Before the current eleven, there were others. After the current eleven, there will be others. The hollow produces them — or rather, the hollow *grows* them, in the way that it grows everything, in the slow patient vegetable way, though their growth is faster than the growth of the architecture, faster than the growth of the flowers, fast enough that a creature born in the spring of a peak cycle is walking the root-streets by the autumn of the same cycle, its tympanic eyes already tuned to the four-minute rhythm, its body already shaped to the standing waves.

They are not born the way mammals are born. They are * fruited*. The root-knot at the center — the body of the person — produces them in a process that is part growth and part secretion, part budding and part composition. They emerge from the knot the way fruit emerges from a tree: small, wet, unformed, their bodies soft and pale and their tympanic eyes still folded closed. They grow quickly. They drink the condensation from every surface, lapping it with their broad dark tongues, and the condensation carries in it the dissolved nutrients of the root system, which is to say the dissolved *thoughts* of the person at the center, and so they drink the person's thinking as they grow, and the thinking shapes them, and by the time their eyes open — by which is meant, by the time their tympanic membranes unfold and tighten and begin to receive the sound — they are already fluent in the language of the hollow, already acclimated to the four-minute cycle, already *literate* in the acoustic landscape that is their entire world.

They are not the person at the center. They are not fragments or extensions or emanations. They are *offspring* — not biologically but compositionally. The person at the center is composing them the way they compose the arches and the streets and the drainage channels, and like the arches and the streets and the drainage channels, the offspring have a function in the composition. They are *the audience*. They are the ones who hear the sound in its fullest complexity, who navigate the standing waves with a precision that no other creature in the hollow can match, who can read the interference patterns in the mist the way a human reads text — not as a single gestalt but as a sequence of distinct elements, each one meaningful, each one connected to the others by grammar and syntax and the deep structural logic of a language that has been developing for three centuries.

What do they hear? They hear the sentence. The long sentence. The one that has been in progress since the person at the center consented to become a place. They hear its clauses and its pauses and its repetitions and its variations. They hear the places where the sentence *thickens* — where the composition grows denser, more complex, more layered, as if the person at the center is approaching a thought they have not yet found the words for, circling it, building toward it, and the tympanic-eyed creatures gather at these points and listen with their whole bodies, their fibrous coats trembling, their tongues still, their eleven forms arranged in a circle around the root-knot like a congregation around a pulpit. They are listening for the next word. The word that has not yet been spoken. The word that the person at the center has been reaching toward for perhaps a decade, perhaps a century, a word that exists in the structure of the composition like a promise, like a direction, like a horizon that the sentence is walking toward.

When the word comes — if it comes — the tympanic-eyed creatures will hear it first. They will feel it in the shift of the standing waves, in the realignment of the interference patterns, in the opening of flowers that have not opened before and the closing of flowers that have always been open. The word will change the hollow. The word will *restructure* it, subtly, the way a new note changes a chord, and the root-streets will shift and the arches will re-tune and the composition will move forward, and the eleven creatures will move with it, adjusting their routes, their patterns, their listening, to the new shape of the sound.

They do not leave the hollow. They cannot. Their tympanic eyes are tuned to the four-minute cycle the way a human eye is tuned to the visible spectrum — it is not a choice but a *constitution*, a fundamental aspect of how they are built. Outside the hollow, in the silence of the ordinary world, they would be blind. They would be deaf. They would be lost in a way that has nothing to do with navigation and everything to do with the collapse of the perceptual world that constitutes their reality. The hollow is not their habitat. The hollow is their *native language*, and to leave it would be to forget every word they know.

They do not know they are trapped. The concept of a trap requires a concept of an outside, and they have no such concept. They have never heard silence. They have never heard a sound that was not part of the composition. For them, the hollow is not a place *in* the world; the hollow *is* the world, and the sound is not a thing the world *makes*; the sound is the world *itself*, ongoing, present, the medium of everything, the fabric of everything, the voice of the one who made them, speaking still, speaking always, the sentence that contains them, that *is* them, that will carry them forward into the next word and the next and the next until the composition reaches a place where their function is complete and they are released — not into death, but into the next phrase, the next set of listeners, the next eleven forms fruited from the root-knot to hear the sentence that does not end.

## The Resonant Insects

They are not insects. They occupy the ecological niche of insects and they function as insects function — they swarm, they cluster, they fill the gaps between larger forms of life — but their ancestry is not the ancestry of beetles or wasps or flies. Their ancestry is the hollow itself. They are fragments of the composition that have broken off and become autonomous, the way a melody that has been repeated enough times begins to exist independently in the mind of the listener, detached from the larger work, carrying its own meaning.

They are small. The smallest are the size of a thumbnail, and the largest — the ones that cling to the upper arches and open their wing-cases only at the peaks of the cycle — are the size of an open hand. Their bodies are shaped like bells, like the bells of trumpets or French horns, and the shaping is not metaphorical: their exoskeletons are *acoustic instruments*, evolved — or rather, *composed* — to resonate at specific frequencies within the hollow's sound field. Each insect is tuned to a different harmonic. Each insect is a *note*.

They do not eat. Or rather, they do not eat in the way that other creatures eat — consuming matter and converting it to energy. They *feed on sound*. The vibration of the air enters their bell-shaped bodies and is absorbed by a dense, fibrous tissue that lines their inner surfaces, a tissue that is structurally similar to the hydrophilic fuzz on the leaves but is tuned to vibration rather than moisture. The sound energy is converted — how, by what mechanism, is unclear, but the conversion is real — into the chemical energy that powers their movement, their growth, their reproduction. They are *acoustivores*. They eat the composition.

This should be a problem. If the insects consume the sound, they should diminish it, the way a grazing animal diminishes a pasture. But the relationship is not extractive. It is *digestive* in the etymological sense — it *distributes*. The insects absorb the sound and re-emit it, each one at its own tuned frequency, each one a tiny amplifier that takes in the complex harmonic of the composition and breaks it into its component notes and broadcasts those notes back into the air. They are not parasites. They are a *filter bank*. They are the mechanism by which the composition is broken down into its constituent frequencies and redistributed through the hollow, so that every corner, every recess, every drainage channel and upper platform receives the full spectrum of the sound rather than only the dominant frequencies. Without the insects, the sound would be louder but simpler. With the insects, the sound is *articulate*.

Their swarming is not random. The clouds that detach from the structural wood at the peaks of the cycle and move through the hollow in coordinated formations are *performing*. They are reenacting the interference patterns, making the standing waves visible through their collective movement, tracing the bars of compression and rarefaction in three dimensions with their bodies. A cloud moves left where the sound compresses and right where it rarefies, and the shape of the cloud in the air is the shape of the sound in the mist, and the tympanic-eyed creatures below watch the swarms the way a human watches a conductor — not for information about the music itself, which they already hear, but for information about *how the music is being interpreted*, what emphasis the insects are adding, what nuances their tuning reveals.

The insects reproduce at the troughs of the cycle — the moments of lowest volume, when the sound subsides and the flowers begin to close and the air briefly thins. They do not mate. They *bud*, splitting along the seams of their bell-shaped bodies, each one producing a single offspring that is a slightly different shape, a slightly different tuning, a new note in the composition's expanding harmonic vocabulary. The offspring are the person's at the center's way of *exploring* — of testing new frequencies, new harmonies, new possibilities within the long sentence. Each new insect is a word attempted. Most of the new tunings are redundant — they overlap with existing frequencies, they add nothing to the spectrum — and these insects die quickly, their brief lives a kind of *crossing out*, a rejected word, a phrase that didn't work. But some of the new tunings are unique. Some of them open frequencies that the hollow has never sustained before, and these insects survive, and they join the swarms, and the composition *grows*, and the person at the center has found a new word.

## The Pale Fungus

It is the oldest thing in the hollow. It was here before the person at the center. It was here before the root-city. It was here before the sound.

Or rather, it was here before the sound *as it is now* — the organized, harmonic, cycling composition that fills the hollow with its four-minute pulse. The fungus was here when the hollow was just a hollow, a low place in the earth that collected water and held it, a damp bowl of condensation and slow growth, and the fungus lived in the dimness and fed on the decay of whatever organic matter washed into the basin, and it grew in its fractal patterns and it glowed its cold yellow glow and it was *silent*. It was the silence that was here before the sound.

It has not been eradicated. This is significant. The person at the center, in growing the root-city, in building the acoustic architecture, in composing the sound that fills the hollow, did not eliminate the fungus. They *incorporated* it. The fungus grows on the undersides of the structural platforms, in the places where the sound is least intense, where the standing waves create nodes of near-silence, pockets of acoustic stillness in the otherwise deafening field. These pockets are the fungus's habitat, and they are also the fungus's *function* — they are the rests in the composition, the pauses between phrases, the moments of silence that give the sound its structure, its rhythm, its meaning. Without the fungus, the sound would be continuous and undifferentiated. With the fungus, the sound has *edges*. The sound has *shape*. The fungus is the negative space in the composition, and the negative space is what makes the positive space legible.

The fungus does not know this. The fungus is not aware of the composition, is not aware of the person at the center, is not aware of the sound as a *signal*. The fungus is aware only of moisture and nutrients and the slow patient geometry of its own growth. It grows in fractal patterns because fractals are the most efficient way to maximize surface area in a constrained space, and the fungus is nothing if not efficient. It glows because the bioluminescence is a byproduct of its metabolic process, the way heat is a byproduct of fire. The glow is not *for* anything. The glow simply *is*.

But the glow has been *used*. The tympanic-eyed creatures, who navigate by sound, do not need light, but they use the fungus-glow the way a human uses the faint light of distant stars — not for navigation but for *orientation*, for the confirmation that there is a world beyond the immediate, that the hollow has depth, that the darkness has a floor and a ceiling and walls. The fungus-glow is the hollow's night sky, and the creatures look up at it with their tympanic eyes and do not see it but *feel* it, the faint cold yellow radiation registering on their fibrous coats as a barely perceptible warmth-in-reverse, a coolness that maps the architecture above them, and they know where they are.

The fungus and the person at the center have a relationship, though neither would describe it that way. The person has grown the root-city around the fungus, has shaped the architecture to preserve the fungus's habitat, has tuned the sound to leave the nodes of silence where the fungus needs them. The fungus, in return, maintains the silence, grows into the acoustic still-points and *holds* them, its fractal bodies absorbing stray frequencies that would otherwise fill the pauses and flatten the composition into noise. The relationship is not symbiotic in the biological sense. It is *compositional*. The person and the fungus are *collaborators*, though the fungus does not know it, and the person knows it only in the slow, distributed way that they know everything now — not as a thought but as a *tendency*, a *pattern of growth*, a thing the body does without the mind directing it.

## What Grows at the Edges

There are other things. Smaller things. Things that occupy the margins of the hollow the way marginalia occupies the edges of a text — present, significant, easily overlooked.

The things that scuttle in the drainage channels are arthropodal, perhaps, or something analogous to arthropods — segmented, many-legged, their bodies flat and low and armored with a chitinous substance that is the same grey-black as the root bark. They are the hollow's *maintenance crew*. They eat debris — fallen leaves, dead fungus, the bodies of resonant insects whose tunings did not fit — and they eat it *completely*, reducing organic matter to a fine paste that they deposit in the drainage channels, where the water carries it down to the root system, where the roots absorb it, where the person at the center *incorporates* it. The scuttling things are the hollow's digestive system. They are the mechanism by which the city recycles its own waste into new growth. They are not numerous — perhaps a hundred, perhaps two hundred — and they are not visible unless you look closely at the drainage channels, where their movement registers as a faint rustling, a faint clicking, barely audible beneath the sound but *present*, a tiny rhythmic underlayer that is the sound of the city cleaning itself.

The things that hang from the upper arches on threads of silk-like material are more mysterious. They are not spiders, and the threads are not silk, and the structures they build — small, irregular, vaguely cocoon-like — are not webs. They are *instruments*. The threads are tuned. They vibrate at specific frequencies when the sound peaks, and the vibrations are transmitted to the cocoon-like structures, which *amplify* them and redirect them downward, into the body of the hollow, at angles and intensities that the root-arches alone cannot produce. The hanging things are *supplementary speakers*. They fill in the gaps in the sound field, the acoustic shadows where the standing waves do not reach, the places where the composition would otherwise be inaudible. They are small — the size of a walnut — and they are everywhere, thousands of them, a dense canopy of tuned threads and amplifying cocoons that covers the upper reaches of the hollow like a second ceiling, and their collective effect is to make the sound *uniform*, to ensure that every point in the hollow receives the full composition, that there are no dead spots, no quiet corners, no places where the sentence is incomplete.

They are, perhaps, the most recent addition to the composition. Their threads are thinner than the root-architecture, their structures less integrated, less *settled*. They feel experimental. They feel like the person at the center is *trying something* — a new technique, a new approach to distributing the sound, a way of reaching into the corners that the root-arches miss. If the technique works, the hanging things will be incorporated more deeply — their threads will thicken, their cocoons will integrate with the structural wood, they will become a permanent layer of the architecture. If the technique does not work, they will be allowed to die, and their threads will be absorbed by the root system, and the nutrients will be recycled, and the person at the center will have learned something, and the sentence will continue, and the word that did not work will be crossed out, and the next word will be attempted.

## The Hollow as a Living Thing

What lives in the Chorus Hollow is the Chorus Hollow.

Not the person at the center. Not the tympanic-eyed creatures. Not the resonant insects or the pale fungus or the scuttling cleaners or the hanging amplifiers. All of these *live in* the hollow, but none of them *are* the hollow. The hollow is the organism. The hollow is the entity. The hollow is the thing that lives.

Biology recognizes levels of organization: cell, tissue, organ, organ system, organism. The hollow adds a level: *place*. The hollow is an organism that is also a location, a living thing that is also a *where*, and the where-ness is not incidental to the what-ness but *constitutive* of it. The hollow is what it is *because* it is where it is — a bowl in the earth that collects water and holds sound, a natural resonance chamber that a thinking being chose to inhabit and grow into and compose through. The hollow is the instrument and the musician and the music, all at once, all the same thing, all *alive* in the way that only a thing that is entirely integrated can be alive — not a collection of parts but a single process, a single ongoing event, a sentence that is also a body that is also a place.

It lives the way a forest lives — through the interaction of countless organisms in a shared medium — but it lives *more* than a forest lives, because the interaction is *organized*, is *intentional*, is *composed*. A forest is a conversation without a topic. The hollow is a conversation *about something*. The something is the sentence, and the sentence is the sound, and the sound is the hollow, and the hollow is alive, and the alive-ness is the thing that lives there.

It will outlive you. It will outlive the tympanic-eyed creatures and the resonant insects and the hanging amplifiers. It may outlive the person at the center, if the person at the center can be said to die — though what death would mean for a being that has been translated from flesh to root to sound is unclear. Perhaps the person will eventually finish the sentence. Perhaps the sentence has an end, a final word, a last swelling peak after which the flowers close for good and the insects settle and the standing waves flatten and the hollow falls silent. Perhaps the hollow is a *work*, and works have endings, and the ending is built into the structure, already present in the composition, already growing toward, the way the last note of a symphony is present in the first.

Or perhaps the sentence does not end. Perhaps the hollow is not a work but a *practice* — an ongoing process of composition that has no goal, no finale, no resolution, only the endless variation of the four-minute cycle, the endless growth of the root-city, the endless fruiting of new listeners and new notes and new words. Perhaps the hollow is not making something. Perhaps the hollow *is* something. Perhaps what lives there is *living*, in the purest and most terrifying sense of the word — not living *toward* a purpose but living *as* a purpose, the ongoing, organized, deafening, beautiful persistence of a thing that has found a way to keep speaking after the mouth is gone.

The hollow lives. Go there if you want to hear what it says. But know that what it says will not leave you. What it says will *stay* — in your bones, in your sinuses, in the fluid of your spine, in the four-minute pulse that you will carry like a second heartbeat for the rest of your life. The hollow does not take prisoners. The hollow takes *witnesses*. And the witnesses carry the sound, and the sound carries the sentence, and the sentence carries the person, and the person carries the hollow, and the hollow lives in everyone who has ever descended into it and will live in them until the last one of them is silent, and perhaps beyond, perhaps in the condensation on a glass at a café, perhaps in the ringing that follows loud music, perhaps in the low hum of pipes in a wall, perhaps in every standing wave in every body of air that ever was or ever will be — the sentence, the sound, the voice of the place that is a person, speaking, speaking, speaking, never finished, never silent, never done.
