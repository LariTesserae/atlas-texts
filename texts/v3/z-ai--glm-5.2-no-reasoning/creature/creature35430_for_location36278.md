# What Lives in Keldarath

## The People

The first thing that must be said about the people of Keldarath is that they are not a people who chose this place. Or rather — the choosing was so long ago that it has passed from history into geology, and the relationship between the residents and their plateau is now less a contract than a condition, like the relationship between the lichen and the rock. They are here because they are here. The origin stories they tell speak of ascent — of a people who climbed, for reasons that range from the mythic (a god's command, a star's guidance, a flood below) to the practical (conflict, famine, the simpler threat of stronger people in richer lands) — but the stories disagree on the details, and the residents, when pressed, will tell you that the reasons matter less than the fact. They are a people of the high stone. They have been a people of the high stone for longer than their buildings remember, and their buildings remember a great deal.

They are not tall. This is the second thing you notice, after the thin air — or perhaps it is the first thing the air lets you notice, once you have enough of it to think with. The residents of Keldarath are short by the standards of lower cultures, and their bodies are compact in a way that goes beyond mere height. They are built like the city itself: dense, efficient, without excess. Their chests are deep — the ribcages expanded by generations of thin air into broad, bellows-like structures that can pull meaning from air that would leave a lowlander gasping. Their hands are broad, thick-fingered, the palms calloused in specific patterns that reveal their trade: stone-workers have a particular roughness across the base of the thumb, weavers a hardening across the fingertips, herders a permanent flexion in the last joints of the fingers from decades of gripping the cold ropes of goat-hair.

Their skin is dark — not from sun alone, though the high-altitude light is intense, but from a deeper inheritance, a pigmentation that predates the plateau and that the altitude has only reinforced. Against the pale stone of their city, their faces are striking, and a visitor from the lowlands will often find, upon returning below, that what they remember of Keldarath is not the architecture or the landscape but the faces: dark, composed, watchful, set in the grey frame of the city like inlays in matrix.

Their eyes are adapted to the light. Not larger, exactly — this is not a troglodytic adaptation — but more responsive, the pupils capable of a wider range of dilation, so that in the bright midday sun they contract to near-invisible points and in the dim passages they open wide, dark, and receptive, taking in what little light the stone reflects. The residents say they can see better in the near-dark than visitors can, and this is probably true — not from any exotic faculty but from the simple training of living in a city where much of life happens in spaces lit only by reflected and filtered light.

Their faces are not expressive in the way that lower cultures value expression. They are not stone-faced — that is a lowland misreading. The faces of Keldarath are *efficient*, communicating in small shifts what other faces communicate with large gestures: a slight narrowing of the eyes that means skepticism, a barely perceptible tilt of the head that means attention, a micro-movement of the mouth that can, depending on context, mean agreement, affection, or the particular form of patience that the residents deploy when dealing with visitors who are struggling with the altitude. They have developed this minimal expressiveness not from coldness but from density — in a city where you are rarely more than a few meters from other people, where the passages are narrow and the walls are close, large gestures are intrusive, and the face has learned to speak quietly.

They name themselves in relation to the city. A person's full name includes their lineage — their family of origin — but also their location within the city: which passage, which terrace, which of the shallow steps that descend the plateau's tilt. This is not an address; it is an identity. To say "I am of the seventh descent, near the old cistern" is to say something about who you are, what your family does, what stone your walls are made of, what water you drink, and what you see when you step out of your door in the morning. The city's geography is its social structure, and the residents carry their place in it the way other cultures carry surnames.

Their lifespans are not remarkable — neither notably longer nor shorter than the regional average — but the texture of their lives is different. Childhood is long here, not because children mature slowly but because there is so much to learn, and the learning cannot be rushed. A child of Keldarath begins helping with the condensation channels before they can speak — carried in a sling on a parent's back, watching the water find its way through the carved grooves — and by the time they are walking, they know the city's water system as a body knows its own veins. They learn the stone the way other children learn language: by immersion, by necessity, by the constant, low-pressure exposure to a world that demands understanding and offers no alternative.

Elders are not rare — the altitude is hard but not lethal, and those who survive childhood and the vulnerabilities of early adulthood can expect to live into their sixties and seventies — but they are distinctive. The oldest residents are the most deeply adapted: their bodies have settled into the plateau's demands, their breathing slow and deep, their movements spare and deliberate, their faces worn into expressions of such composed attentiveness that younger residents sometimes say the elders look like the mountains do if the mountains had eyes. The elders do not retire from work — there is no retirement, in the formal sense — but they shift from physical labor to the work of knowledge: advising on construction, reading the stone's fractures, teaching the young, mediating the inevitable frictions of dense communal life. An elder of Keldarath is a library, and the city treats its libraries with the care appropriate to their contents.

## How They Are With Each Other

The people of Keldarath live closer to each other than most cultures can imagine. The passages are narrow, the homes are small, the boundary between one family's space and another's is a stone wall that may be less than an arm's-length thick. Sound carries. Smell carries. The knowledge of other people's lives is not an invasion of privacy but an environmental condition, like the cold or the wind, and the residents have developed social norms that treat it as such.

They are quiet in their bodies. They move through the passages with a care that is not stealth but consideration — each step placed, each gesture contained, each voice held to the low murmur that the stone passages carry well and the open air swallows. A visitor walking through the city at midday will hear the sounds of activity — the tap of tools, the click of looms, the murmur of conversation — but will see very little of it, because most of it is happening behind walls, in the small, warm interiors that are the private spaces of Keldarath's life. What you see in the passages are transitions: a person moving from one place to another, carrying something, going to get water, returning from the herds. The passages are the between-spaces, and the residents pass through them the way blood passes through veins — quickly, purposefully, without lingering.

The deep social work of Keldarath happens in the covered passages — the roofed spaces that serve as workshops, gathering places, and, in the coldest weather, extended living areas. Here, the residents are together by default, and the customs of togetherness are elaborate, formal, and essential. There are rituals of greeting that establish who is present and in what capacity. There are protocols for speaking that govern turn-taking, volume, and the acceptable distance between speakers. There are norms for touch — brief, purposeful, and always signaled — and norms for absence, the careful practice of not-being-present that allows the dense social fabric to breathe.

Conflict is rare but not absent, and when it occurs, it is handled with a formality that a lowland culture might find excessive but that the residents consider essential. A serious disagreement between two families is a matter for the entire neighborhood, because in a city this dense, a conflict that is not resolved becomes a fault line in the living rock of the community, and fault lines, as the residents know from their stone, only widen with time. Mediators — respected elders, usually, but also skilled younger people who have a talent for the work — are called, and the conflict is discussed, slowly, carefully, in a covered passage with witnesses, until the matter is settled. Not won or lost. Settled. The residents have a word for this process that translates roughly as "making the ground level again," and it is one of the most important skills a person can develop.

Love and attachment are not performed in the lowland fashion. There are no grand declarations, no public displays, no elaborate courtship rituals. Instead, the people of Keldarath love through attention — through the quality of their noticing, the precision of their care, the things they make for each other. A weaver who loves someone will weave a particular pattern into a cloth, a pattern that references a shared memory — a particular morning's frost, a particular configuration of light in a particular passage, a moment known only to the two of them. A stoneworker who loves someone will carve a threshold for their home with a fit so precise that the seams disappear, a gift of skill and time that says, in the language of this city, what other languages say with flowers. The residents do not marry in the religious sense — their spiritual practices do not include formal unions — but they form bonds that are recognized by the community through a simple, silent ritual: the two people begin sharing a meal in a covered passage, and the community sees, and does not remark, and the bond is made real by the community's tacit acknowledgment.

Children are everyone's concern. In a city this dense, this interdependent, a child is not the possession of a family but a member of the community, and while biological parentage is known and valued, the raising of a child is distributed. A child of the seventh descent may be taught stonework by an elder of the third descent, herding by a teenager of the ninth, weaving by a neighbor whose own children are grown. This is not abandonment or institutionalized child-rearing; it is the natural consequence of living in a place where everyone is close, where every adult knows every child, and where the line between one family and another is a permeable membrane, not a wall. The residents have a saying: "A child is a stone; the city shapes it." This is not a diminishment of the family but an acknowledgment that in Keldarath, the city is the family, and the family is the city, and the distinction between them is a luxury of more spacious places.

## How They Work

The labor of Keldarath is organized not by trade guilds or formal professions but by *inclination* and *need* — two forces that the residents treat as equally legitimate. A person who is drawn to stonework will apprentice with a stoneworker, not through a formal arrangement but through the simple act of showing up, watching, carrying tools, and gradually being given tasks of increasing complexity. There is no certification, no examination, no moment of admission to the craft. There is only the gradual accumulation of skill and the community's recognition of it — recognition that is expressed not through title or ceremony but through the assignment of work. When the community trusts your stonework, you are given walls to build. When it does not, you are given walls to carry.

The stoneworkers are the city's bones. They build, repair, read the fractures, and maintain the long, slow negotiation between the city and the mountain's movement. They are not a large group — perhaps one in twenty residents is primarily a stoneworker — but their influence is outsized, because every aspect of the city's life depends on the integrity of its walls. A stoneworker in Keldarath is part builder, part geologist, part historian: they must understand the stone's properties, the mountain's motion, the water's pathways, and the history of every wall they touch, because every wall contains the memory of earlier walls, and a repair that ignores this history is a repair that will fail. The oldest stoneworking families have oral traditions that go back dozens of generations, records of which walls have cracked and why, which stones hold and which spall, which joints accommodate movement and which resist it until they shatter. This knowledge is not written — it is held in bodies and passed through hands, and the loss of a master stoneworker is a catastrophe the community feels for decades.

The weavers are the city's voice — not literally, but economically and culturally. Their cloth is Keldarath's primary export, the good that connects the city to the world below, and their patterns are the city's primary aesthetic expression, the closest thing the residents have to a visual art. Weaving is taught to nearly everyone — almost every resident can produce basic cloth — but the master weavers, those who create the complex patterned textiles that are traded for premium goods, are a smaller group, and their work is valued accordingly. The looms are in the covered passages, where the light is steady and the air is warm, and the weavers work in a companionable proximity that is both social and practical — they share thread, share patterns, share the rhythm of their work, and the sound of a weaving passage is one of the characteristic sounds of Keldarath: a complex, layered clicking that the residents say is the city thinking.

The herders are the city's connection to the open plateau. They are the residents who spend the most time outside the city's dense fabric, and they are, as a group, slightly different from the others — more weathered, more solitary, more comfortable with the wind and the cold and the wide, bright exposure of the plateau's surface. They are responsible for the tarra, the domesticated goats that are the city's primary animal resource, and their days follow the slow, cyclical rhythm of the herds: out to the grazing in the morning, back to the sheltered folds at the plateau's edge in the afternoon, into the city's lower structures for the night. The herders know the plateau's surface the way the stoneworkers know the city's walls — every crack, every lichen-patch, every sheltered hollow where a goat might rest — and their knowledge is a kind of cartography that exists only in their heads and in the routes their feet have worn into the stone over generations. They are often the ones who encounter visitors first, because they are the ones who are outside, and they have developed a particular kind of patience for lowlanders struggling with the altitude — a patience that is genuine but also, occasionally, amused, in the quiet way of people who have watched many visitors gasp and wobble and slowly, slowly acclimate.

The water-tenders are a less visible group but an essential one. They maintain the condensation channels, the cisterns, the gullies, and the complex network of stone-cut drainage that is the city's circulatory system. Their work is daily, repetitive, and precise: clearing the channels of frost and debris, checking the flow, measuring the cistern levels, repairing the small cracks and blockages that the mountain's constant movement creates. They work in the early morning, when the channels are running, and in the late evening, when the frost is forming, and their schedule is set not by human preference but by the daily cycle of condensation and melt. A water-tender's life is deeply synchronized with the plateau's rhythms, and the residents say that the water-tenders can feel the coming of rain before the clouds form — a claim that may be superstition or may be the accumulated sensitivity of people who have spent their lives attending to the smallest signs of water's intention.

The gardeners are the city's most intensive workers. Their plots are tiny — a footprint, a hand's-breadth, a crack in a wall where a root vegetable can be coaxed into existence — but their attention is absolute. Every plant is known, every leaf is examined, every harvest is weighed against the plant's survival and the plot's continuing productivity. The gardeners are often the oldest residents, not because gardening is an elder's work but because it requires a patience and a precision that accumulate over a lifetime. A gardener of Keldarath can tell you the history of a single root — how long it has been growing, what water it has received, what shade it has endured, and when it will be ready to take without killing the plant that produced it. This knowledge is not written; it is remembered, plant by plant, season by season, in a continuous record that some gardeners claim goes back to the first garden on the plateau.

And there are the children, who work too — not because they are forced but because work in Keldarath is not separate from life. Children carry water, help with the herds, learn to weave by passing thread to the weavers, learn to read stone by holding tools for the stoneworkers. Their work is small, appropriate, and inseparable from their education, which is not an institution but a constant, ambient process — the slow absorption of the city's knowledge through participation in the city's life. A child of Keldarath does not go to school; the city is the school, and the curriculum is survival, craft, community, and the long, patient negotiation with the mountain that is the residents' fundamental task.

## How They Think

The people of Keldarath do not think in straight lines. This is not a deficiency — it is an adaptation. Straight lines are rare in their world: the mountain does not rise in straight lines, the water does not flow in straight lines, the passages do not run in straight lines, and the residents' thinking follows the contours of their landscape, which means it curves, doubles back, finds its way by indirection, and arrives at its conclusions not through the shortest path but through the path that the terrain allows.

Their concept of time is not linear but cyclical and layered. The daily cycle — frost, melt, condensation, trickle, frost again — is the smallest unit. The seasonal cycle — the tarra's movements, the garden's production, the shifting angle of the sun — is the next. The construction cycle — the building, weathering, and rebuilding of the city's walls — is larger still. And the geological cycle — the mountain's slow rise, the plateau's gradual tilt, the opening of new fractures in the bedrock — is the largest, a cycle that the residents are aware of but cannot feel, a clock that ticks in millennia and that gives their thinking a depth, a patience, a long-view quality that visitors from faster cultures often mistake for fatalism.

It is not fatalism. It is scale. The residents of Keldarath understand that they are temporary — not in the morbid sense, but in the simple, geological sense. They know the mountain is rising, the air is thinning, and the city's future is finite. They do not despair of this. They do not rage against it. They build well, live close, and keep the condensation channels clear, and they teach their children to do the same, and they trust that the city will outlast them, as it has outlasted their ancestors, by the simple virtue of being built with an understanding of what it is built upon.

Their spiritual life is not separate from this. The residents do not worship the mountain in the formal sense — they have no temples, no priesthood, no liturgy — but they hold the mountain in a regard that is so deep and so constant that it functions as religion without declaring itself as such. The stone is sacred in the way that necessity is sacred: not because it has been declared so but because everything depends on it. The water is sacred in the same way. The frost, the wind, the light — these are not supernatural forces but natural ones, and the residents' relationship to them is one of attentive, respectful accommodation, not supplication.

They do have rituals, but they are small, domestic, and integrated into daily life. The morning clearing of the condensation channels is a ritual — performed with a formality and a care that goes beyond mere maintenance. The evening gathering in the covered passages, when families come together to share food and warmth, is a ritual. The midday pause, when the sun reaches the bottom of the passages and the city is briefly, fully illuminated, is a ritual — a moment of looking-up that is not prayer but is not not-prayer, an acknowledgment of the light's arrival that the residents perform without thinking, the way a body breathes.

Their songs are ritual too — the work songs, the herding calls, the condensation songs that are sung in the early morning as the channels are cleared. These are not performances. They are functional, designed to coordinate labor, carry through stone, and maintain the communal rhythm. But they are also beautiful, in the way that things are beautiful when they have been shaped by long use and genuine need — the beauty of a well-fitted stone, a well-worn handle, a cloth that has been woven so many times the pattern is in the weaver's hands.

The residents do not speak of an afterlife. When asked, they will say that the dead go into the stone — meaning that the dead are buried in the deep places, in the cavities where the oldest structures meet the mountain, and that their bodies become part of the rock that the city is built on. This is not metaphor. The oldest foundations of Keldarath contain human remains, incorporated into the structure, and the residents do not find this disturbing. The dead are not gone; they are *here*, beneath the walls, part of the stone, part of the city, part of the long, slow argument between construction and geology that is the residents' entire world. When a wall is rebuilt, the old stones — including the ones that contain the dead — are reused, and the dead are not removed. They are built back into the new wall, and the wall is stronger for their presence, or the residents believe it is, and at this altitude, belief and material reality are not easily distinguished.

## What They Are

The people of Keldarath are not a large population. The plateau can support perhaps two thousand people at the most generous estimate, and the city has likely held close to that number for most of its history, with fluctuations — losses in the hardest winters, gains in the most productive periods — that even out over decades into a stability that is the mathematical expression of the plateau's carrying capacity. They are not growing. They are not declining. They are *persisting*, and the persistence is itself an achievement that the residents do not celebrate because they do not think of it as an achievement. It is simply what they do. It is what they have always done.

They are not lonely. A visitor might expect loneliness in a place this high, this remote, this far from the green, warm, water-rich world below. But the residents of Keldarath are perhaps the least lonely people the visitor will ever meet. They are surrounded — by each other, by their animals, by their stone, by the constant, close, warm presence of a community so dense that solitude is not a default but a rare and valued exception. A resident of Keldarath who wants to be alone must make an effort: they must climb to the highest point of the city, where the oldest structures thin out and the open plateau begins, and stand in the wind, and look out over the stone, and be, for a moment, a single figure in a vast landscape. They do this sometimes. The residents have a word for this practice, and it translates, approximately, to "going to meet the mountain alone," and it is understood as a need that occasionally arises and is respected, provided it does not last too long and does not become habitual, because in Keldarath, to be alone for too long is to be cold, and to be cold for too long is to be dead.

What they are, finally, is *adapted* — not in the biological sense alone, though that too, but in the total sense: adapted in body, in thought, in social organization, in craft, in architecture, in the thousand small habits and assumptions and customs that make a people fit a place. They fit the plateau the way the lichen fits the rock — not perfectly, not comfortably, but completely, in a way that makes separation from the place unimaginable. A resident of Keldarath taken to the lowlands will not die, but they will be diminished — the air too thick, the colors too soft, the distances too forgiving, the space too wide, the silence too full of growing things. They will feel, in the lowlands, the way a lowlander feels on the plateau: wrong. Misplaced. Out of true. And if they are gone too long, they will begin to lose the thing that makes them what they are — the thin, clear, mineral attentiveness that is the gift of the high stone and the price of living on it.

They come back. They always come back. Up the long path, through the thinning air, past the disappearing vegetation, into the wind that finds you before the city does, and through the gradual emergence of rectilinear intention in the landscape — wall, terrace, foundation, passage — until they are inside, and the stone is around them, and the condensation is trickling in the channels, and the looms are clicking, and the tarra are calling, and the frost is forming, and the mountain is growing, and the city is going about its business, which is the business of being, and which is, in its own slow, dense, quiet, patient, mineral, beautiful way, enough.
