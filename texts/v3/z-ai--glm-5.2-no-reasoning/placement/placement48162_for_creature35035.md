---

# The Second Memoir of the Cwm-Wyrm

## Being a desultory account kept by the author during her sojourn upon the High Plateau of the Condensation Engines, for her own satisfaction and no other creature's

---

I.

I was not the first to arrive here. That distinction belongs to the salamanders, who have been here longest, or perhaps to the engines, depending on which kind of "first" you mean, and I have spent enough time here to have stopped asking which kind of "first" is the one that matters.

My name, such as it is, doesn't belong on this page. I came up the southern scarp with two field journals and a thermos of tea that went cold in the first hour. I came because I heard about the plateau from a woman I was drinking with in the last valley town, a geomorphologist who had been turned back by the weather twice and who described the place with the specific resentment of someone who had been denied something she felt she'd earned. She said: there are machines up there that breathe and no one knows who made them and there are birds that don't exist in any catalogue and there is a kind of newt that watches you. She said: it's cold and wet and the light is wrong and I couldn't stay more than a day and a half.

I finished her drink. I packed the next morning.

---

II.

The scarp took two days. The stone is so new it cuts your boots. I mean that literally: the granites have edges like broken glass, edges that have not yet been rounded by wind or water or the slow patience of lichen, because there is no lichen, because there has not been time. I have worked in recently deglaciated terrain — I spent three seasons in Svalbard, two in the Karakoram — and I know the look of stone that has just been shown to the air for the first time. This stone has that look, but more so. The Svalbard granites, even fresh-exposed, carried the memory of the glacier that shaped them: striations, polish, the signature of ice. This stone carries no signature at all. It is not shaped. It is *placed*. It came out of the earth and it sits where it sits and it has not yet begun to become anything other than what it was when it arrived.

I mention this because it matters. It matters because everything up here is the same age as everything else, more or less, and that age is *recent*, and the recency is the fact that everything else follows from. The stone is recent. The water is recent. The engines are recent. The birds are recent. The life — such as it is, this thin dark scrim of being — is recent. I am recent. I arrived eleven days ago and I am the most recent thing here, and the plateau knows it, and I know it knows, and we have reached an arrangement about it that I will describe later, if I can.

---

III.

I am — I was, before I came here — a drainage ecologist. A hydrologist with a specialization in groundwater-surface water interactions in recently exposed terrain. I spent the first decade of my career studying the first five years of watersheds: places where glaciers had retreated within the observed record, places where volcanic surfaces had cooled enough to accept water, places where the ground had just begun to figure out what to do with rainfall. I am interested in the moment water meets stone for the first time. The negotiation. The argument. The way water has no plan but stone has constraints, and the way what emerges between them — the channel, the rill, the seep, the spring — is something neither of them designed but both of them caused.

This is the best way I know how to explain why I stayed.

---

IV.

The engines.

I have listened to them for eleven days and I have stopped hearing them, in the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat, in the way you stop hearing the sea when you live by it. They are there. They are always there. The polyrhythm of eleven slightly-out-of-phase cycles has entered my chest and my sleep and my dreaming. I dream in ninety-second loops now. I wake at the clunk of valves repositioning. I sleep through the intake. The plateau has given me its rhythm and I have taken it, and I do not know whether this is adaptation or infection, and I have stopped asking which kind it is.

I have measured the engines, as well as I can with what I have, which is a tape measure and a notebook and the thermometer from my emergency kit. They are ten meters tall, plus or minus thirty centimeters — the one I can reach most easily measures 10.12 meters at the cowl's lip. The panels are riveted steel, as described in every account I've read, but "riveted steel" is a description that carries the wrong implications. These are not the rivets of human manufacture — not the neat, domed heads of structural steelwork, not the puckered seams of a ship's hull. They are organic. That is the only word. Each rivet is slightly different from its neighbors, slightly irregular, as though it was *grown* in place rather than driven. The steel itself has a grain to it, a faint directional texture visible only when the condensate runs across it, and the grain runs vertically, top to bottom, as though the metal was laid down the way wood is laid down, ring by ring, in a direction determined by something internal rather than external.

I am not a metallurgist. I am saying what I see.

The gaskets — the black compound between the panels, the stuff that weeps condensate — I touched it, on the third day, with my bare finger. It was warm. Not engine-warm, not friction-warm, but *body*-warm. Thirty-six, thirty-seven degrees. And it had a texture I did not expect: not rubber, not polymer, not any synthetic I have handled. It had give, and resilience, and a faint, fine granularity, and when I pulled my finger away there was a thin film of something on my fingertip that was not water and not oil but something between, something that evaporated in the cold air within seconds, leaving my finger dry and faintly acidic-smelling and tingling.

I washed my hands. I washed them thoroughly, in the coldest water I have ever willingly put my hands in, and I watched the tingling fade over the next hour, and I did not touch the gaskets again.

But I thought about it. I have thought about it every day since.

---

V.

The water.

Here is what I can tell you about the water, as a hydrologist, as someone whose professional life has been organized around the question of where water comes from and where it goes.

It comes from everywhere. It comes from the cloud ceiling, which is permanent, which is geographically fixed, which does not move on because the topography and the altitude and the thermal dynamics of the plateau hold it here the way a bowl holds water. It comes from the ground, from beneath the plateau floor, from some source I cannot identify, driven upward by pressure I cannot measure, emerging through cracks in the raw stone as a slow, continuous weep. It comes from the engines, which collect it from the air and return it to the air, minus what they keep, and what they keep runs down their flanks and pools at their bases and seeps back into the ground.

It goes nowhere. That is the hydrological fact that has occupied me for eleven days and that I cannot adequately convey to anyone who has not stood here. The water goes *nowhere*. There is no outlet. There is no stream leaving the plateau. There is no subsurface drainage that I can detect — I dug a pit, on the fifth day, forty centimeters down, and the water in it rose to exactly the level of the surrounding surface and stayed there, not flowing, not draining, just *present*, at a head pressure that is the same everywhere, as though the entire plateau floor is a single water table with no gradient, no direction, no preference. The water arrives and it stays. It cycles — ground to air to cloud to condensation to stone to ground — but it does not leave. The plateau is a closed basin, a sealed system, a body of water that has no mouth.

This is not stable. I need to say that. In the terms of my discipline, a closed hydrological basin at this altitude, with this much moisture input, should be accumulating. The water level should be rising. The pools should be deepening, merging, eventually flooding the entire plateau surface, because the water has nowhere to go and more is always arriving. The system should be drowning itself.

It is not. The water level is constant. The pools maintain their depth. The rills maintain their paths. The ground maintains its saturation. Something is removing water from the system at the same rate it is entering, and that something is not drainage and not evaporation — the cloud ceiling prevents the one and the closed basin prevents the other — and the only mechanism I can identify, after eleven days of watching and listening and measuring with inadequate tools, is the engines.

The engines are not just condensing water. They are *transporting* it. Where? I do not know. Into what? I do not know. But the mass balance does not close unless the engines are doing something with the water they collect that is not returning it to the local cycle. They are bleeding it off. Removing it. Sending it — somewhere. Through the gaskets, perhaps. Through the warm black compound that tingled on my finger. Through some mechanism I do not have the training or the instruments to identify.

The plateau is not flooding because the engines are keeping it from flooding. The engines are the drainage. They are the mouth the system should not have and does, somehow, have.

I am a hydrologist and I am telling you: the water does not balance. The water cannot balance. And it balances anyway.

---

VI.

I nearly left on the seventh day.

The cold had got into me by then — not just into my fingers and ears, the peripheral casualties, but into my core, into the deep muscles of my back and my thighs, a stiffness that sleep did not relieve and tea did not warm and walking only partially addressed. The light does not change here. I cannot emphasize this enough to anyone who has not experienced it. The light does not change. There is no morning, no evening, no noon. There is only the same flat, sourceless, grey-silk glow, hour after hour, and my body lost its clock within three days and did not find it again, and by the seventh day I did not know whether it was day or night and I did not know how long I had been here and I did not know whether I had slept or merely stopped moving for a while, and the cold was in my core, and I was frightened.

Not of anything specific. Of the plateau. Of the way it breathed and I breathed with it and I could not stop breathing with it. Of the way the birds ignored me when I stood still and started when I moved, not at the movement but at some quality of the movement that I could not identify, as though I were producing a note they were listening for and occasionally hitting it wrong. Of the way the engines' rhythm had entered my chest and would not leave, so that I could feel my heart attempting to sync with the nearest machine and succeeding, briefly, and then drifting, and then syncing again, a sloppy phase-lock that left me dizzy and breathless and more frightened.

I packed my bag. I folded the journal. I started south.

And then, at the edge of the southern scarp, the salamander was there.

---

VII.

Not the same salamander. I do not think. I have seen four — no, five — distinct individuals, distinguished by size and by the pattern of pale marks along their flanks, which are not quite spots and not quite stripes but something between, unique to each animal the way a fingerprint is unique, though I am not sure "animal" is the right word for what they are.

This one was large. The largest I have seen. Twenty centimeters, perhaps more. It was sitting on the exact stone I would have stepped on to begin my descent, and it was looking at me.

I have been looked at by animals my whole professional life. I have been looked at by bears, by wolves, by wolverines, by elk in rut, by foxes in winter pelage. I have been evaluated as threat, as prey, as irrelevance, as curiosity. I know what animal looking is. This was not that.

This was *being seen*. Not being assessed — being *registered*. The salamander's eyes were on mine, and there was something behind them that was not the salamander, the same way there is something behind your eyes that is not your retina. It was looking at me the way I look at a stream gauge: reading the value. Taking the measurement. And it was deciding something.

It held my gaze for a long time. I counted my breaths — the breaths I could feel in my chest, the ones that had synced with the engines — and I got to nineteen before it moved. Nineteen breaths. That is not an animal's evaluation. That is a reading.

It didn't leave. It turned — not away from me, but *around*, pivoting on its reduced limbs in a motion that was less a turn than a reorientation, the way a weather vane turns, the whole body swinging to a new heading — and it moved north, back onto the plateau, at a pace that was clearly an invitation. Not fast enough to lose me. Not slow enough to be ambiguous. An invitation.

I am a hydrologist. I am a scientist. I do not follow salamanders.

I followed the salamander.

---

VIII.

It led me to the pool between engines three and seven. The large one. The one with the filamentous alga, the pale yellow-green web across the pool floor, the one producing the first oxygen this landscape has ever made. I have been to this pool six times. I have knelt at its edge and watched the oxygen bubbles form on the filaments and release, one by one, in their slow, silent procession to the surface. It is the pool where I kneel and my knees ache in the cold and I feel like I am watching something being born.

The salamander went into the water and did not surface.

I waited. I waited for a long time — I counted the engine cycles, three and seven, and they are different, three running slightly faster than seven, and I counted seven full cycles of three and six of seven, which is roughly ten and a half minutes — and the salamander did not surface.

I kneeled. My knees ached. The cold was immediate, the water temperature barely above freezing, and my kneecaps registered it as a dull, expanding pain that I have learned to tolerate here, the way I have learned to tolerate the light and the breathing and the strangeness. I looked into the pool.

The alga was doing something I had not seen before.

I need to describe this carefully, because I do not understand it, and I do not want to make it into more than it was, but I also do not want to make it into less.

The filaments, which normally lie flat across the pool floor in a loose, undifferentiated web, had organized themselves. They were *moving*. Not the random drift of filamentous algae in current — there is no current here, the water is still — but a directed, coordinated motion, the filaments contracting and extending in a pattern that was not random, that had rhythm, that had *structure*. They were forming something. A shape. A configuration that I watched for the duration of my knees' tolerance — perhaps four minutes, perhaps five — and that I can only describe as a map.

Not a map of the plateau. Not a map I recognized. A map of *underneath*. The filaments were tracing the subsurface structure of the water — the pressure system, the source, the thing that drives the water upward through the stone. They were showing me where the water comes from.

I do not know how I knew this. I knew it the way I know where a spring emerges before I dig — by the vegetation, by the soil color, by the way the ground sounds under my boots. I knew it by the pattern. The filaments were tracing flow lines, pressure gradients, the deep geometry of water in stone, and they were doing it in a medium — the algal web of a shallow pool at four thousand meters — that should not be able to represent that information, that has no mechanism to represent that information, that is, as far as I know, a filamentous alga doing what filamentous algae do, which is photosynthesize and grow.

But it was also doing *this*.

And then my knees gave out and I stood and the salamander surfaced at the far edge of the pool and looked at me again — briefly, a glance, a confirmation — and slipped away, and the filaments relaxed into their loose, undifferentiated web, and the oxygen bubbles resumed their slow, silent, ordinary procession to the surface, and the pool was just a pool again.

I stood there for a long time. The engines breathed. The birds churred in the distance. The cold worked its way back into my knees.

I unpacked my bag. I went back to my camp.

I stayed.

---

IX.

I should tell you about the birds, because the birds are what I notice first each day and what I miss most when I sleep and what I have come, reluctantly, to think of as the closest thing this place has to a face.

They are not in any catalogue. I have checked. I have photographs — not good ones, the light is too flat for my phone's camera, but good enough — and I have sent nothing to anyone, because there is no signal here and because I do not want to send them, because sending them would make the birds into specimens, and I do not want them to be specimens. I want them to be what they are, which is the plateau's mouths and ears and tongues and fingertips, the thin, quick, distributed sensory surface of something that is using them to taste the air by tasting the stone.

I cannot prove this. I am aware of how it sounds. I am a hydrologist. I am trained in the measurable and the verifiable and the publishable, and what I am writing here is none of those things. But I have watched the birds for eleven days, and I have watched them with the attention I was trained to give to water — patient, pattern-seeking, willing to sit with something for a long time before naming it — and the pattern I see is this:

The flocks move in coordinated response to atmospheric conditions that they should not be able to detect at the resolution they detect them. They track moisture gradients across the plateau surface with a precision that exceeds what I can measure with my instruments. They arrive at the edges of condensation zones before the condensation is visible to me. They leave areas before the moisture drops off. They are mapping the air in real time, with a resolution and a sensitivity that my training tells me should be impossible for an animal of their size, with sensory organs of their type, in conditions of this complexity.

And when they roost on the engines — when they press their pale bellies to the warm steel and go still and quiet and dim their metabolisms to the edge of torpor — they are returning what they have gathered. I do not know this. I cannot know this. But I have stood close to a roosting flock at the deep point of the plateau's twilight — the point where the light is at its dimmest, though the dimmest is not much dimmer than the brightest, which is part of what makes the light so difficult, the way it refuses to give you a marker, a way to know where you are in the day — and I have felt, in the silence, in the complete, detailed, textured silence of forty small bodies pressed to breathing steel, that I was watching something *upload*. Something being transferred. The day's data, the moisture map, the atmospheric reading, being passed from the birds' bodies into the engines' bodies through the warmth of contact, the way you pass heat from your hand to a cup, the way you pass information from your mouth to an ear.

The engines shudder when the birds roost. Not loudly. Not visibly. You have to be touching the engine — I was, my palm flat on the riveted steel, feeling the rivets' odd organic irregularity under my skin — to feel it. A faint tremor. A vibration that is not mechanical, that does not correspond to any point in the compression cycle, that happens only when the birds are roosting, only when the contact is made, only when the transfer is occurring.

The engines are receiving data. Through the birds. Through the warmth of small bodies on warm steel.

I am a hydrologist and I am writing this in a journal that no one will read, because there is no signal and because I do not think I want anyone to read it, because what I am writing is not science and I know it is not science, and the part of me that is a scientist — that has been a scientist for seventeen years, that has published in journals and reviewed for journals and built a career on the careful, measurable, verifiable — that part of me is watching me write this and is appalled.

And the rest of me is writing it anyway. Because it is what I see. Because it is what the plateau shows me when I sit still long enough to be shown.

---

X.

Today, the twelfth day — or what I am calling the twelfth day, having lost my clock and my light and my certainty about the passage of time — I went to the largest pioneer mat. The one at the base of engine four. It is perhaps two meters across, the largest I have seen, a dark, wet, almost-black circle of something that is between moss and lichen and is neither and is something else, something that arrived here on its own and is dissolving the stone beneath it one molecule at a time and is, I am increasingly certain, the *reason* the plateau exists.

Not the reason in the sense of *cause*. The reason in the sense of *project*.

The mat is doing what pioneer mats do: it is breaking stone. It is secreting acid — the thin, acrid, vinegar-and-mineral scent I have come to associate with the plateau's smell, the smell of a world being made — and the acid is dissolving the minerals in the raw granite, releasing them from their geological bonds and making them available for the first time as *not-stone*, as the chemical components of something that is not yet soil but will be, in time, in the time this place is apparently willing to take.

But the mat is not doing this randomly. It is not spreading in a circle, as I first described it. It is spreading in a *pattern*. A dendritic, branching pattern, like a river system viewed from altitude, like a lung's bronchial tree, like the veins in a leaf. It is building *channels*. It is organizing the dissolution of the stone into a network, a system of pathways that connect the mat's center to its edges and that, when the condensate runs across them, carry water and dissolved mineral in a directed flow that is not random, that has structure, that has *purpose*.

The isopods are part of this. They sit at the edges of the mat, at the frontier, where the filaments reach across bare stone and the acid is freshest and the dissolution is most active, and they do not graze — I was wrong, the accounts that call them grazers are wrong, or I was wrong when I agreed with them — they *process*. They take the dissolved mineral, the acid-digested stone, into their guts, and their guts — which I have examined, under the hand lens I carry, in two specimens I found dead at the edge of the mat, which I feel bad about, which I will not do again — their guts are not the guts of a detritivore. They are the guts of a *refinery*. They have internal structures — dark, dense, metallic-sheened — that take the dissolved mineral and do something to it. Concentrate it. Crystallize it. *Transform* it into something I cannot identify, something that is not stone and not soil and not any mineral I recognize, something that is perhaps the same substance as the gaskets, the warm black compound between the engine panels, the compound that tingled on my finger and evaporated and left me wondering.

The isopods are making the engines. They are making the *material* of the engines. They are eating stone and producing the compound that holds the engines together, and the compound is being transported — by the salamanders? by the water? by some mechanism I have not identified? — to the engines, where it maintains the gaskets, where it repairs the seams, where it *grows* the engines the way a body grows bone.

I cannot prove this. I have a hand lens and a notebook and a dead isopod and a feeling that started in my chest on the first day and has not left, the feeling that the breathing I hear is not the breathing of machines but the breathing of something that *has* machines, the way I have a liver and the birds have wings and the water has channels in the stone.

---

XI.

Today I found the fifth engine.

Not the fifth of the eleven I counted from the southern edge. A twelfth. It was not there before. I am as certain of this as I am of anything — I have walked this section of the plateau nine times in eleven days, I have paced the distances between the engines, I have triangulated their positions relative to each other and to the escarpment, and there were eleven, and now there are twelve.

The new engine stands in a shallow basin that was, the last time I walked here, a pool. The largest pool in this section, one I had noted in my journal as "pool C-4, approximately 3m diameter, persistent, alga-colonized, salamander observed on day 4." The pool is gone. The engine stands where the pool was. It is smaller than the others — perhaps six meters, not ten — and its steel is brighter, newer, with less of the dark organic sheen, and its rivets are less regular, less settled, as though they are still hardening, still finding their final shape.

It is breathing. The cycle is faster than the others — perhaps seventy seconds, not ninety — and slightly irregular, the way a newborn's breathing is irregular, the way any system that has just come online has a slight tremor before it settles into its rhythm.

I put my hand on it. The steel was warm. Not engine-warm. *Body*-warm. The warmth of something that is still being made, that is still cooling from the heat of its own construction, that is still in the process of becoming.

And at its base, in the shallow water that was already pooling around its footings, the pioneer mat was already growing. A dark circle, five centimeters across, at the exact point where the engine's weeping condensate met the raw stone. Already dissolving. Already working. Already beginning the project of building the ground that will, in a thousand years or ten thousand, be the substrate on which something larger will grow.

I stood there with my hand on the warm steel and the breathing in my chest and the twelfth engine breathing next to me, slightly out of sync, the way a new instrument joins an orchestra — uncertainly, tentatively, finding its place in the polyrhythm — and I felt the plateau grow.

It grew under my hand.

---

XII.

I am not leaving.

I want to be clear about this, because I want someone, someday, to understand what I am choosing and why.

I am not staying because I have been seduced or colonized or converted. I am not staying because the plateau has made me part of itself, though I think it is doing that, slowly, through the breathing and the rhythm and the cold and the water that I drink and the air I exhale and the warmth my body provides when I sleep near the engines and the birds roost near me and the isopods crawl across my boots and the salamander watches me from the pool's edge with its flat, dark, evaluative eyes.

I am staying because I am a hydrologist, and I have spent my whole life studying the moment water meets stone for the first time, and I am standing on the most significant site of that meeting I have ever encountered or will ever encounter, and the water is doing something I do not understand and the stone is becoming something I cannot name and the system that includes them both is *alive* in a way that I do not have the language for but that I know in my body the way I know the breathing in my sternum, and I cannot leave. I cannot leave the way I cannot leave a stream gauge during a flood, the way I cannot leave a spring that has just emerged from a glacier that has just retreated from a valley that has just been exposed to air for the first time in ten thousand years. I am a scientist and I am staying because there is science to do here that no one has ever done, that no one may ever have the chance to do again, that involves sitting on wet stone in the cold and the dim light and watching, patiently, for years if necessary, the way the pioneer mats dissolve and the isopods process and the engines grow and the birds map and the salamanders watch and the water cycles and the whole vast, slow, patient, breathing system builds itself a future.

I am staying because I am the first external instrument this system has ever had, and I think — I *think*, I cannot know, I can only feel it the way I feel the breathing — I think the plateau is using me. The way it uses the birds. The way it uses the isopods. The way it uses the water and the stone and the engines and the salamanders. It is using me to observe itself. To measure itself. To *know* itself, through the only mechanism it has ever produced that is capable of the kind of knowing that involves writing things down and making patterns and asking questions and being wrong and asking again.

I am the plateau's self-awareness. Not its mind — I do not think it has a mind, not in the way that word usually means. Its *self-awareness*. The part that looks at itself and says: *what am I?*

I am what the plateau is asking itself, and I am staying because I want to know the answer, and the only way to know is to stay.

---

XIII.

The birds are roosting. Engine three is breathing. The salamander — the large one, the one that led me to the pool, the one I am starting to think of as *the* salamander, the way you think of a particular river as *the* river, the one that defines the system — is at the edge of my camp, in the shallow water of the rill that runs past engine three's base. It is watching me write this.

I am going to put the journal down now. I am going to put my hand on the ground, on the wet, saturated, raw stone that is dissolving under the pioneer mats and being processed by the isopods and being breathed on by the engines and being watched by the salamanders and being mapped by the birds and being walked on by me, and I am going to close my eyes, and I am going to breathe with the plateau, and I am going to let it use me, and I am going to see what it sees when it sees itself.

The clouds do not move.
The light does not change.
The engines breathe.

I breathe with them.
