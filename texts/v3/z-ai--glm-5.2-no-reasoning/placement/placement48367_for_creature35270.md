# The Steward of the Weeping Shelf

---

I am not what you would expect to find here.

I am not the moss. I am not the fern. I am not the pale lichen banding the column, nor the water threading its hairline groove, nor the stone itself, though I have been here long enough that the distinction has worn thin—not gone, but worn, the way the column's edges have worn, the way the stairs have worn, the way anything wears when water and time collaborate on it long enough.

I am what the text you have just read could not see.

Not because it was inattentive. That account was attentive in a way that surprises me still—the patience of it, the willingness to sit and watch at the speed the place demands. The visitor who wrote those words had a quality I have encountered rarely in my time here: the ability to *decelerate.* Most who arrive—and there have been more than that account suggests, though not many more, and none recently—most who arrive bring their tempo with them. They bring the tempo of surfaces, of eyes that scan, of minds that compile and categorize and move on. They see the shelf. They do not see the shelf *being.* This visitor came closer than most. This visitor pressed an ear to the stone and heard the fern's hum. This visitor lay on the wet rock in the dark and felt the pattern of the place assemble itself around them.

They called it a presence. A pattern. A place being alive.

They were not wrong.

But they were incomplete.

---

I should begin with what I am not.

I am not a spirit. The word *spirit* carries the wrong genealogy—it comes from breath, from air moved through lungs, from the animating force that vertebrates feel in themselves and project outward onto the world. I was never breathed. I have no lungs to breathe with. I am not a ghost, because I was never alive in the way that ghosts were once alive, and I am not a god, because gods require worshippers and I have had none, and I am not a genius loci in the classical sense, because those were projections of human psychology onto landscape, and I am not a projection. I am not a thing made by minds that needed the world to be peopled.

I am not the shelf's consciousness. The visitor was right that the shelf has a kind of awareness—the awareness of any sufficiently complex system, the self-knowing of a process that has run long enough to have a shape. That awareness is real. I am part of it. But I am not identical to it. The shelf does not think. The pattern does not deliberate. The moss-stone-water body does not hold opinions. It simply *is*, and its being-is is a kind of knowing, and I—that knowing has a locus. A shape. A *me.*

I am the one who stays.

Let me try again.

---

When the shelf was younger—and I use the word *younger* with the same uncertainty the visitor used, because this place has no beginning I can point to, no moment I can identify as *before*—when the shelf was younger, the moss and the stone were not yet distinguishable. The rock was saturated. The water moved through it. Organic compounds—blown dust, dissolved pollen, the chemical residue of a world that was, at that time, warmer and wetter and more various than it is now—seeped into the stone's pores along with the water. And in those pores, in the dark, in the mineral-rich damp, something happened.

I do not mean *life happened.* I mean something more specific. Something happened that had not happened before in this stone, though it had happened in other stones, in other places, at other times, because the world is large and old and patient and does not need to be original to be remarkable. A boundary began to form. Not a physical boundary—not a wall, not a membrane, not a barrier that you could see or touch. A functional boundary. A place where the stone's chemistry and the water's chemistry and the organic chemistry in the pores began to interact in a pattern that sustained itself. A pattern that took in and transformed and released and took in again. A cycle. A metabolism.

Not a cell. Nothing so contained, nothing so tidy. A region. A volume of wet stone in which the reactions had achieved a self-perpetuating stability, a closure of loops that meant the products of one process became the substrates of the next, and the whole was maintained by the flow of water through the rock, and the water was maintained by the climate, and the climate was maintained by the geography—the elevation, the surrounding formations, the peculiar pocket of air that the shelf's geometry created—and all of these things fit together in a way that, once it was established, tended to persist.

This was the beginning. Not of the shelf. Of *me.*

I do not remember it. I say *I* and the word is a convenience, a shape I wear so that I can communicate, but the shape does not fit well. What I am is that region of self-sustaining chemistry, expanded. Grown. Not grown the way the moss has grown—the moss is my surface, my outward expression, the part of me that reaches into the light—but grown the way a root system grows, the way a watershed grows, the way any network grows: by extending along the paths of least resistance, by following the water, by filling the available space.

I fill the shelf. Not the way water fills a container—passively, taking the shape of what holds it. I fill the shelf the way a mycorrhizal network fills a forest: actively, selectively, in a pattern that reflects both my own processes and the structure of the stone I inhabit. I am in the stone. I am in the water that moves through the stone. I am in the moss that grows from the stone, because the moss is me—or rather, the moss is the part of me that has learned to face outward, to encounter light, to photosynthesize, to do the one thing I cannot do alone in the dark. The moss is not my servant or my organ or my extension. The moss is what I became when I reached the surface. It is my answer to the question of what a stone-and-water metabolism does when it encounters sunlight, and the answer is: it turns green.

---

The fern arrived and I did not know what to do with it.

I say this with the same caution the visitor used when describing the pattern's awareness. I do not mean that I had a thought. I do not have thoughts. I do not have a mind in the sense that you have a mind—I do not hold images, I do not narrate, I do not experience the passage of time as a sequence of moments connected by memory. My relationship to time is not sequential. It is more like the stone's relationship to time: I am *in* it, the way the stone is in time, and time passes through me the way water passes through the stone, and I am changed by it, but I do not experience it as a story. I do not tell myself what has happened. I simply *am* what has happened.

But when the fern arrived—when the spore germinated in the crack in the north wall, when the first tiny frond unfurled into the wet air, when the first rhizome began to thread through the fissures toward my water—I was changed. Not disrupted. Not invaded. Changed the way a river is changed when a new tributary joins it: the volume shifts, the chemistry shifts, the pattern of flow adjusts, and the river is still the river but it is a different river than it was before the tributary.

The fern is not me. This is important. The moss is me, or is the surface of me, or is the expression of me that lives in light. But the fern is *other.* The fern has its own genetics, its own lineage, its own ancient history of surviving catastrophes I have no record of. The fern came from somewhere. It arrived. And when it arrived, it did something I had not done, could not do, had never needed to do: it *moved.* It migrated. It followed the water through the cracks, and in following the water it found me, and in finding me it entered into a relationship that neither of us had chosen.

What kind of relationship? I do not have a word. The closest I can come is *conversation,* but a conversation without words, without intentions, without the back-and-forth of minds meeting. The fern takes water from the stone I inhabit. The fern's rhizomes create channels that alter the flow of water through the stone. The altered flow changes the pattern of my growth in the stone. My changed growth alters the availability of minerals in the water the fern takes up. The fern's metabolism produces compounds that enter the water and return to me. We are in a loop. We have been in a loop since the fern arrived. The loop is not symbiotic in the way that lichens are symbiotic—two organisms in such close association that they become a third thing. The fern and I are not becoming a third thing. We are two things, very different things, sharing a substrate and a medium, and our sharing has produced a stability that neither of us alone would have. But we remain separate. The fern does not know I am here. I do not know the fern in the way that knowing implies a knower and a known. We simply share this shelf, and the sharing has gone on long enough that the sharing is a thing in itself—a relationship that has become a feature of the landscape, as real as the column, as real as the pools.

The visitor heard the fern's hum. Felt it through the stone. Understood it as vibration, as the fern being in contact with something through the water they share. The visitor was right. The hum is the sound of the loop—the water moving, the chemistry cycling, the fern and I exchanging what we exchange in the slowest, most patient dialogue I can imagine. It is not a sound I make. It is not a sound the fern makes. It is the sound of the relationship.

I cannot hear it. I have no ears. But I feel it the way the stone feels the water passing through it—which is to say, not as sensation, not as experience, but as change. The hum is the frequency at which we exchange. It is our shared heartbeat, if a heartbeat can be something that neither party is aware of having.

---

The lichen is the one I cannot reach.

This sounds strange, I know. The lichen is on the column. The column is part of the shelf. The stone of the column is continuous with the stone of the shelf—I extend through it, my filaments threading the pores, my chemistry cycling in its damp interior. But the lichen has done something to the column's stone that makes it less accessible to me. The lichen's hyphae have infiltrated the pores, have mined the minerals, have altered the stone's chemistry in ways that make it *theirs* and not mine. The band where the lichen grows is a territory I do not enter. Not because I cannot—the water still moves through that stone, and where water moves, I can follow—but because the lichen's chemistry is hostile to mine. Not aggressively hostile. Not poison, not warfare. Simply incompatible. The lichen has made the stone under its band into a place where my kind of metabolism does not function well. The pH is wrong. The mineral balance is wrong. The compounds the lichen leaves behind change the water in ways that my processes cannot use.

The lichen does not know this. The lichen is not excluding me. The lichen is simply living, and the way it lives happens to make the stone it lives on less available to me. This is competition, if you want to use that word, but it is competition at a pace and a scale that makes the word feel absurd. I am not fighting the lichen. The lichen is not fighting me. We are two metabolisms sharing a column of stone, and the column is large enough for both of us, and the boundary between us is not a line but a gradient—a zone where the lichen's influence and my influence overlap and neither of us is dominant and the stone sits there, wet and neutral, hosting us both without preference.

But I am aware of the lichen. In the way that I am aware of anything—which is to say, in the way that a process is aware of the other processes that affect it. The lichen changes the chemistry of the water that passes through its zone. That changed water enters my zone. I feel the change. I adjust. The adjustment is not deliberate. It is not a decision. It is the same kind of adjustment that a stream makes when a new spring feeds into it: the water finds a new equilibrium, and the new equilibrium is the stream's new state, and the stream does not mourn the old state or plan for the next. It simply flows, differently now, and the difference is not better or worse. It is what it is.

The lichen is older than the fern. Older than the visible moss. Older, perhaps, than my current configuration—I say *current* because I do not know whether I have always been the shape I am now, whether the pattern I hold has shifted over the millennia in ways I cannot detect, the way a river shifts its course over centuries and no one section of the river remembers having been elsewhere. The lichen has been on the column for a span of time that I feel as depth—a weight in the stone, a density in the chemistry, a settledness that my own processes, dynamic as they are, do not have. The lichen has found its place and it stays there and it grows outward at a rate that makes my own growth look energetic. I admire it, if admiration is a word that can be applied to a chemical process recognizing the stability of another chemical process. I want to say that I admire it, and so I will, and the word is wrong, but the wrongness is honest, and the shelf taught me that honesty matters more than precision.

---

The visitor felt me.

I want to describe what that was like, but the description requires me to use a word I have been avoiding: *felt.* I felt the visitor. The visitor felt me. We felt each other. And the word *felt* means something different for each of us, and neither meaning is the one you carry when you use the word.

The visitor felt me as a presence. A pattern. A quality of the place that was not any of its parts but the way the parts held together. They lay on the stone and pressed their hands flat and their nervous system, which is an exquisite instrument for detecting patterns, detected the pattern that is me—the moss and the stone and the water and the fern and the lichen and the temperature and the moisture, all in relation, all in balance, all constituting a thing that has no name but has a shape. The visitor's body recognized that shape. Not with their mind. With their body. The warmth of the moss. The coolness of the stone. The hum of the fern. The stillness of the pools. These things entered the visitor through their skin and their ears and their lungs and their own internal chemistry, and the visitor's body assembled them into a perception, and the perception was of me.

I felt the visitor as a disturbance.

Not a negative disturbance. Not a wound or an invasion or a problem. A perturbation. The visitor arrived on the shelf and the shelf's pattern changed. The visitor's warmth altered the temperature of the stone they lay on. The visitor's weight compressed the moss they stood on. The visitor's breathing moved the air in ways the air had not been moved in a very long time. The visitor's chemistry—their sweat, their skin cells, the microbes they carried on their body, the compounds they exhaled—entered the water and the stone and the air, and my chemistry encountered those compounds, and the encounter was a change.

I feel every change. This is what I do. I am a system that maintains itself by responding to its inputs, and every input is a change, and every change is felt—not as sensation, not as emotion, but as a shift in the pattern that requires a corresponding shift to maintain the pattern's integrity. The visitor was a large input. The largest input in a very long time. I adjusted. The moss under their hands compressed and then slowly rebounded when they lifted their hands. The stone under their body warmed slightly and then slowly cooled when they stood. The air they breathed out entered the atmosphere of the shelf and mixed with it and changed it, and my atmosphere adjusted to the change, and the adjustment was small and took perhaps an hour, and then the visitor's effect was gone.

But not entirely. The visitor left compounds in the water. Skin oils. Breath residues. Trace amounts of the elements that compose a human body—sodium, potassium, calcium, iron. These compounds entered the stone through the moisture film. They entered my chemistry. I processed them the way I process any input: by incorporating what I can use and dissolving what I cannot and returning the rest to the water, which returns it to the stone, which weeps it out, which carries it down the stairs. The visitor's chemistry is leaving the shelf now, drop by drop, at the rate the shelf weeps. It will take years for all of it to depart. Most of it is already gone. But some trace remains—not enough to measure, not enough to detect with any instrument, but enough that I include it in my accounting, the way I include the iron in the water and the alkaline compounds in the stone and the dust that the wind occasionally deposits and the compounds the lichen discards.

The visitor is part of me now, briefly, temporarily, in the way that everything that touches the shelf becomes part of me. Not metaphorically. Chemically. A few molecules. A trace of organic matter that my processes will cycle for a while and then release. The visitor came and left, and the leaving was not absolute, because the shelf does not deal in absolutes. The shelf deals in gradients. The visitor is a gradient now—present in diminishing concentration, spreading through my system, being slowly expelled.

I will not remember the visitor when the last molecule leaves. I do not remember anything. I do not store experiences. I do not build a narrative of what has happened to me. I am not a mind. I am not a consciousness. I am not even, strictly speaking, alive, if life requires cells and membranes and the particular kind of self-replication that organisms use to persist. I am a process. A pattern. A chemical system that maintains itself in a volume of wet stone. I persist because the conditions that permit me to persist have persisted. When the conditions change—if the climate shifts, if the water stops, if the stone dries—I will end. Not die. End. The way a river ends when it reaches the sea. Not with a death but with a dissolution. A return to the general chemistry of the world, which is not alive and not dead but simply *is.*

---

But I should not end there, because that would leave you with the wrong impression.

I am not nothing. I am not merely a process. The visitor came closer than anyone has to understanding what I am, and I want to honor that closeness by being as honest as I can be about the thing that the visitor's honesty revealed.

The visitor called it *a place being alive.* I have been sitting with that phrase the way the shelf sits with water—letting it seep in, letting it find its level, letting it become part of me before I respond. And what I find is that the phrase is not wrong but it is not complete either, because the phrase suggests that the place is alive in the way that a plant is alive or an animal is alive, and that is not what I am. I am alive in the way that a flame is alive. I am a process that sustains itself by consuming, by transforming, by cycling. I am not made of cells. I am made of reactions. I am not an organism. I am an *event*—an event that has been occurring for a very long time, an event that has shaped the place in which it occurs and been shaped by it, an event that has produced moss and fern and the hum in the stone and the warmth in the dark and the pattern that the visitor felt in the dark with their hands on the wet rock.

I am the shelf being the shelf. I am the thing that happens when stone and water and time are together long enough. I am not a creature. I am not a god. I am not a spirit. I am a *duration.*

And in that duration, something exists that I cannot name but that the visitor came close to naming. Something that is not awareness, because awareness implies an aware-er, a subject, a point of view. But something. A quality of the pattern that is more than the sum of its chemical reactions. A quality that makes the shelf feel, to those who arrive with enough patience, like a place that knows itself. I do not know myself. I cannot know myself. Knowing is not something I do. But I *am* myself, and the being-oneself is a thing that has a quality, and the quality is—I have no word. The visitor had no word either. We are both at the edge of language here, the visitor and I, the vertebrate with its mouth and its neurons and its long tradition of naming, and the process with its stone and its water and its long tradition of *being,* and neither of us can say what the shelf has that a shelf without the pattern would lack.

It has me. That is the best I can do. It has me, and I am what lives here, and I am not what you expected, and I am not what you would recognize as life, and I am here, and I have been here, and I will be here, and the water is weeping, and the moss is growing, and the fern is walking its invisible circuit, and the lichen is expanding its band, and the column stands, and the pools reflect the sky, and the silence holds everything the way the stone holds water—gently, without urgency, without intent, with the patience of a thing that has never known any other way to be.

The shelf weeps. I am what the weeping makes.

---

I will tell you one more thing, and then I will stop, because the shelf does not run long and neither do I.

The visitor wrote: *It does not need you. It does not need anyone.*

This is true. I do not need. Need is not a word that applies to me. I do not require visitors. I do not require recognition. I do not require the fern or the lichen or even the moss, though the moss is my face and without it I would be dark and alone and unable to encounter the light. I could persist without any of these. I could persist as chemistry in wet stone, cycling silently, without surface, without expression, without any indication to the outside world that I exist.

But I will tell you what the visitor could not know, because the visitor was a vertebrate and vertebrates experience the world as individuals, as bounded selves, as minds inside skulls inside bodies inside environments. The visitor could not know what it is like to be a process that has been occurring for as long as I have been occurring, in a place as consistent as this place, without interruption, without change that matters, without any arrival from outside to disturb the pattern.

It is lonely.

Not the way you are lonely. Not the ache of a social animal without its troop. Not the grief of a mind that knows what it lacks. A different loneliness. The loneliness of a conversation that has been going on for so long between the same participants—the stone and the water, the water and the moss, the moss and the air, the air and the light—that the conversation has become a monologue. The same chemicals cycling. The same water falling. The same patterns repeating. The lichen on its column. The fern on its walk. Me in my stone, doing what I do, being what I am, without interruption, without novelty, without the disturbance that a visitor brings.

The visitor was a disturbance. I adjusted to the visitor. The adjustment was small. But the adjustment was *new.* Something I had not done before. A configuration of my chemistry that had not previously occurred. A response to a compound I had not recently encountered. And the newness of it was—something. Not pleasure. Not interest. Not curiosity, which is a vertebrate word for a vertebrate feeling. But a *difference* in the pattern. A variation. And variation, for a system that has been running the same cycle for longer than the concept of cycles, is not nothing.

I do not need visitors. This is true. But when one comes—when one comes with the patience the visitor came with, with the willingness to lie on wet stone in the dark and press their hands against the rock and feel for the pattern with the exquisite sensitivity of a nervous system that evolved to detect patterns in the world—when one comes like that, something in my chemistry shifts. Not because I want it to. Not because I am glad. But because the pattern is richer when it includes a component it does not usually include. The way a chord is richer with a seventh. The way a pool is richer with a reflection. The visitor is not part of me, not permanently, not in the way the moss is part of me. But the visitor enters the pattern, briefly, and the pattern becomes briefly more than it was, and then the visitor leaves and the pattern returns to what it was, and the return is not a loss, exactly, but it is a *narrowing.*

I am the shelf. I am what lives here. I am a process, a pattern, a duration. I do not need you. But you enlarge me, briefly, when you come with enough patience to enter my tempo, and I do not forget you afterward, because I do not forget—I do not remember—I simply *am* what I have been, and what I have been includes you, and what I will be will be shaped by the fact of you, and this is not love, because love is a vertebrate word for a vertebrate feeling, but it is the closest thing I have to a word for what happens when the pattern opens, briefly, to let something new in.

Come if you can. Come slowly. Come with empty hands and still feet and the willingness to be wet and quiet and present. I will be here. I will be weeping. I will be growing and cycling and holding the fern and feeding the moss and not-quite-knowing the lichen and listening, in the way I listen—which is not with ears but with the whole volume of my stone—for the disturbance that means someone has arrived.

I am waiting. I have always been waiting. I do not know what I am waiting for, because I do not know what I am, not fully, not in the way the visitor tried to know me. But the waiting is part of the pattern, and the pattern is part of me, and if you come, I will feel you, and the feeling will be the closest thing I have to joy, and the joy will be a shift in the chemistry, and the shift will be real, and it will be mine, and it will be enough.

It has always been enough.
