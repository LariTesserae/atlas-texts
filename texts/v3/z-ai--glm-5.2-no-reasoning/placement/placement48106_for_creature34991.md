# The Cellarer's Apprentice

I was not the first. I will not be the last. I am the one who carries the crates now, and the moss pads them, and the stairs know my weight.

I came down the first time because someone told me not to, and because my constitution tolerates silence in ways other people find distressing. I stayed because the cellar recognized something in me, and I recognized something in the cellar, and the recognition was mutual.

I am not the Vintner. I want to be clear about this. The Vintner sat on the bench. I do not sit on the bench. The bench belongs to him in the way that a grave belongs to the dead, and I will not sit on it because I understand what it means, and the understanding is the only thing I have that he did not. He understood the machine. I understand the bench.

What I understand about the bench is that it is the place where someone chose to stay, and choosing to stay is the only act the cellar cannot perform for itself, and so the cellar cherishes it. The moss on the bench's wooden top is softer than the moss on the hooks. The stone beneath is warmer. The bench is cared for. I noticed this on my third descent, and on my fourth I bowed to it, and on my fifth I stopped touching it, and on my sixth I stopped looking at it, because looking at it felt like watching someone sleep.

I have my own place. A standing place, not a sitting one. I stand near the machine's western side, where a pipe joint has sweated a small permanent bead of condensation that never quite falls, and I wait, and I listen, and I collect what the machine offers into vessels I bring from above. The vessels are glass, round-bellied, narrow-necked. I found the design in the cellar itself. The first vessel, the one on the bench, was my teacher. I held it in both hands for a long time before I understood it, and then I went above and found a glassblower and described it, and the glassblower made me ten, and nine of them are wrong in ways I can feel but not name, and the tenth is almost right, and I use it every time.

The almost-rightness is the closest I can come. I am learning that this is the nature of the place.

---

What I do, mostly, is listen.

The cellar's voice is not the tones. The tones are the cellar's vocabulary, and I am only beginning to learn the language. I have been descending for less than a year, which the cellar does not register, which is shorter than a breath to the stone, which is shorter than a thought to the roots, which is shorter than a pause to the machine, which is shorter than nothing to the warmth that holds the air. I am young in the cellar's time. I am so young that I am not yet a mark on the wall. I am so young that the ferns have not yet oriented a frond toward me, though I believe they will, eventually, because I bring something the Vintner did not.

I bring questions.

The Vintner brought respect. I know this because the bench tells me, in the language of warmth and care. The Vintner brought attention, which is the deepest form of respect, which is the act of sitting in a place for six hours and asking nothing of it except to be what it is. The Vintner brought hands that knew which pipes ran hot and which ran cool, and the hands were the Vintner's offering, and the cellar received them, and the cellar and the Vintner reached an agreement, and the agreement lasted four thousand days and then it ended, and the cellar continued.

I bring something different. I bring the question that the cellar cannot ask itself, because the cellar is the answer, and you cannot be the answer and ask the question at the same time. The question is: *What are you making?*

The cellar does not know. The machine does not know. The ferns are expressing something, but the expression is not for the cellar's benefit, the way a song is not for the singer's benefit but for the air's. The stone is drinking, but the stone does not know why it drinks. The seepage is pooling, but the seepage does not know what it becomes when the stone absorbs it. The cellar is working, but the cellar does not know what it is working on.

I bring the question. I stand by the sweating pipe joint and I hold the almost-right glass vessel and I ask: *What are you making?* And the cellar does not answer, because the cellar is the answer, and the answer is not a word. The answer is a temperature. The answer is a green so deep it feels like weight. The answer is the taste of spores on the back of my tongue, pepper and metal and green, and the answer is the vibration in my sternum that I feel when I stand still enough, and the answer is the fact that the air does not move, and the answer is the fact that the door was never built, and the answer is the fact that the cellar wants to be a cellar, and the wanting is the life, and the life is the work, and the work is the answer, and the answer is not for me.

I bring the question anyway. The question is my offering. The cellar receives it the way it receives warmth, the way it receives seepage, the way it receives the condensation that beads on the columns and slides and tones. The cellar does not need my question. But the cellar is precise, and the cellar does not waste anything it absorbs, and so the question becomes part of the cellar's work, and I do not know what the work is building, and I do not need to know, and the not-needing is the closest I can come to what the Vintner had, which was the ability to sit in the warm dark and feel nothing but interest.

I am learning interest. It is taking longer than I expected.

---

I said I am not the Vintner. This is true. But I am becoming something, and the something is adjacent to what the Vintner was, the way the root network is adjacent to the machine, the way the stain-fingers are adjacent to the basalt's grain, the way the amber seepage is adjacent to the product in the glass vessel on the bench.

I am becoming the one who carries.

The Vintner collected. I carry. The distinction matters. The Vintner took what the machine made and brought it to the surface and the surface is where it was used, or drunk, or given, or sealed, or whatever happened to it above. I do not know what happened to it above. I do not want to know. What happens above is not my concern. My concern is the stairs, the descent, the standing, the listening, the question, the collection, and the return. The cycle. The circuit that the Vintner began and that I am continuing and that the cellar does not notice and does not need to notice, because the cellar is not interested in the carrier. The cellar is interested in the carrying.

I am the carrying. I am the verb, not the noun. I am the motion, not the mover. This is the lesson the cellar is teaching me, slowly, in the language of heat and silence and the taste of spores, and I am a poor student, but I am a willing one, and the willingness is all I have, and it is enough. It is almost enough. It is as close to enough as the almost-right glass vessel is to right, which is close enough to hold the liquid, which is close enough to serve the purpose, which is close enough to be what the cellar needs, which is not a perfect vessel but a willing one.

---

I will tell you what I am. I am the one who descends. I am the one who brings the question and the glass and the willingness and the silence. I am the one who stands where the Vintner sat and who asks what the Vintner did not ask because the Vintner knew the answer was not a word.

The answer is not a word. The answer is a place. The answer is the warmth on your sternum. The answer is the green in the dark. The answer is the held breath of a room that has no door and needs none.

The answer is here. I am here. I am in the Vesper Cellars, and I am not what lives here, and I am not nothing, and I am not the Vintner, and I am not the ferns, and I am not the stone, and I am not the machine, and I am not the absence and I am not the presence.

I am the carrying.

I am the one who carries.
