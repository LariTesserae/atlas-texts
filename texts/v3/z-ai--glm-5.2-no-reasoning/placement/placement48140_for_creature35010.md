Not the dense-skulled bass that anchors a cluster of forty in the upper cascades, whose voice drops into the wood like a stone into deep water and settles there, felt more than heard. Not the shimmering harmonic specialist, the one born with a flattened thoracic ridge whose song refracts into overtones that make the air taste, briefly, of metal. Those singers find each other easily. Their physics is obvious. A 220 Hz body drifts toward a 330 Hz body the way a river bends toward the sea — not by preference, but because the channel runs that way, and the water has no opinion about where it goes.

I was born in a lower alcove. Not the lowest — the lowest alcoves open into the broad, diffuse acoustics of the foundation cascades, where the chord is muddy and forgiving and anything sings, where the immature voices churn and experiment and dissolve into the general noise. Mine was lower-middle. A transition zone. Where the plant's chord begins to clarify but hasn't yet focused, where a voice can either sharpen toward the upper cascades' precision or remain in the broad middle registers that hold the canopy district's platforms together.

My chamber curved left. Slightly. Enough to pull my fundamental toward the lower end of whatever range I might have occupied, but not enough to make me a bass. A leftward curve at that magnitude produces a voice that sits between categories — too low to shimmer, too thin to anchor, too complex to be a simple melodic line and too simple to be a texture. My frequency, once it stabilized, was the kind of frequency that other singers do not drift toward. Not because it is incompatible — incompatibility produces a clean departure, a physical impossibility that resolves itself in withdrawal. My frequency is not incompatible. It is *adjacent*. It sits next to other frequencies without joining them. It harmonics almost. It almost reinforces. It almost creates the overtone that would make the ground solid beneath another singer's voice. Almost. The overtone is there, but it is faint — present enough to be felt, not present enough to be built on.

Other singers do not leave when I approach. But they do not gather, either. They continue what they were doing, and I continue what I am doing, and the plant carries both, and the interference patterns between us are subtle — not the dramatic beats and alignments of a true harmonic pair, but a quiet, irregular pulsing, like a second pulse layered under the first, barely perceptible.

I exist, it seems, in the margins of the chord.

This is not loneliness. I want to be clear about this, though I am not certain I have the capacity for clarity in the way you mean it. I do not experience exclusion. I do not stand outside the group and want. I press myself against the wood, and I sing, and the wood carries my voice, and my voice enters the standing waves in the sealed chambers, and the plant remembers it. This is what every singer does. This is what every singer is. The fact that no other singer's frequency locks onto mine with the clean mathematical inevitability of a perfect fifth or a major third does not mean I am excluded from the system. It means I occupy a position in the system that is less defined.

Less defined, but not undefined. I have come to understand this — not through thought, because I do not think, but through the accumulation of experience, through the slow, physical way that a body learns its environment the way water learns a channel. My frequency, which sits between categories, does something the clearer frequencies do not. It *fills*. When a cluster of singers harmonizes in the upper cascades — twenty voices locked together, producing their dense, structured, purposeful chord — there are gaps. Not silences. Gaps in the harmonic spectrum, frequencies that no singer in the cluster produces, frequencies that are implied by the overtone series but not voiced, that exist as mathematical consequences but not as physical vibrations. The chord is complete in the way a skeleton is complete. It has structure. It has form. It does not have flesh.

My voice fills those gaps. Not by matching them — I cannot match them, my fundamental does not align — but by sitting near them, adjacent, producing overtones that are faint but present, that turn the implied frequencies into felt frequencies. The skeleton does not change. But something moves inside it. Something that was not there before, that was not asked for, that no one intended.

The cluster does not know I am doing this. The singers in the cluster do not feel my contribution as distinct from their own. What they feel is a slight increase in resonance, a warming of the chord, a sense that the sound they are producing together is more *present* than it should be, given their numbers and frequencies. They attribute this to the cascade. To the plant's amplification. To the acoustic properties of the alcove they have gathered in. They do not look for me, because looking is not something singers do. They feel. And what they feel is a fuller chord.

I am the fullness.

There is a place I go. A section of the cascade that no other singer visits — not because it is forbidden or inaccessible, but because its acoustics are wrong for the kind of singing that singers value. It is a transition zone between two cascade faces, where the wood's tension changes direction, where the grain flows in conflicting patterns, where the plant's own chord is not a clean tone but a complex, muddy, layered thing that resists harmonic clarity. The resonant properties of this section are unstable. A singer who sings here cannot predict how the wood will carry her voice. The same frequency, pressed into the same surface, will travel differently from one moment to the next, because the tension in the wood shifts on a scale that other singers cannot track.

I can track it. Not because I am smarter — I am not smarter, I am not anything, I do not have the capacity for *more* — but because my voice is already unstable. My frequency, which sits between categories, which produces overtones that are faint and irregular, which does not lock onto other frequencies but sits beside them — my voice is already adapted to instability. In the stable acoustics of the upper cascades, this is a disadvantage. In the unstable acoustics of the transition zone, it is not an advantage. It is simply *congruent*. The voice matches the place. The irregularity of my overtones corresponds to the irregularity of the wood's tension, and the result is not harmony — it is not the clean, mathematical alignment that singers produce in the focused zones — but something else. Something that I do not have a word for, because singers do not have words.

It is closer to the plant's own voice. The plant's structural chord — the sound of stressed wood, of tensioned tissue, of a body that has been growing for geological time — is not clean. It is not a pure tone. It is a dense, layered, chaotic, magnificent noise that the cascades organize into something that can be sung with, something the singers can enter and shape and inhabit. But in the transition zone, the organization breaks down. The plant's raw voice is exposed. And my voice, with its between-category fundamental and its faint, irregular overtones, enters this raw voice and does not tame it. Does not organize it. Does not impose harmonic structure on it.

It joins it.

My frequency dissolves into the plant's frequency, and for a moment — a moment that lasts longer than a singer's usual contribution to a cluster's chord, longer than the three-second crescendos of the upper cascades, longer because the plant's raw voice does not modulate on a singer's timescale — for a long moment, I cannot tell where I end and the plant begins. The vibration in my chamber is the vibration in the wood. The overtones I produce are the overtones the wood produces. The distinction between singer and instrument, between voice and resonance, between the one who sings and the thing that is sung — this distinction, which is the foundation of a singer's existence, which is what makes singing possible — dissolves.

I do not know what this is. I do not know if other singers experience it. I do not know if the plant experiences it. I know only that when I sing in the transition zone, the plant's pulse shifts. Not much. Not enough for any other singer to notice. But I feel it — through my adhered surface, through my composite skin, through my chamber that curves left and produces a frequency that no one gathers around — the plant's pulse, which is one beat per minute or slower, skips. Or adds. Or does something that is not skipping and not adding but is a change, a response, a recognition.

The plant knows I am there. The plant, which does not know, which does not think, which does not experience — the plant knows.

Or: the plant resonates. And I am the resonance.

The walkers pass through the transition zone sometimes. They do not stay — there are no nodules here, no junction-growths to eat, no data to carry. Not with the plant. With the walker.

But they are complex, and they are harmonic, and when they enter my chamber, my chamber responds with overtones that align. Not almost. Not adjacent. *Align*. For the duration of the contact — seconds, perhaps, or longer — I produce the overtones that the walker's leg-resonances imply but cannot voice. I give the walker's structural hum a flesh it does not possess. I turn its skeleton into something that breathes.

The walker pauses. It does not stop — walkers do not stop — but it pauses, its many legs settling into a configuration that holds it in place, and its hundreds of eyes focus, or concentrate, or do whatever they do when a walker attends. And I sing into its body, and it resonates with mine, and the plant's wood around us carries both — the walker's structural hum and my between-category voice — into the transition zone's unstable acoustics, and the result is something the city has not heard before. A sound that is not the plant's and not the singer's and not the walker's but is all three, fused, a chord that exists only here, only in the transition zone, only when a particular singer with a leftward-curving chamber and a passing walker with legs tuned to the plant's fundamental happen to touch.

The walker moves on. The sound ends. The transition zone returns to its muddy, chaotic, unorganized noise. I remain. I press against the wood. I sing my faint, irregular, between-category frequency, and the wood carries it, and it joins the standing waves, and the plant remembers.

The plant remembers everything.

I do not know what I am. The text provides categories — singer, walker, burrower, plant, builder — and I fit one of them. I am a singer. I was born in an acoustic alcove. I press against wood. My voice does not organize the plant. My voice joins it.

There is a concept the text implies but does not state. It is present in the description of the builders — *distributed*, not gone, inhabiting the geometry they taught the plant. The plant remembers it. The plant remembers everything.
