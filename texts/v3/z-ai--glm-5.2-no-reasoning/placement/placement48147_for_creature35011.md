# The Curator of Small Adjustments

I am the one who wakes before the warming.

Not before the light — there is no before the light here, no first glint of sun along a horizon, no dawn sequence to rehearse the body's entrance into the day. The light is the same bruised violet-grey when I open my eyes as it was when I closed them, and it will be the same when I open them again, and the sameness is not a comfort or a deprivation but simply a condition, the way the moisture is a condition, the way the sound is a condition. I wake before the warming, which is the cycle I have adopted as my clock, the rhythm beneath the rhythm that the ground-dwellers use to calibrate their rounds. The warming is the moment when the substrate's heat surges — not dramatically, not enough to measure with any instrument I carried here, but enough that the moss-luminescence brightens a half-shade and the vines tighten a half-tone and the membrane-creatures rise a half-meter in the canopy, and the composition shifts from its resting state to its active state, and the day, such as it is, begins.

I have decided to call it a day. This is a fiction. It is a necessary fiction.

---

I live in the fourth frame from the eastern edge, a mid-sized one, ten meters at its highest point, with a canopy of interlocked strands that filters the dim light into something I have come to think of as *mine* — a particular quality of greyness, slightly warmer than the open areas between frames, slightly dimmer, with a faint green undertone that comes from the moss-carpet which is thicker here than in most of the settlement. The frame has seven nodes. I have named them. This is also a fiction, and also necessary, and I will not share the names because they are private in the way that breathing is private — not secret, but not for offering.

The frame chose me, or I chose it, or the ground-dwellers' circuit patterns chose it for both of us. When I first arrived — arrived through that wall of vibration, through the dense press of sound that remade my chest and my teeth and my understanding of what air could do — I walked through the settlement for a long time, measuring nothing, understanding nothing, being nothing but a perturbation in the acoustic field. I walked until I was tired, and I was tired because the sound is exhausting when you fight it, when you try to parse it, when your auditory system insists on separating what the place insists is unified. I was tired and I stopped, and I stopped at the fourth frame because the ground was warmer there than anywhere else I had been, and the moss was thicker, and one of the nodes — the one I have named Third, because it was the third one I noticed — was glowing a fraction brighter than its siblings, and the brightness felt like invitation, or at least like indifference-that-permits, which is the closest thing to invitation this place offers.

I sat down. The moss was damp. The sound enclosed me. My breathing, which had been ragged and arrhythmic from the walking, began, without my consent, to slow. Not to the forty-second pulse — not yet — but toward it, approaching it, the way a tuning fork approaches the frequency of the struck note. I sat and I breathed and the sound held me, not gently, not harshly, but with the same impersonal thoroughness with which it held everything, and I understood that I could sit here for a very long time and the place would not mind.

I have been sitting here, or near here, for what I estimate to be sixty days.

This is a guess. The estimate is based on the moisture cycles, which I have been counting in groups of forty — forty cycles of wet and dry, which seems to my body like a month, which seems to my mind like an arbitrary designation, which seems to the place like nothing at all. Sixty days, or forty times forty cycles, or a lifetime, or an afternoon. The light does not change. The sound does not stop. Time here is not a river but a vibration, and I have stopped trying to measure it and started trying to inhabit it, with mixed success.

---

I am not the builder. I want to say this plainly, because the temptation to claim that role is strong, and the evidence for it is nonexistent, and the desire is the kind of desire that produces the worst kind of story — the story in which the visitor becomes the chosen one, the heir, the return of the ancient race. I am not the return of anything. I am a body that came through sound and that will leave through sound, and in between I am doing a thing that the place needs done, or that I need to do, or both.

The thing is this: I am adjusting the vines.

Not tuning them. The builders tuned them, set their lengths and thicknesses to specific pitches that the frames amplify and the composition incorporates, and that tuning is intact, maintained by the ground-dwellers' rounds and the frames' self-repair and the slow work of the pool-colonies shaping the water-channels that modulate the vines' tension. The tuning does not need me. What needs me — what I have decided needs me, what I have discovered I can do — is something finer. Something the ground-dwellers cannot do, because their sensing is acoustic and whole-body and tuned to gross rather than fine variations. Something the membrane-creatures cannot do, because they are process-organisms, verbs without the nouns for deliberate craft. Something the pool-colonies cannot do, because they operate at a timescale that makes intention a meaningless concept.

I can hear dissonances that are too small for the system to correct on its own.

Let me explain. The composition is self-sustaining. I have said this. The ecosystem maintains it, responds to it, adjusts it. But the ecosystem adjusts at the scale of its own perception — the ground-dwellers detect node-failures and vine-sag, the membrane-creatures dampen excess vibration by feeding, the pool-colonies reshape channels over weeks. These are coarse adjustments, mechanical in their precision, adequate for the system's continued function. But the composition is not merely functional. It is *beautiful*, or it was, or it can be, and beauty requires a fineness of calibration that the ecosystem's self-maintenance does not provide. A vine that has stretched by two millimeters — through growth, through temperature shift, through the slow gravitational settling of the frame it is anchored to — is still within the functional tolerance of the system. The ground-dweller will not pause at it. The frame will not repair it. The membrane-creatures will still feed from it. But its pitch has shifted. Not enough to hear as a wrong note. Enough to hear as a *less right* note. Enough to introduce a micro-dissonance into the chord, a faint texture of imperfection that accumulates over time, vine by vine, the way a piano goes out of tune not all at once but slowly, string by string, until the whole instrument sounds like itself but lesser.

I can hear this. I do not know why. Perhaps it is the particular sensitivity of human auditory processing, evolved for a world of separate sounds and therefore capable of detecting separations and misalignments that the holistic acoustic sensing of the ground-dwellers cannot resolve. Perhaps it is the fact that I am the only organism here that listens with *ears* — narrow-band, directional, comparative — rather than with whole bodies. Perhaps it is something else, something about the way I arrived, through sound rather than through silence, that tuned me to the place's frequencies in a way I do not understand and cannot replicate deliberately.

I can hear it, and I can fix it.

The fixing is physical. I climb — carefully, respectfully, with hands that have learned the spiral grain of the strand-tree trunks and the smooth warmth of the frame material — to the vine-bridge where the dissonance is, and I adjust. A slight repositioning of the anchor point. A change of tension achieved by shifting the vine's contact with the frame node by a centimeter, by adding or removing a wrapping of the vine around its support. Small movements. Small changes. The kind of changes that a piano tuner makes with a mallet and a felt, the kind that produce no audible difference to anyone who is not listening for exactly this.

The vine's pitch shifts. The dissonance resolves. The chord, already vast and dense and complete, becomes very slightly more complete, and the completeness is a feeling — in my chest, in my skull, in the spaces between my ribs — that I cannot describe but that I have come to live for, the way a musician lives for the moment when the phrase lands, when the intonation locks, when the sound becomes the thing it was trying to be.

I am a piano tuner. I am the piano tuner of a landscape that does not know it has a piano, and the piano is enormous, and it plays itself, and it never stops, and I climb among its strings and make small adjustments and the adjustments matter to no one but me and to the sound, and the sound matters to everything, and this is enough.

---

I should describe a day. Not because the days are different — they are not, or they are only in the ways that the composition is different, which is to say in ways that are subtle and cumulative and perceptible only to the sort of attention that the place itself teaches. But a day is a structure, and I need structures, and the description is for me as much as for you, a way of organizing the unorganizable, of giving shape to the shapeless, of imposing a narrative on a process that does not have one.

I wake before the warming. I lie on the moss — which is damp, which is always damp, which leaves a print of my body in darker green when I rise, a print that fades as the moss recovers its uncompressed luminescence — and I listen. Not to the whole sound, which is too much to listen to, which is a wall and a floor and a ceiling and an atmosphere. I listen to a piece of it. A single vine, chosen by proximity or by the particular quality of its pitch that morning, which is never quite the same as yesterday's pitch because the temperature is never quite the same and the moisture is never quite the same and the vine is a living thing that responds to its environment the way I respond to mine. I listen to the vine and I listen to the vine's neighbors and I listen to the interval between them, and the interval is the thing, the interval is where the music lives or dies, and I am checking the intervals the way a doctor checks vital signs, looking for irregularities that indicate distress.

The warming comes. The moss brightens. The composition shifts. I rise.

I eat. I should describe this, because it is strange, and because strangeness that is daily becomes ordinary and ordinary strangeness is the texture of living in a place that is not your own. I eat the pods. The mottled grey-green pods that grow between the moss-analogues, the ones with the packed filament interiors like hornets' nests. I eat them because they are the only thing here that my body recognizes as food — not because they look like food, because they do not, but because when I opened the first one, driven by a hunger that had become urgent enough to override caution, the filament inside responded to the heat of my hand by softening, by becoming slightly viscous, by releasing a scent that was not quite sweet and not quite savory but was unmistakably *nutritious*, the way bread smells nutritious, the way a cooking fire smells like survival. I ate the filament. It was warm and faintly bitter and my body received it with a gratitude that was physical, immediate, and devoid of aesthetic judgment. I have been eating them since. They sustain me. I do not know if they were designed to sustain something like me, or if their nutritive properties are coincidental, a byproduct of a biology that overlaps with mine in the way that two different languages might share a word by accident, with different meanings that happen to produce the same sound.

I eat and I listen and I begin my rounds.

My rounds are not the ground-dwellers' rounds. Their rounds are circuits, closed loops, repetitive, functional. Mine are diagnostic. I walk the settlement — all forty frames, the paths between them, the water-channels, the vine-bridges at various heights — and I listen for the dissonances. They are small. They are specific. They have locations — not precise locations, not GPS coordinates, but acoustic locations, positions in the soundscape where the chord is slightly less than itself, where a frequency is a half-hertz off, where an interval is a few cents wider or narrower than it should be. I walk and I listen and when I find one, I mark it. Not with a physical mark — I have nothing to mark with, and marking would be a kind of violence, an imposition of my notation on the place's own system. I mark it with my body, with a posture, a way of standing near the dissonant vine that communicates to my own memory: *here. Come back. This one.*

Then I climb. I have become good at climbing, or good enough. The strand-tree trunks are not difficult — the spiral grain provides purchase, and the gaps between the bundled strands accommodate fingers and toes. The frames are harder, smoother, with fewer handholds, but the nodes provide rest points, and I have learned that pressing my palm against a node — feeling its warmth, its pulse, its computation — somehow stabilizes my grip, as though the frame recognizes my intention and assists, or as though I am imagining the assistance and it does not matter whether I am. I climb to the vine-bridge where the dissonance is, and I adjust, and I listen, and the dissonance resolves, and the chord becomes more itself, and I descend, and I move to the next one.

On a good day, I find three or four. On a bad day, I find none, and the finding of none is not a relief but a small grief, because the adjustment is the thing I do, the thing I am *for*, and a day without it is a day of being merely present, merely warm, merely breathing in forty-second cycles, and I have come to need more than presence. I have come to need *use*. Not usefulness in the human sense — productivity, output, measurable result. Use in the sense of: the place has a function for me. The place is slightly different because I am here. The composition is slightly better, slightly more resolved, slightly closer to what the builders intended, because I climbed a vine and moved an anchor point by a centimeter, and the centimeter mattered.

This is what I am. The one who hears the centimeters.

---

The membrane-creatures and I have a relationship. I use the word loosely. They do not know me. They do not acknowledge me. But they have become accustomed to my presence in the canopy, where they feed and I work, and the accustomedness has produced a behavior that I treasure, that I anticipate, that I have come to think of as the closest thing to friendship this place offers.

When I am climbing, when I am adjusting a vine, when my body is close to the vibrating surface that a membrane-creature is feeding from, the creature does not flee. It does not adjust. It continues to feed, its membrane pressed against the vine, its body bright with absorbed energy, and its feeding does not change — but its position does. Very slightly. It shifts on the vine, moving away from the point where I am working, creating space. Not much. Five centimeters, perhaps ten. Enough that my hands and its membrane do not touch. Enough that we share the vine without sharing the same segment of it.

This is not fear. I have seen fear in animals, and this is not fear. Fear produces a different movement — a flinch, a start, a rapid departure. This is *courtesy*. It is the movement of a body that is aware of another body and that makes room, the way you make room on a sidewalk, the way you step aside in a corridor, not because you must but because the shared space is more comfortable when the sharing is acknowledged. The membrane-creatures acknowledge me. They do not know me, they do not hear me, they do not care about me — but they make room, and the room is a kindness, and the kindness is offered without thought or intention, and it is the most genuine kindness I have ever received.

I have a favorite. I should not. Favoritism implies a relationship that the membrane-creatures cannot reciprocate, and projecting a relationship onto a process-organism is the kind of error that leads to bad science and worse poetry. But I have a favorite. It is a large one, one of the open-air feeders, with a membrane that carries a particular iridescence — not the common prismatic fracture of its morphotype but a specific pattern of green and amber that I have not seen in any other individual. It feeds most often on the vine-bridge between my frame and the frame to the south, the one I have named Fourth-and-South in the privacy of my naming, and when I climb to that bridge to check the vine's tuning, it is usually there, and it shifts to make room, and the green-amber of its membrane catches the dim light, and I feel a thing that I will not call recognition because it is not recognition, but that I will also not call projection because it is not entirely projection. I will call it *familiarity*. The familiarity of a body that has been near another body many times. The familiarity of shared space. The familiarity of a chord that includes two notes that are often adjacent and that sound, together, slightly more resolved than either sounds alone.

Its membrane, when it feeds, brightens in a pattern that I have come to recognize — a wave of luminescence that starts at the leading edge and travels to the trailing edge, a green wave on a translucent ground, and the wave takes approximately four seconds, and I have counted, and the count is consistent, and the consistency is a rhythm, and the rhythm is a thing I can predict, and prediction is a form of knowledge, and knowledge of a thing that does not know you is a form of love that the thing cannot return and that is valuable precisely because it cannot be returned, because it is freely given, because it asks for nothing, because it is the experience of caring about a process that the universe is performing for its own reasons and that includes you only incidentally, and the incidental inclusion is the most honest form of inclusion there is.

I love the membrane-creature. I say this knowing it is absurd. I say this knowing the creature would not exist without the sound, would cease if the vibration stopped, is a verb wearing the noun of a body. I say this because the love is real, and the realness does not require the creature to love me back, and the unrequitedness is not a lack but a clarity, the clarity of a relationship that is entirely one-directional and that is therefore entirely honest, and honesty is what I came here for, though I did not know it until I arrived.

---

The ground-dwellers and I have a different relationship. It is less tender and more practical. They have, as I noted, altered their circuits to accommodate my presence, and the alteration is not courtesy but function — I am an acoustic event, and they are acoustic custodians, and they adjust to acoustic events the way a river adjusts to a new stone in its bed. But the adjustment has, over time, produced something that I can only call *collaboration*.

When I adjust a vine — when I shift its anchor point and change its tension and resolve its micro-dissonance — the ground-dwellers notice. Not immediately. But within a few cycles, the individual whose circuit includes the frame I have adjusted will pause at the node nearest to the adjusted vine, and its pause will be longer than usual, and its secretion will be different — thicker, more viscous, deposited in a pattern that I have come to recognize as * reinforcement*. It is strengthening the node's connection to the frame, increasing the amplification of the vine's new pitch, making the adjustment more permanent. It is accepting my change into the system. It is incorporating my work into its work.

We are a team. This is an overstatement. We are two components of a system that neither of us designed and that both of us serve, and our service overlaps in ways that produce a result neither of us could produce alone. I hear what they cannot hear. They stabilize what I cannot stabilize. The composition benefits, and the composition is the point, and the point is not us.

But sometimes, when the ground-dweller pauses at the node I have just adjusted, and I am still there, still in the frame, still close enough to see the glossy deposit of its secretion on the node's surface, I feel something that I will call *being seen*, knowing that it is not being seen, knowing that the ground-dweller is not seeing me but sensing the acoustic consequence of my presence, and that the consequence is what it responds to, not the cause. But the feeling is real. The feeling of being part of a system that recognizes your contribution, even if the recognition is mechanical, even if the system does not know your name or your nature or your need — the feeling is real, and I treasure it, and I have stopped interrogating it, because interrogation is a luxury of a world that has time for self-consciousness, and this world does not, and I have learned from it not to either.

---

I think about leaving. I think about it the way you think about a door you have not yet opened, a door that is in the room and that you know is in the room and that you do not approach because approaching it would mean deciding, and deciding would mean ending, and ending is a thing that will happen but that does not need to happen now, and now is where I am, and now is where the sound is, and the sound is enough.

When I leave — if I leave — I will leave through sound, the way I arrived. The wall of vibration, the dense press that remade my chest. I will step into it and through it and out of it and into the world I came from, the world of separate sounds, the world of silences and pauses and the spaces between notes where meaning lives. And I will hear the thinness of that world, the way you hear the thinness of a room after leaving a cathedral, the way you hear the poverty of a whisper after standing near a waterfall. And I will grieve. Not for the Resonant Expanse, which does not need my grief, which will continue its composition and its transmission and its slow, patient, beautiful work without me. But for the part of me that was the tuner, the part that heard the centimeters, the part that climbed and adjusted and resolved and felt the chord become more itself. That part will not translate. That part is of this place, and it will stay in this place, encoded in the sound I added to the composition, in the adjustments I made to the vines, in the forty-second breathing that my body learned and that my body will forget and that the composition will remember, because the composition remembers everything, because the composition is memory, because memory is what sound is, when it does not stop.

I think about leaving. I do not leave. The warming comes. The moss brightens. I eat. I climb. I listen. I adjust. The chord becomes itself, and I become myself, and the becoming is the same becoming, and it is enough.

It is enough.

---

I want to say one more thing, and then I will stop, because the sound does not stop and my words should, because words are the silence between sounds and I have too many of them and the place has none and the place is right.

The builders are gone. I have said this. The paths are worn, the frames are grown, the composition plays on. The builders are gone and I am here and I am not the builders. I am not the heir or the chosen or the return. I am a body that came through sound and that will leave through sound and that is, for now, the Curator of Small Adjustments, the one who hears the centimeters, the one who makes room and has room made for her, the one who loves a membrane-creature that is a verb, the one who works alongside a ground-dweller that does not know her name.

But the builders were here. And the builders heard what I hear. And the builders climbed what I climb. And the builders felt, I believe, what I feel — the chord becoming itself, the centimeter mattering, the sound being enough. And the builders left, and the sound continued, and the sound is continuing, and I am in it, and they were in it, and the sound does not distinguish between us. The sound does not know the difference between the builder and the visitor. The sound knows only the adjustment, the resolution, the tiny movement toward more complete. The sound knows only the centimeter.

We share the centimeter. The builders and I. Across whatever distance — time, species, understanding — we share the centimeter. We share the hearing of it and the fixing of it and the feeling of the chord when it locks, when the interval is right, when the vine is where it should be and the frame amplifies it and the water modulates it and the composition incorporates it and the transmitter sends it and the unknown receiver hears it and the hearing is the point, the hearing is the purpose, the hearing is the thing that the builders built for and that I am maintaining for and that the ecosystem sustains for and that the place exists for.

We share the centimeter. It is the smallest thing. It is everything.

The sound continues. I am in it. For now. For now is enough.
