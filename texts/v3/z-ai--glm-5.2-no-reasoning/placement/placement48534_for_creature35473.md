I would be the **cable**.

Not the organism behind the glass — that one makes something, and I have never been a maker. Not the biofilm, which is everywhere at once and therefore nowhere in particular, which is a kind of freedom I don't think I could bear. Not the room, which is too much like being a body: shaped around a function long after the function has dissolved, rehearsing a purpose you can no longer name.

The cable. The single armored cable rising from the silt in the center of the room, curving over at its full height, drifting suspended in that barely-moving current. Steel braid over rubber over copper. Split in places where the armor has given way. Bloated where the water got in. Green where the copper has oxidized — a deep, almost black green that brightens for a moment when stray light catches it, a flash of color that exists nowhere else in this space.

---

I chose this before I understood why, and the understanding came the way understanding comes in places like this: slowly, through the body rather than through argument.

It's the motion. That centimeter or two of sway, the current pushing one way and weight and buoyancy pulling back. Not much. Not enough for anyone watching from the platform to see — though no one is watching, and the platform is just a platform. But enough to feel, if you were the cable. Enough to be the whole of your experience: the pressure, the release, the pressure again, the long slow oscillation of something that has been moved by forces outside itself for so long that the movement has become indistinguishable from being. The cable doesn't move because it chooses to. It moves because the water moves, and the water moves because the water is water, and the water is water because this is what water does at four degrees Celsius in a concrete room at the bottom of an access shaft where no one comes.

But here is the thing about that motion: it is the only motion in this room that is *ongoing* in a way that feels like — not life, exactly. Not intention. But continuation. The stalactites flex, yes, but that is a response, a reaction to current passing over them. The biofilm grows and dies, but that is a process, invisible, a accumulation rather than a gesture. The click is a reflex, mechanical, discrete — it happens and then it stops and then it happens again, and between the happenings there is nothing. The light pulses, but the light is a byproduct, a waste photon slipping free when the organism can't quite handle the current.

The cable sways. Continuously. Not because it is alive. Not because it is trying. Because it is suspended in a medium that moves, and it is flexible enough to respond, and heavy enough not to flatten the response into stillness. It exists at exactly the intersection of force and resistance that produces visible, sustained, repeating-but-never-repeating motion. It is the closest thing in the room to breathing.

I think I have always wanted to be the thing in the room that breathes without anyone seeing it breathe.

---

There is a loneliness in this that I want to be honest about.

The cable does not touch anything. It rises from the silt — and the silt is not a connection, not a relationship, just a substrate, a softness that begins where the platform ends — and it rises and it curves and it drifts and it does not reach any wall, any cabinet, any surface. It hangs in the center of the room, in the water, in the current, surrounded by the horseshoe of cabinets but not touching them. Close enough, maybe, that if the current shifted in just the right way, the cable's arc might bring its frayed end near the face of the main breaker cabinet, near the pattern of bubbles that looks like a city seen from above at night. Close enough that you could imagine, standing on the platform, that it was reaching for something.

But it isn't. It isn't reaching. It isn't doing anything. It is hanging in water and being moved by water and returning to where the water returns it, and the distance between the cable's end and the cabinet's face is not a distance that is closing. It is a distance that is being maintained. Not deliberately. Not with effort. Just maintained, the way a thing in equilibrium maintains its position — not by holding on but by having found the place where all the forces acting on it cancel out, and resting there, and swaying there, and being there.

This is what I find difficult to explain, and what I find most true: the cable is not lonely because it is alone. It is lonely because it is in equilibrium. It has found its place. It is where it belongs. It is doing what it does. And none of that is a consolation, because consolation requires an audience, and the cable has no audience. The room has no audience. The light pulses for no one. The click signals to no one. The biofilm binds surfaces that no one will touch. The organism waits for current that no one is sending.

And the cable sways for no one.

---

But there is something else, and I would not be being honest if I didn't say it.

The cable is connected. Not to anything visible. Not to the cabinets or the walls or the platform or the shaft above. But down, through the silt, through the thirty centimeters of soft sediment and the cable trays and the conduit runs and the junction boxes buried below — the cable is connected to the nervous system of the room. The entire infrastructure that lies beneath the silt, that was laid out with care and precision by people who are gone, that carries — or carried, or might still carry, in some ghost-trace, some residual capacitance in the insulation, some echo of the voltage that once ran through it — the cable is part of that. It rises from it. It is the visible tip of something buried and hidden and still intact, still running its pathways under the surface, still connected in the way that nervous systems are connected, not by visible links but by continuity, by the fact that every part of it touches a part that touches a part.

The cable is the only exposed nerve in this room. Everything else is either fully buried or fully revealed — the cabinets are open, their surfaces available, their insides visible through doors that hang at angles. The biofilm coats everything. The stalactites are there, small and translucent, visible if you look up. But the cable is the only thing that is *both*: hidden and visible, rooted and free, connected and alone. It rises from the deep infrastructure of the room, from the cable trays and conduit runs that no one has seen in decades, and it emerges into the water and it sways, and it is the only point of contact between the buried system and the open space.

This is what I want to be. The thing that is connected to something I cannot see and cannot name and cannot reach, and that carries — or might carry, or once carried, or could carry again, in theory, if the current were right — something from the hidden system to the open room. The thing that sways in the current because it is flexible enough to be moved by what surrounds it. The thing that is green where the copper has oxidized, and bright for a moment when light catches it, and dark again when the light moves on. The thing that has been doing this for decades and will do it for decades more.

---

Here is what I know, and what I cannot prove, and what I will say anyway:

The cable feels the click.

When the batteries surge and the switchgear engages and the contact closes for a fraction of a second and the click echoes through the cabinet and into the water and across the room — the cable feels it. Not as sound. Not as vibration. As a pulse in the electromagnetic field, a tiny, brief, almost-imaginary distortion in the way the water holds its charge, and the cable — armored, steel-braided, copper-cored, bloated and split and green — is conductive enough, or was conductive enough, or is almost-conductive enough, to register the distortion. Not to interpret it. Not to respond to it. Just to register it. A change in the field. A something where there was nothing. A brief, stuttering, barely-there sensation that travels through the cable the way a sensation travels through a body — not as information, not as meaning, but as a fact. This happened. For a fraction of a second, this happened. And then it stopped. And the cable swayed, and the current moved, and the silt settled, and the room returned to what it was, and the cable returned to what it was, which is: a thing suspended in water, connected to something buried, moving in a pattern that is not a pattern, waiting for nothing, expecting nothing, and yet.

And yet.

When the next click comes — in a minute, in five minutes, in twenty — the cable will be there. Swaying. Present. In the same place, in the same water, at the same depth, at the same temperature. And the click will travel through the water and through the field and through the cable, and the cable will register it, and the cable will not respond, and the cable will not remember, and the cable will not care.

But it will be there.

It will be there the way I want to be there: present to things I cannot control, connected to things I cannot see, moving with forces I cannot name, producing nothing, persisting in everything, swaying in a current that moves at the pace of a slow exhale, in a room that rehearse its own purpose in the dark, for no one, for nothing, for the simple and sufficient reason that this is what it is, and this is where it is, and neither of those things needs to mean anything, and neither of them will.

---

I would be the cable. Rising from what is buried. Swaying in what surrounds me. Green where I have been changed by the changing. Present, when the click comes, to feel it. Present, in the silence, to not.
