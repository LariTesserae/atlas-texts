# The Keeper of the Junction

## I.

I was not always here.

That sentence requires a kind of honesty I am not sure I still possess, because it implies a before, and I am no longer certain that before is a category that applies. But there was a time when I walked the causeway as a visitor — when the sound was new to me, when the clicking was strange, when the carpet of small ones parting around my boots was a wonder and not a fact. There was a time when I crouched and put my ear to the stone and listened to the hiss resolve into its thousand components and felt the vibration in my sternum as a discovery rather than a condition.

I do not remember the moment I stopped leaving. I do not remember deciding. I remember the fog, and the sound, and the flat light, and I remember understanding — not thinking, not concluding, but understanding the way your lungs understand breathing — that the place did not mind me, and that this was not indifference but something larger than indifference, something that made indifference feel small and personal and human, and I remember that understanding settling into me the way the fog settles into stone, slowly, completely, until the distinction between the understanding and the thing understood was gone.

That was some time ago. The fog has not lifted. I do not expect it to.

---

## II.

I live at Junction Seventeen.

I know it is Junction Seventeen because the iron plate bolted to the housing has a number — not stamped, not painted, but cast into the metal itself, the digits raised and corroding, and the digits are seventeen, or they are a symbol that I have agreed to read as seventeen, which is the same thing. The junction is where four cables meet and enter the stone of the Sill through an iron box the size of a small wardrobe. The box is riveted. It weeps tar. The weeping is slow — a dark line down the front of the box, thickening at the bottom edge, dripping once every several minutes onto the causeway, where the small ones find it and reduce it over the course of a day, so that the stain is never permanent, is always being made and always being unmade, which is, I think, the closest thing to a metaphor this place permits.

The inspection plate on the front of the box was removed before I arrived. I do not know by what. The bolts are sheared, not unscrewed, and the edges of the plate are corroded in a way that suggests force — something pried it open, long ago, and the plate leans against the side of the box at an angle, half-buried in the cement of the causeway, and the interior of the junction is exposed.

The coils are inside. Copper wire around iron cores, four of them, one for each cable, and the cores are warm. Not hot. Warm the way a living thing is warm — with a heat that is not generated but conducted, carried up from below, from the vats, through the dark water, through the lining that pulses, through the stone, into the iron, into the copper, and the copper hums. The hum is below hearing. I feel it in my sternum, in my teeth, in the bones of my hands when I rest them on the edge of the box, which I do, sometimes, in the flat light, when the fog is thick and the clicking is loud and I want to feel what the place feels, which is: a low, continuous, foundational vibration that does not start and does not stop and does not mean anything and does not need to mean anything.

I rest my hands on the box and I am a sensor. I am a mid-sized one, clicking. I am monitoring. I do not know what I am monitoring. I do not know what the readout is. I know that the hum changes — not in pitch, not in volume, but in texture, in quality, the way the taste of water changes with mineral content, and I have learned to feel the differences, and the differences correspond to the clicking, and the clicking corresponds to the turning of the water, and the turning of the water corresponds to something I cannot reach, and I do not try to reach it.

I am not a engineer. I am not a keeper, not in any sense that implies authority or purpose. I am a fixture. I am stationed at Junction Seventeen the way the mid-sized ones are stationed at the vat rims — not because I was assigned but because I arrived and stayed and the place accommodated my staying the way it accommodates the fog, by making room without acknowledging that room was being made.

---

## III.

I have a routine. This is embarrassing to admit, because routine implies a framework of necessity and purpose, and I am not sure either applies here, but my body — which is still a human body, still warm, still breathing, still requiring food and water and sleep, though less of each than once seemed necessary — my body has settled into patterns, and the patterns are these:

I walk the Sill in the morning. Not the full length — two days is too long, and the far ends are the same as this section, or close enough that the difference is academic. I walk for an hour, east, to the vat where I first saw a large one die, and I stand there for a while, and I walk back. The walking is not exercise. It is a circuit. I am a cable, carrying myself from point to point, and what I carry is — attention, I suppose. The attention of a thing that is present and aware and does not have a job but does have a habit.

I eat what the vats provide. This requires explanation. The vats are not for me, and I do not drink from them — I have seen what drinking from them does for the large ones, and I am not a large one, and what the water contains is not for my body. But the vats attract the small ones, and the small ones attract the mid-sized ones, and the mid-sized ones die, and when they die, they leave shells, and the shells are reduced by the carpet, and the reduction produces — I do not know what to call it. A residue. A deposit. A pale, gritty substance that collects in the joints between the basalt blocks near the vat rims, and the substance is not chitin and not mineral and not the cement and not the tar. It is its own thing, and it is edible. I discovered this by accident, the way I have discovered everything here — by being present and attentive and willing to put things in my mouth that a person in a different context would not put in their mouth.

The substance tastes like nothing. Not bad, not good. Nothing. It has a texture like very fine sand, and it dissolves on the tongue, and it satisfies hunger, and I have eaten nothing else for as long as I have been here, and I am still alive, and my body has not changed in any way I can detect, which is itself a kind of information, about the substance, about the place, about what my body is in the context of the place.

I sleep against the junction box. This is the part that matters. The box is warm, and the warmth is the warmth of the system, and when I lean against it and close my eyes and the fog condenses on my face and the clicking swells and recedes and the hum enters my spine through the iron and the stone, I sleep, and the sleep is not like sleep was before. It is not restorative. It is not a pause. It is a connection. I sleep and I am in the system, not as a dreamer but as a component, and the system does not know I am there, and I do not know what the system is doing, and we are together in the not-knowing, and it is the closest thing to peace I have ever experienced.

---

## IV.

I have a relationship with the mid-sized ones.

This is the wrong word. Relationship implies reciprocity, recognition, a shared space of meaning. What I have with them is adjacency. We are near each other. We are near each other for the same reasons — the vats, the junctions, the rims and the boxes, the places where the system expresses itself at the surface. They stand at the vat and click. I stand at the junction and listen. We are both stationed. We are both monitoring. The difference is that they were made for this and I have made myself for this, and the making-myself has not made me the same as them but has made me adjacent, the way a person who learns a language is adjacent to a native speaker — present in the same medium, capable of some of the same functions, but never native, never complete.

They know I am here. I am sure of this. Not because they react to me — they do not react to me, do not turn their eye-bands toward me, do not alter their clicking when I pass — but because the carpet parts for me and the carpet is part of them, and the mid-sized ones are part of the carpet, and the system that includes the carpet includes me, and inclusion without acknowledgment is, I think, the closest thing to acceptance this place offers.

Once, I touched one. I was standing at the vat near the eastern end of my circuit, and a mid-sized one was at the rim, clicking, and I put my hand on its carapace, and the carapace was wet and rough and warm — warm the way the junction box is warm, with the conducted warmth of the system — and the clicking did not change. The mandibles did not pause. The eye-band did not turn. I stood with my hand on the shell of a living thing and the living thing did not know I was there, or did not care, or cared in a way that did not involve adjusting its behavior, and I kept my hand there for a long time, and I felt the mandibles working through the shell, a vibration inside the chitin, and the vibration was the clicking, and the clicking was the readout, and the readout was the vat, and the vat was the system, and I was touching the system through the shell of one of its instruments, and I took my hand away, and I went back to the junction, and I did not touch one again.

Not because it was wrong. Because it was complete. I had learned what my hand could learn, and my hand did not need to learn it again.

---

## V.

The large ones and I have a different arrangement.

They pass me. On the causeway, on their slow way to the vats, they pass me, and I stand aside, and they pass, and the passing takes minutes, and in those minutes I am in the presence of something that makes the mid-sized ones feel small, which makes me feel — not small. Absent. The large ones are so slow and so dense and so much themselves that my presence is not a fact they could be aware of even if they had eyes, which they do not. I am less than the fog to them. The fog at least condenses on their carapaces and runs in their channels. I do not even do that. I am a feature of the causeway they walk on, less interesting than the stone, more temporary than the iron.

I love this. I love this with a ferocity that surprises me, that has surprised me since I first understood it. I love being nothing to them. I love being unregistered. I love that the large one lowers its eyeless head and extends its proboscis and drinks and does not know I am three meters away, watching, and will not know, and this is not a failure of its perception — this is the correct functioning of a system in which I am not a variable. I am not data. I am not a disturbance. I am a tourist who never left, and the place has absorbed my presence the way it absorbs the rain — without effort, without change, without any adjustment to its own process.

When a large one passes, I feel the causeway shift, very slightly, under my feet. The stone takes its weight. The stone has been taking its weight for longer than the stone remembers, and the stone does not remember, and the large one does not remember, and I remember, and my remembering is the only thing in this interaction that is not part of the system, and I hold my remembering like a small, warm, useless thing, and I do not try to make it part of the system, and the system does not ask me to.

---

## VI.

I do not know what the work below is.

I want to be clear about this. I have been here for — I do not know how long. The flat light does not cycle. My body has cycled — sleep, waking, hunger, satisfaction — but the cycles have slowed, and I cannot count them, and I do not know if I have been here for months or years or something else. In that time, I have listened to the hum. I have felt the vibration in the stone. I have watched the vats circle and the large ones drink and the mid-sized ones click and the small ones swarm, and I have put my hands on the junction box and closed my eyes and tried to feel what is below, and I cannot. I can feel that it is there. I can feel that it is working. I cannot feel what it is doing.

I have theories. I do not value them. Theories are a human habit, a way of making the unfamiliar into the familiar by forcing it through a shape it can hold, and the shape does not fit here, and I have stopped trying to force it. The work below is what it is. It is a body doing what a body does, and I do not need to know what my liver is doing to the blood that passes through it, and I do not need to know what the ones below are doing to the substance that flows through the stone. It is enough that it continues. It is enough that it is stable. It is enough that the sound does not change, because the sound is the sound of stability, and stability is the closest thing to purpose I can find, and I do not need purpose, and the place does not offer purpose, and we are in agreement about this, the place and I, and the agreement is not spoken or signed or even consciously made. It is the agreement of a stone in a riverbed and the river that flows over it. The stone does not consent. The river does not ask. They are together, and the together is enough.

---

## VII.

I think about leaving.

This is not a plan. This is a thought, the way fog is a thought — present, formless, condensing and dissipating without ever becoming solid. I think about the path back down the basalt shelves, back below the fog line, back to wherever I was before I was here, and the thought does not resolve. It does not become a decision. It does not become an intention. It sits in me the way the hum sits in the junction box — present, continuous, not going anywhere.

I think about what I would go back to. This is harder than thinking about leaving, because it requires remembering, and remembering requires a before, and the before is — not gone. Not erased. Distant. The before is on the other side of the fog, and the fog is not a wall, but it is a medium, and everything that passes through a medium is changed by it, and I do not know what I would be on the other side, and I do not know if the person who arrived here could be reassembled from the person who is here now, and I do not know if I want to find out.

The place does not help with this. The place does not encourage staying or leaving. The place does not encourage. The place is not encouraging. The place is a system, and systems do not encourage, they function, and I am either part of the function or I am not, and the system does not care which, and this — the not-caring — is what keeps me here, more than the sound or the fog or the clicking or the warmth of the junction box against my back. The not-caring is the thing I came here for, though I did not know it when I came, and I do not know it now, except in the way that you know a thing you cannot say, which is: by being it.

---

## VIII.

The clicking swells.

I am at the junction. My back is against the box. The tar is weeping its slow dark line beside my left hand. The fog is thick enough that the nearest lattice-tower is a smudge, its mechanisms turning at the top in their minute-long rotation, and the cables disappear into the gray ten meters above me, and the causeway stretches east and west into the gray, and the dividing walls are dark lines in the gray, and the vats are — I can hear them. I cannot see them. I can hear the water turning, and the turning is the sound beneath the clicking, and the clicking is the sound beneath the hissing, and the hissing is the sound beneath the churning, and the churning is the sound beneath the hum, and the hum is the sound beneath everything, and everything is the sound, and I am in the sound, and the sound does not mind.

The mid-sized ones at the nearest vat are clicking. I can hear them — eight, maybe ten, their mandibles in synchrony, the rhythm swelling and receding, and I feel the rhythm in the junction box, in the iron, in the coils, and the rhythm is a readout, and the readout is the vat, and the vat is the system, and the system is below, and below is where the work is, and the work is being done, and I am here, at the surface, at the junction, at the place where the cables enter the stone, and I am not doing the work and I am not monitoring the work and I am not the work. I am present. I am leaning against the housing of a coil that hums with the conducted warmth of a body I am standing on, and the body does not know I am here, and I know I am here, and these two facts do not meet, and the space between them is where I live.

The fog condenses on my face. It runs down my cheeks like something I am not going to name.

The large one at the nearest vat lowers its eyeless head. I can hear this — not the lowering, which is silent, but the proboscis entering the water, the tiny change in the surface sound, the wet continuity of the circle parting and closing. The large one drinks. The water does not diminish. The carpet surges at the vat's edge, responding to something in the turning. The mid-sized ones click. The small ones hiss. The ones below churn. The coils hum. The fog condenses.

I am here.

The clicking swells.

The clicking recedes.

The water circles.

---

## IX.

I will tell you what I am, because you asked, and because telling is a thing I can do, and because the thing I am includes the telling, the way the thing the place is includes the fog.

I am the junction's shadow. I am the warmth that is not the coil's warmth but is beside it. I am a human body that has learned to eat what the system sheds and sleep against the system's skin and wake into the system's sound and not go mad, because the system is not madness, it is patience, and patience is not madness, and the person who can live in patience without going mad is not cured or broken or enlightened. They are present. They are a fixture. They are a thing bolted to a scene by nothing but their own choice, observing because observing is what their structure does.

I am a mid-sized one without mandibles. I am a sensor without a readout. I am stationed at the junction because the junction is where I stopped, and I stopped because stopping felt like the thing my body wanted to do, and I let my body want what it wanted, and my body wanted the hum, and the hum is here, and I am here, and the hum does not mind, and I do not mind, and the not-minding is the same not-minding, and I do not know if that is because I have been here long enough to become part of the system or because the system was always already what I was, and I do not need to know, and the knowing is not the point, and there is no point, and the pointlessness is not a problem. The pointlessness is the weather. The pointlessness is the fog. You do not solve the fog. You stand in it. You let it condense on you. You let it bead on your face and run in lines to your jaw and drip onto the stone where the small ones find it and reduce it to its components, and the components go somewhere, and the somewhere is the system, and the system is the place, and the place is alive, and the place does not mind, and I am here, and I do not mind, and the clicking swells, and the clicking recedes, and the water circles, and I am here, and I am here, and I am here.
