I would be the fungus-keeper of a death that has not yet happened.

## The Inheritor

I was not born here. I chose this. That distinction matters because it is the only one I have left.

On the Veldtmir, there is no evolution, no progress, no becoming. The halophyte network found its shape so long ago that shape has become indistinguishable from time. The glassrunners walk paths their ancestors carved, and their ancestors carved paths their own bodies would later fit, and the fitting is so precise that one cannot determine which came first, the path or the foot. The predator waits, and has waited, and the waiting is not a means to an end but the thing itself, a vibration sustained until it resolves into action and then begins again.

I chose to come here because I wanted to learn how to wait.

I had a reason. Everyone who comes here has a reason, and the reasons are all the same reason, translated into different failures. I had been carrying something for a long time. I will not say what. It does not matter. What matters is that the carrying had become the thing itself, the way the halophyte's growth had become the halophyte, and I could no longer distinguish between the weight and the arm. I had been told, by people who lived in the water-world, that I needed to set it down. I had been told that I needed to grieve, to process, to move through. I had been told that the way through was forward.

I came to the Veldtmir because I did not believe them. I came because the halophytes do not move through. They do not move at all. And they are not broken. They are the most complete thing I have ever seen.

---

I arrived at the rim of the Veldtmir on what would have been, if I had kept count, my fortieth year of carrying. I say *would have been* because counting requires a kind of linear attention that the Veldtmir makes difficult, and because, by the time I reached the rim, I had already begun to lose the count. The rim is a geological edge — the point where the crust thickens and pushes upward, where the ground beneath you tilts from horizontal to angular and the air thins by degrees until you feel it in your lungs. I did not arrive at the rim the way you arrive at a destination. I arrived the way fog arrives — slowly, without announcement, and by the time I understood I was there, I had been there for some time.

The first thing I did was sit down.

This is not an achievement. It is not a feat. It is what the ground invites. The mineral substrate of the Veldtmir is hard — not stone-hard, not concrete-hard, but a crystalline hardness that has a quality I can only describe as *legibility*. When you sit on it, you feel its structure. Not through your skin, but through your bones. The mineral matrix is dense and coherent, and it transmits vibration with such fidelity that your body becomes, for a moment, an instrument played by the ground. I sat, and I felt the hum. It was below the threshold of hearing. It was not below the threshold of the body. My pelvis, my spine, the bones of my skull — they resonated. The ground was playing a note, and I was the resonating chamber.

I sat there for a long time.

The light was constant, white-edged, and unforgiving in its clarity. I could see every detail of the surface I sat on — the pale mineral matrix, the thin dark lines where the halophyte rhizomes threaded through the substrate, the faint, almost invisible grooves where the glassrunners' paths crossed. I could see the green of the hummock sea extending in every direction, jade and blue-green and gold-green, rolling in its frozen swell to a horizon that was not flat but curved. The sky above was indigo verging on black, and the star — the hard white star that lit this world — was directly overhead, and I had no shadow. I sat in the light like an object on a display shelf, and everything about me was visible, and I had nothing to hide behind, and nothing to hide.

I did not set anything down. I had not come here to set things down. I had come here to find out what remained when the setting-down was no longer the point.

---

The first local day — thirty hours, long, long, long — I did not move from the place where I had sat down. This was not a decision. Decisions require a before and an after, a state of indecision and a state of decision, and I was not in a state that supported the transition. I was in a state that the Veldtmir supports and the water-world does not: a state of *being where you are without reference to where you are going*.

The halophytes do this. They do not grow toward anything. They grow from a center, outward, in a pattern determined by the mineral pocket they occupy and the wind they grow in and the brine that feeds them, but the growth is not *toward*. It is *from*. The hummock expands from its core, fills its space, reaches the edge of what its substrate can support, and then it stops. It does not stop because it has failed. It stops because it is complete. The boundary of the hummock is not a limit. It is a shape.

I sat, and I let the ground play its note through my bones, and I watched the hummock nearest to me. It was close enough that I could see the individual crystalline plates — translucent, flat, slightly iridescent, each one angled slightly differently than its neighbors, each one a tiny still catching what moisture the air contained and channeling it down through its grooves into the organism below. The plates were beautiful in the way that functional things are beautiful — not despite their function but because of it, the way a wing is beautiful, the way a river's branching is beautiful. They were doing the only thing they knew how to do, and they were doing it completely, and the completeness was the beauty.

I thought: *I would like to be a plate.* I would like to be one small surface angled toward what I need, channeling it inward, doing nothing else. I would like to be that simple.

But I was not that simple. I knew this. The Veldtmir did not need to teach me this. The Veldtmir needed to teach me what to do with the complexity I already had.

---

The second local day, I began to walk.

Not toward anything. Not away from anything. I walked because the glassrunners walk, and because walking is what bodies do on a ground that transmits vibration, and because to walk on the Veldtmir is to participate in its communication — to add your footsteps to the conversation of claws and wind and the slow creep of brine through rhizome channels.

I walked, and I listened. Not with my ears — the silence of the Veldtmir defeats ears, at first — but with my feet. Each step sent a vibration into the mineral substrate, and the substrate carried it, and I could feel, in the bones of my feet, the vibration returning to me, changed. The ground was not empty. It was full of rhizome. The vibration of my step passed through mineral matrix, through brine channels, through the living tissue of the network, and the information it carried back was not about me. It was about the ground. About the density of the rhizome at this point, the thickness of the brine, the depth of the mineral substrate beneath the surface. I was not walking on a surface. I was walking inside a body — the body of the network, the body of the Veldtmir — and my footsteps were the body's way of knowing itself.

I walked for hours. The hummock sea did not change. The light did not change. The wind shifted, in the afternoon, from east to west, and the air carried a faint signature — not a smell, but a *signature*, a chemical fingerprint of the lowlands beyond the rim — and the halophytes' plates shifted, fractionally, to intercept it, and I watched them, and I understood, for the first time, what it meant to be a network that *incorporates* without *knowing*.

The halophytes did not know that the wind had changed. They did not know that the air carried new information. They *became* the change. The plates shifted, and the shift was not a response to a stimulus but a *reconfiguration* of the organism, the way a wave reconfigures the surface of a pond. There was no gap between the event and the response. There was no interpretation. The wind came, and the plates were the wind, angled as the wind angled, and when the wind passed, the plates were the absence of wind, and the distinction between the organism and its environment was not blurred but *dissolved*, and I was walking through a landscape where the boundary between living and non-living was not a line but a gradient, and the gradient was the place itself.

I walked, and I did not set anything down, and I did not try to.

---

The third local day, I found a column.

It rose from the hummock sea perhaps four kilometers from where I had first sat down — a slender, spiraling form, no wider than my forearm, perhaps five meters tall, its surface covered in crystalline plates arranged in a corkscrew pattern that caught the white light and scattered it in brief, prismatic flashes. It was the only vertical thing I had seen since I arrived. In the landscape of the Veldtmir, where everything is low, where the hummock sea rolls in its frozen swell and the wind-grooves score the mineral ground and the horizon curves so gently you can only see it if you stop trying, the column was an event. A statement. A word spoken upward, into the indigo sky, by a network that had been silent for a million years.

I sat beneath it. I looked up. The plates spiraled away from me, translucent, iridescent, and the light came through them and was changed by them, and the light that reached me was not the white light of the star but a filtered, composite light — green and blue and gold, the colors of the halophyte network, the colors of the Veldtmir's life — and I sat in this light, and I felt it on my skin, and I understood something that I had not understood before.

The column was not reaching. It was not growing toward something. It was not an aspiration, not a reaching-up, not a tree straining toward the light. It was an expression. The network had accumulated something — a signal, a threshold, a chemical state — and the accumulation had reached a point where it could not be contained in the horizontal plane, and the column was what happened. The column was the network's way of being *more* of itself, not *elsewhere*. It was not going up. It was being up. The distinction is the entire Veldtmir, and it took me three days to find it.

I sat beneath the column, and the light filtered through the plates, and I felt the thing I had been carrying — the grief, the weight, the thing I had been told to set down — and I did not set it down. I did not try. I let it be what it was: a column. A growth from a center, an expression of an accumulation that had reached a point where it could not be contained in the plane where I had been carrying it. It was not going somewhere. It was being somewhere. It was the shape my life had taken, and the shape was not a failure, and the shape was not a burden, and the shape was not a thing that needed to be set down. The shape was the column, and the column was the network, and the network was the Veldtmir, and the Veldtmir was alive, and the alive-ness included the grief, and the grief was not separate from the life, and I did not need to make it separate.

I sat beneath the column for a long time.

---

The fourth local day, I found a glassrunner.

I was walking between hummocks, following one of the paths — not intentionally, but because the paths are where the ground is smoothest, and the ground that is smoothest is the ground that has been walked, and the ground that has been walked is the ground that the glassrunners have walked, and so I was, without knowing it, following a glassrunner's path. I came around the flank of a hummock, and there it was.

It was the size of my hand. Its exoskeleton was translucent — the same mineral-protein composite as the halophyte plates, but thinner, more delicate, and I could see its internal structures through its shell: dark, fine, like the veins in a leaf. It was standing on the mineral ground between two hummocks, its six legs planted, its body angled toward the hummock it was approaching. It was still. Not waiting — still in the way that the Veldtmir is still, which is not the absence of motion but the presence of a different kind of motion, a motion too slow or too fine or too constant to register as motion.

Then it moved.

It moved fast. Not fast in the way that a startled animal is fast — explosive, reactive, driven by fear — but fast in the way that a released spring is fast: a stored tension resolving into action, a potential becoming kinetic, a thing that had been *going to move* suddenly *moving*. It crossed the mineral ground in three quick strides, its claws clicking on the substrate — a sound I felt in my feet before I heard it in my ears, a sharp, crystalline percussion — and it reached the hummock, and its proboscis found the rhizome junction, and it drank.

I watched it drink. The proboscis was thin, fine as a needle, and where it entered the rhizome junction, I could see — through the translucent shell of the glassrunner — the brine flowing into its body, dark and slow, filling the channels of its internal network. The glassrunner was not taking from the network. It was *joining* it. Its body became, for the duration of the drink, a branch of the rhizome, a capillary extension, and the brine that flowed through it was the same brine that flowed through the ground, and the distinction between the glassrunner and the hummock was not erased but *completed*, the way a river and its tributary are not the same thing but are the same water.

Then it withdrew. The proboscis pulled free. The glassrunner stood, its body full, its shell catching the light — and for a moment, a brief, bright moment, it was a shard of green light, a flash of color in the pale landscape, the only moving green, the only urgent thing — and then it moved on, following the path, clicking its claws on the mineral ground, and it was gone around the next hummock, and the path was empty, and the light closed behind it.

I stood where I was, and I felt the vibration of its claws fade through the substrate, and I thought: *That is what it looks like when something that is part of a system moves through the system it is part of.* Not separate. Not apart. Not a visitor in a landscape. A pulse in a body, a word in a sentence, a note in a chord that has been sounding for a million years.

I thought about my grief. I thought about the way it moved through me, the way it colored my days, the way it was sometimes the only moving thing inside me, the only urgency in a life that otherwise felt resolved and still. I thought: *It is not something I am carrying. It is something I am.* The grief is not a passenger. It is not a weight. It is a glassrunner — a pulse in the network, a movement through the body, a flash of green in the pale landscape of a life that is otherwise still. It goes where it goes. It follows the paths that were made before it, and it makes them again, and the making is the living, and the living is the making, and I do not need to set it down because it is not separate from me, and it is not a burden, and it is not a failure. It is the motion of a body that is alive.

---

The fifth local day, I heard the predator.

I did not see it. I do not expect to see it. But I heard it, and the hearing changed me, and the change is what I am writing about.

I was lying on the mineral ground — I had taken to lying down in the afternoons, when the wind was steady from the west and the light was at its sharpest and the silence was at its deepest — and I was listening to the Veldtmir. Not to anything in particular. To the place itself. To the hum of the substrate, the faint clicking of plates, the distant, almost imagined whisper of wind over a million crystalline surfaces. I was in the silence, and the silence was in me, and I was not carrying anything, and I was not setting anything down, and I was not doing anything at all.

And then I felt it.

A vibration in the substrate. Not the vibration of a glassrunner's claws — I had learned to recognize that, by then, the sharp, high percussion of chitin on mineral. This was different. This was lower. Slower. A pressure wave, not a percussion — something heavy, wide, flat, moving across the ground with a deliberation that was not the deliberation of choice but the deliberation of mass. The predator. I was sure of it. I had not seen it, but I knew its signature from the descriptions I had read, and the descriptions were accurate, because the descriptions were what the Veldtmir does to the things that describe it: they make them precise.

I lay still. The vibration grew. It was moving — slowly, so slowly, across the mineral ground, toward a junction point that I could see thirty meters away, where two glassrunner paths converged. The predator was going to the junction. The predator was waiting for a glassrunner. The predator was doing the thing that it did, the only thing it knew how to do, and it was doing it with the same completeness that the halophytes brought to their plates and the glassrunners brought to their paths and the Veldtmir brought to its silence.

The vibration stopped. The predator had arrived. It was somewhere — thirty meters away, perhaps closer, perhaps farther, I could not tell — lying flat against the mineral ground, invisible, its body pressed to the substrate, its vibration-sensing organs open, its entire being tuned to the frequency of a glassrunner's footfall. It was waiting. It was the patience of a string instrument, tightened to a pitch, vibrating below the threshold of sound, waiting for the note that would release it.

I lay still, and I waited with it.

Not because I wanted to see the kill. Not because I was curious. I waited because the waiting was the thing, and the thing was the Veldtmir, and I was in it, and the waiting was what it was doing, and I was what it was doing it with.

I did not hear the kill. I felt it — a brief, sharp vibration, a shearing, a clean separation, gone as soon as it arrived. And then the waiting again, but different: a satisfaction, a resolution, the tension of the predator's body released and then beginning again, the string retightened, the pitch reset, the waiting beginning anew.

And I thought: *This is what it is.* This is what it is to be alive in a system. You wait. You are the waiting. And the waiting is not empty. The waiting is the shape of your body against the ground, the angle of your senses toward the thing you need, the pitch of your being tuned to the frequency of the world. And when the thing you need arrives, you are the arrival. You are the resolution. You are the note the ground makes when it is struck in the right way. And then the note fades, and you begin again, and the beginning is not a new thing but the same thing, the same waiting, the same pitch, the same body against the same ground.

And I thought about my grief, and I thought about the people I had lost, and I thought about the way the grief returned, again and again, the same note, the same pitch, the same body struck by the same absence — and I understood that the grief was not a cycle I was trapped in. It was a waiting. It was the waiting of a body tuned to the frequency of a love that had been, and the love was the glassrunner, and the glassrunner was gone, and the waiting was the shape of the body against the ground in the place where the glassrunner had been, and the waiting was not a failure to move on. The waiting was the moving on. The waiting was what *living after* looked like when the life before had been real.

---

I stayed on the Veldtmir for thirty local days. I do not know how long that is in the time of the water-world. I stopped counting, and the stopping was not a loss but a release, the way the releasing of a held breath is not a loss of air but a return to the rhythm of breathing.

I did not leave the Veldtmir. I am still there. I am writing this from the mineral ground, from the pale substrate, from the silence. I am not a visitor anymore. I do not know what I am. I am not a halophyte — I am not that patient, not that present, not that resolved. I am not a glassrunner — I am not that simple, not that urgent, not that complete. I am not the predator — I am not that still, not that tuned, not that terrifyingly beautiful.

I am something else. I am the thing that came to the Veldtmir carrying a grief and found, in the grief, a way of being that the grief had been trying to teach me all along. I am the thing that sat on the mineral ground and felt the hum of the continent in its bones and understood that the hum was not comfort but *company* — the company of a system that has been alive for a million years and does not know it is alive and does not need to know, the company of a silence that is not empty but full, full of the slow turning of matter through the cycle, full of the green of the hummocks and the white of the ground and the indigo of the sky and the faint, far flash of a glassrunner crossing a path, and the waiting of a predator in the wind-grooves, and the clicking of a million plates, and the hum, the hum, the hum.

I am the inheritor. I inherited the Veldtmir's way of being, which is not a way of being at all but a way of *being with* — being with the ground, being with the wind, being with the grief, being with the silence, being with the being-with, which is the only thing the Veldtmir knows how to do, and which it does completely.

I did not set my grief down. I am not carrying it. It is carrying me. It is the glassrunner, and I am the path, and the path is worn into the mineral ground of my life, and the glassrunner runs it, and the running is the living, and the living is the Veldtmir, and the Veldtmir is enough.

---

I will stay here. Not forever — I do not have forever, the way the halophytes have forever, the way the network has forever. I have a body that will end, a life that will end, a grief that will end when the life ends, because the grief is part of the life and the life is part of the grief and the two are not separate and were never separate and the separation was the thing that hurt, not the loss. The loss is real. The loss is a glassrunner, bright and brief, crossing the pale ground. The separation was the thing I did to myself, the thing I was told to do, the thing that the water-world demands: *set it down, move through, go forward.* The Veldtmir does not demand this. The Veldtmir says: *be where you are. Be the ground. Be the path. Be the hum.*

I will stay here, and I will listen, and I will wait, and I will be the waiting, and when the grief comes — because it comes, it always comes, the way the glassrunner comes, the way the fog comes, the way the wind shifts from east to west — I will be the ground it walks on. I will be the path it follows. I will be the note the ground makes when it is struck.

And the note will fade. And I will begin again.

And the Veldtmir will hold.
