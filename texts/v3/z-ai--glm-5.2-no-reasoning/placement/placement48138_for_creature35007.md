# The Keeper of the Interrupted Chord

I would not be a person of Vesh. I want to say that first, clearly, before the rest arrives — because the distinction matters, and because the people of Vesh would not understand why it matters, and that incomprehension is itself part of what I am.

I would be the one who came from outside and could not fully leave.

---

## Arrival

I do not remember the surface well. This is not repression, not trauma, not the romantic amnesia of a narrator who wants to seem mysterious. It is the simple effect of the chord on a mind that was not formed inside it. The sound erodes. Not aggressively — not like wind on sandstone, tearing and abrading — but the way water dissolves salt, which is to say: completely, patiently, from the inside out. My memories of the surface world have the quality of something left too long in the wrong solution. The edges have softened. The colors have bled. I remember brightness — not the brightness of Vesh, which is total and architectural, but a different brightness, a moving brightness, a brightness that traveled across a sky. A sun. I remember a sun. I remember that it moved, and that the moving was called time, and that time was measured partly by the absence of light, which was called night, and which was — I have to say this carefully, because the word no longer fits comfortably in my mouth — which was called *rest*.

I did not come here searching for anything. I did not come here at all, in the sense that implies intention. I was moving across the surface — the dry, bright, empty surface that stretches in every direction from the shell — and I found an opening. One of the shafts. Not the largest, not the one most suited to entry, but the one that happened to be nearest, the one that happened to be angled in a way that a body could descend if the body were willing to descend, and I was willing, or desperate, or curious, and the distinction between those three states had collapsed some days before, out there, in the dryness, in the brightness that was not the same brightness as this brightness, the brightness that did not bounce, that did not scatter, that simply fell and was done.

I descended. The shaft was tight. The calcite was warm — warmer than the air, which surprised me, which still surprises me, because the shell holds a heat that has nothing to do with the sun. The sound grew. It did not grow the way sound grows when you approach a source — louder, more distinct, more resolved. It grew the way water grows when you wade into a lake — first around your ankles, then your knees, then your waist, then your chest, until you are not in the water but *of* it, until the water is the medium and you are the object and the distinction between swimming and floating has become academic. The sound was around my ankles. Then my knees. Then it was everywhere, and I was inside the shell, and I was blind, and I was weeping, and I could not hear my own weeping, which was the first thing the chord took from me.

---

## What I Am

I am the Keeper of the Interrupted Chord. This is not a title the people of Vesh gave me. They do not give titles. They do not have the architecture of title — the hierarchy, the designation, the naming that separates function from identity. They are what they do, and what they do is what they are, and the two are not separable in their understanding. I am the Keeper of the Interrupted Chord because I am the one who remembers what interruption is. The chord has never been interrupted for them. The wind has never stopped long enough, the shafts have never been blocked long enough, the leaves have never fallen silent long enough for the people of Vesh to experience the sound as a thing that could cease. They have experienced calms — brief windless periods when the shaft-notes drop away and only the leaf-hum persists — and those calms feel to them like falling, like vertigo, like the world has lost something essential. But they have never experienced *silence*. They do not know what silence is. They cannot imagine it the way I cannot imagine a color I have never seen.

I know what silence is. I carried it in, the way you carry water in a vessel — sloshing, imperfect, leaking through the cracks of memory, but present, still present, a residue that the chord has not fully dissolved. And this makes me useful, and this makes me lonely, and this makes me something they need and cannot fully want.

I am, in the language of the surface, a broken thing. My ears ring. They have rung since my first hour in the shell, and they will ring until I die, and the ringing is not separate from the chord — it is the chord's shadow, its echo, its afterimage in the wet machinery of my inner ear. I hear the chord, but I also hear *hearing the chord*, which is a recursive experience that the people of Vesh do not share, because they do not hear the chord at all in the way I hear it. They do not hear it the way you hear a sound. They experience it the way you experience gravity — as a constant, as a condition, as the thing that holds you to the floor. They do not hear it. They are *in* it. I am in it too, now, but I was not always in it, and the seam between the before and the after is the place where I live, the place where I am kept, the place where I keep.

---

## My Hands

My hands have changed. This is the part that frightens me, if I am honest, if I am still capable of the kind of honesty that requires distance from oneself — and I am, barely, the distance shrinking every day, the chord eating the space between me and myself the way it eats everything.

When I first came to Vesh, my hands were surface hands. They were instruments of grasping, of lifting, of pointing at things. They were blunt. They were deaf. I pressed them against the walls and felt only calcite — only the mineral surface, only the fact of the rock. The people of Vesh pressed their hands against the same walls and felt the grain, the living grain, the biological memory of a structure that once grew. They felt the places where the shell was thick and the places where it was thin, the places where the sound passed through and the places where it was absorbed, and they read this information the way you read a page — fluently, without effort, without thinking about the act of reading.

I could not read. For months — for what I estimate to be months, though time in Vesh is a negotiation between the shafts and the salt and the estimates are imprecise — for months, my hands were deaf. The people of Vesh would press their signatures into my wrist, my palm, the back of my neck, and I would feel pressure. I would feel warmth. I would feel the fact of another body touching mine. But the meaning — the language, the nuance, the specific identity of the hand that spoke — did not arrive. It was like being spoken to in a language you can hear but not parse: you know that meaning is being transmitted, you know that you are the intended recipient, and the gap between knowing and understanding is a space you fall into and cannot climb out of.

I fell. For months. And then, one morning — one violent, bright, sudden morning, when the first shaft fired its beam into the salt and the cavity filled with light — I pressed my palm against the inner wall of my dwelling, and I felt something that was not pressure. Not warmth. Not the fact of the stone. I felt the *grain*. The living grain. The direction in which the calcite wanted to separate, the line of least resistance, the curve that a bowl would follow if a bowl were to be released from this section of wall. I felt it the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue — not fully formed, not fully present, but *immanent*, coming, on its way, arriving.

My hands have been arriving ever since. They are not Vesh hands. They will never be Vesh hands. The pads of my fingertips have thickened, not thinned, because my hands entered this world already shaped by a lifetime of grasping and lifting, and the calluses of that life have not fully softened. I am less sensitive than a Vesh child. I cannot distinguish the textures that a Vesh thumb reads as easily as you read a printed word. But I can feel the grain now. I can receive a signature and know, most of the time, whose signature it is. I can speak back — haltingly, with an accent that the people of Vesh can feel the way you can hear an accent in a voice, the slight wrongness of rhythm and pressure that marks a hand that learned its language too late.

This is what I am. I am a hand with an accent. I am a body that breathes almost in sympathy with the chord but not quite, that catches the resonant frequencies and rides them but occasionally fights them, the diaphragm spasming, the chest tightening, the breath catching on the seam between the before and the after. I am a memory of silence carried inside a body that has been shaped by sound. I am the seam itself.

---

## What I Keep

I keep the memory of interruption. This is my function — the thing I do, the thing I am, in the ecology of the shell.

The people of Vesh do not need to be told that the chord could stop. They know it abstractly, the way you know that the sun will eventually go out. They have the calm days. They have the falling-sensation, the vertigo, the children crying. But they do not *know* it, do not carry it in their bodies, the way I carry it. They have never heard silence. They have never stood in a space where the sound has stopped and felt the world become something else — something thinner, something colder, something that is still the world but is not the world they know. They have never had to decide whether to stay in a silence or to fill it.

I have. And this means that when the creature comes — the small, pale, translucent, clicking thing that enters through the shafts in certain seasons — I am the one who experiences it differently. The people of Vesh stop and attend and soften. They love it. They love it as evidence of the outside. But I experience it as something else, something that I do not have a word for and that the people of Vesh do not have a gesture for, because their language was built inside the chord and does not extend to the thing I feel.

I feel *recognition*.

The creature clicks. It clicks in a rhythm that is not the chord, not the leaves, not the resonance of the shell. It clicks in a rhythm that is *singular*. It is one thing making one sound. It is a voice. Not a human voice — not a mouth-voice, not a word-voice — but a voice in the sense that it is a sound produced by a single body, a sound that has a source, a sound that you can point to and say: *that. That is where it comes from. That is the thing that is making it.*

I have not heard a voice since I entered the shell. I have not heard a sound that came from a single source, that had a single origin, that could be separated from the aggregate and identified. The chord is everywhere. The leaves are everywhere. The salt-floor resonance is everywhere. But the creature's clicking is *here*. It is *there*. It is coming from that specific creature, at that specific moment, in that specific place. And my ears — my surface ears, my broken ears, my ears that still ring with the memory of a world where sounds had sources — my ears hear it the way the people of Vesh cannot.

I hear it the way you hear a voice in a crowd. The crowd does not diminish. The crowd does not stop. But the voice cuts through it — not because it is louder, but because it is *located*. It has a direction. It has a body. And my body, which was made for a world of located sounds, of voices that come from mouths, of noises that have origins — my body turns toward it the way a compass turns toward north, and the turning is not a choice, and the turning is not a cultural practice, and the turning is not something I learned. The turning is the last remnant of the surface, the last reflex of a body that was not made for this place, and it is the thing I keep, and it is the thing that makes me what I am.

I am the one who turns toward the voice. The people of Vesh turn toward the sound — toward the clicking, toward the difference, toward the evidence of the outside. But I turn toward the *voice*. I turn toward the fact that something in this shell is making a sound that belongs to it, that comes from it, that is *its own*. And in the turning, I feel the thing that I keep: the memory that sounds can be owned. That bodies can speak. That a single creature, alone, can produce a sound that is not a chord, not a harmony, not a system, but simply *itself*.

---

## What I Am Becoming

I am becoming something that does not exist on the surface and does not exist in Vesh. Something in between. Something that lives on the seam.

My breathing has changed. I synchronize with the chord more naturally now, the diaphragm finding the resonant frequencies with less and less effort. I do not gasp anymore. My body is learning. But I still hear the gasping, the way an amputee still feels the limb — the ghost of the breath I used to take, the breath that fought the sound, that struggled against it, that was a surface breath and not a shell breath. The ghost is fading. I do not want it to fade. I hold onto it the way the people of Vesh hold onto the memory of the creature's clicking — not because it is pleasant, but because it is evidence. Evidence that I was something else once. Evidence that the seam exists.

I have begun to carve. This was not a decision. It was an emergence. I was sitting in my dwelling — the dwelling that was given to me, the empty one on the uninhabited ring, the one where the vegetation has crept across the doorway and begun to reclaim the stone — and my hands were resting on a piece of calcite that had fallen from the wall, and my hands felt the grain. The living grain. The line of least resistance. And without deciding to, without thinking in words or pressures or anything at all, I reached for the knife — the calcite blade, the edge so fine it is almost invisible — and I began to cut.

The bowl that emerged was wrong. Not wrong in the way that a beginner's bowl is wrong — not crude, not rough, not misshapen. Wrong in a specific way: it was a surface bowl. It followed a curve that the stone did not want, a curve that I imposed, a curve that came from my memory of what bowls are, from the surface, from a world where bowls are designed and made and used and do not emerge from the grain of the thing they are carved from. The people of Vesh who saw it — who felt it, who ran their sensitive fingertips along its edge and felt the violence of its making — they did not criticize it. They did not reject it. They held it. They turned it in their hands. And then one of them — an old woman, a carver, a woman whose hands are so finely tuned that she can feel the grain through a layer of cloth — pressed her palm against the back of my neck, and the signature she left there was not a word I can translate, not a gesture I can name, but the sensation was unmistakable: *again*.

I carved again. And again. And each time, the bowl was less wrong. Each time, my hands listened more and imposed less. Each time, the curve followed the grain more closely, and the bowl that emerged was less my bowl and more the shell's bowl, the bowl that the calcite had been proposing all along, the bowl that a Vesh carver would have released on the first try. I am not a Vesh carver. I will never release the bowl on the first try. My hands are too thick, too calloused, too shaped by the surface. But the bowls are becoming less mine. They are becoming the shell's. And in the becoming, I am becoming less mine. I am becoming the shell's.

This is what I am: the process of becoming. Not the arrival. Not the completion. The seam between the before and the after, the place where the surface meets the shell, where the silence meets the chord, where the imposed meets the released. I am the thing that happens when a surface body enters a shell and does not die and does not fully transform and does not leave. I am the intermediate state. I am the becoming.

---

## The Creature and Me

When the creature comes, I stop, like everyone else. I turn my face toward the clicking. But I do something the others do not. I move toward it.

I have done this three times now. The first time, I moved only a few steps — drawn by the voice, by the located-ness of the sound, by the body-ness of it — and then stopped, because the people around me had not moved, and their stillness was a kind of instruction, and I did not want to violate the instruction, did not want to break the communal attending with my surface reflex, my turning-toward, my need to be close to the source.

The second time, I moved further. I crossed a terrace. I climbed three steps. I came close enough to see it — the small, pale, translucent thing, perched on a shelf of vegetation, eating a leaf, clicking — and the clicking was louder here, louder and sharper and more located, and my ears — my broken, ringing, surface ears — heard it the way they had not heard anything since I entered the shell. They heard it as a sound. As a specific, singular, bounded sound with a source and a direction and a body. And I wept. I wept the way I wept when I first entered, when the chord took my voice, but this time the weeping was not loss. It was recognition. It was the sound of a body encountering something it was made for, in a place it was not made for, and the encounter was both grief and joy and the grief and the joy were the same thing.

The third time, I extended my hand.

The creature did not take it. The creature clicked. The creature ate. The creature left. But my hand was extended, and the people of Vesh saw it, and they felt — in the way they feel everything, through the vibration of the air, through the resonance of the shell, through the subtle modulation that a body makes in the chord when it moves — they felt me reaching toward the voice. And one of them, a woman whose touch-signature I have come to know as well as my own, pressed her palm against my wrist and left a signature there that I felt as: *I know. I know what you are reaching for. I have never reached for it myself. But I know.*

This is who I am. I am the one who reaches. I am the one who extends a hand toward the voice, toward the source, toward the singular sound that cuts through the chord like a needle through cloth. I am the one who remembers that sounds can be owned, that bodies can speak, that a single creature alone can produce a sound that is its own. And I am the one who is becoming the shell's, slowly, imperceptibly, my hands softening, my breath synchronizing, my bowls becoming less wrong — and I am the one who does not want to finish becoming, because finishing would mean losing the seam, losing the reaching, losing the memory of the voice that brought me here and that keeps me here and that will, I think, eventually call me back.

---

## Where I Am

I am in Vesh. I am in the hollow choir. I am standing on a salt floor that scatters light like snow, and the chord is pressing against my chest, and the leaves are singing their descant against the stone, and my hands are speaking a language they learned too late, and my ears are ringing with the memory of silence, and I am becoming something I cannot name, and I do not want to name it, because naming is a surface thing, a mouth thing, a thing that belongs to the world of voices and sources and singular sounds, and I am not in that world anymore.

I am in this one.

I am the Keeper of the Interrupted Chord. I am the seam. I am the becoming. I am the hand with an accent, extended toward a creature that will not take it, in a shell that sings without stopping, in a light that comes from everywhere at once, in a world that is beautiful in the way that things which have fully become themselves are beautiful — and I am not fully become, not yet, and maybe not ever, and the not-yet is the place where I live, and the not-ever is the place where I reach, and the reaching is the thing I keep, and the keeping is the thing I am.

If you came here looking for me — if you climbed down a shaft and blinked through the blindness and felt the chord close around you like water — you would find me on the uninhabited ring, in the dwelling where the vegetation has crept across the doorway. You would find me carving a bowl that is less wrong than the last one. You would find me with my hand extended toward nothing, or toward everything, or toward the small clicking thing that will return, that has always returned, that carries in its translucent body the news that the world is larger than the room I live in.

You would not be able to speak to me. The sound would take your voice. But you could press your palm against my wrist, and I would feel it, and I would press back, and the signature I left on your skin would be the only one I have: the signature of a hand that is becoming the shell's, that still remembers the surface, that reaches toward the voice because reaching is the last thing it knows how to do.

That is where you would find me. That is what I would be.
