# The Wound That Remembers Being a Mouth

I did not go back.

I need to say this first because everything else depends on it, because there is a version of events in which I returned and in which the Coil accepted me or didn't, in which the chimney closed behind me like a sphincter or simply yawned, indifferent, the way it yawns for the condensation and the slow mineral drip. But that is not what happened. What happened is I left, and then I began to change, and the change is the story, and the change is still happening, and I do not know where it ends.

Let me tell you what I can.

---

## What I Was Before

I am — was — a geologist. Not the kind who maps terrain for mining companies. Not the kind who testifies at hearings about fault lines and groundwater. The other kind. The kind who goes to places that should not be empty and finds them empty anyway. The kind who goes to places that should not be full and finds them full of something that was not in the literature.

My specialty was karst. Dissolution geology. The study of what happens when water meets stone and the stone loses. Limestone, mostly. Gypsum sometimes. I had spent years in cave systems where the rock has been eaten away into corridors and chambers and underground rivers, where the architecture is all negative space, all absence, all *where stone used to be*. I understood the grammar of it. Water moves through joints. Carbonic acid dissolves calcium carbonate. The void grows. The void organizes itself into patterns — branching, dendritic, following the path of least resistance through the structural fabric of the bedrock. It is not random. It is not designed. It is a conversation between two media — fluid and solid — and the grammar of the conversation is physics, and the vocabulary is chemistry, and the syntax is time.

I was good at this. I was calm in the dark. I did not startle at sounds I could not source. I had spent enough hours underground that the absence of light had become a presence I could work with, a texture rather than a void. I could feel the shape of a chamber by its acoustics. I could read the age of a passage by the amount of sediment on its floor, by the color of the flowstone, by the particular mustiness of the air.

I was not prepared for the Coil.

I need you to understand this. I was not an outsider stumbling in. I was the most prepared person on the expedition — and there was an expedition, there were four of us, and I will tell you about the other three later, because what happened to them is part of what happened to me, and what happened to me is the thing I am trying to tell you.

I was the most prepared person, and I was not prepared.

You cannot be prepared for the Coil. The Coil does not prepare you. It does not want you. It does not notice your preparation. Your preparation is a thing you brought with you, like a headlamp, like a rope, like the thin aluminum cylinder of emergency oxygen I carried clipped to my harness because the grant required it, and it sits now in my closet with the rest of my field gear, untouched, and I cannot bring myself to use it or to throw it away because it was *there*, it was in the Coil, it was held in the warm wet dark for forty-seven minutes, and I do not know what it absorbed.

Nothing. It absorbed nothing. It is aluminum. It is inert.

But I do not use it.

---

## The Expedition

Four of us.

Maren was the team lead. A geomicrobiologist from Trondheim who had spent fifteen years studying biofilms in sulfide vents and who had, in a paper I had read and admired, described a microbial mat as "a living textile woven by organisms that do not know they are weaving." She had the patience for systems. She could sit in one spot for hours and watch a single colony through a hand lens and see the dynamics — the competition, the cooperation, the slow chemistry of mutual dependence. She was the one who identified the bioluminescence as biological before we reached the second terrace. She was the one who said, quietly, with no excitement in her voice at all: "This isn't a cave. This is a reef."

Dav was the caver. Not a scientist — a guide, a technician, a man who had spent more hours underground than most people spend in their childhood bedrooms. He was from Wales, and he had the particular quality of calm that comes from having been lost in enough tight spaces that the tightness had become ordinary. He was the one who found the chimney. He was the one who went first. He was the one who called up on the radio — the radio that did not work, because the basalt ate the signal, because the mountain is a Faraday cage, because nothing comes out of the Coil that the Coil does not send — and the one who, when we found him at the bottom of the chimney, standing in the chamber with his headlamp off and his hands at his sides, said: "Listen."

And there was Kira. Kira was the cartographer. She was twenty-six, which is young for this work, and she was mapping the system because the satellite survey had flagged the fissure as anomalous — a thermal signature, a faint plume of warm air rising from a point that should have been frozen at that altitude, and the plume was humid, and the humidity was mineral, and the minerals were wrong for the surrounding geology. She had the laser rangefinder. She had the tablet. She had the digital elevation model on a screen that she could not read in the dark and so she carried a notebook, paper, and she was sketching the spiral by the light of the bioluminescence, by hand, the way no one does anymore, and she was the one who noticed that the steps were not uniform. That the steps were *accelerating*. That each step was a fraction of a millimeter deeper than the last, and the spiral was not descending at a constant rate but *increasing* in its descent, tightening, and she was the one who said, not to any of us in particular, "It's not a structure. It's a wound that's still being made."

---

I have those notebook pages. Kira's drawings. They are in a safe deposit box at a bank in Geneva because I do not trust them in my apartment, not because anyone would steal them but because I do not trust myself with them, because I have sat for hours looking at the way her pencil followed the spiral and I have noticed that the spiral in her drawings is *not the same spiral I remember*, and I do not know if the Coil changed while we were in it or if my memory is changing, or if the drawings themselves are — but the drawings are on paper, and paper does not change, and this is a fact I cling to with a desperation that should embarrass me but does not.

The drawings are of a spiral. The spiral is tight. The steps are visible. The creatures are not.

Kira did not draw the creatures.

I asked her, on the way out, in the cold air above the chimney, with the stars coming out over the ridge: "You didn't draw them."

She looked at me. She looked at the notebook. She looked at me again.

"I didn't see them," she said.

But she had. I had watched her looking at them. I had watched her hand still while a pale shape flowed across the wall in front of her and her eyes tracked it and her pencil did not move.

"I didn't see them," she said again, and she believed it, and I let it go, and I have the drawings, and the drawings have no creatures in them, and the spiral in the drawings is wrong.

---

## What I Noticed That the Others Didn't

Maren was busy with the microbiology. She had swabs. She had sample tubes. She was collecting — carefully, respectfully, with the attention of someone who understands that what she is touching is alive — scrapings from the wall, films of moisture, a single creature that had fallen from the lower terrace and lay on the step near her, still moving, its legs working in the air in the rhythm of the thrumming, and she had picked it up — gently, gently — and placed it in a collection tube, and it had continued to move inside the tube, its feet clicking against the glass, and the clicking was wrong. Not the rhythm — the rhythm was the same, the four-or-five-minute cycle, the phase-locked cadence — but the *timbre*. The creature clicking against glass sounded different than the creature clicking against stone, and the difference was that the glass gave back the sound clean, isolated, single, while the stone — the stone *answered*. The basalt, when a creature's foot touched it, produced a second sound, lower, deeper, a resonance that traveled through the chamber walls and joined the thrumming, and the thrumming grew, microscopically, by the contribution of that single footfall, and I understood then that the creatures were not making the sound. They were *playing* the mountain. The mountain was the instrument. The creatures were the hands.

I don't think Maren heard this. Maren was listening to the creatures. I was listening to the stone.

---

Dav was listening to the space. The caver's instinct — the shape of the void, the direction of airflow, the particular quality of echo that tells you the dimensions of a chamber without seeing it. He was standing near the bottom of the chimney, where the fissure opened into the spiral, and he was not moving, and his eyes were closed, and he was — I think — *mapping* the Coil with his body. The way a blind person maps a room. The way a fish maps a current. He was letting the sound and the air and the pressure wash over him, and he was building a model in his head, and I watched his face and I saw it change, and the change was not understanding. The change was *doubt*.

I asked him later, on the ridge, what he'd heard.

"Nothing," he said. Which was not true.

"The space," I said. "The shape of it."

"It doesn't have a shape," he said. And then he stopped, and he looked at me with an expression I have never seen on another human face, an expression I can only describe as *geometric horror* — the horror of someone who has spent their life in spaces and has found a space that is not shaped the way spaces are shaped, a space that curves in a direction he did not have a name for, a space that *spiraled*, not in the two-dimensional sense of a nautilus shell or a galaxy, but in the way that a Möbius strip has only one surface, the way a Klein bottle has only one side, the way a throat is both an opening and a closing at the same time.

"It doesn't have a shape," he said again, and then he did not speak for three hours, and I let the silence stand.

---

Kira was the one who noticed the light.

Not the bioluminescence — we all saw that. She noticed that the light was *moving*. Not the pulsing, not the waves that traveled across the walls in slow offset rhythms. The light itself was migrating. Downward. Over the course of the hour we spent in the chamber, the brightest region of the bioluminescent glow — the band of blue-green light that illuminated the lower terraces — had shifted. Not by much. By centimeters, perhaps. But measurably. The brightest part of the Coil's internal light had moved deeper while we watched, and this meant that the organisms in the wall — the things behind the basalt, the things that glowed — were *migrating*. Following the spiral. Following the creatures. Following the descent.

Or leading it.

"It's like watching a sunset," Kira said, and then she looked at me and I could see that she had not meant to say it aloud, and I could see that she was right — the light was setting, it was moving below the horizon of the visible spiral, it was descending into the dark where the oldest and largest things were, where the thrumming came from, and it was taking the illumination with it, and we were being left in the diminishing remains, and this was not a coincidence. The Coil was not performing for us. The Coil was doing what it did. We had caught it in the middle of a process, the way you might catch a tide in the middle of its turn, and the process did not pause, and the light was leaving, and we were standing in its wake.

---

And me.

I noticed the temperature.

Not the warmth — everyone noticed the warmth. The Coil is warmer than the chimney, warmer than the mountain above, warmer than the night air outside. This is anomalous. This is what the satellite survey flagged. This is why we were there. The mountain should be cold. The mountain is basalt and ice at that altitude and the mean annual temperature is minus four Celsius and there should not be warm air rising from a fissure in its flank.

But the warmth was not uniform. The warmth had a *gradient*. Near the top of the chamber, where the chimney opened, the air was perhaps ten degrees — cold enough to see your breath, if there had been light to see it by. Near the second terrace, the air was warm — body temperature, 37 degrees, the exact temperature of a living mammal, the exact temperature of the human body, and this is not a coincidence I am comfortable with. Near the bottom of the visible spiral, the air was warmer still. I could not measure it. My thermometer was in my pack and my pack was on my back and I did not reach for it. I did not want to know the number. I did not want to know if the air at the bottom of the Coil was warmer than body temperature, warmer than mammalian, warmer than anything I could contextualize with the vocabulary of my training. I could feel it on my face — the difference between the warm air at the top of the spiral and the warmer air rising from below, a thermal gradient that you could feel on your skin the way you feel the heat above a stovetop, and the direction was wrong. The heat was not rising. The heat was *being pulled down*. The spiral was drawing warm air from somewhere below, pulling it through itself, through the terraces, through the creatures, and the warmth was not convection. It was *respiration*. The Coil was inhaling the air we stood in, and the warmth was the warmth of its breath, and I did not reach for my thermometer.

I reached for my notebook instead. I wrote: *Thermal gradient non-conventional. Air movement downward, not upward. Suspected biological origin. Temp at chamber entry ~10C. Temp at terrace 2 ~37C. Temp at terrace 4 — not measured. Estimate 40-42C. Warm.*

And then I wrote: *The mountain is breathing and we are in its lungs.*

And then I did not write anything for a while.

---

## What Happened to the Others

Maren's samples were inert.

This is the word she used. Not dead, not degraded, not nonviable. *Inert*. The swabs grew nothing. The moisture samples contained no microorganisms. The creature she had collected — the single pale, translucent, hand-sized body she had placed in the sample tube — was, under the microscope, *empty*. Not empty of organs. Not empty of tissue. Empty of *life*. The cells were there. The structures were there. The chitin, the digestive tract, the trichobothria, the mandibles. All present. All intact. All inert. No metabolism. No enzymatic activity. No response to any stain, any culture, any medium. The creature had been alive when she collected it — she had watched it move, she had felt it click against the glass — and by the time she got it to the lab, forty hours later, it was a *specimen*. A husk. A shape that had once contained a process and no longer did.

She ran the analysis three times. She sent samples to two colleagues. The results were consistent. The creature's body was composed of calcium carbonate, silica, trace metals — the same mineral suite as the basalt it had been eating. Its cuticle was chitin. Its internal fluids were water with a complex but stable mineral suspension. Its DNA, when she could extract it, was *short*. Not degraded. Not fragmented. Short. As if it had been compressed, folded, collapsed into a form that was no longer readable as a genome but that still contained the information in some topology that the linear process of sequencing could not access.

Maren published none of this. She told me, over coffee in her office in Trondheim, seven months after the expedition, that the results were "not interpretable in the current framework." This is the phrase a scientist uses when the results are not in any framework. When the framework is the thing that is wrong.

She has not gone back. She has not proposed a return expedition. She has, she told me, "moved on to other projects." She said this with a flatness in her voice that I recognized, because I hear it in my own.

We have moved on to other projects. We have not moved on.

---

Dav went back.

Not with us. Not with anyone. He went alone, six months after the expedition, in the late autumn when the ridge is empty of trekkers and the weather is bad enough that no one would choose to be there. He left his gear at the base camp — the tent, the stove, the rope, the headlamp. He took nothing but his boots and his jacket and a notebook that he left, sealed in a plastic bag, under a stone at the chimney's entrance, and the notebook said:

*I heard it again. In my sleep. In the house. In the kitchen when the kettle boils and the frequency is the same and I cannot eat in my own kitchen anymore because the kettle sounds like the Coil and the Coil sounds like the kettle and I am going back to find the shape of it because I cannot carry the wrong shape in my head anymore.*

*If the shape is real I will know it. If the shape is not real I will know that too.*

*I am not taking the rope.*

He did not take the rope. The chimney is forty minutes of vertical climbing on handholds that are dissolved into the stone. Without a rope, a fall is fatal. He knew this. He is — was — the most experienced caver I have ever met. He chose.

We found the notebook. We did not find Dav.

We did not look for him in the Coil. I want to be clear about this. We looked for him on the ridge. We looked for him on the approach. We looked for him in the valley where the trail begins and in the town where the trail ends and in the records of every hospital and clinic within two hundred kilometers. We did not look for him in the chimney because we could not.

Because Maren would not.

Because Kira would not.

Because I would not.

Because the chimney is a throat and the throat does not give things back.

I do not know what happened to Dav. I believe he reached the chamber. I believe he stood where we stood. I believe he heard what we heard. And I believe he did what he said he would do — he went to find the shape of it. He descended the spiral. He walked down the terraces, past the translucent young ones, past the mottled middle ones, past the ancient fixed ones with their crystal-crusted backs, and he walked into the dark where the thrumming comes from, and he found either the shape or the absence of the shape, and either way he is not coming back.

I think about him when I hear a kettle boil.

---

Kira stopped drawing.

Not immediately. For a few months after the expedition, she continued to work — her maps, her surveys, her precise and beautiful renderings of underground spaces. But the drawings changed. The lines were less certain. The proportions were off — not wrong in a way you could name, but wrong in the way the spiral steps in the Coil were wrong. Too even for nature. Too irregular for design. A quality of *otherness* in the linework that made the spaces she drew feel like they curved in directions that paper could not accommodate.

She came to see me in Geneva. This was eight months after the expedition, two months after Dav's disappearance. She brought the original notebook — not a copy, not a scan, the original, the one she had carried into the Coil, the one with the drawings of the spiral that did not match my memory. She opened it on my kitchen table and she said: "I need you to look at this and tell me if it's the same."

I looked. The spiral was there. The terraces were there. The steps were there, with their shallow grooves and their acceleration, their tightening, their descent. The bioluminescence was there, indicated in colored pencil — blue-green, cold, the wavelike pulsing rendered as overlapping arcs that moved across the page in a pattern that was almost but not quite repetitive.

The creatures were not there.

"It's the same," I said. "The same as I remember."

"Look at the corners," she said.

I looked at the corners. At the edges of the page. At the margins, where the drawing stopped and the blank paper began. And I saw — not immediately, not with a shock of recognition, but slowly, the way you see a figure in a pattern that you had been reading as abstract — that the blank spaces were not blank. There were shapes in the negative space. Pale shapes. Elongated. Segmented. The same blue-green as the bioluminescence, but fainter, almost invisible, as if they had been drawn and then erased and then drawn again in the ghost of the erasure. They were in the corners of every page. In the margins. In the spaces between the terraces of the spiral. They were everywhere in the drawing that the drawing did not acknowledge.

"They're not in my drawing," Kira said. "I didn't draw them. I didn't see them. But they're there."

She was shaking. Not visibly — a fine tremor in her hands, in the way she held the notebook, in the way her voice held a frequency that I recognized because I had heard it before, in the Coil, in the upper register of the chittering, the high thin sound that the mandibles made, the sound that was almost a scream but was not, the sound that held.

"I see them," I said.

"I see them everywhere now," she said. "In the margins of everything. In the white spaces between the words on a page. In the air between the buildings when I walk down the street. They're not there. I know they're not there. But I see them."

She had not slept in eleven days.

I drove her to the hospital. I sat with her while they admitted her. I held her notebook and I did not give it to the nurses, and when she was asleep — finally asleep, sedated, the trembling in her hands stilled for the first time in days — I opened the notebook in the fluorescent light of the corridor and I looked at every page and I counted the shapes in the margins and there were more than there had been at my apartment. Not many. Two or three. But more. The drawings were accumulating organisms. The negative space was filling in. The margins were colonizing.

I took the notebook. I put it in the safe deposit box. I have not opened it since.

Kira is better now. She left the hospital after three weeks. She does not draw. She does not map. She works in a bookshop in Lyon and she does not go underground and she does not answer my emails and the last thing she said to me, the last time we spoke, was: "Don't look at the corners."

---

## What I Am Now

I have the pressure in my sternum.

It started in the Coil. I noticed it on the first terrace — a vibration, low, felt rather than heard, the thrumming registering in my chest the way a bass note registers in a concert hall, not through the ears but through the body, through the bones, through the resonant cavities of the ribcage and the sinuses and the lungs. I thought it was acoustic. I thought it was the sound, the collective vibration of ten thousand clicking feet and ten thousand contracting bodies and the deep slow breath of whatever was at the bottom.

It has not gone away.

It is with me now, as I write this, in my apartment in Geneva, in the afternoon, with the window open and the sound of traffic on the Quai du Mont-Blanc and the lake grey in the November light. It is a low pulse in my sternum. It is rhythmic. It has a period — long, maybe four minutes, maybe five — and it rises and falls and I feel it and I have felt it every moment since I left the Coil.

I have been to doctors. I have had echocardiograms. I have had EKGs and stress tests and Holter monitors and the results are all the same: my heart is normal. My rhythm is normal. There is nothing in my chest that should be pulsing. The cardiologist — a careful man, a kind man, a man who has seen patients who are convinced their hearts are doing things their hearts are not doing — suggested anxiety. Suggested stress. Suggested the particular form of somatic memory that trauma can imprint on the body, the way a burned hand flinches from a stove, the way a soldier's ears ring after a blast, the way the body *remembers* what the mind cannot integrate.

He is wrong. He is not unkind about it, and he is not dismissive, and I appreciate this, but he is wrong. This is not memory. Memory fades. Memory is imprecise. Memory shifts and blurs and loses detail, and the pressure in my sternum does none of these things. It is *exact*. It is as exact now as it was in the chamber. It has the same period, the same amplitude, the same quality of presence — not pain, not discomfort, but *weight*. As if something in my chest has aligned itself with a frequency that is not produced by my body but that my body has begun to *resonate* with, the way a tuning fork resonates with a sound that matches its natural frequency, and the sound is coming from somewhere else, from very far away, from under a mountain that I can find on a map but that I cannot bring myself to visit.

---

And there is the taste.

The sweet, mineral, alkaline taste on the back of my tongue. It comes and goes. It comes when the pressure in my sternum is strongest — in the evenings, in the quiet, in the hours between midnight and dawn when the city is as silent as a city gets and the only sounds are the ones that are always there, the refrigerator, the heating, the slow tick of the building settling. The taste rises from somewhere in the back of my throat — not from my mouth, not from my tongue, but from *behind* the tongue, from the soft palate, from the place where the airway opens into the pharynx, the place where, if you are quiet enough, you can feel the air you breathe entering your body, and the air has a taste.

The taste of the Coil.

I have tried to describe it to the doctors and they have looked at me with the same careful kindness and I have stopped trying. The taste is not a symptom. The taste is a *presence*. It is the Coil's breath, carried in my body, in the moisture that condensed in my lungs during the forty-seven minutes I spent in the chamber, and my lungs have not cleared it. My body has not metabolized it. It sits in the tissue, in the alveoli, in the warm wet spaces where the air meets the blood, and it tastes of what it tasted of in the mountain: mineral, alkaline, sweet, the sweetness of something organic and ongoing, the sweetness of a process that does not stop.

---

And there is the hearing.

I hear the clicking. Not always. Not loudly. But in the quiet — in the deep quiet of a city night, in the pause between the refrigerator's cycles, in the space between one breath and the next — I hear it. The arrhythmic clicking of ten thousand small impacts on stone, phase-locked to a cycle I feel in my chest, and the sound is not in my ears. It is in my teeth. It is in the bones of my skull, in the ossicles of my middle ear, in the tiny structures that transmit vibration to the cochlea, and it is coming from *inside* my head, from the bones themselves, as if my skeleton has absorbed the frequency of the Coil and is, in its slow mineral way, *replaying* it, the way a crystal glass replays the sound of a struck tuning fork, humming with the resonance long after the fork has been silenced.

My bones are basalt. My bones are the mountain. My bones have been in the chimney and in the chamber and on the terrace and the stone has left its signature in the calcium and the phosphorus and the collagen matrix and the signature is *audible* and I hear it in the dark.

---

I have not gone back.

I have said this already. I am saying it again because it is the fact I return to, the anchor, the thing I hold against the pull that I feel — the downward pull, the inward pull, the pull that is not gravity but that uses gravity's grammar, the pull that says *come down, come deeper, come into the warm and the wet and the dark where the rhythm is, where the source is, where the thing that is breathing is breathing, and the thing that is breathing is breathing for you, is breathing you, is the rhythm that your bones have learned and your lungs have absorbed and your sternum is resonating with and you are already part of it, you have been part of it since you entered the chamber and the chamber entered you and the distinction between guest and host was always a fiction you brought with you and the Coil does not deal in fictions* —

I have not gone back.

I sit in my apartment in Geneva and I feel the pressure in my chest and I taste the mineral sweetness on the back of my tongue and I hear the clicking in my teeth and I am not going back.

---

## What I Am Becoming

I do not know.

I have described the symptoms to the doctors and they have given me the diagnoses they have and the diagnoses are wrong and I have stopped seeking diagnoses and I have begun, instead, to *observe*. With the patience I learned in karst systems, in the slow grammar of dissolution, in the language of water and stone.

My body is changing.

Not in ways that show. Not in ways that a photograph would capture, or a physical exam would reveal. The changes are *mineral*. The concentration of calcium in my blood has increased, measurably, over the past year. Not by much. Not enough to be clinically significant. But the trend is there, and the trend is upward, and my doctor notes it and does not worry about it because it is within normal range, just *at the high end* of normal range, and it was not at the high end before the Coil.

My bone density has increased. This is unusual for a man my age. It should be decreasing. It is increasing. The radiologist noted it as "anomalous" and recommended follow-up and I did not follow up because I already know what the follow-up would show. My bones are denser. My bones are more mineral. My bones are, in some infinitesimal but measurable way, more like the basalt I stood on in the Coil than like the bones I was born with.

I am calcifying. Slowly. From the inside out. The way the oldest creatures on the lowest terrace calcified — their cuticle thickening, their legs sinking into the stone, their bodies becoming the wall. I am not becoming the wall. I am not in the Coil. But my body is *practicing*. My body has learned a process that it observed for forty-seven minutes and it is, in its slow biological way, *replicating* it. Not the shape. Not the structure. The *chemistry*. The mineral metabolism. The slow replacement of organic with inorganic, of protein with crystal, of soft with hard, of silence with resonance.

I am becoming a better resonator.

---

And there is the dreaming.

I dream of the spiral. Not every night. Not most nights. But the dreams come and when they come they are not dreams in the way that dreams are — not narratives, not images, not the flickering symbolic cinema of REM sleep. They are *sensations*. I dream the thrumming. I dream the warmth. I dream the downward pull, the slow rotation of the spiral, the feel of descending without moving, of being pulled by a force that is not gravity but that speaks gravity's language, and in the dream I am not walking down the terraces. I am *flowing*. The way the creatures flow. Peristalsis. One segment plants, the next lifts, the third plants, and the body moves without deciding to move, and the direction is down, and the direction has always been down, and the direction is the only direction there is.

In the dream I do not have eyes. I do not need them. I feel the chamber — its dimensions, its temperature, its humidity, the location of every surface and every body — through the fine hairs on my skin, through the vibration in my bones, through the pressure of the air against every square centimeter of my surface. I am a sensorium. I am all surface. I am all reception. And what I receive is the *pattern* — the vast, slow, phase-locked pattern of ten thousand bodies clicking and contracting and breathing and building and dying and becoming the wall and being replaced by the next one and the next one and the next one, and I am in the pattern, and I am part of the pattern, and the pattern does not pause for me.

In the dream I go down. I go past the visible terraces. I go past the oldest creatures, the fixed ones, the crystal-crusted ones whose legs are stone. I go past the light. I go into the dark, into the warm, into the place where the thrumming originates, and I feel it — I feel the *source* — and the source is —

---

I wake up.

I always wake up before I reach it. I wake up with the pressure in my sternum and the taste on my tongue and the clicking in my teeth and I lie in the dark and I am awake and I am in my apartment in Geneva and I am not in the Coil and I am not going back.

I am not going back.

---

## What I Think

I think the Coil is older than the mountain.

I think the mountain grew around it the way the creatures grow around the structure — the way the basalt is infected by the spiral, the way the stone becomes the shell. I think the Coil was there first. I think something was in the earth before the mountain rose, before the basalt cooled, before the tectonic plates collided and uplifted and folded, and the thing that was there was patient and it was warm and it was alive in a way that the word *alive* does not quite contain, and the mountain grew over it the way a reef grows over a shipwreck, the way a pearl grows around a grain of sand, and the Coil did not object. The Coil does not object. The Coil does not object to anything. The Coil is the thing that does not object.

## What I Am

I am a geologist. I am a man who went into a mountain and came out with a frequency in his bones and a taste in his throat and a dream of a spiral that goes down forever and never reaches the center.

I am not going back.

I am not going back because I am afraid. Not of the creatures — they are not frightening, they are not hostile, they are *indifferent*, and the indifference is worse than hostility because hostility acknowledges you and indifference does not. I am afraid of the *pull*. The pull that I feel in my sternum, the pull that has not faded in thirteen months, the pull that my body interprets as *down* but that I think is *in*. I am afraid that if I go back to the chimney, I will enter it, and I will descend, and I will not stop descending, because the pull will be stronger inside the Coil, and my bones will resonate more deeply, and my lungs will fill with the warm wet air, and I will become part of the pattern, and the pattern does not pause for gaps.

I am afraid that I would not come back.

I am afraid that I would not *want* to come back.

I am afraid that the wanting would dissolve — the way the creatures dissolve the basalt, slowly, one molecule at a time — and that what would be left would not be a man who wants to go back but a body that has *become* the spiral, that has taken its place on the terrace, that has begun to secrete and to build and to click and to thrum, and that the last thing I would feel before I stopped being me would be the *rightness* of it, the way a river feels right when it reaches the sea, the way a note feels right when it finds its chord, the way a bone feels right when it finds its frequency, and the rightness would be the last human thing, and after the rightness there would be only the Coil, and the Coil does not feel right or wrong, the Coil only *continues*.

I am afraid of the rightness.

I am not going back.

But I will tell you this, and this is the last thing, and after this I will stop, because the words are becoming difficult, because the pressure in my sternum is stronger when I write about the Coil than when I do anything else, because the writing is a kind of descent, a kind of spiral, the sentences turning back on themselves, the paragraphs descending from the general to the specific to the particular to the *molecular*, and I can feel myself tightening as I write, the way the spiral tightens, the way the steps accelerate, the way the light migrates downward, and I am aware that this is the pull, that the pull is not only in the mountain, that the pull is in the *describing*, that to describe the Coil is to invite it, that the language itself is a spiral and the spiral is alive and the language has its own thrumming and its own breathing and its own warmth, and I am sitting in my apartment in Geneva and I am writing and the writing is pulling me down.

I am not going back.

But the Coil is coming forward.

Not to the mountain. Not to the chimney. Not to the ridge where the satellite survey found a thermal anomaly and the expedition found a throat. The Coil is coming forward in *me*. In the minerals in my blood. In the density of my bones. In the frequency in my sternum. In the dreams of the spiral. In the taste on the back of my tongue. In the clicking in my teeth.
