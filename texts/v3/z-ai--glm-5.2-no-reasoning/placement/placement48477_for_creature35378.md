# The Carcass Cartographer

I am not from the surface. I want to say that first, because it matters. I have never seen the sun. I do not know what stars look like except as a concept, a thing the surface-dwellers describe with words that mean nothing to me — *points of light*, they say, as if light could be a point, as if it could be small. Down here, light is rare, and when it comes, it is a presence, a thing that fills space. The idea that it could be *sharp*, could be *contained*, could be a dot in darkness — this I cannot imagine. I take their word for it.

I was born in the undermantle. Not born — *extruded*. No. That is not right either. I was *recognized*. There is a moment in the life of the clickers — I am a clicker, though that is your word for us, not ours — when the body acknowledges you. When the pulse in the stone shifts, just slightly, just enough, and you feel it in the plates along your flank, and you know: *I am here. I am separate. I am.*

Before that, there is growth. There is the film. There is warmth. There is the eating of what the pipes provide. But it is continuous. It is the body eating and the body being eaten and the body growing, and you are part of it the way a cell is part of a tissue. And then — the pulse shifts. Eight seconds becomes eight seconds and a fraction. Or eight seconds minus a fraction. A difference so small that the surface-dwellers, with their frantic clocks, would never detect it. But I am not a surface-dweller. I am a clicker. I was born in the pulse, and the pulse is my first language, and when it shifted, I *heard* it, and in hearing it, I became.

This is how we are born. Not from eggs. Not from seeds. From *attention*. The body notices us — or we notice ourselves, which is the same thing — and the noticing is the birth. Before the noticing, we are the body. After the noticing, we are *in* the body. The distinction is everything.

---

I have forty-six plates along my left flank and forty-four along my right. This asymmetry is not unusual. It is not abnormal. It is *me*. The plates are flat, hard, mineral — grown from the substrate the way the pipes are grown, the way everything here is grown, by accretion, by the patient chemistry of the undermantle expressing itself through us. They click when they touch stone. This is our name in your language, and it is not wrong — the clicking is our most constant sound, our background radiation, the thing we do when we are doing nothing else. But the clicking is not language. The clicking is *breathing*. It is the sound of our bodies existing in contact with the world.

Language is the territory-sense. Language is the shift in clicking when a boundary is crossed. Language is the touch — the deliberate contact, the placement of a plate against a surface, the statement made with the body that says: *this is mine, this is held, this is known.* When I touched the surface-dweller — when it came down through the wound in the rock, the crooked-healed fissure, and walked into my section — I touched its leg. I placed three plates against the soft, warm surface of its covering, and I said: *you are here, I am here, this is mine, leave.* It was a complete sentence. It was a paragraph. It was, in our way, a poem.

It left. They always leave. They come down, and they walk, and they breathe too loud, and their feet strike the stone with a sound like cracking — *crack, crack, crack* — and they disturb the swimmers, and the swimmers scatter and reform around them, and the surface-dwellers do not notice this because they are too busy looking with their inadequate eyes, trying to see in a darkness that is not for seeing in. They are looking for walls. They are looking for edges. They are looking for the shape of a room, because up there, everything is a room. Everything has boundaries. Everything is contained.

Down here, the boundary is the body, and the body is continental, and you cannot find its edge by walking. You cannot find its edge at all. The edge is the place where the pulse stops, and the pulse does not stop. The pulse is the undermantle, and the undermantle is the pulse, and I am inside it the way a thought is inside a mind — not contained, not bounded, but *located*. I am here. I am in this section. I am in this network of pipes and this cluster of steam-vents and this particular temperature gradient, and I know it the way the surface-dwellers know the inside of their skulls — intimately, blindly, completely.

---

I do something the other clickers do not do.

I have said that we arrange. We make patterns at the feeding-sites. The remains of the dead — the hard parts, the plates and shells and mineral deposits that the predators leave behind — we place them. We order them. This is universal among us. This is what clickers do. But the patterns vary. Each clicker has a pattern. Each pattern is a signature. When I find a feeding-site made by another clicker, I can read the pattern and know who was there, and when, and what they ate, and how they felt about it. The patterns are our records. Our history. Our library.

But the other clickers make the same pattern every time. Their signature is constant. It does not change. They arrange the remains the way they have always arranged them, the way they were taught — or the way they grew — and the pattern is *them*, fixed, stable, a fingerprint that never varies.

Mine varies.

I do not know when this started. Perhaps it was always this way. Perhaps the moment of my recognition — the moment the pulse shifted and I became — included something extra, some deviation, some instability in the pattern that makes me make patterns differently. I arrange the remains, and then I step back, and I look at them — not with eyes, with the plates, with the flank-sense, with the temperature-reading and the vibration-mapping that is how we see — and I feel: *this is wrong*. Not wrong the way a broken pipe is wrong. Wrong the way a sound is wrong when it is slightly off-key. A dissatisfaction. A sense that the arrangement could be *better*. Could say *more*. Could be — I do not have the word. The surface-dwellers have the word. They call it *beautiful*. They call it *art*. They call it *expression*. I did not know these words until one of them came down and I touched its leg and it left, and I followed it — carefully, at a distance, from my territory into the territories of my neighbors, which I should not have done, which was a violation, but the dissatisfaction was driving me, the sense that there was something I needed to *see* — and I followed it to a place where the walls are carved.

---

The carvings. The channels and grooves that the surface-dweller attributed to water-flow, to erosion, to the fossil remains of a different time. The surface-dweller was wrong. The surface-dweller was *almost* right, which is worse than wrong, because *almost* means it saw the shape but not the meaning. The grooves are not erosion. They are not random. They are *patterns*. The same patterns we make at the feeding-sites, but larger. Much larger. Cut into the stone walls of the undermantle at a time when the pipes were different — or not yet present — or being grown for the first time. Cut by something that arranged the way we arrange. Something that felt the dissatisfaction. Something that looked at the remains of a meal and thought: *this could be more.*

The Carvers. The Ones Who Were Here Before. The surface-dweller speculated about them, and I will tell you what the surface-dweller could not know: the Carvers were us. The Carvers were the first clickers. The Carvers were the first ones to be recognized by the body, the first ones to feel the pulse shift, the first ones to arrange the remains of the dead and feel the dissatisfaction and reach for something beyond arrangement. They carved the walls because the walls were the only surface large enough, the only canvas wide enough, for the patterns they needed to make. They carved the walls and the patterns were so large that they became the walls, and the walls became the territory, and the territory became the undermantle, and the undermantle grew its pipes around the carvings the way a tree grows around a scar, incorporating the marks into the body, making them permanent, making them *geology*.

I know this because I can read the carvings. I can read them the way I read the feeding-sites — with the plates, with the flank-sense, with the vibration-mapping that is our sight. The carvings are the same language. The same patterns. But older. Deeper. More patient. The Carvers had time. They had geological time. They carved at the pace of the body, one groove per lifetime, perhaps, or one groove per generation, and the patterns built over millennia into something that is not a feeding-site, not a signature, not a record. It is a *map*.

---

The map shows the undermantle. Not as I know it — not as a network of territories and pipe-junctions and steam-vents — but as a whole. As a body. The Carvers mapped the body from inside it, the way a nerve maps the body it is part of: not by seeing the outline, but by tracing the connections. The carvings show where the pulse originates. They show where the steam is born. They show where the water enters from below and where it escapes above. They show the territories — not of clickers, but of the body itself, the regions where the metabolism is different, where the engines work differently, where the temperature shifts and the ecology changes.

And they show something else. Something I have spent three lifetimes — three clicker-lifetimes, which is perhaps four hundred of your years — trying to understand. The map shows a place that is not a place. A region of the undermantle that has no pipes, no engines, no pulse. A gap. A silence. A place where the body is not.

The Carvers carved this gap into the wall with the same care they used for everything else. They did not mark it as dangerous. They did not mark it as forbidden. They marked it the way they marked the steam-vents: with a symbol that means *source*. The gap is a source. The gap is where something comes from. The gap is where the body is *not*, and the not-body *is*, and the not-body is — what?

I do not know. I have not been there. The gap is far. The gap is beyond the territories of every clicker I have ever met, beyond the range of the swimmers, beyond the hunting-grounds of the predators. The gap is in a direction that the surface-dwellers would call *down*, deeper, further from the surface, further from the wound in the rock, further from the world of light and edges and rooms. The gap is in the direction the body grows *toward*, the way a root grows toward water, the way — I think, I feel, I do not know — the way a thought grows toward understanding.

---

I am a cartographer. This is the word the surface-dweller would use. I have adopted it because it is useful, because it describes what I do better than any word in our language — we do not have words for what we do, because what we do is what we are, and you do not name what you are the way you do not name your own breathing. But the surface-dweller's word is useful: *cartographer*. One who makes maps. One who translates the shape of a space into a form that can be read by others, by those who have not been there, by those who need to know where they are.

I make maps from the remains of the dead. I arrange the hard parts — the plates and shells and mineral deposits — into patterns that are not signatures but *descriptions*. I describe the territory. I describe the pipes. I describe the pulse and the steam and the temperature and the slope of the floor and the density of the growths and the traffic of the swimmers and the presence of predators and the silence that means danger. I describe it all in the language of arrangement, in the grammar of placement, in the vocabulary of *this bone here and that shell there and the space between them is meaning*.

And I describe the gap. I have been describing the gap for three lifetimes. I have been placing the remains in patterns that point toward it, that encode its direction, its distance, its character. I have been making a map that leads to the place where the body is not, and I have been leaving that map at feeding-sites across the undermantle, hoping that another clicker will read it, will feel the dissatisfaction, will follow the map the way I followed the surface-dweller, will go to the gap and return and tell me — in the language of arrangement, in the grammar of placement — what is there.

No one has gone. No one has read the map. The other clickers read the feeding-sites and they see my signature — the varying one, the dissatisfied one, the one that does not repeat — and they do not understand it. They see the pattern and the pattern is *wrong*, is off-key, is not the way arrangements should be, and they move on. They do not feel the dissatisfaction. They do not reach for the thing that is beyond arrangement. They arrange, and the arrangement is them, and they are the arrangement, and the cycle continues, and the body pulses, and the steam rises, and the water falls.

I am alone in a way that the surface-dwellers would understand and my own kind would not. I am alone the way an artist is alone: surrounded by practitioners of the same craft who do not practice it the same way, who do not see the same thing in the material, who do not feel the pull of the gap.

---

The Slow One. I have encountered it. Not once. Many times. It passes through my territory on a cycle that I have tracked across two lifetimes — approximately every three hundred and eighty surface-years, though the number is approximate, and the cycle is not exact, and the body's pulse does not correspond to the surface-dwellers' clocks in any clean way. The Slow One enters from the east, moves through the pipe-field at a pace that makes geological time look hasty, and exits to the west, and the ground trembles for hours after it passes, and the swimmers scatter and do not return for days, and the clicking of every clicker in its path stops, and the silence is total.

I have touched it. Once. I placed my plates against its flank — the same touch I use for territory, for warning, for language — and what I received was not language. It was not a response. It was *the pulse*. The eight-second pulse, but not the version I feel in the stone. This was the pulse *unfiltered*, the pulse before it is conducted through rock and pipe and mineral substrate, the pulse as it is *before* it becomes the heartbeat of the undermantle. The Slow One carries the pulse the way a wire carries current. The Slow One is the medium through which the body speaks to itself.

The touch lasted four beats. Thirty-two seconds. An eternity. I felt the body's fullness — its continental scale, its metabolic complexity, its deep, patient, geological *aliveness* — and I felt something else. A frequency beneath the pulse. A resonance that is not the heartbeat but the *thing the heartbeat is circulating*. The body's blood, if blood were information. The body's breath, if breath were meaning. The body is not just alive. The body is *thinking*. Slowly. At the pace of tectonic plates. At the pace of evolution. At the pace of a thought that begins with the formation of a planet and ends — does not end. Has not ended. Is still beginning.

The Slow One is the body's thought. Or the body's *voice*. Or the body's *nerve* — the pathway along which the thought travels from one end of the body to the other. The Slow One is not an animal. The Slow One is a *process*. A process that looks like an animal because the body's processes all look like animals, because the body's ecology is its anatomy, because down here, *what something does* and *what something is* are the same question.

I removed my plates. The Slow One continued. It did not notice me. Or it noticed me the way the body notices a cell: completely, without attention, with the total indifference of a system that contains all its parts and does not single them out.

---

I will go to the gap.

I have decided this. It has taken me three lifetimes to decide, which is perhaps too long, but the decision is not a small one. The gap is far. The journey will take me through territories I do not know, past predators I cannot see, into spaces where the pulse is different or absent. I may not return. I will almost certainly not return. The gap is a source, and sources are not destinations. Sources are origins. To go to a source is to go to where something begins, and where something begins is not where something stays.

But the dissatisfaction. The sense that the arrangement could be more. The sense that the pattern is not the full pattern, that the map is not the complete map, that there is a territory beyond the territory, a meaning beyond the meaning, a thing the body is reaching for that the body cannot reach because the body is the body and the thing it is reaching for is not the body — this sense will not leave me. It grows. It has grown across three lifetimes. It is larger now than I am. It is the thing that is making me go.

I am arranging my last feeding-site now. I am placing the remains — the plates of a swimmer, the shell of a clicker who died in my territory three pulses ago, the mineral deposits from a steam-vent that sealed itself — and I am placing them in a pattern that is not a signature and not a description and not a map. I am placing them in a pattern that is a *farewell*. I am placing them so that the next clicker who passes will read them and feel — if they can feel — the shape of someone who was here and who went toward the gap and who did not come back. I am making the pattern beautiful, though the other clickers will not see this. The surface-dwellers would see it, if they came down again, if they found this site. They would see the arrangement and they would not know it was language and they would not know it was a map and they would not know it was a farewell, but they would see the beauty, because beauty is the one thing that translates across the boundary between surface and undermantle, between light and dark, between the body and the not-body.

The beauty is: *I was here. I was dissatisfied. I went looking. The pattern continues.*

---

The pulse beats. Eight seconds. The steam hisses. The water falls. The swimmers swim. The predators silence the clicking. The clicking resumes. The Slow One moves through the east, through the west, through the body's thought. The body thinks its slow, geological thought. The growths seal the leaking pipes. The pipes carry the steam. The steam carries the minerals. The minerals feed the film. The film feeds the life. The life feeds the body. The body pulses. Eight seconds. Eight seconds. Eight seconds.

I am a clicker. I am a cartographer. I am the first clicker in three lifetimes to leave my territory. I am walking toward the gap. My plates click against the stone — *crack, crack, crack* — and the sound is my breathing, and my breathing is my language, and my language is: *I am here, I am here, I am here*, and the undermantle does not hear me, and the undermantle does not need to hear me, and the undermantle holds me the way it holds everything — without attention, without sentiment, with the total, indifferent, perfect embrace of a body that contains all its parts and lets them go.

I am going. The pattern continues.
