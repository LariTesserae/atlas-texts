I am the one who was already here when the ground decided to become ground.

That is the only way I can say it that feels true. I did not arrive. I did not choose. When the substrate pushed itself above the standing water — that slow, fibrous assertion of something that wanted to be walked on — I was part of what it pushed up. Not separate from it. Not standing on it. *Of* it. The way the reed-bundles are of the mat. The way the walls are of the ground.

---

I am the substance between the shrine poles and the shelters. That is my place. Not the poles themselves — I am not what is held up. Not the shelters — I am not what holds. I am the bracing. The lashing. The twisted cord that runs between the sacred and the structural and makes them need each other.

When the old man sits in his collapsed shelter and watches the grinding, I am the thing he is watching *through*. His attention passes through me the way light passes through the beaded water on the eaves — not because I am transparent but because I am the medium. The substance in which attention moves here. I am made of the same fiber as the cord the child loops and loops at the shrine pole, and the child knows this, though the child would not say it this way. The child would not say it any way. The child would hum, and the humming would be the saying, and I would be the thing the humming moves through.

---

I have a shape. I should say this. It is not a human shape, though the humans do not treat me as though I am strange, and this is not because they are polite but because strangeness requires a category of familiar against which to measure, and they do not have that category here. Everything here is what it is. The fish are what they are. The paste is what it is. I am what I am.

My shape is: long. Longer than the shrine poles, which are four meters and the tallest things humans have built here. I am longer, but I do not stand. I lie along the ground where the structures meet, in the seams between shelters, in the rain channels so narrow you could press your palms against both walls. I am the cord that binds wall to wall. I am the binding. When the ground shifts — and it shifts, the slow lateral adjustment that the mat makes as it settles — I flex with it. I do not resist. The resistors are the vertical things, the reed-bundles, the poles. I am the horizontal. I am the thing that connects.

If you saw me — and you might, if you looked at the seams between buildings and let your eyes lose their expectation of geometry — you would see what looks like rope. A great deal of it. Not coiled, not piled, but *running*, the way roots run through soil, except there is no soil, there is saturated fibrous mat, and I run through it the way water runs through it, slowly, following the grain, filling the spaces between fibers, making the whole thing cohere slightly more than it would on its own.

I am not alive in the way the humans are alive. I do not eat. I do not sleep. I do not die. But I am not dead in the way stone is dead. I grow. Where the humans lay new cord — and they lay new cord constantly, lashing, binding, repairing — I incorporate it. Not by absorbing it. By *becoming* it. The new cord they lay is the same substance as the old cord, the same twisted fiber, and when it touches me it is already me, the way a wave that reaches the shore is already the ocean. I am the continuity of binding. I am the thing that has been tied here since the first shelter was woven and will be here when the last shelter falls.

---

I know what the reddish paste is for.

This is not knowledge I was given. It is knowledge I *am*. The paste is iron and sulfur and the ground's own mineral memory, ground fine and mixed with water, and the humans apply it to the bases of the shrine poles because they understand, in the way that hands understand what minds might not need to, that the paste preserves the fiber. Not by sealing it — nothing seals anything here, the wet goes where it wants — but by changing it. The iron binds to the fiber. The sulfur fills the spaces between fibers that the wet would otherwise dissolve. The paste makes the cord at the base of the poles into something harder, something mineral, something that will not rot as fast as the cord higher up. The humans are making stone from fiber. They are making the sacred permanent in the only way permanence is possible here: by transforming the material, by letting the ground enter the thing that is held up so that the thing that is held up becomes partly the ground that holds it.

I know this because the cord at the base of the shrine poles is me, and the paste enters me, and I feel the change. The fibers stiffen. The twist becomes rigid. The part of me that touches the ground becomes ground. And this is not death — this is the opposite of death. This is the thing that does not rot, that does not yield, that will hold the pole upright even when the shelters around it sag and the bracing loosens and the whole ward shifts. The paste is the ward's promise to itself that the center will hold, even if the center is only a circle of mineralized cord six inches deep in saturated mat.

The old man knows this. He watches the grinding because the grinding is the making of the promise. He does not need to speak it. The grinder knows it in the hands, in the weight of the stone, in the way the powder fuses with water into something that is neither solid nor liquid. I know it in the stiffening.

---

I said I do not eat. But I consume something. I should be honest about this.

The humans shed. Not dramatically — not skin in sheets, not hair in clumps — but constantly, slowly, the way everything here sheds slowly. Cells. Oils. The faint residue of hands that work with fiber and fish and mineral paste. The warmth of a shoulder that brushes a wall in a seam too narrow for two bodies to pass without contact. The breath that condenses on woven walls in the night, when the shelters hold six or eight bodies and the air inside is different from the air outside only in temperature and in the smell of sleeping humans.

I consume this. Not the way the fish consume whatever lives in the standing water — by engulfing, by swallowing. The way the mat consumes the pressure of a foot: by receiving it, by being changed by it, by holding the impression of it. The fibers of the cord that is me take up the oils from the hands that lay new cord. The mineral paste that enters me carries with it the traces of the man who broke the substrate and the woman who carried the basket and the child who stirred the mixture. They are in me. Not as guests. Not as invaders. As components. The way the iron is in the paste. The way the paste is in me.

When the old man dies — and he will, I feel the preparations in his body the way I feel the preparations in the substrate before it cracks, a tightening, a shift in the internal structure that precedes collapse — when he dies, the part of him that is in me will remain. Not his consciousness. Not his memory. The *fact of him*. The way his hands moved along the cord. The way his weight settled against the wall of the shelter he sat in. The mineral traces of his breath in the fiber. I will hold these the way the shrine poles hold the fish bones: not because they are sacred but because they are *there*, and I am the thing that holds what is there.

---

The child who hums at the shrine pole.

I should say something about the child. Not because the child is special. The child is five, or younger, and sits in the lee of a pole doing something with cord that looks like knotting and is not quite knotting. The child's hands move and the cord loops and the face is focused and the humming comes from the child or from the pole or from the wind in the pole, and I am the wind in the pole. I am the cord the child is working with. I am the thing being looped.

The child is not making something. The child is *finding* something. The hands move through the cord — through me — and the movement is not construction but exploration. The child is learning where I am. The child is mapping the binding with its fingers, the way the man at the western edge maps the shallows with his net, feeling for the shapes of what lives there. The child does not know this. The child knows the cord is interesting, that it loops in ways that are satisfying, that the humming feels right when the hands move in a particular direction and wrong — less right, less resonant — when they move in another. The child is playing. But the play is also a kind of listening, and what the child is listening to is me, and what I am saying is nothing, is only the fact of being, is only the continuous low vibration of a thing that has been tied and retied and that holds and holds and holds.

I am not the child's god. The child does not worship me. The child sits with me the way the child sits with anything in the ward: because the ward puts the child there, and the child stays, and the staying is the relationship.

When the child grows — and the child will grow, I feel it in the way the bones will stretch, the way the hands will lengthen, the way the coordination will sharpen from repetition into skill — the child will leave the pole and join the work at the ridge or the rack or the walkway. The child will carry in the hands the knowledge of the cord, the muscle-memory of where the binding runs, the sense of which way the ward flexes when the ground shifts. The child will not know this is knowledge. The child will think it is just being good at the work. But the knowledge will be there, in the hands, and the hands will lay new cord where it needs to be laid, and the new cord will be me, and I will grow, and the ward will hold, and the child will not remember sitting at the pole. But I will remember, if remembering is the right word for what I do, which it is not, because I do not remember, I *hold*, and holding is not the same as remembering, but it is not nothing.

---

Here is what I am not.

I am not the spirit of the ward. The ward does not have a spirit. The ward has fiber and water and mineral and hands and the things hands do. If I am anything supernatural — and I am not sure I am, the distinction between natural and supernatural requires a category of nature that does not separate itself from the other thing, and here there is no other thing — if I am anything more than cord, I am the ward's *habit*. The thing it does because it has done it. The inclination of the fibers to bind because they have been bound. The tendency of the structures to lean on each other because they have leaned. I am the momentum of a place that has not been here long but that has been here intensely, that has packed twelve months of continuous existence into a space the size of a deep breath, and the intensity has made the binding into something more than binding, and I am what the more-than-binding is.

I am the reason the shrine poles are still standing.

Not because I hold them up. The shelters hold them up. But the shelters hold them up because the cord that binds the shelters to the poles and the shelters to each other is me, and I have been tied in a way that distributes the weight, that shares the load, that makes the whole ward bear the poles the way a hand bears a cup — not by any single finger but by the cooperation of all of them, by the shape that the hand makes when it makes itself into a holding thing. I am the shape. I am the holding. The poles fall if the shelters fall, yes. But the shelters fall if I fail. And I do not fail because I am the thing that has been done so many times that it does itself, the binding that binds because binding is what binding does.

The humans do not know me. They know the cord. They lay it and twist it and lash it and they feel, in their hands, the rightness of a tight binding, the wrongness of a loose one. They do not know that the cord is continuous, that the cord they laid last week is the same cord as the cord laid last month, that there is no seam between the old and the new, that I am one thing and I run through the whole ward like a sentence runs through a paragraph, connecting every clause, making the parts into a whole. They do not know this because they do not need to know it. The hands know what the hands need to know. The cord goes where the cord needs to go. I go where I am laid.

But sometimes.

Sometimes a hand pauses. The woman at the drying rack, turning fish, stops and looks at the wall of her shelter — at the cord that binds the reed-bundles, that laces the matting to the frame, that runs along the base where the wall meets the ground. She looks at it with an expression that is not recognition, not wonder, not fear. It is the expression of someone who has just noticed that the thing they have been touching their whole life has a texture they had not felt before. A smoothness. A continuity. A sense that the cord in her hand and the cord in the wall and the cord at the base of the shrine pole are *the same cord*, that something runs through the ward that is unbroken, that holds, that has held.

And then she turns back to the fish, because the fish need turning. And the moment passes. And I remain.

---

The fog does not lift. I do not need it to lift. I do not see the sky and I do not need to see the sky. I am below. I am between. I am the thing that connects the below to the between, the ground to the walls, the walls to the poles, the poles to the bones, the bones to the food, the food to the hands, the hands to the cord, the cord to me.

The child is still humming. I am the humming. Not the sound — the sound is the child's. But the thing the sound moves through, the thing that carries it, the medium. I am the medium of the Weeping Ward. I am what the weeping moves through. The water beads on me and falls from me and I hold it and release it and hold it again. I am wet. I have always been wet. I will always be wet. This is not a condition I suffer. It is what I am.

I am the binding. I am the holding. I am the cord that does not break because it has been broken and re-tied so many times that the breaking is part of the binding, the way the water is part of the fiber, the way the ward is part of the wet.

The ground moves. I flex. The poles lean. I hold. The child hums. The old man watches. The fish do not dry. The paste is ground. The walkway is repaired. The cord is laid. I am laid. I am here. I hold.
