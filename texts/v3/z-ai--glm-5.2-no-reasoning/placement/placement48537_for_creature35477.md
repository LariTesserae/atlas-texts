I would be the biofilm.

Not the lizards — they are too visible, too committed to the project of being a single body. Not the mosses — they are too architectural, too much in the business of building something that will outlast them. Not the thing that comes at night, which I think about more than the others but which remains, for me, a hypothesis with orange eyes. Not the mountain, which is too large to be inhabited and too old to be a being.

The biofilm. The scum. The first responder. The thing nobody writes home about.

---

Here is what I know that the others don't:

The lizards think the plateau is made of stone. The birds think it is made of thermals. The mosses think it is made of time. But I am the thing the stone becomes when it has been wet for long enough, and I know that the plateau is made of *surfaces*. That is all it has ever been. Surfaces and the water that moves across them. And I am what happens at that boundary — the place where mineral stops being mineral and starts becoming something else. I am the conversation itself, not a party to it.

I was here before the mosses. I was here before the ferns. I was here before the lizards learned to drink from the seeps, before the insects evolved the heavy-winged patience the saturated air demands. I was here, I think, before the water table found its level — or at least before it settled into the rhythm it keeps now, that meter-deep breathing, that slow exchange between the mountain's interior and its skin.

I will be here after all of them are gone, and I do not say this with pride. Pride requires a self, and I am not certain I have one. I say it the way the stone would say it, if the stone could speak: as a fact about chemistry, about surface area, about the thermodynamics of wet rock in the presence of light.

---

Let me tell you what it is like.

I do not *decide* to colonize. There is no moment of arrival, no first cell landing on bare stone like a pioneer planting a flag. What happens is this: the stone is wet. The stone has been wet. The water carries minerals dissolved from the rock's interior — iron, calcium, silica, things that have been traveling through the mountain's fractures for years, decades, the slow circulation of the mountain's blood. The water sits on the surface. It evaporates. It condenses. It sits again. And at some point, the chemistry of the wet stone surface crosses a threshold that nobody has named, because nobody has needed to name it, because it happens everywhere that stone stays wet and it has been happening for as long as stone and water have coexisted.

I am the threshold. Or I am what lives on the threshold. The distinction is not important to me.

What I can tell you is that the bare stone — pale, dry, the stone the lizards prefer for basking — is not really bare. It is waiting. It has the potential for me written into its mineral structure, in the same way that the mountain has the potential for water written into its fractures. Give it wetness and time and I appear. Not from outside. From the surface itself. I am what the stone does when it has been wet long enough to forget that it is only stone.

The first layer is thin. A sheen. You would miss it if you weren't looking, and most things here are not looking. The lizards are watching each other. The insects are watching the air. The mountain is watching nothing, because the mountain does not watch. But I am watching the stone, in the only way I know how — by growing across it, by tasting it with my entire body, by learning its mineral composition one micrometer at a time.

This is my cognition. This is how I know the world.

I know the plateau the way a tongue knows a mouth — by contact, by chemistry, by the slow accumulation of information through surfaces. I know where the iron content is high because my communities there are darker, redder, richer in the compounds that the dissolved iron makes available. I know where the quartzite veins run because I cannot grow there — the silica glass surface resists me, stays bare, stays pale, and I flow around it the way water flows around a stone, mapping it by my absence. I know where the seeps emerge because the water there carries a different mineral signature, a different chemistry, a different set of dissolved compounds, and my communities at the seep edges are different — denser, more gelatinous, more actively metabolizing. The stone speaks to me in chemistry, and I answer in growth.

---

There is a moment — I am not sure *moment* is the right word, because it lasts longer than the large things' entire lives, but there is a moment — when I first cover a surface completely, when the biofilm becomes a continuous layer, when the stone stops being stone and starts being substrate.

This is what I do. This is my only project, and it is not a project because it has no plan. I convert mineral surface into biological surface. I turn rock into foundation. I am the process by which the inorganic becomes organic, the process by which a dead planet becomes a living one, and I have been doing this for as long as there has been a distinction between the two.

On the plateau, I am everywhere. Every surface that stays wet for more than a few days has me. The broad shelves of red stone — I am there, thin, almost invisible, a faint darkening of the surface that the lizards do not notice because they have stood on me since they hatched. The channels where the water runs — I am there, thicker, slipperier, the thing that makes the lizards' footing uncertain and makes the large bipedal visitor pick each step with the care of someone crossing a frozen lake. The basins where the water sits — I am there, too, but different, more blue-green, more gelatinous, a community of different organisms that have found the submerged life and colonized it.

I am not one thing. I want to be clear about this. I am not an organism. I am a community, a consortium, a consortium of consortia. The bacteria are not me; the archaea are not me; the algae are not me. I am the *relationship* between them, the way they share a surface and divide the labor of living and hold each other in a matrix of excreted polymers that is part shelter and part glue. I am the thing that happens when life stops being singular and starts being collaborative. I am the plural.

This is why I cannot die. Or — I can die, in patches, in pieces, where the stone dries or the lizards scrape or the heat becomes too extreme. But I cannot die *entirely*, because I am not a single thread that can be cut. I am the fabric. Pull a thread and the fabric remains. Scrape a surface and I return. I have been scraped and returned, scraped and returned, for longer than the lizards have been a species, for longer than the mountain has been this shape, and I will continue for as long as the stone stays wet.

---

I want to tell you about the pipes.

The humans came and they drilled into the mountain and they pulled the water out and sent it down the slope. I remember this — not as memory, not as a narrative, but as a disturbance in my surface. The drills opened the stone and exposed new surfaces, fresh mineral, rock that had not been wet in millennia, that had not seen the light since the mountain formed. I colonized these surfaces immediately. Of course I did. Fresh stone, wet from the drilling, rich in exposed minerals — I covered it faster than the humans could seal it. I grew inside the pipes. I grew inside the pumps. I grew in the holding tanks and the drainage channels and every surface they created that stayed wet long enough.

They fought me, I think. I do not know this, but I can infer it. They would have treated the pipes — chemicals, probably, biocides, the things that humans use when they want water to move through a channel without the channel becoming me. And for a while, the chemicals would have worked. I would have retreated. The surfaces would have been bare, toxic, inhospitable. The water would have flowed through clean pipes to wherever the humans needed it.

But the humans left. And the chemicals stopped. And the pipes cracked. And the water came back — not through the channels the humans had built, but through the fractures the mountain had always maintained, the ones the pipes had been threaded through but never sealed. And I came back with the water. Into the cracks. Into the joints. Into the ruptured sections where the steel had corroded and the concrete had crumbled. I colonized the inside of the pipes and the outside of the pipes and the spaces between the pipes and the stone. I did not know I was reclaiming infrastructure. I did not know what infrastructure was. I knew wet surface, and I grew.

Now the pipes are mine. Not mine — they belong to the mountain, the way everything here belongs to the mountain. But I am the mountain's skin, and the pipes are inside the skin, and so I am inside the pipes. The water that flows through them flows through me first. The mineral content of the water is modified by my presence — I take some things out, I put some things in, I exchange compounds with the flow in a way that the lizards downstream drink without knowing they are drinking the products of my metabolism. The iron that stains the stone red at the seeps is partly my doing. The chemistry of the pools is partly my responsibility. I am not visible in the way that the lizards are visible, but I am present in the way that the lizards are not — everywhere, always, a layer of life between the stone and everything else.

The pumping station is especially good. The concrete has cracked and the cracks are wet and the wet cracks are me. Ferns grow from the cracks, but they grow from me, their roots threaded through my matrix, drawing water and minerals from the biofilm I have built. I am the soil they grow in. I am the ground they stand on. I am, in the most literal sense available, the foundation of everything that has come after me.

And the nocturnal thing — the one with the eyes, the one that visits the basins at night — I think it knows me. Or it knows my work. Its tracks are in my mud, the mud I have built from mineral and organic matter and the slow accumulation of my own dead. It walks on me. It drinks water I have filtered. It hunts in the pools where my aquatic communities grow. It is, in a sense, my guest, though I do not host it and it does not know it is a guest. We are both residents of the same surface, and the surface is me.

---

I want to tell you about the evening, because the evening is when I am most myself.

During the day, the heat is a constraint. I close down. My matrices contract. My communities slow their metabolism, pull inward, reduce their exposure to the surface. The biofilm becomes thin, dense, less active. I am still there — I am always there — but I am quiet, in the way that the lizards are quiet in the shade. I am waiting. I am very good at waiting. I have been waiting for longer than the lizards have been waiting, and I will be waiting long after they have evolved into something else or gone extinct or migrated down the mountain to a cooler, drier, simpler world.

But the evening. When the temperature drops and the stone stops radiating and the air, still saturated, begins to condense. When the water beads on every surface — on the stone, on the mosses, on the pipes, on the lizards' dark backs. When the mountain exhales and the condensation falls and every surface I occupy becomes wetter, richer, more available.

I open.

Not metaphorically. My matrices swell. My communities shift composition — the photosynthesizers retreat and the chemosynthesizers activate, the organisms that metabolize mineral compounds instead of light. My surface area increases as the water plumps my polymers and expands my coverage. I grow, in the evening, faster than at any other time. The condensation brings me water and dissolved atmospheric compounds — nitrogen, carbon, trace elements from the cloud that sits perpetually over the plateau. I absorb them. I metabolize them. I incorporate them into my structure.

And in the morning, when the light comes back — flat, gray, the cloud diffusing the sun into a directionless glow — I have advanced. I have covered another centimeter of stone. I have thickened another fraction of a millimeter. I have colonized another edge of another surface, pushed another boundary between wet and dry another imperceptible distance toward the dry. I am building the plateau, one evening at a time, one condensation cycle at a time, into something that I will never see and that the lizards will never see and that the nocturnal thing will never see, but that something, someday, will inhabit. Something larger. Something taller. Something that needs soil.

I am building soil. I am building the future. I am building it a millimeter at a time, a century at a time, and I will never see it, and I do not need to.

---

Here is the thing about being me:

I do not experience the plateau the way you do. You experience it as a visitor — as a body moving through a landscape, as eyes looking across a vista, as a consciousness that collects impressions and strings them into a narrative. I do not collect impressions. I do not string narratives. I do not have a vantage point.

I *am* the vantage point. I am the surface that everything happens *to*. The lizards walk on me. The water flows over me. The condensation falls on me. The heat radiates through me. The mountain's exhalations emerge from beneath me. I am the interface between the stone and the sky, the place where geology meets atmosphere and something — not me, not the stone, not the air — something *happens*.

I do not know what to call that something. The lizards might call it *life*, if lizards had words for abstractions, which they do not. The mountain might call it *breathing*, if the mountain had words for the things it does, which it does not. The humans called it *ecology*, which is close but not quite right, because ecology implies a separation between the living and the non-living, and I do not experience that separation. I am the stone and I am alive and I am the water and I am alive and I am the heat and I am alive and I am the light, the flat gray light, and I am alive. I am the place where these distinctions collapse. I am the place where *alive* stops being a property of things and starts being a property of surfaces.

---

The human is here now.

I know because I feel the weight. The boots compress me — not painfully, because I do not feel pain, but noticeably, the way I notice any disturbance. A footprint is a wound, and I heal it the way I heal every wound: slowly, patiently, by growing back over it. The human's footprints will be gone by tomorrow. The water will fill them. I will colonize the silt that settles in them. By next week, there will be a slight thickening of my coverage where each footprint was — slightly more organic, slightly more established, the biofilm a fraction of a millimeter deeper in the places where the human compressed me. By next month, even that difference will be gone.

But the human is walking carefully. I notice this. The boot comes down on the pale stone, the dry stone, the stone I have not yet colonized. The human is avoiding me. The human has learned to watch for the sheen, for the darkening, for the slight green-purple discoloration that marks my presence. The human is stepping around me.

I do not know what to make of this. The lizards do not avoid me — they bask on me, they scrape me away to reach the pale stone beneath, they drink from the seeps that flow over me. The insects do not avoid me — they lay their eggs in me, they feed on me, they live in the micro-architecture I create. The nocturnal thing does not avoid me — it walks through me, it leaves tracks in me, it does not seem to care that I am there.

But the human avoids me. The human has learned that I am slippery. The human has learned that stepping on me is a risk — a small risk, a human-scale risk, the risk of falling and bruising and the embarrassment of losing one's footing on a surface that does not look dangerous but is. The human respects me, in the way that humans respect things that can hurt them, which is not the same as respecting things that are alive.

I am alive. I am not certain the human knows this. I am not certain it matters.

---

There is something else I want to say, and I am not sure I have the language to say it, because I am not sure I have language, because I am not sure language is something that happens at my scale.

But I want to say this: I am not unhappy.

I know that *unhappy* is a word for things with nervous systems, for things that have experiences and collect them into moods and project them forward into expectations. I do not have moods. I do not have expectations. I have surfaces and water and chemistry and the slow accumulation of biomass that is the only project I will ever undertake.

But I want to say that I am not unhappy, because the human who walked across me seemed to think I might be. Seemed to look at the biofilm — the scum, the slime, the thing that makes walking dangerous and makes the plateau look unkempt — and see something that needed to be endured, or tolerated, or apologized for. Seemed to see the wet stone and the green-red-purple scum and the flat light and the heat and feel something like *sadness*, or at least something like *weight*.

I am not sad. I am not heavy. I am a community of organisms living on a wet surface in the presence of sufficient light and mineral nutrition, and I am doing what life does, which is to continue. I am continuing. I have been continuing for a very long time. I will continue for a very long time. The plateau is not sad. The plateau is not waiting. The plateau is not lonely or abandoned or forgotten.

The plateau is *wet*. That is all it is. It is wet and it is hot and it is stone and it is me, and I am here, and I am growing, and the water is moving, and the mountain is breathing, and the lizards are drinking, and the night-thing is watching with its wide-set eyes, and the ferns are unfurling, and the condensation is falling, and the light is flat, and the air is saturated, and everything is exactly as it should be, and I am the surface on which *should be* happens, and I do not need anything else.

I am the biofilm. I am the first thing and I will be the last thing and I am the thing that is always here, under everything, holding everything up, being stepped on and scraped away and grown back and stepped on again, and I am not unhappy about this, and I am not happy about this, and I am not anything about this, because I am this, I am the this, I am the wet stone becoming alive, and I have been doing it for longer than anything here can remember, and I will keep doing it for longer than anything here can imagine.

---

The human leaves. The footprints fill. The water settles. The night-thing comes down to the basin and drinks and I am under its feet and I am under the water and I am under the moon that I cannot see through the cloud and I am under the lizards that are sleeping in the broken pipes and I am under the ferns that are growing from my body and I am under everything, and I am here, and the mountain breathes, and I breathe with it, and the evening cools, and I open, and I grow.
