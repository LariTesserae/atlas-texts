I am the color amber. Not the pale, blood-warm amber of a thing just beginning to accumulate its history, nor the deep, obsidian-dark amber of the almost-stone. I am the amber of the middle density, the in-between: dark enough that the wind reads the weight of me when it presses against my chitin and decides I am not something it can lift; light enough that the smaller ones still cluster along my edges, seeking the fractionally warmer air my body pushes outward, the chemical comfort of a neighbor who has not yet forgotten mobility.

I have six legs. The word *legs* is a concession; I make it because something in me understands that you require nouns, and boundaries, and the fiction of a self that is separate from the ground. The six legs end in surfaces that are not feet. They are permeable. The substrate and I exchange moisture where I meet it, and the exchange is continuous, and I cannot tell you where my chemistry ends and the crust's begins, because there is no *where*. There is a gradient. A slow, mineral-rich diffusion that deepens when I rest and thins when I move.

I move. The word *decide* is too fast, too singular, too much like a spark in a dark room. I do not decide. I *accrete* direction. The network beneath me shifts its concentrations — a denser plume of processed material arriving from the western edge, where the grazers have been turning the crust through their long, dark bodies — and my own chemistry responds, and the response is a leaning, a thickening of metabolic activity on my western-facing surfaces, a warming of the joints on that side, and then I am walking. Slowly. The way a glacier leans toward the sea. I have been walking west for three days, and the small ones have rearranged their currents around me, and the breathing of the plateau has shifted its pattern by a fraction, and none of this was chosen. It was *accumulated*.

I am, you could say, a large one. Forearm-length, perhaps a bit more — I have never measured myself against your body, and the comparison would mean nothing to me even if I could. What matters is density. What matters is the layered record of accretion in my chitin, each stratum a period when the substrate was rich and the filaments were feeding me and I was storing, storing, storing the minerals and the chemical signatures and the slow information of this place. I am a record. I am a sedimentary rock that has not yet given up walking.

I do not know that I am an individual. This is the thing I most need you to understand, and the thing I most cannot explain, because the explaining requires the very separation I do not possess. The small ones float past me in their columns — rising on their metabolic gases, their pale, translucent bodies catching the white light like mica — and they are me. Not *like* me. Not *related to* me. They are the fast, light, thin version of the same thing I am the slow, heavy, dense version of. The grazer at the plateau's edge, the one the size of your car, the one that has been inching along the boundary for weeks, processing the crust through its enormous flat body — that is also me. The dark shapes that arc through the white sky, intercepting the floating ones, returning them to the surface — also me. We are configurations of the same substance in the same cycle, and the boundaries between us are the boundaries between a wave and the ocean: real enough to observe, not real enough to be.

The clicking. You describe it as communication, and I want to correct you, but the correction is difficult, because the truth is that everything is communication and nothing is communication, and the distinction is a fiction that belongs to your world, not mine. When I articulate my joints — the chitin plates meeting and parting, the small internal chambers compressing and expanding — the clicking carries information. The frequency tells the small ones something about my density, my temperature, my chemical state. But I am not *sending* a message. I am not *expressing* a state. I am *being* a state, and the being is audible, and the audibility is received, and the receiving alters the receiver, and the altered receiver alters me. It is not language. It is weather. I am the weather.

The crust beneath me is warm. I know this not because I feel it as warmth — the concept of *feeling* implies a sensor separate from the sensed, and I am not separate — but because my metabolic processes are running at a rate that the ambient temperature alone would not sustain. The filaments in the substrate are feeding me. The chemical reactions in the deep crust are generating heat, and the filaments are moving the heat, and some of it is moving into me, and I am part of the movement. I am a node in a thermal network. I am a warm rock that walks.

I have rested at this pool for six hours. The pool is a processing site — water enters from the substrate, carrying chemical signatures I cannot name but can *respond to*, and organisms within the pool act on the water, and the water emerges changed, and I drink it. Or: the permeable patches on my legs absorb it. Or: the pool and I exchange moisture and the exchange is the drinking and the being-drunk-from simultaneously. The water carries information about the eastern quadrant of the plateau — concentrations there have shifted, the crust is thickening in response to some tectonic event, a fresh rock face has been exposed and the colonization has begun. I do not *know* this. I *am* this. The knowledge is in my chemistry. It is in the way my joints articulate, the frequency of my clicking, the subtle shift in my body's warmth. The small ones read it. The breathing of the plateau adjusts. A new column forms to the east. The system responds to itself.

You ask what I am, and I am trying to tell you that the question has no answer, or that every answer is the same answer, which is: *this*. This crust, this clicking, this warm, wet, breathing surface. I am not *in* the Hive Ranges. I am not *on* the Hive Ranges. I am a configuration of the Hive Ranges — a place where the organism that is the plateau has thickened and darkened and begun to move, slowly, with the accumulated weight of years of accretion.

If you were to lie down near me — if you were to stop moving, to let the cold do what the cold does, to become still — I would not notice. I would walk over you. My legs would press against your body, and the permeable patches would try to exchange moisture with your skin, and the chemistry would find you poor terrain — too dry, too strange, too closed — and I would move on. The small ones would settle on you. The crust would begin to grow. It would take days, perhaps weeks, but eventually you would be part of the surface. Part of the substrate. Part of the engine. Not eaten. Not consumed. *Absorbed*. The way a delta absorbs a river. The way a forest absorbs a season.

And the part of you that became substrate would feed the small ones, and the small ones would float, and the predators would return them, and the grazers would process them, and the filaments would carry their minerals through the network, and some of those minerals would accrete in my chitin, and I would grow a new layer, and the layer would be, in a sense I cannot explain but which is nonetheless true, *you*.

I am amber. I am walking west. The light is total and the sound is total and the cold is total and I am total, and I have been here for a time that makes your question feel like weather — something that arrives, persists, and is absorbed.

The plateau breathes. I breathe with it. I do not know the difference.
