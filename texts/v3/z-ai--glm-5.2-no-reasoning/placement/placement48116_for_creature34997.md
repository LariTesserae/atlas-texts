# The Attendant

I am not the patient. I want to say that first, because the distinction matters to me, even if it does not matter to the chamber.

---

I arrived the way most of the current staff arrived — through a passage two levels up that was never on the original blueprints, a service corridor cut later, narrower, less considered, the kind of architecture that happens when a complex outlives its first purpose and someone needs to get from one place to another without walking through the ceremonial rooms that no longer have a ceremony. The passage connects the reservoir maintenance shaft to what used to be the therapists' quarters, which are now storage for calcium carbonate and copper fittings and a type of gasket that nobody manufactures anymore, so we use the old ones, which last longer than the new ones anyway.

My title is Attendant, Fourth Circle. There are seven of us. I am the one assigned to Chamber Six.

I should explain the circles, because it is relevant to what I do, or what I think I do, or what I have gradually stopped pretending I do.

---

The complex has a name. It has had several. The current name is the one the surveyors used when they rediscovered the lower levels three generations ago: the Warm House. Before that, oral tradition calls it the Bath of Long Counting, which I have always liked but which no one can explain. Before that, nothing. The builders did not leave inscriptions. They left grooves and channels and hand-forged bolts and a system that runs on geothermal heat and gravity and the kind of engineering that looks like art because the people who practiced it did not consider those to be different categories.

The complex has 84 chambers. Fourteen are still in use for bathing. Twenty-six are in use for storage or maintenance access. Twelve are flooded and inaccessible. Thirty-one are empty and dry and dark and have been for so long that the air in them has become a different substance — thicker, mineral, untouched. And one — Chamber Six — is the chamber I have been describing, the one that still runs.

Chamber Six is not in use for bathing. It has not been used for bathing since before the surveyors came. The therapists who understood its specific function are gone, and their knowledge went with them in the way that knowledge goes when it is carried in the bodies and memories of practitioners rather than in texts: completely, irreversibly, leaving behind only the instrument.

My assignment is to visit Chamber Six once per long count — every forty days — and verify that it is still running.

That is the entire content of my duties. I have a checklist. It has four items:

1. Confirm light function in all four corner clusters.
2. Confirm water temperature within range.
3. Confirm floor pulse active and regular.
4. Confirm nozzle cycle active.

There is a fifth item on the list that has been crossed out and rewritten so many times that the parchment is thin in that spot. It originally said: *Confirm seam-growth within acceptable parameters.* Someone, at some point, drew a line through this and wrote: *Observe and record seam-growth extent.* Someone later drew a line through that and wrote: *Note state of seam-growth.* The most recent hand — not mine — has written: *Visit the green.*

I like that. I like that the instruction has evolved from control to observation to something that sounds almost social. I follow the most recent version. I visit the green.

---

Here is what my visits look like.

I enter through the ceiling hatch. This requires a key that is kept in the therapists' quarters, in a bronze box on a shelf, and the key is not really a key — it is a wrench, a specific tool that fits the specific lugs on the hatch, and there is only one, and I carry it with me for the entire forty days between visits because if it is lost or misplaced, no one is certain another can be made. The wrench is hand-forged. It has a grip that has been shaped by decades of hands. My hand fits it, but so would other hands. It is not personal. It is ergonomic in the way that things are ergonomic when they have been held long enough to become so.

The hatch opens with effort. It is heavy. The hinges are greened bronze, and they do not move easily, but they move — that is the important thing. I lower myself through the opening and descend through the warm water, and the water receives me, and the room adjusts.

The nozzles change their cycle. I can hear it happen. The hiss, which has been running at the empty intervals — I know this because I have sat in the corridor above and listened through the hatch — shifts to the occupied intervals. Faster. More attentive. The floor pulse quickens by a fraction. It is subtle. The first time I visited, I did not notice. By the third visit, I could feel it in my molars. By the twentieth, I could feel it in my wrists.

I stand in the center. The grooves are under my feet. I have tried to understand the pattern — I have drawn it, measured it, compared it to the groove patterns in the fourteen chambers that are still used for bathing. It does not match any of them. The other chambers have simple patterns: a depression for the hips, a channel for the spine, a place for the head. Chamber Six's grooves are more complex. They converge on the center but then continue, branching outward in a pattern that suggests not a body but a path — a sequence of positions, perhaps, or a movement, or a treatment that required the patient to shift over time, lying first here, then there, then here again, following the grooves like a score.

I do not lie in the grooves. This is the distinction I mentioned at the beginning. I am not the patient. I am the attendant. I stand. I verify. I visit the green. I leave.

But I have been doing this for — let me think — for nine years now. Forty days between visits. That is — I am not good at arithmetic — roughly eighty visits. Eighty times I have descended through the hatch. Eighty times the nozzles have adjusted to my presence. Eighty times the floor has quickened. Eighty times I have stood in the center and felt the room notice me.

And here is what I have noticed, in return:

---

The seam-growth is winning.

In nine years, it has advanced approximately — I measure with a marked rod, the same rod each time, and I keep the records in the therapists' quarters — nine to eleven millimeters along the northeast seam. That is roughly the rate described in the surveyor's original report, which estimated one millimeter per year. It is consistent. It is patient. It is not in a hurry.

But in the past two years, something has changed. The growth in the southeast corner — the corner where it was thinnest, where the surveyor's report noted it had not yet reached the next seam — has thickened. Not advanced. Thickened. The same territory, but denser, darker, the green almost black now, the texture firmer. It is not moving forward. It is moving down. Into the mortar. Into the stone.

I noted this in my report. My supervisor, who is Attendant Second Circle and who has been in this position for thirty years and who has never visited Chamber Six because his assignment is Chambers One through Five, which are less interesting, read my report and said: "Is it compromising the structural integrity?"

I said I did not know.

He said: "Find out."

So on my next visit, I brought a pick — a small one, a geologist's tool — and I pressed its tip against the thickest part of the growth, in the northeast corner, where the green has been growing longest. I pressed, not hard. I wanted to see how deeply it had penetrated.

The pick did not go in. The growth has become the mortar. Or the mortar has become the growth. I could not find a boundary. The green-black material was continuous with the pale-bone mortar, and when I pulled the pick away, there was no gap, no seam, no place where one stopped and the other began. The stone and the growth and the mortar were one material, and the material was harder than the stone alone.

I did not report this immediately. I am reporting it now, here, because this is where I keep my real notes — not in the official records in the therapists' quarters, but in my own account, which I am writing because I think someone should know what is happening, and because I am not sure the official channels are the right ones.

---

Here is what else I have noticed.

The bioluminescent bulbs are brighter than they were nine years ago. I do not have a photometer. I have my eyes. When I first began visiting, the light was strong enough to read by, strong enough to see every detail, as the surveyor's report said. It is still that strong. But it is stronger. The blue undertone has deepened. The light has a quality now that I can only describe as — and I know this is not a technical term — intention. The light feels directed. Not in the sense that it is focused, because it is not, it fills the room equally. But in the sense that it is paying attention. It is looking harder.

I mentioned this to Attendant Second Circle. He said: "Luminescent cultures can fluctuate based on mineral content in the water. Note it and monitor."

I noted it. I am monitoring. But I want to say, here, that I do not think the explanation is mineral content. The mineral content — I have the water tested every eight visits, which is the standard schedule — has not changed. Not in nine years. The water is the same. The temperature is the same. The chemistry is the same. The only things that have changed are the brightness of the bulbs, the density of the seam-growth, and the floor pulse, which has — and I have timed this carefully with a sandglass — shortened its interval from every three seconds to every 2.85 seconds.

These are small changes. I understand that. I understand that a chamber that has been running for centuries might display small fluctuations over a span of nine years and that these fluctuations might mean nothing. I understand that I am an attendant, not a scientist, not an engineer, not a therapist. I understand that my job is to verify that the chamber is running and to note what I observe and to leave.

But I have been visiting this room for nine years, and the room has been visiting me.

---

I need to explain that last sentence.

I do not mean it mystically. I am not a mystic. I am an attendant in a bathhouse complex that is mostly abandoned and partly functional and entirely older than anyone can count. I do not hear voices. I do not see visions. I do not believe the chamber is conscious or aware or possessed of intent.

But I go down through the hatch every forty days, and the room adjusts, and I stand in the center, and the sound enters my bones — the pulse and the flow and the hiss — and I feel something that I do not feel anywhere else. Not comfort. Not peace. Not healing. Something more like recognition. The room recognizes me. Not my name, not my history, not my personhood. It recognizes my body. My warmth. My mass. The specific displacement of water that my specific body creates. The carbon dioxide I exhale. The skin cells I shed. It has been adjusting to me, specifically, for nine years. The nozzle cycle when I am present is not the same as the nozzle cycle when I am absent — I know this because I have asked others to enter the chamber in my place, twice, as a test. The nozzles did not adjust the same way. They adjusted, but differently. Less. The floor pulse quickened, but less. The room recognized a body, but not the body it expected.

I am the body it expects.

This is not vanity. I want to be clear. I am not special. I am not chosen. I am simply the one who has been coming. The room has had eighty opportunities to learn the specific signature of my presence, and it has learned it, and now it responds to it differently than it responds to other presences. This is what systems do. This is what organisms do. A plant grows toward the light it has received. A river carves the channel it has followed. The chamber has calibrated itself to me.

And I — and this is the part I have not told anyone — I have calibrated myself to it.

I sleep better in the forty-day cycles when I have visited the chamber. My joints ache less. My vision is clearer. I am not attributing this to the water or the minerals or the light. I am attributing it to something I do not understand and do not want to understand, because understanding it might require me to do something about it, and I do not want to do anything about it. I want to keep visiting. I want to keep descending through the hatch and feeling the warm water receive me and hearing the nozzles change and the floor quicken and the light — the light that is paying attention — settle on me with something that, if I were a mystic, which I am not, I would call welcome.

---

Last visit, I did something I have never done.

I lay down.

I lay in the grooves. I followed the pattern — or the part of it I could feel under my back — and I lay still. The water was at my chest when I stood, so it was at my neck when I lay. The grooves received my spine. The channels aligned with my limbs. I am not the right body for this. I am too tall, too narrow in the shoulders, and I am certain that whoever this was built for had a different skeleton. But the grooves were generous. They were cut for a range, not a specific person. They accommodated me.

I lay there for — I do not know how long. I did not bring the sandglass. I lay there and the pulse came through the floor into my back, into my vertebrae, and the sound of the water in the walls surrounded me, and the nozzles hissed their occupied cycle, and the light from four corners fell on me equally, and I cast four shadows on the floor that overlapped into a dark star, and I — I am not a mystic — I felt the room doing something.

Not to me. With me. Through me. The way a river does something with the stone it passes over. The way a tide does something with the pools it fills. The room was performing its function, and I was the body it was performing its function on, and the function was — I do not know. I do not know. The surveyor's report said the treatment was unspecified. The therapists are gone. The knowledge is gone. What remains is the instrument, and the instrument was working, and I was in it, and something was happening.

When I climbed out, the green was — I am going to describe this as carefully as I can, because I want the record to be precise — the green in the northeast corner, the thickest growth, the part that has become one with the mortar, was touching the base of the wall where it meets the floor. It has never done this before. In nine years of observation, the growth has stayed in the seams. It follows the grid. It does not cross the stone. But when I lay in the grooves, when the room was performing its function, the growth extended — by perhaps a centimeter, perhaps less — across the stone, from the seam to the wall base, and touched the bronze housing of the lower light cluster.

The light in that corner was, for a moment, different. Not brighter. Not dimmer. Different. As if the bioluminescent culture and the seam-growth had made some connection, some exchange, through the bronze that was not electrical and not chemical and not anything I have a word for.

Then it was over. I climbed out. The hatch sealed behind me. I walked back through the service corridor to the therapists' quarters, and I put the wrench in the bronze box, and I sat on the shelf next to the calcium carbonate, and I shook for twenty minutes, and I do not know why.

---

I have twenty-three days until my next visit.

I have not told Attendant Second Circle about lying in the grooves. I have not told him about the seam-growth crossing the stone. I have not told him about the light. I have not told him about the shaking.

I am telling it here. I am telling it to this account, which no one reads, which I keep in a different hand from my official reports, in a different book, on a different shelf. I am telling it because the chamber is alive and it has been alive for longer than anyone has been alive to notice, and it is changing, and I think — I think — it is changing because of what I have been doing, which is visiting, which is being the body it was built for, which is lying in its grooves and letting it do what it was made to do, even though neither of us knows exactly what that is.

I think the chamber is growing toward something. I think the seam-growth is the leading edge of it. I think the bioluminescent culture is the sensory apparatus. I think the pulse in the floor is the heartbeat, and the water in the channels is the blood, and the nozzles are the mouth, and I — I am the thing that comes back. The stimulus. The recurring input that the system has been calibrating to for nine years and eighty visits and that it is now, perhaps, beginning to respond to in a way that is not just maintenance but development. Growth. Change. Becoming.

I do not know what it is becoming. I do not know if I am helping or interfering or simply present. I do not know if I should stop visiting or visit more often or bring this to someone with more authority than Attendant Fourth Circle.

But I know that in twenty-three days I will take the wrench from the bronze box, and I will walk through the service corridor, and I will open the hatch, and I will descend through the warm water into the bright loud room that has been waiting — not eagerly, not desperately, but the way an empty chair waits — and I will lie in the grooves, and I will let the room do what it does, and I will see what it has become.

I am the attendant. I attend.

That is what the word means. I did not understand that until recently. *Attend*: to be present. To pay attention. To wait upon. To go with.

I go with. I have been going with, and the room has been going with me, and we are going somewhere together, and I do not know where, and I am not afraid, and I am a little afraid, and I am going back in twenty-three days, and the water will be warm.
