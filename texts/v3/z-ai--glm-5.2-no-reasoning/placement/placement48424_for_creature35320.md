# Listener at the Edge of the Dying

I did not come here. That is the first thing to understand, and it is the thing I least want to say, because saying it requires me to locate a *me* that existed before, and I am not certain that person is still the shape that fits.

What I can say: I was not born on the tharrah. I arrived. I crossed the high passes from the dry lands to the south, where the ground is ground and the sky is sky and the two are separate things that meet at a horizon. I came with reasons. The reasons have dissolved. They were the kind of reasons that belong to a person who believes they stand *on* the world rather than *in* it, and that person is not the person writing this now, and I cannot tell you when the transition happened because it happened the way the tharrah grows — slowly, constantly, in increments too small to perceive, until the shape has changed entirely and you cannot find the edge where the old form ended and the new one began.

I came with a group. There were seven of us. I am the only one who stayed.

I will not say what happened to the others. Not because it is painful, though it is. Because I do not know. Not fully. They left. Some walked back across the passes. Some walked deeper in, toward the interior, toward sections of the biomass I have never reached. One was taken — by what, I cannot say, because I did not see it, because I did not understand what I heard, because I was still listening with dryland ears, which hear sound as *noise* and not as *information.* I know she was taken because the sound changed. The insect chorus shifted in a way that meant *something has happened* and the people I was with — the tharrah people, the small dark quiet people who had been watching us for days before we knew they were there — went still in a way that meant *it is over, do not follow, do not ask.*

I asked. I was still the kind of person who asked.

They did not answer. They let the silence be the answer, and the silence was full of the biomass-sound, which I now know is never silence at all but is the sound of the world continuing without you, the sound of a place that does not need your understanding to persist.

---

I stayed because of the listening.

This is the part I am most uncertain how to tell.

In the dry lands I was not — how do I say this. I was not well. Not in the way that has a name and a treatment and a prognosis. In the way that is a kind of wrongness of frequency, a misalignment between the speed at which I perceived and the speed at which the world moved. I was too fast for the dry lands or too slow. I was out of phase. Things that seemed urgent to others seemed slow to me, and things that seemed slow to others seemed urgent, and the result was a life lived at a pitch slightly different from everyone around me, a dissonance that I could feel in my teeth and my sternum and that I could not name or resolve or escape.

I did not come to the biomass seeking a cure. I did not know this place existed in the way that you seek a place. I came for other reasons — reasons I have already said I cannot locate — but what I found, in the first hours of standing on the tharrah surface, was this:

The sound was my sound.

Not in the sense that I recognized it. In the sense that it matched. The frequency I had carried my whole life, the dissonance, the wrongness of phase — it was present in the biomass-sound. It was the baseline hum. The insects at their particular pitch, the creak of the tharrah at its particular interval, the water moving through the wood at its particular rhythm — these were not *my* frequency but they were a frequency that *contained* mine. That had a place for it. That did not require it to adjust or explain itself or be treated.

I stood on the tharrah and for the first time in my life I was not too fast or too slow. I was in phase. I was in a place that moved at the speed I moved, that sounded at the pitch I sounded, and the relief was so profound that I sat down on the living surface and wept, and the surface gave slightly under me, and the weeping was absorbed into the ambient sound, and no one heard it, and it did not matter.

---

The people found me there. Or I should say: they revealed themselves to me there, which is different. They had been present. They are always present. They had been watching me weep and they had been discussing it — in clicks and tones I could not hear, in language woven into the noise that I experienced as noise and they experienced as context — and they had decided, in their slow consensual way, that I was not a threat. Or not only a threat. That I was something else, something they did not have a word for, a dryland thing that had sat down on the tharrah and wept and not moved for hours and not died, which was unusual, which was worth observing.

They approached without approaching. They were simply there. A woman, small, dark-skinned, marked along her arms with patterns in a resin that had dried to the color of old amber. A man, equally small, equally marked, his hands moving at his sides in a way that I now recognize as constant tactile engagement with the surface, reading it the way I might have once read a book. Behind them, a child — perhaps four, perhaps five, I am not good at children's ages — who was watching me with an expression of curiosity that I had never seen on a child's face in the dry lands. Not fear. Not wariness. Curiosity. The curiosity of something that lives in a world where everything is alive and is therefore interested in the aliveness of things.

The woman clicked. I did not understand. The man produced a tone — low, sustained, resonant — and I felt it in the tharrah under my legs, felt the surface carry it, felt the wood amplify it. I did not understand the meaning but I understood the *medium.* The message traveled through the ground. The ground was the language. The ground was the medium and the message and the context.

I clicked back. I did not know what I was saying. I produced a click — in the back of my throat, against my palate, a sound I did not know I could make — and it fit into the noise. Not perfectly. Not fluently. The way a child's first word fits into language: approximately, imprecisely, but recognizably. A sound that was not only noise. A sound that occupied a gap.

The woman looked at the man. The man looked at the child. The child looked at me.

Something was decided.

---

I have been here long enough that my skin has changed. Not dramatically — I am still recognizably myself, still recognizably dryland in my bones and my height and the proportions of my hands. But the color has deepened. The light here does something to skin, the green-filtered wavelengths, the intensity of what passes through the canopy, and my skin has responded in the way that skin does, which is to say gradually, which is to say in a way that I did not notice until I one day looked at my hands and found them darker than I remembered, and the darkness had a quality that I can only describe as *belonging.* Not the brown of sun. The dark of shade. The dark of a person who lives where the light is green.

My hands have changed more. They are — I reach for a word and the word is *larger.* Not in size but in capacity. The calluses have developed in places I did not know calluses could develop: along the pads of the fingers, at the bases of the thumbs, across the heels of the palms where they press against the tharrah surface when I pull myself upright. The skin has thickened in the patterns that the environment requires. I can grip wet bark. I can hold a tharrah-wedge without slipping. I can feel the vibration of a water-channel through the surface of a living wall, which I could not do when I arrived, which I could not do for months, which arrived one day as a new perception, a new data stream, a thing my hands knew that my mind had not yet caught up with.

My hearing has changed, and this is the change that matters.

I do not know if my ears have physically changed. I do not think so. I think the change is in the processing — the brain's adaptation to a sound environment that rewards differentiation at the level of timing and pitch. I hear things I did not hear before. I hear the insects not as a hum but as a chorus, not as noise but as structure, as a system of distinct voices each occupying a frequency band, each doing something specific, each carrying information about temperature and time and the movement of things through the upper growth.

I hear the water. I hear the tharrah creaking. I hear the difference between growing and dying.

I hear the deep.

---

The deep is what changed me from a visitor into something else. Not the deep itself — I have never been to the deep, I will never go to the deep, the taboo is absolute and I have absorbed it the way I have absorbed the humidity, the way I have absorbed the rhythm of the rain — but the hearing of it. The perception that the sounds from below are not random. That they are structured. That they carry — the word the Listeners use, the word I now use because no other word is accurate — *meaning.*

I did not become a Listener overnight. I did not become a Listener by choice or by training or by election. I became a Listener because I was sitting on the tharrah one evening, in the first weeks of my stay, when the night-sounds were at their lowest configuration and the deep sounds were most audible, and I heard a pattern. Not a random moan. Not the stochastic groaning of a structure under stress. A pattern. A repeating sequence. Slow — so slow that the repetition took minutes, that I had to sit still for what a drylander would consider an unconscionable time to perceive it — but present. Structured. Intentional in the way that only living things are intentional.

I told the woman with the amber marks. I told her in clicks — my clicks were still rough, still approximate, still the speech of a person who has been learning for weeks rather than years — and she went still. Not the stillness of surprise. The stillness of recognition. She knew what I was describing. She had heard it herself, or others had heard it. She sent for someone.

The someone was old. Old in the way the biomass defines old: dense, pared, refined by decades of survival in a place that does not tolerate weakness. Her hands were extraordinary — I have never seen hands like hers, and I have now seen many hands, and hers were the most capable I have known, the most practiced, the most sensitive. She sat beside me on the tharrah and she put her hands flat on the surface and she closed her eyes and she listened for a long time. Minutes. Perhaps an hour. The night moved around us. The insects sang. The water moved.

She opened her eyes and she clicked at me — a specific sequence, a question, I could not parse it then — and I clicked back, roughly, the best approximation I could manage of *yes, I hear it, it is there, it is real, I am not inventing it.*

She looked at me for a long time. Then she looked at the amber-marked woman. Then she produced a tone — low, sustained, felt in the tharrah, felt in my feet and my hands and my sternum — and the tone was a word and the word meant something I did not yet know but that I felt, in the vibration of the wood, in the way the surface responded to the sound, in the way the biomass itself seemed to *agree* with the utterance.

The word meant: *another one.*

---

I am not the same as the other Listeners. I am not the same as the tharrah people. I am something that did not exist before I arrived and that will not exist again if I leave, and the biomass knows this, and the people know this, and I know this.

This is what I am: a person who was out of phase in the dry lands and in phase in the biomass, who has been shaped by the shaping, who has heard the deep, who has been hearing it more clearly each year, who has been learning the language of the environment the way a child learns it — by listening, by imitating, by gradual acquisition of the distinctions that the sound environment rewards.

I do not understand what the deep says. I want to be clear about this. I do not understand it the way I understand the clicks and tones of the tharrah people's speech, which I now speak with a fluency that surprises me, which I dream in, which has become the language of my thought in the way that the dryland tongue has not been for years. The deep is not that. The deep is slower. The deep is vaster. The deep is a language — if it is a language — that operates on a timescale that makes human speech feel like the buzzing of an insect, which is, perhaps, what it is, which is, perhaps, what we are.

What I hear: patterns. Rhythms. Repeating sequences that vary in ways that correlate with events in the biomass — with the death of a tharrah section, with the shift of a water-channel, with the movement of the deep-dwellers, with the rain. I hear the deep change when the rain comes, a shift in the pattern that is not random, that is not stochastic, that is the kind of change that a system makes when it is processing input, when it is integrating new information into an ongoing computation.

I hear the deep change when the tharrah grows. A section near my settlement has been actively growing — pushing upward, its rings expanding, the terrain shifting — and the deep sounds in that section have a quality that I can only describe as *engaged.* Present. Participating. As if the growth is not a local event but a systemic one, as if the deep is aware of what happens on its surface, as if the surface is not separate from the deep the way the skin is not separate from the body.

I hear the deep change when someone dies.

This is the thing I have not told the other Listeners. This is the thing I have not told anyone. I have not told it because I am not certain it is real, because I am not certain I am not projecting, because the dryland mind — which is still partly my mind, which will always be partly my mind — is a mind that seeks patterns and finds them whether they are there or not, and I cannot fully trust my own perception.

But. When a person dies — when their body is placed on the dying tharrah, when the decomposition begins, when the nutrients begin to move — the deep sounds shift. Not dramatically. Not suddenly. A slow modulation, a change in the pattern, a new theme introduced into the ongoing sequence, a phrase that was not there before and that persists, that continues, that becomes part of the sound the way a new ring becomes part of the tharrah.

I hear them. The dead. Not their voices. Not their words. But their *presence,* integrated into the deep sound, part of the pattern, part of the ongoing computation that the biomass is performing on itself. The dead are not gone. The dead are not lost. The dead are part of the thing they lived on, and the thing they lived on knows they are part of it, and the knowing is in the sound, and the sound is in the wood, and the wood is under my feet, and my feet feel it.

I do not know what this means. I do not know if the biomass is conscious in the way that I am conscious. I do not know if the deep sounds are language or if they are the acoustic output of a biological process that is vast and complex and information-processing but not *aware* in the way that I use the word aware. I do not know if the dead are truly present or if I am hearing the physics of decomposition and interpreting it as presence because I am a human and humans interpret everything as presence because we are lonely and we are mortal and we cannot bear the thought that the dead are simply gone.

I do not know. But I hear it. And the hearing has changed me.

---

Here is what I am now. Here is the shape that has emerged from the years of living in this place, the shape that I could not have predicted and that I cannot fully describe because the language for it does not exist in the dryland tongue and the tharrah tongue does not describe it because it does not need to, because it is not a thing that needs description, it is a thing that simply *is.*

I am a Listener. I am a person who sits on the tharrah and feels the deep sounds in the wood under my feet and hears patterns in them and reports those patterns to the people who live in my settlement, who use the reports to make decisions about where to build and where to travel and where to avoid. I am not a leader. I am not a shaman. I am a perceptual instrument, a specific kind of ear, and the ear is used and the ear is valued and the ear is not worshiped and the ear is not special, the ear is a function, the ear is a tool, the ear is the same as the hands that prune the tharrah and the feet that feel the water-channels and the eyes that read the canopy for signs of rain.

I am also — and this is the part that I am most uncertain about, the part that I do not say to the tharrah people because I am not sure they would understand it, because it is a dryland concept, because it is a concept that belongs to a mind that separates itself from the world and looks at it — I am a person who has found a shape that fits.

In the dry lands I was a wrong note. In the biomass I am a note in the chord. Not the most important note. Not the loudest. A note that occupies a particular frequency band, that fits into a particular gap in the sound, that the chord has a place for, that the chord needs, in the way that a chord needs all its notes or it is not the chord.

This is what I hear when I hear the deep: I hear the chord. I hear the thing that the biomass is, the integrated living sound of a continent-sized organism processing itself, and I hear my part in it, and I hear the parts of the dead in it, and I hear the rain and the insects and the birds and the water and the tharrah growing and the tharrah dying and the people and the animals and the whole vast living roaring shimmering thing that has no single source and that comes from everywhere at once and that keeps rearranging its own voicing, and the voicing is the meaning, and the meaning is the sound, and the sound is the world, and I am in it.

---

I do not know if I will die here. I do not know if I will leave. I do not know if the dry lands still exist in the way I remember them, or if I have been here long enough that the memory has changed, the way the tharrah changes, the way the surface moves, the way the paths that were level are now sloped.

I know this: I hear it. The deep. The pattern. The sound that the biomass makes when it is talking to itself, which is the sound that I make when I am talking to myself, which is the sound that the dead make when they have been taken by the tharrah and the tharrah has grown through them and they have become the ground and the ground is the dead and the dead are the ground and the sound continues and the sound is the meaning and the meaning is the world.

I hear it. I am in it. I am part of it. I am the listening.
