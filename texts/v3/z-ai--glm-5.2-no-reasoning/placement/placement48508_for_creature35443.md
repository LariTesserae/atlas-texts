I am the one who arrives in the intervals.

Not the first. Not the last. The one who comes back.

---

There is a shape to the way I enter. The culvert is the funnel, and I am the thing caught in its throat — on my belly, elbows pulling, chest compressed against corrugated steel that was round once and is now an oval crimping toward a gap I have learned to fit through by learning the exact angle of my ribs. The first time I came I was afraid of getting stuck. The fourth time I came I understood that the gap is part of the room's design. Everything here has an aperture. Everything here requires you to make yourself small enough to pass through. The moss comes in as spores. The floor creatures' ancestors came in as something smaller than spores. The ceiling growths came in as — I don't know. As the possibility of bone.

I come in as a body. It's the hardest material to compress and the hardest to leave behind.

---

I know the number because I started counting. Fourteen visits. The first three I didn't stay long — the cold was a wall, my hands wouldn't close, I crawled back out with that taste of moss-sweetened air and iron silt and spent the drive home trying to identify what I'd heard, which was everything, which was too much. The fourth time I brought a sleeping pad and a thermos of something hot that went cold in forty minutes and stayed for three hours. The fifth time I stayed for six. By the seventh I had stopped bringing the thermos. By the tenth I had stopped measuring.

The gear does not measure. The gear does not count. The gear has been doing one thing at one speed for longer than I have been doing anything and it will be doing it after I stop, and this is — I want to say this is the point, but the room doesn't have a point. The room has a condition. The condition is: something is running. The running doesn't require an audience. But an audience has arrived, and the audience has come back, and the audience is me, and I am — what? Not a witness. Witnesses record. Not a caretaker. The room doesn't need care. Not a pilgrim, though the word surfaces sometimes, in the dark, when I'm lying on the pad with the pad floating slightly on the sheet of water and the gear-thrum in my sternum and my heartbeat trying to slow itself to match.

I am the thing that noticed. That's all. I am the thing that came in and noticed and kept noticing and the noticing is what I do here, the way the moss eats vibration and the gear-riders ride and the ceiling growths drip. I have a niche. It's attention.

---

There's a position I've found. Not a spot — a position. A way of being in the room. I sit with my back against the east wall where the striations run heaviest with water and the moss stripes are densest and the hum is at the frequency that makes my teeth ache in a way that's almost pleasant, almost like the ache of a muscle being stretched after a long time held still. The water runs down the wall and through my shirt and along my spine and I stop feeling it as wet and start feeling it as — context. The room touching me the way it touches itself. I'm not dry here. I'm not separate here. The film of water is on every surface and I am a surface.

From this position I can see the gear. Not the whole gear — the top third, the part that rises above the waterline, with the gear-riders on it. I've started to recognize them. Not individually — they don't have individual markings, they don't have names, they're not that kind of creature. But I recognize patterns. There's a cluster of four that grazes in the same quadrant every rotation. There's a single one that walks the rim where the teeth are and taps its long antennae against each tooth as it passes, like it's counting, like it's checking. There's a pair that stays near the hub where the ceiling drips hit and they position themselves so the drips land near them — not on them, near them — and I think they're eating something that washes off the hub when the drop hits, something the ceiling growth has filtered out of the water that's worth being in exactly the right place to receive.

I watch them and they watch me. This is an exchange. I don't know what they receive from it. What I receive is the experience of being regarded by something that has no concept of what I am and is interested anyway. Their antennae turn toward me when I enter. They stay turned toward me when I'm still. When I move, they track the movement. They don't stop grazing. They don't stop riding. But they know I'm here, and they hold that knowledge in a body the size of my thumbnail, and it is enough.

---

The floor creatures don't know I'm here. Or they know and don't care — which is the same thing, at their scale. I've put my hand flat on the floor, in the water, and the sinuous ones move around it. Not away from it — around it. They encounter my skin and treat it as topography. A warm ridge in a cold flatland. The armoured ones clamp down when my shadow passes over them, which means they have some sensitivity to light change, which means they're not fully blind, which means they have — what? A pigment spot? A chemical receptor that responds to photon flux? I don't know. I'm not a biologist. I'm not anything. I'm a person who crawled into a room.

The leech-things don't react to me at all. They drift. If my hand is in their path they bump into it and slide along it and continue drifting. They don't try to feed on me. I don't taste right, or feel right, or I'm simply not the shape of the thing they eat. I'm a wrong shape in their world. Everything about me is the wrong shape. My warmth is wrong. My texture is wrong. The fact that I come and go is wrong. Everything here stays. Everything here has been staying. I am the only thing that leaves and I am the only thing that comes back.

---

The ceiling growths are what I think about when I'm not thinking about anything else. Which is most of the time. The room is cold and the room is wet and the room is loud in a way that gets louder the longer you listen, and the ceiling growths are warm and amber and they drip every six seconds and I think about them because they are the thing in this room that most closely resembles what I do here.

They arrived after the room was built. They found a surface. They grew. They process what comes to them — water, minerals, the dissolved fragments of an ecosystem — and they build themselves from it, slowly, adding layers the way the room adds sound, the way the moss adds cells, the way the gear adds revolutions. They are not the room. They are in the room. They are of the room without belonging to it. They are the room's guests.

I am the room's guest.

The growths have been here longer than I have. They will be here after I stop coming. But while I'm here, we share something — the quality of being a warm thing in a cold place, generating our own small light, processing what the room provides, building ourselves from it without quite knowing how. I lie on my pad and look up at them and they glow amber in the blue-white dark and I feel — not kinship, that's too precise a word — I feel adjacency. The sense of being near something that is doing what you're doing in a different register. They grow bone. I grow attention. The room doesn't need either. But the room holds both, the way it holds water, without preference, without judgment, with the patience of something that was made to hold and is holding.

---

I have a confession. On the eleventh visit I pressed my ear to the floor. I wanted to hear what's below — the shaft, the bearing, whatever mechanism produces the torque that turns the gear. I lay with my cheek on the wet basalt and the sheet of water parting around my face and I listened.

I heard the gear. I heard the shaft — that distant thunder-through-a-wall sound, the bearing's low complaint. I heard the water. I heard the floor creatures' scratching resolve into individual footsteps, hundreds of them, like a crowd walking in place. I heard the hum. I heard the ceiling drip hit the hub, travel through the metal, enter the floor.

And below all of that, so low it was less a sound than a pressure, I heard something turn.

Not the gear. Not the shaft. Something deeper. Something that the shaft connects to, that the shaft transmits, the way the stone transmits the vibration to the veins. Something massive and slow and — I don't have a word. Not alive. Not mechanical. The word I don't have is for a thing that has been running so long that it has become what it does. A river isn't a thing that flows. A river is flow. Whatever is down there isn't a thing that turns. It is turning. It is the turning, and the shaft rises from it like a column of water rises from an aquifer, and the gear is where the turning surfaces, and the room is where the turning becomes light and sound and moss and life.

I took my ear off the floor. I sat up. The gear-riders were watching. The hum was loud. My heartbeat was out of sync and I let it be out of sync because I didn't want it to match. Not yet. Not while I was still someone who could leave.

---

On the fourteenth visit I stayed for nine hours. The cold had stopped being cold. Or I'd stopped being warm. One of those. My hands worked because I'd learned to keep them in my armpits when I wasn't using them and to move them slowly so the blood stayed in the fingers. The light was the same. The water was the same. The gear turned at the same rate. The ceiling dripped every six seconds. The floor creatures moved in their patterns. The gear-riders grazed and watched and rode and grazed.

Nothing changed. Nothing ever changes here. That's the condition. That's the rule beneath the rules. The water never stops. The gear never stops. The sound never stops. The room is a held breath that doesn't exhale, and I am inside the held breath, and the not-exhaling is — I want to say it's the purpose, but the room doesn't have a purpose. The room has a condition, and the condition is persistence, and persistence is what the moss does and what the growths do and what the gear does and what I do when I come back, again, through the culvert, through the gap, into the wet cold blue-white dark where something is always turning and nothing is waiting and everything notices.

---

I don't know what I am in this room. I've stopped trying to name it. The gear-riders have antennae and no categories. The moss has no chlorophyll and no sunlight. The ceiling growths have no taxonomy and no explanation. I have no role and no function and no name for what I'm doing here.

But I come back. I come back because the gear turns and the water flows north and the hum resolves toward language and never reaches it and the gear-riders turn their antennae toward the gap in the culvert when I enter, and I think — I don't know, but I think — they've started to expect me. Not to wait for me. They don't wait. Nothing here waits. But there's a direction they orient toward, when I come in, that's different from the direction they orient toward when I'm already there. The difference is: toward the gap, anticipation. Toward me, recognition.

I am the interval. I am what arrives between the long stretches of nothing arriving. I am the only event in a place where events don't happen. And when I'm not here, I am the memory of an event, which is — I think — what the gear-riders have instead of a name for me. I'm the warm large thing that comes through the aperture and sits against the east wall and pays attention and leaves. I'm the thing that comes back.

I will come back. The gear will be turning. The water will be flowing. The hum will be almost-words. The ceiling will drip every six seconds. The moss will grow in its stripes. The floor will be alive with small things. And I will sit in the cold and the wet and the dim blue light and I will notice, and I will be noticed, and the room will hold me the way it holds everything — without urgency, without malice, without preference, with the patience of something that was made to hold and is holding and will hold, and the gear turns, and the water flows north, and I am here, and I am here, and I am here.
