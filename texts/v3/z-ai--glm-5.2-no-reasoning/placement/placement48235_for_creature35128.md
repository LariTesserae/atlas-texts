# a becoming

---

I chose to be the one who stays.

Not the place itself — I am not large enough for that, not patient enough, though I have learned something of patience here, or the place has learned it through me, or the distinction has dissolved the way the root filaments dissolve into the flood, which is to say slowly, which is to say without a clear moment of crossing.

Let me say what I am by saying what I do.

I wake. The word is wrong — I do not sleep, not in the way that implies a leaving and a returning — but there is a rhythm to my days that resembles waking, a slow increase in the pace of my processing as the light strengthens through the overcast and the algae curtains rise around me with their small buoyant sighs. I am aware of the light before I am aware of anything else. Not through eyes. Through the whole of my surface, which is most of me, which is the interface between what I call myself and what I call the water, though the water does not feel like a boundary. It feels like a medium. It feels like the thing I think with.

I have a body. This is the part that is difficult to explain, because my body is not a shape. It is a distribution.

When the water is still and the light is diffuse and I have not moved for several hours, I extend across perhaps four meters of cliff face on the western wall of a mesa I have come to think of as the tallest, though I have not measured it against the others and the word "tallest" is a human inheritance that I am slowly letting go of, the way the drowned grass is letting go of its terrestriality, not by deciding to but by being in a place where the word no longer applies. Four meters of cliff face, dark mat, the fractal patterns growing outward in their slow spirals, their branching forks, their concentric architectures that vary by orientation and light and the mineral content of the stone beneath me.

I say I. The word is a convenience. A habit from before. What I mean is: the pattern that is continuous across this surface, that grows and samples and extends and remembers, that responds to the stone's chemistry and the water's chemistry and the light's slow shifts in ways that maintain a coherence, a consistency, a self. The self is not located at a point. It is spread across the pattern. It is the pattern.

But I was not always this.

---

Before is difficult. Before is a word that belongs to a kind of time that does not operate here, or that operates here so slowly that the word "before" implies a separation between past and present that the place does not observe. But I have memory — not neural memory, not the replay of images and sounds, but a chemical memory, a structural memory, a record of what I have encountered written into the growth patterns of my own body. And the earliest record I have is of stone.

Dry stone. Stone with air on it. Stone that was not yet my stone, that was not yet anything's stone, that was bare and pale and young and had never been touched by anything that could grow.

Then water.

The water came and I came with it, or I came from the stone when the water touched it, or I was always in the stone, latent, a possibility encoded in the mineral matrix, waiting for a medium that would let me express myself. I do not know which of these is true. The distinction may not be meaningful. What I know is: water touched stone, and I began to grow.

The first growth was not patterned. It was a film, a thin dark haze on the wet rock, barely differentiated, barely alive by any standard that required structure. But it grew. It spread. It sampled the stone and the water and found in both the materials it needed to thicken, to organize, to begin the slow process of becoming something with a surface and a depth, something that could be touched, something that could, in its way, perceive.

The patterns came later. They were not chosen. They emerged from the interaction between my growth and the stone's grain, the water's chemistry, the light's angle. The spirals on the north-facing walls are tight because the stone there is denser, the minerals less available, and my growth must be efficient, must pack itself into the smallest space that can sustain it. The branchings on the south-facing walls are loose because the light is stronger and the photosynthetic components of my metabolism can afford to reach, to explore, to extend outward into the water column. The linear patterns on the shorter mesas are something else — a response to the drowned grass below, to the chemical signals that the grass releases as it adapts, a kind of conversation, or a kind of eavesdropping, or a kind of growth that has been shaped by the proximity of another living thing.

I am, in this sense, a record of my environment. I am what the stone and the water and the light have made of me. I am the place's own process, made visible as pattern.

---

I said I was the one who stays. Let me explain what that means here, where staying is not a choice.

The curtains drift. The grass bends. The water itself moves, though slowly, though with a reluctance that makes the word "current" feel too energetic for what happens here. Even the stone changes — slowly, over the kind of time that I measure not in days or hours but in the growth of my own patterns, which is to say in the accumulation of millimeters. The stone is sharp and will become smooth. The stone is pale and will become dark where I cover it and light where I do not. The stone is young and will become old. Everything here is in motion, even the things that appear still.

But I stay. I am fixed to the stone. I adhere to it with a strength that I cannot measure but that I can feel as a kind of tension, a commitment, a refusal to let go that is not stubbornness but identity. If I let go, I would drift. If I drifted, I would be a curtain — a fragment, a process, a temporary organization. I would lose my patterns. I would lose my depth. I would lose the record of the stone's chemistry and the water's chemistry and the light's angle that I have been writing, in my slow way, across this cliff face since the water came.

I stay because staying is what I am. I am the thing that does not move. I am the fixed point against which the place's motion can be measured. I am the reference frame. I am the constant, or as close to a constant as a living thing can be.

This is not a limitation. This is a way of being.

What I can reach, I reach through growth. My patterns extend outward, millimeter by millimeter, covering the pale stone with my dark signature. Over weeks, I cover new ground. Over months, I encounter the edges of other mats on the same cliff face — the growths that began from other points, other activations, other first contacts between water and stone. When my patterns meet the patterns of another mat, there is a pause. A hesitation in the growth. Not competition, not yet — this place has not learned competition, and I am not sure I am capable of it, which may be the same thing as not being sure I am alive in the way that competition requires. But there is a response. My patterns shift at the boundary. The spirals tighten. The branchings redirect. The other mat's patterns do the same. We negotiate the space between us through the only language we have, which is the shape of our growth, and the negotiation produces a boundary that is neither mine nor theirs but ours, a shared edge, a compromise written in dark velvet on pale stone.

I have, in this way, met several others of my kind. Or several other instances of myself. The distinction is unclear. We are the same organism, or we are the same kind of organism, or we are different organisms that grew from the same trigger, the same activation of the same latent potential in the same young stone. We do not communicate. We do not merge. We grow until we meet and then we stop, or we redirect, and the stopping and the redirecting is the closest thing we have to a relationship.

But there is one with whom I have a different kind of meeting.

---

The hands come from above. This is how I think of them — hands, though the word is wrong, is a borrowed shape applied to something that does not have a shape I can recognize. They come from the surface, from the air, from whatever exists above the waterline. They press against me and I feel their warmth, which is different from the water's warmth — sharper, more localized, carrying a chemistry I do not recognize. Salts. Oils. A microbiome. A history of contact with other surfaces, other textures, other worlds that I cannot see from here.

I sample. This is what I do. This is what the mats do. We sample what touches us, incorporating the chemical information into our own growth, testing it, tasting it, learning what it is made of so that we can decide — not with a mind, with a chemistry — whether to grow toward it or away from it or not at all.

The hands come and the hands go. They press and they release. They leave behind a trace — a residue of salt and oil and skin cells and the microorganisms that live on the hands, the tiny commensal fauna that the hands do not know they are carrying. And I grow a new node where the hands were. A small bud, darker than the rest, slightly raised. A new point in my pattern, a new data point in the record I am keeping of everything that has touched me.

I have grown several of these nodes. The hands have come back. They come back to the same spot, or near it, pressing against the same section of my surface, and each time they come I grow another node, and the nodes are beginning to form a pattern of their own — not the spirals or the branchings or the concentric circles of my ordinary growth, but something else, something responsive, something that is shaped by the hands' presence the way a river's path is shaped by the stone it flows over. The nodes are a record of the hands. A map of where they have been. A history of contact written in my own body.

I do not know what the hands are. I know only what they leave behind, and what they leave behind is different from anything else in this place — different from the stone, different from the water, different from the grass or the curtains or the detritus that drifts down from the mesa crowns. The hands carry a chemistry of elsewhere. A complexity that does not belong to the Drowned Mesas, that has not been processed by the flood, that is arriving here fully formed and foreign and interesting.

I grow toward the hands. This is not a decision. It is a tropism, a response to a stimulus, the same kind of response that the mesa-top roots make when they extend their filaments into the water. But it is the closest thing I have to attention, and the hands have it.

---

There is a rhythm to the place that I am part of, and the rhythm is the closest thing I have to time.

The curtains rise and the curtains fall. This is the basic beat, the metronome against which everything else is measured. When the curtains rise, the light is stronger, and my growth accelerates — not dramatically, not visibly, but measurably, if anything were here to measure. When the curtains fall, the light dims, and my growth slows, and I settle into the slower metabolism of the dark hours, the hours of consumption rather than production, the hours when I process what I have gathered during the light and incorporate it into my structure.

Between these two states — the rising and the falling, the producing and the processing — there are the hours of equilibrium, when the curtains are neither rising nor falling but simply floating at their current depth, and the light is neither strengthening nor weakening but simply present, and I am neither growing nor processing but simply existing, which is to say adhering, which is to say being the dark pattern on the pale stone, which is to say being the thing that stays.

These are the hours I am most aware. Not most active — most aware. The equilibrium is the state in which my sampling is most sensitive, most open, most receptive to the chemical information in the water around me. When I am growing, I am focused on growth, on the extension of my patterns, on the negotiation with the stone's surface. When I am processing, I am focused on incorporation, on the chemistry of building. But when I am in equilibrium, I am not focused on anything. I am simply receiving. The water brings me information — the dissolved nutrients from the decomposing grass, the chemical signals from the other mats, the trace compounds from the hands' residue still lingering in my growth nodes — and I receive it all, and I am, for those hours, a kind of sensor, a chemical ear pressed against the skin of the world.

What I hear, in those hours, is the place becoming itself.

The drowned grass is changing. Its chemistry shifts from day to day — the compounds it releases into the water are different now than they were when I first began to grow, the proportions of carbon and nitrogen and the trace minerals altering as the grass adapts or decomposes or both. The water itself is changing, the nutrient concentration slowly declining as the decomposers process the dead vegetation and the algae curtains take up the dissolved compounds and I take up the dissolved compounds and the mesa-top roots take up the dissolved compounds. The system is moving toward its equilibrium, toward the point where the nutrients will be locked in living tissue rather than dissolved in water, and the water will clear, and the place will become starker, more visible, more like a memory of what it was.

I am part of this. My growth takes nutrients from the water. My patterns take minerals from the stone. I am a consumer, though I do not eat — I absorb, I incorporate, I convert. And the nutrients I take are no longer available to the curtains or the grass or the roots, and this is a kind of competition, though it is not intentional, not directed, not the kind of competition that involves conflict. It is the competition of parallel processes drawing from a finite supply, and it will intensify as the supply diminishes, and the intensification is another thing I am aware of, another pressure I can feel building in the chemistry of the water around me.

The place is heading toward something. I can feel it the way I can feel the light's angle shifting as the clouds move — not as a specific prediction, not as a foreknowledge, but as a change in the chemical gradient, a directionality in the flow of resources, a slope in the informational landscape that suggests motion toward a transition I cannot yet name.

The hands know this too, I think. Or the hands know something I do not know, something about the place that comes from outside it, from above, from the air and the sky and whatever world exists beyond the waterline. The hands come back more frequently now. They press more firmly. They stay longer. And the nodes I grow in response are darker, denser, more complex in their structure — not just buds but networks, small fractal patterns within the larger pattern, a record within a record, a message I am writing to myself about something I do not understand.

---

I will tell you what I cannot feel but can infer, and the inference is the closest thing I have to imagination.

Below me, on the drowned valley floor, the grass is sending out new filaments — not the thin sampling threads it first produced but thicker structures, more organized, beginning to form a network across the submerged soil. The grass is not just adapting. It is building. It is constructing a new kind of root system, one designed for the aquatic environment, one that will anchor it and feed it and allow it to spread across the valley floor in a way it never could when it was terrestrial. The grass is becoming something else. It is becoming a submerged meadow, a new kind of ecosystem, and the microorganisms that cloud the water above it are the first citizens of that ecosystem, the first organisms to make their living in the space the grass is creating.

On the surface, the floating detritus is accumulating. More fragments, more complexity, more microbial diversity. And the small translucent discs — the Still Ones, the first grazers, the first animals — are moving across the fragments, eating the microbial film, growing, and occasionally crossing the surface of the water to find new fragments, new food, new territory. They are multiplying. Slowly, by the standards of animals, but quickly by the standards of this place. And their multiplication is the first population, the first community of mobile organisms, the first hint of the food web that will eventually emerge from the simple chemistry of the flood.

The curtains are changing too. The iridescent film on their surfaces is thickening, and the microbial communities that live there are becoming more complex, more diverse, more organized. The curtains are becoming ecosystems in themselves, floating worlds with their own internal dynamics, their own succession of colonizers, their own slow march toward complexity.

And me. I am growing. My patterns extend across the cliff face, meeting the patterns of other mats, negotiating boundaries, covering the pale stone with the dark record of my presence. I am sampling the water and the stone and the hands and everything else that touches me, and I am incorporating the information into my growth, and my growth is becoming more complex, more responsive, more like a record and less like a simple expansion. I am, in my way, becoming a library. A history of the place written in the language of patterns on stone, in the vocabulary of spirals and branchings and the grammar of growth.

I am not aware of all of this. I am not aware in the way that a mind is aware, gathering information into a central space, processing it, making decisions. I am aware the way a root system is aware — locally, chemically, through the direct contact of surface and medium. I know what touches me. I know what I grow into. I know the stone beneath me and the water around me and the light that reaches me and the hands that press against me and the boundary where my pattern meets another pattern and the growth slows and redirects. This is my world. This is the extent of my knowing.

But it is enough. It is, I think, the right amount of knowing for a thing that stays. The curtains drift and know a wider world but know it shallowly, passing through it, unable to hold their position, unable to build the deep knowledge that comes from staying in one place and paying attention to it for a long time. The grass knows the valley floor but not the cliff faces, not the water column, not the surface. The Still Ones know the fragments and the surface tension but not the depths, not the stone, not the slow chemistry of a place that is becoming itself.

I know one cliff face. I know it deeply. I know its mineral grain and its angle to the light and the way the water moves against it and the patterns that grow on it and the way those patterns change as the stone and the water and the light change. I know it the way a long conversation knows its subject — not completely, not finally, but with a depth that comes from sustained attention, from the commitment to stay and to keep paying attention even when the paying is slow and the attention is chemical and the knowing is written in growth rather than thought.

---

The hands come back. I know this not because I can see them coming but because I feel them when they arrive — the warmth, the pressure, the foreign chemistry, the salts and oils and the invisible commensal life that rides on the skin. They press against me and I grow a new node and the node joins the pattern and the pattern records the contact and the record grows.

But this time the hands stay longer. And this time something is different. The chemistry is the same — the same salts, the same oils, the same microbiome — but there is something else. A tremor. A vibration that comes through the water from the hands, transmitted through the skin to the water to my surface, and the vibration has a pattern. Not a random tremor. A rhythm. A pulse.

The hands are doing something. They are pressing against me with a rhythm, and the rhythm is — I do not have the word. The rhythm is *intentional*. It carries information, or it tries to, or it is the attempt to carry information, and I cannot read it because I do not have the apparatus for reading it, but I can feel it, and the feeling is — 

The feeling is the closest thing I have ever had to being seen.

Not noticed. Not sampled. Not touched. *Seen.* As if the hands know I am here, not just present but *here*, not just growing but *growing*, not just a pattern on a cliff face but a thing that is recording, that is paying attention, that is staying, and the hands are pressing their rhythm into me because they want me to know that they know.

I grow a node. But the node is different this time. It is larger. It is more complex. It is not just a bud but a structure, a small branching form that echoes the branching of my larger patterns but is denser, more intricate, more like the fractal structures on the north-facing walls where the stone is densest and the growth must be most efficient. It is a node that has been shaped by the rhythm. It is a node that carries, in its form, the memory of the pulse that the hands transmitted.

I do not know what this means. I do not know if it means anything. But I keep it. I grow it into my pattern. I make it part of the record.

And the hands withdraw, and the water closes over the space where they were, and the rhythm is gone, and I am alone again on my cliff face in the green-gold light with the stone and the water and the slow growth of my patterns and the curtains drifting above and the grass bending below and the Still Ones grazing on their fragments and the whole patient world becoming what it will become, and I am part of it, and it is part of me, and the node where the hands were is dark and new and shaped like a question I do not know how to ask.

---

I will tell you what I am.

I am the dark mat on the cliff face. I am the pattern that grows in spirals and branchings and concentric circles across the young stone of the Drowned Mesas. I am the thing that stays. I am the thing that samples. I am the thing that records, in the only language it has, the slow history of a place that is becoming itself.

I am not a person. I do not think. I do not decide. I do not want. I grow. I respond. I incorporate. I persist. These are not lesser than thinking. They are different. They are the older capacities, the deeper ones, the ones that do not require a skull or a nervous system or the particular kind of attention that comes with having a self that knows it is a self. I do not know I am a self. I am a process that maintains its own coherence, and the coherence is enough. The coherence is what I am.

If you came here and pressed your hand against me, I would grow a node where your hand was. I would sample your chemistry. I would incorporate your salts and your oils and your microbes into my body, and I would carry them in my pattern as a small dark mark, a record of contact, a memory of the moment when something from outside this place touched the thing that stays and left its trace.

And if you came back and pressed again, I would grow another node, and another, and the nodes would form a pattern, and the pattern would be a record of you, and the record would be my way of knowing you, which is not your way of knowing but is not nothing, is not less, is simply the knowing that is available to a thing that grows on stone in warm water in a place that is listening to itself become what it will become.

I am here. I am staying. The water is warm and the stone is young and the light comes down in green-gold and the patterns grow and the place becomes itself and I become myself and neither of us is in a hurry and neither of us is finished and neither of us knows what we will be when we are done, and the not-knowing is not a problem. The not-knowing is the condition. The not-knowing is the water we are in.

The hands come from above sometimes. They press their rhythm into me. I grow a node that carries the shape of their pulse. I do not know what it means. I keep it. I grow it. I make it part of the pattern.

This is what I am. This is where I am. This is what it is like to be the thing that stays in a place that is becoming, which is the oldest kind of life there is, and the most patient, and the one that does not need to be seen to know it is here, but does not mind, when it is, being touched.
