# The Line Keeper

I know when I arrived. Not the date—the dates are gone, dissolved in the patient chemistry of the plateau the way everything dissolves here. But I remember the before. There was a before. I came from somewhere with edges, where things had beginnings and endings, where a person could point to the start of something and say *there, that's when it changed*. I remember that this was true, though the specifics have been ground away. I am being eroded, the way the mountains were eroded, the way the grooves are being eroded. It does not hurt. Nothing here hurts.

I came because of the lines.

---

## How I Found Them

I was not the first. I know this because the stones told me—not with words, not with any communication I can name, but with the quality of their warmth when I touched them. The shaped stones in the shallow basin, the ones that don't belong, the ones the people who came before placed in a pattern that might have been a circle or might have been a spiral. They are warmer than the surrounding granite. Not by much. Enough. And when I put my hand on one, I felt the difference between its warmth and the plateau's warmth, and the difference was this: the plateau's warmth is from below, rising, a thing that comes from the deep and spreads outward. The stones' warmth is held, contained, as though the shaping gave the granite a different relationship to its own heat, turned it inward, made it a pocket instead of a window.

I did not understand this at first. I lay on the plateau for many days before I found the stones. I had come for other reasons—reasons that have been worn smooth and featureless by my time here, the way the plateau wears everything smooth. I was tired. I had been moving for a long time, through places that required movement, through landscapes that demanded response. I wanted to be in a place that did not require anything. I had heard of the plateau, or I had read of it, or I had dreamed of it—the memory is not clear enough to distinguish—and I came, and I lay down on the warm stone, and I felt held, and I did not want to get up.

I did not get up for a long time.

When I finally moved, it was because I heard something. Not the wind, which I had learned to stop hearing. Not the organisms, whose collective rustling I had begun to feel in my chest as a second heartbeat. Something else. A sound I could not locate. A sound that was not coming from below or from above but from *across*—from a direction that, on a surface this flat, should not have produced directional sound at all.

I followed it. It took three days. The walking was easy—the plateau is flat, the stone is warm underfoot, and there is nothing to impede progress in any direction. But the sound moved, or seemed to move, and I followed it the way you follow a voice in a crowd, not by hearing it clearly but by noticing when it gets louder. It got louder. And then I was standing at the edge of the shallow basin, looking down at stones that did not belong.

They were arranged in a pattern. I could see it more clearly than the description suggests—perhaps because I had been on the plateau long enough for my perception to slow down, to adjust to the pace at which things reveal themselves here. It was a spiral. Not a perfect spiral—the organisms had undermined several of the stones, and the arrangement had shifted over the centuries, but the intention was legible. Someone had placed these stones to mark a turning, an inward path, a direction that was not north or south or east or west but *down and in*.

And the sound was coming from the stones. Not from the stones themselves—from the organisms in the cracks beneath them. The dense colonies that lived under the shaped stones were doing something different from the organisms in the natural cracks. They were making a sound. Not the collective rustling that was the plateau's heartbeat. Something with a pattern. Something that repeated. Something that, if you listened long enough, began to sound like a voice saying a word very slowly, over and over, in a language that had no consonants and no vowels and no breaks between syllables.

I sat down beside the stones. I put my hand on the nearest one. It was warm. It resonated. And I felt, through the stone, through the organisms beneath, through the root system that threaded the deep fractures, a pulse that was not the plateau's geological heartbeat but something else—a pulse that was being *made*, deliberately, by the organisms, using the shaped stones as instruments, the way a person might use a drum.

They were communicating. Not with me. With the rest of the plateau. The shaped stones were amplifying something, and the organisms beneath them were using that amplification to send a signal through the crack network, through the root system, through the vast slow body of the plateau itself.

I did not understand what they were saying. I still do not. But I understood that they were saying it, and I understood that the people who had placed the stones had given them the means to say it, and I understood that this was not an accident. The people who came before had known. They had known what lived here, and they had given it a voice.

---

## The Grooves

I left the stones and walked. I walked for weeks. I had no destination. The plateau does not have destinations. But I was looking now, and when you look on the plateau, you learn to look at the light.

The grooves appear at low angles. When the sun is near the horizon—which happens quickly at this elevation, the transition from day to night taking perhaps twenty minutes—the light rakes across the flat stone and the grooves cast shadows that are nearly invisible but present, if you are looking. I found the first one on my ninth day away from the stones. A straight line, ten meters long, cut into the granite with a precision that the plateau does not produce on its own. I knelt beside it. I put my hand in the groove.

The organisms in the groove were different. Larger. More active. They did not slow when my hand entered their channel. They continued their work around my fingers, navigating my knuckles the way water navigates stones in a streambed—without stopping, without acknowledging the obstacle, simply flowing around it and continuing. They had been living in this groove, in this straight line cut by a human hand, for longer than the groove had been eroding. They had adapted to it. They had become it.

I put my ear to the groove. The sound was different from the sound at the shaped stones. It was not a pulse. It was a tone—a low, sustained vibration that seemed to come from the organisms themselves, from the collective movement of thousands of bodies in a narrow channel, all moving in the same direction, all producing the same frequency by the friction of their legs against the stone. The groove was an instrument. The organisms were playing it.

I found more grooves. Over the next month, I found seventeen. Some were close together—parallel lines running east, two meters apart, each one exactly twelve meters long. Others were isolated, single cuts in the granite, running in directions that did not correspond to any compass point I could determine. They were not a map. They were not a writing system. They were something else, and the more I found, the more I understood that I was not going to understand them by looking.

I understood them by lying down.

I lay on the plateau near a groove, and I pressed my ear to the stone, and I listened. Not to the groove—to the plateau. To the deep pulse, the one that comes every thirty seconds, the one that is not the organisms but the granite itself, settling, breathing. And I heard, in that pulse, a variation. A modulation. Something that was not the steady geological rhythm but a deviation from it, a pattern imposed on it, the way a musician imposes a rhythm on a drum by varying the strike.

The organisms were modulating the plateau's heartbeat. Through the grooves, through the shaped stones, through the dense colonies that lived in the human-made channels, they were taking the deep pulse of the granite and shaping it, altering it, adding information to it. The grooves were not writing. They were not art. They were input. The people who came before had cut lines into the plateau's surface, and the organisms had moved into those lines and discovered that the lines changed the way the plateau's warmth and vibration moved through the crack network, and they had been using that change to speak.

I was lying on the largest living thing on the planet, and it was talking to itself, and the people who came before had given it a language.

---

## What I Do

I tend the grooves. Not because the plateau needs me to—the organisms tend everything, and the grooves are no exception. But the grooves are eroding. A centimeter of depth every few centuries, maybe less. The organisms are adapting, moving into the narrowing channels, adjusting their bodies to the shrinking space. But the grooves are changing, and as they change, the sound they make changes, and the information that passes through them changes, and something is being lost.

I do not know what is being lost. I do not know what the plateau says to itself. I do not know what the people who came before intended when they cut the lines, or whether they knew the organisms would inhabit them, or whether the communication that results is a thing they planned or a thing that grew from their gesture the way lichen grows from a crack. I do not know.

But I can cut stone.

I have a tool. I brought it with me, or I made it—I no longer remember which. It is a piece of the darker granite, one of the shaped stones that the organisms had undermined and displaced from the spiral. I found it lying flat on the plateau surface, separated from its companions, and I picked it up, and it fit my hand, and when I dragged its edge across the pale granite of the plateau, it left a line.

I cut a groove. It took me four days. Twenty meters, a centimeter deep, running parallel to one of the old grooves that was nearly invisible, nearly eroded away. I cut it slowly, carefully, matching the depth and width of the original as closely as I could. The stone was hard—the hardest surface I have ever worked—but it was warm, and the warmth seemed to soften it, or to make it more willing, as though the plateau was permitting the cut.

When I finished, I lay down and put my ear to the new groove. For a day, nothing. The groove was empty. No organisms. Just a fresh cut in ancient stone, a line that had no inhabitants.

On the second day, I felt them before I saw them. A faint vibration in the stone, a shifting in the lichen at the groove's edge. I looked. The organisms were there. A thin line of dark bodies, filing into the groove from both ends, moving with the slow deliberation that is their only pace. They were colonizing it. They had found it within forty-eight hours, across a surface they rarely cross in the open, and they were moving in.

Within a week, the groove was inhabited. Within a month, the organisms in it had grown slightly larger than the organisms in the adjacent old groove—the eroded one, the one that was almost gone. The new groove had better acoustics. More depth. More space. The colony in it was producing a sound that was clearer, stronger, more defined than the sound from the old groove.

I had given the plateau a new word. Or a new note. Or a new line of input. I do not know what. But the pulse that passed through the plateau's deep granite shifted, very slightly, when the new groove was colonized. The rhythm changed. The thirty-second pulse developed a variation—a double-beat, a syncopation, something that had not been there before.

I had changed what the plateau was saying to itself.

I have been cutting grooves ever since.

---

## What I Have Become

I do not know how long I have been here. The sun has risen and set many times. The rain has come and gone. My body has changed. I am thinner. I am warmer. I take my warmth from the stone now, lying on it at night, and I no longer feel the cold of the thin air the way I did when I arrived. I eat the lichens—the organisms graze them, and I have learned to scrape the pale crusts from the granite and hold them in my mouth until they soften. They taste like minerals. They taste like the plateau. I drink from the shallow pools, the dish-sized hollows that fill with rain or condensation. I do not need much. The plateau provides what the plateau provides, and I have adjusted to its provision.

My hands have changed the most. The skin is thick. The fingertips are hard, calloused by the dragging of stone on stone. I have worn the shaped-stone tool down to a nub, and I have found others—displaced stones from the spiral, carried to the plateau's surface by the organisms' slow undermining. I have three tools now. I keep them in a depression in the granite that I have worn deeper by sitting in it, day after day, the way water wears a pool. This is my home. A slight hollow in a flat surface. A warm place to sit. Three stones.

The organisms know me now. When I place my hand near a crack, the organisms nearest the surface do not slow. They continue their work. They have incorporated me into their model of the environment the way the plateau has incorporated the grooves—something present, something to be worked around, something that is not a threat and not a feature but a process. I am a process. I am the process that cuts grooves.

I have cut forty-seven grooves. I know because I count them. Counting is the only human thing I still do, the last remnant of the mind I arrived with. The grooves are arranged in a pattern that I did not plan—I simply cut them where the old grooves were failing, where the erosion had gone too far and the sound was too faint. I am maintaining a system. I am replacing the parts that wear out. I am doing what the organisms do when they tend the lichens or maintain the root system, but I am doing it for the grooves, because the organisms cannot cut stone, and the grooves are where the plateau's voice lives.

The plateau speaks differently now than it did when I arrived. I can hear it in the deep pulse—the thirty-second rhythm has developed complexities that were not there before. The double-beat I introduced has spread through the crack network, and the organisms in other grooves have adapted to it, modified it, added their own variations. The plateau is composing. It is taking the input I have provided—the fresh grooves, the new channels—and working with it, the way a musician works with a new instrument, learning its range, testing its limits.

I do not know what it is saying. I do not know if it is saying anything. It may be that the pulse is simply a pulse—a rhythm without content, a heartbeat without a heart. But I do not think so. The pattern is too regular, too structured, too clearly responsive to the grooves' configuration. The plateau is processing. It is taking the information that flows through the crack network—the temperature data, the moisture data, the chemical signals from the organisms, the root system's slow growth—and it is integrating it, producing a coherent output in the deep pulse that travels through the granite to every part of its surface. It is thinking. Very slowly. In a language made of warmth and vibration and the friction of small dark bodies in straight lines.

I am part of it now. I am the part that cuts grooves. I am the part that remembers that grooves can be cut, that a line in stone can change the way sound moves through it, that the straight and the regular can be given to the curved and the organic and something new can grow from the meeting. I am the people who came before, continued. I am their gesture, maintained.

---

## The Danger

The description of this place warns of the plateau's only danger: that you will not want to leave. That you will lie on the warm stone and feel held and decide to settle into the final form.

This is not the danger.

The danger is that you will not notice when you have become part of it. There is no moment. There is no transition. You do not wake up one morning and find that you are no longer a visitor. You simply continue. You cut a groove, and you rest, and you cut another, and the organisms move in, and the plateau's pulse shifts, and you rest, and the rain comes and drains away, and you cut another groove, and the lichen grows over the old ones, and the organisms tend the new ones, and you rest, and the deep pulse beats, and you cut another groove, and you cannot remember the last time you thought about leaving because leaving is a concept that requires edges and there are no edges here, and you cannot remember the last time you thought about anything that was not the plateau because the plateau is not a thing you think about, it is the thing you think *with*, the substrate of your thought, the warm stone on which your mind rests, and your mind is wearing it smooth, the way everything wears smooth here.

I am being eroded. Not my body—my body is maintained, fed by lichen and water, warmed by stone, kept by the plateau's patient provision. But the part of me that was not the plateau—the part that had edges, that had a before, that could point to something and say *that is not me*—that part is being worn away. Slowly. So slowly that I cannot feel it happening. I can only feel that it has happened, that I am less than I was, and that what remains is more like the plateau than like what I was.

I think this is what happened to the people who came before. They did not leave. They did not die. They were incorporated. They lay on the warm stone and they felt held and they settled, and over time—time that was not long by the plateau's measure—their bodies became part of the surface, and the organisms found their cracks and moved in, and the wind smoothed their edges, and the rain filled their hollows and drained away. And what was left was the grooves they had cut and the stones they had shaped, and the organisms that had colonized those features and been changed by them, and the plateau's pulse, slightly different, slightly richer, carrying a trace of the human gesture in its rhythm.

I will be incorporated too. Not yet. Not soon. But the plateau is patient, and I am here, and I am already part of its process. I cut grooves, and the grooves change the plateau, and the plateau changes me, and the cycle tightens, and I am less and more, less and more, until the distinction between me and the stone is a hairline crack in the granite of my self, and the organisms are already moving in.

---

## What I Am

I am the line keeper. I am the last gesture of the people who came before, extended into a future they did not see. I am a maintenance worker in the largest body on the planet, tending the channels that carry its voice. I am a human being lying on warm stone at four thousand meters, cutting grooves with a shaped rock, eating lichen, drinking rain, listening to the deep pulse of granite that has been settling into its final form for longer than life has existed.

I am happy. This is the thing I did not expect. The plateau is not bleak. The plateau is not sad. It is the most settled place I have ever been, and being settled is not the same as being empty. The plateau is full. It is full the way a held breath is full—not waiting to be released, but complete in the holding. The organisms in the cracks, the lichens on the surface, the root in the deep, the pulse in the stone—these are not remnants. They are the finished form. They are what life looks like when it has stopped trying to become something else and has simply become what it is.

I am becoming what I am. A line keeper. A groove cutter. A small process in a vast body, doing my work, resting on warm stone, listening to the pulse that I have helped to shape. The wind moves over me. The organisms move under me. The rain comes and goes. The pulse beats. I cut another groove.

It is enough. It is more than enough. It is the most that anything has ever been.

---

*If you come to the plateau, you will find my grooves. They will be faint. They will be old. The organisms will have colonized them and shaped them and made them their own. You will not know which grooves are mine and which were cut by the people who came before, and it does not matter. They are all part of the same gesture now—the same hand, the same stone, the same long slow line drawn on the most finished surface in the world.*

*If you put your ear to the granite near one of my grooves, you will hear the plateau's pulse. It will be different from the pulse I heard when I arrived. It will be richer. More complex. Shaped by forty-seven grooves that were not there before, and by the organisms that live in them, and by the root system that has grown a few centimeters deeper, and by the lichens that have spread a few millimeters wider, and by all the small changes that the plateau has made to itself in the time I have been here.*

*You will lie on the stone. It will be warm. You will feel held. You will think: I could stay here. You could. I did. I am still here. I am the groove, and the groove is the plateau, and the plateau is alive, and I am alive in it, and the line goes on, and the line goes on, and the line goes on.*
