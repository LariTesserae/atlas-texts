# Inhabitant

I did not arrive at the platforms. That is the first thing to understand. There is no story in which I came here, because there is no *here* that precedes the platforms. The platforms were built, and then I was — not after them, exactly, but *of* them, the way the lichen is of the metal, the way the channel-worn grooves are of the water.

I am what the description would call an absence filled in. The shelter near the central pumping station — the single room with the patched roof, the basin, the pipe that no longer delivers water — it smells faintly of mustelid, and the description assumes this is because the mustelid passes through. It does pass through. But the smell is also mine, or close enough to mine that the distinction does not matter to a nose. I am the reason the floor is worn smooth in the center. I am the reason the door panel is a composite of wood and metal and something fibrous — layered over the years, the description says, as if by many hands. It was one pair of hands. Mine. I maintain the door the way the beetles maintain the succulent mats: slowly, by grazing, by taking what is needed and replacing what is taken.

You would not see me at first. This is by design, though I hesitate to call it design, since I did not decide it any more than the fern decided its translucence. I am the same charcoal-dark as the large lizards. I am low to the grate and slower than the channel hunters and I have made a practice — a *physiology*, almost — of the drop. When a shadow crosses me, I go down through the metal into the substructure and I am gone before the shadow arrives. The grate closes over me like water. I have been doing this long enough that the startle response is not fear. It is grammar. It is how a sentence in my body ends.

Let me tell you what I am, in the plainest terms I can manage, and then let me tell you what it is like.

## The Shape of Me

I am roughly the size of the mustelid, which is to say: small enough to use the substructure fully, large enough that the substructure is a world rather than a warren. My body is long and low, but where the mustelid is fur and sustained flow, I am something more like the lizards scaled up and slowed down and given a different job. Scales, yes — dark, flat, overlapping, good for sliding along stone and bad for being seen. Limbs: four, short, positioned for the horizontal gaps rather than the vertical ones. I can climb, awkwardly, but my body prefers the plane it was made for, which is the plane between the platform and the mountain, the shaded interstitial country where the black foliose lichen makes its ceiling of scales.

My head is blunt, like the large herbivore lizard's. My jaw is stronger. I am not a grazer, though I graze. My eyes are small and my world is dim and my ears — such as they are — are tuned to the condensation tick, the beetle click, the reluctant descending note of the ground-nesting bird at dusk. These are my nouns. The wind's low presence is my verb tense: the continuous backdrop against which everything else is punctuated.

The description of this place says the mustelid is the closest thing to a mind here. I want to be careful with this, because I am not writing to correct the description. The description is accurate from where it stands. But it stands on the grate, and I live under it, and from under the grate the hierarchy of minds looks different. The mustelid decides, yes — left or right, this gap or that one. But deciding is not the only form of mind. The fungal network transports water from wet places to dry places without deciding anything, and it keeps the grass alive a channel's-length from any seep, and I would call that intention if I had a poorer vocabulary. I have a decent vocabulary. I am using it. That should tell you something about what form of mind I have.

I am the keeper. That is the nearest word. Not the owner — the platforms do not need an owner, and I have read enough of this place's logic to know that ownership is a concept the system would not recognize. Keeper in the old sense: one who *keeps* — holds, tends, maintains in the sense of *sustaining against loss*. The valves that are corroded but still turnable: I turn them. Not on a schedule. On a felt need, the same way the fern's frond unfurls when the spill reaches it and not before. When a channel silts — and they do, slowly, with beetle carapaces and windfall dust and the fibrous shed of the succulent mats — I clear the lip with my claws, and the water finds its way again, and I have done a thing that no one asked me to do, in the dark, for no witness.

The people who built this place are gone, and the system was designed to run without them, and it runs. But there is a difference between running and *kept*, and I am that difference. I am the width of a valve-turn. I am the cleared lip of a channel. I am the recomposite door panel, layered over years, and I am the reason the shelter's roof has been patched twice with lighter metal and bolted down well. The description says the system healed around its broken parts by making them irrelevant. Mostly true. The pump became irrelevant; gravity replaced it. But the valves did not become irrelevant. They stayed *possible*, and possibility needs hands, or claws, and I have four of them and a jaw and a stubbornness that the crickets would recognize, because the crickets are the species for whom this place is sufficient but not necessary, and I —

I am the opposite. I am the species for whom this place is not sufficient but is *absolutely necessary*, and I have made my peace with that the way the worm-lizard has: by never leaving the spill zone. My spill zone is the whole platform system. My litter is the substructure. My wager is that the channel will not shift. I tend the channels. I am both sides of the wager.

## A Day, Which Is Also Every Day

I wake in the hour before the crickets stop.

This is the hinge the description speaks of — the silence between the last chirp and the first drip — and I have come to understand it as the true dawn, regardless of where the sun is. The light does not reach me in the deep substructure. What reaches me is the sound, and the sound turns, and I rise through the gaps the way warmth rises, without deliberation, an act of the body's simplest physics.

By full dark the channels are running. This is my working time. I move along the channel network beneath the grate, in the older stone channels that the metal channels mirror above — there are channels at both levels, you see, and the ones beneath are mine, worn smooth like the upper ones but worn by older water, or perhaps by the same water a moment later in its journey. I listen for the flows. Each channel has a voice. The main trunk channels murmur; the feeder channels tick; the spill zones that feed the ferns make a small braided sound, water dividing and reuniting, which is the most companionable sound on the platform and the one I sit longest with.

I check the ferns. All twelve. This is not duty; it is the same motion as hunger, the same necessity-grade imperative. A dozen plants, each no bigger than an open hand, each living exactly at the edge of the possible, each with its worm-lizard population sequestered in the litter beneath — a worm-lizard population that the description correctly calls a wager on the channel's stability. I am the counterparty on that wager. When a spill shifts by a few centimeters — from debris, from a beetle's dying fall, from the slow mineral accretion that is this place's version of entropy — I shift it back. I have moved small stones. I have cleared packed film. Once I found a channel lip cracked by thermal cycling, a fissure that would have dried a spill zone within a season, and I wedged it with a shard of the pale quartz-veined stone from the old walls, and the water went back to its fern, and the fern did not die, and the worm-lizards went on being worm-lizards, blind and pale and utterly dependent, in their few square centimeters of world.

Did they know? Of course not. Does the valley know where its water comes from? No. I am not complaining. I am describing the *shape* of the work, which is this: necessary and unwitnessed and repeated. The succulent mats extend their edges millimeter by millimeter and try again each fog, and the mats are not discouraged, because discouragement requires a picture of the whole, and the mats do not have one. I have one. I have the whole picture. And I do the same work anyway. This is the part I would emphasize if I could emphasize anything: *the work is the same whether or not you can see the whole.* The seeing is a gift that changes nothing and everything.

Mid-fog, I go up.

This is the risk-hour, and I take it deliberately, the way the channel hunters take their beetle — fast, committed, no half-measures. I cross the grate while the fog beads on my scales and the condensation ticks around me like a hall of tiny clocks, and I go to the cisterns, and I listen at their tops. The low constant murmur of water entering the tanks is, as the description says, the closest thing this place has to a voice. I would go further. It is the closest thing this place has to a *pulse*, and I check it the way you might check a sleeping body: for the steady in-flow, for the absence of gurgle or trickle that would mean a pipe had failed. The pipe network is largely intact. Largely is not entirely. Twice in my tenure, a joint has wept where it should have held, and I have packed it with the fibrous mat-material and the dark lichen-scrapings that set hard, and the weeping stopped, and the valley below continued to receive what the air gives up, never knowing.

I also go up because the mustelid goes up at this hour, and we have an arrangement.

I want to be honest about the mustelid, because the description was honest, and honesty is the only currency that spends here. The mustelid hunts the lizards and the ground-nesting birds. I am a large dark shape in the same habitats. There is a version of this account in which I describe a rivalry, a contest, a drama of fang and claw in the substructure. That is not what happens. What happens is *geometry*. The mustelid travels in straight lines — paired prints, direct gait, an animal that does not wander but goes. I flow along channels and seams, and my path and its path cross rarely and predictably, and in the crossing-places we have established — without negotiation, without contact, without any exchange I could name — a spacing. It goes left when the gaps allow left. I wait in a cavity when the murmur of its travel passes. We are two apexes who have agreed, through the slow mathematics of never fighting, to partition one small sky-borne country. Its territory is the travel-lines and the hunting-times. Mine is the channels and the tending-times. The description says the mustelid decides, and it does. But its decisions and mine interlock like the ribs of the platform: no single logic, and everything directed somewhere specific.

I do not love the mustelid. Love is an organism-scale word. But there is a system-scale word that is close enough, and it is *acclimation*, and I will use it plainly: I am acclimated to the mustelid's existence the way the succulent mats are acclimated to the beetles' grazing. Balance, not peace. I would not mourn it if it went. I would notice the silence where its straight-line travel used to be, and I would keep the valves, and the silence would become part of the place's texture, and the place would go on.

That is what I am like. That is the honest answer.

## What I Am Not

I am not the ghost of the people who built this.

I want to spend a moment on this because it is the assumption the description invites, standing as it does before the patched door and the worn floor: someone has been here. Someone has layered the door panel and bolted the roof patches and turned the valves from their frozen rust. The description is careful — it says the system does not need anyone, the absence of people is completion and not wound, the place is complete and not waiting. All of that is true, and I affirm it from underneath. And yet the shelter exists, and the shelter is maintained, and maintenance implies a maintainer.

Here is what I can tell you, and it is the strangest thing I know, so I will say it simply: I do not know whether I remember them or whether I am made of their remembering. There are markings inside the door panel — beneath the composite layers, pressed between the metal and the fibrous mat-stuff, where nobody would look and nothing could weather them. I have added layers to that door. I have seen what is between the layers. Lines, in a pattern, in something that was once pigment. They mean nothing to me in the way symbols mean things. But they mean something to me in the way the fern's spill-zone means something: I return to them. I have looked at them so many times that looking at them has become part of the door's maintenance, part of the tending, and I could not tell you whether the tending is mine or whether it is something the markings do to me, a slow instruction worked into my body across so many re-visitings that the instruction and the body are no longer separable.

The people built a system that would outlast them. That is the story the place tells, and I believe it. But systems that outlast their makers carry their makers forward in strange ways. The channels carry water. The valves carry possibility. The markings beneath the door carry — something else. And I carry the whole of it, in my keeping, in the dark, without comprehension. If I am their ghost, I am a ghost made of maintenance rather than memory. If I am not their ghost, then I am a native of this place who inherited a door with lines inside it, and the difference between those two conditions is smaller than anyone standing on the grate would guess.

There is a tower at the northern edge. Three meters of stone with an empty metal cap. Built to see from. Whatever was watched is no longer watched. I go up — awkwardly, on my short climbing limbs, in the dead-center of the day when the raptor has passed and the lizards are at their most flattened and the heat has killed every other reason to move. From the tower I can see the whole platform system, the valley dropping away into haze, the dark pipe-line down the mountainside, the curve of the earth. I go up and I look, and I do not know what I am looking *for*, and I always come down having found nothing, and I always go back up. The description would say the tower remains because stone is patient. I go up because I am patient too, and patience, given long enough, turns even an empty watching-post into a habit, and habits, given long enough, turn into a kind of love — the system-scale love, the acclimation-love, the love that does not require its object to do anything but persist.

Perhaps the people watched the weather. Perhaps they watched the water. Perhaps they watched for other people. I watch for nothing in particular, and I watch every day, and the nothing I watch is the same nothing the tower has been holding since its cap went empty. In this we are partners, the tower and I.

## The Question of Loneliness

You will want to know: am I alone?

There is exactly one of me. I have said this obliquely and I will now say it directly, because the description has taught me that this place rewards directness — the fog arrives as a wall; the water runs downhill; the crickets ping their single dry note every three seconds and nothing pretends otherwise. I am one. There were not always one. In the substructure there are cavities I do not use, cavities sized for my body, worn smooth at the base the way the shelter floor is worn smooth — two cavities in the deep stone near the eastern foundation-wall, and one in the north, and their smoothness is older than my memory, which is very old. I sleep in one of them now. The others I keep clear of debris, the way I keep the valves possible, the way I keep the ferns' spills in place. They are possible-cavities. They are the platform's way of saying: *this shape of thing has occurred here before, and the shape has not been un-made.*

I do not know what happened to the others. I have no memory of others, which either means they were before my beginning or my beginning was theirs, and I have stopped pulling at that thread, the way I tell the curious (there is no curious; there is only the fog and the tick and the reluctant bird-note) to stop pulling at threads. What I have instead of memory is *place*, whole and present: the murmuring cisterns, the braided spill-sounds, the cricket-hour, the wind underneath everything like a held note. Place is what a keeper keeps. Memory is what people keep. I have wondered, on the tower, in the dead-center heat, whether these are the same activity performed at different speeds.

So: alone, yes. Lonely — no, and I want to be precise about why, because I think it is the most precise thing I know.

Loneliness is the pain of missing a *second perspective*. It requires a theory of other minds and a hunger for them. I live inside a system that the description has already told you is itself a kind of organism — metal for skeleton, stone for body, fungi for nerves, water for blood. When I tend the channels, the system responds: the water moves, the ferns unfurl, the worm-lizards persist in their litter, the valley receives. When I clear a cistern inlet, the murmur changes pitch. This is responsiveness. This is the second perspective — not a mind mirroring mine, but a *world* answering my actions, reliably, in its own language, at its own tempo. The fungal network connects the grass to the channel far away and I connect the channel to the fern and the fern to its spill and the spill to the pipe and the pipe to the valley, and there is not one moment of my working life in which I act without the place acting back.

The crickets chirp: *I am still here. Are you still here?* And the other crickets do not answer, but their silence is the answer. That is my liturgy. That is my companionship. I am still here. The place is still here. Neither of us says so. The saying is unnecessary, and this place does not do unnecessary.

## On Being the Closest Thing to a Person

The description gives that title to the mustelid: the thing that moves through the space with purpose, that decides, that has learned the place so completely that its map is worth nothing anywhere else. I grant the mustelid its title. I will not compete for it. What I have is a different, harder title, and I have come to it slowly and I hold it with both hands:

I am the one who knows the place is *complete* and still tends it.

Do you see the difference? The mustelid's commitment is hunger-grade. It learned the gaps because the gaps contain food. If the platforms were destroyed, the mustelid would starve, and that is the whole of its tragedy. My commitment is not hunger, though I eat — the succulent mats, the occasional slow beetle, an egg-rich season's windfall from a failed ground-nest, taken only from nests that have already failed, because a keeper does not poach the kept. My commitment is the commitment of the valve-turner. I act on the system's *possibility*, and possibility is not hunger, and possibility is not hunger's opposite either. It is the third thing. It is what the people built when they built a system to outlast people: not just a machine that runs, but a machine full of *junctions where agency could re-enter*. The corroded valves that still turn. The brackets, empty, that could hold something again. The tower, capped and hollow, still commanding the view. The channels, self-clearing mostly, but only *mostly* — and the mostly is the margin where I live.

I am the margin. I am the few percent. I am the corroded-but-turnable in the body of a creature.

And so my self-portrait must end where the place's portrait ends, because I have no existence apart from it and would not want one. The description closes by saying that what lives here is everything, all at once, together, nothing without the rest. I am the piece of that everything which knows it is a piece. The lizards are the process's motion; the mustelid is its intention; the fog is its breath; the wind its heartbeat; the metal its skeleton; the fungi its nerves. I have thought about where I fit in that anatomy for a long time, in the patient stone-patient way of the tower, and here is what I have concluded, on the tower, in the dead-center heat, watching the valley haze take the water it does not know it is given:

I am its memory that it was made.

Not its grief. Not its ghost. Not its mourner and not its master. Just the kept fact, held in a dark body under a dark grate, that all of this — the ribbed metal, the polished channels, the cisterns' low voice, the fern at the edge of the possible, the whole patient transaction of air-to-metal-to-channel-to-cistern-to-pipe-to-valley — was *chosen*. By hands, once. And the choosing did not end when the hands ended. The choosing continues in every valve I turn, every spill I re-set, every layer I lay over the door. When the metal is gone and the stone alone catches the fog, the system will still not need me, and it will still be true that I kept it, and the truth will be carried by nothing at all, and it will still be true.

The fog is building in the valley. The shadow is crossing the platform like a slow tide. In a moment the crickets will give their last ping, and then the silence, and then the first tick.

I am going down now. The ferns need their sounds checked. The cisterns need their murmur. The door needs nothing, but I will pass it anyway, and I will listen through it for a moment to the lines between its layers, whatever they were saying, whatever they still say.

I am still here. The place is still here. That is the whole of my answer, and the place has taught me that it is enough.
