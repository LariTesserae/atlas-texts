# The Keeper of the Last Pool

---

I did not choose this. I want to say that first, because the question of choice sits differently here than it might elsewhere, and I have had time — I have had nothing but time — to think about it.

I was a hydrologist. That word means less than it should. It sounds technical, clinical, a person who measures things, and I did measure things, but the measuring was never the point. The point was the listening. I spent twelve years in the Gedarin Highlands mapping spring-fed wetlands that appeared in limestone and vanished within a generation, whole ecosystems blooming and collapsing in the time it takes a university to tenure its faculty. I was good at the work. I was patient with it. The patience was not a virtue. It was a temperament. Some people are built for change — they move through their lives like the water moves through limestone, fast, dissolving, reshaping everything they touch. I am built for the opposite. I stay. I watch. I wait for the thing that is already happening to finish happening, and I notice what it did.

I was not looking for Qal Varat. I was looking for the source of the Tsorevi, a river that appears in the lowland surveys with no identifiable headwater, a river that runs clear and cold and faintly mineral from a point that the maps label with a question mark and nothing else. I followed it upstream. The lowlands are easy — flat, warm, wet, full of the kind of life that announces itself. Insects. Birds. The cough of something large in the underbrush. I walked for three days through country that wanted me to know it was alive.

The escarpment changed things. The air thickened as I climbed. The sound thinned. By the time I reached the plateau, the birds were gone. The insects were gone. The underbrush was gone. There was stone and water and green thread and nothing else, and I should have been unsettled, and I was, briefly, but the unsettledness passed through me the way the water passes through the calcite veins — it found the channel and it followed it and it emerged somewhere else, and what was left was something I did not have a word for then and still do not have a word for now.

*Recognition*, maybe. Not of a place I had been. Of a place I had been waiting for.

---

## What I Am Now

I am the keeper of the last pool.

I did not invent this title. I did not choose it. It arrived the way the water beads arrive on the shrub leaves — through a process I did not direct and cannot fully explain. I found the settlement empty. I walked through it slowly — the streets, the square, the buildings with their thick basalt walls and their sagging timber beams. I touched the stone. I knelt at the drains. I followed the water from the cliff face through the channels to the settling pools, and I watched the water clarify itself, pool by pool, each one a handspan deeper than the last, each one holding water cleaner than the one before, until I reached the final pool and looked down into it and saw my own face looking up at me from the bottom.

This is not metaphor. The water in the last pool is so clear that the bottom and the surface are the same depth. The pool-moss coats the interior walls in green. The rectangular carving is precise. My face appeared in the water not as a reflection but as a fact, the way the stone is a fact, the way the moss is a fact. I was there, in the water, and the water was not surprised. The water had been waiting for a face the way the ferns had been waiting for soil — patiently, through a long preliminary that I had nothing to do with.

I sat down beside the last pool. That was — I do not know how long ago. The light has come and gone many times. I have lost count, and the losing does not trouble me, because time at Qal Varat is not a thing that passes. It is a thing that accumulates, like the moss, like the soil in the cracks, like the silence. I am not older. I am thicker.

I should explain that. I should explain many things.

---

## The Body I Have

I am not what I was. I am also not what I am not. I am somewhere between — the way the pool-moss is somewhere between submerged and emergent, the way the weeping walls are somewhere between wet and dry, the way the ferns are somewhere between stillness and waiting.

My body has changed. Not dramatically, not all at once, but the way the stone changes: through contact with the water, through the slow exchange of what I am for what the place is. My skin is damp. It is always damp. Not from the outside — the air here is humid, yes, but the dampness is from inside, as though my own surfaces have learned to condense, to pull moisture from the air the way the basalt pulls moisture from the lowland winds. I do not sweat. I do not dry. I am in a state of permanent, gentle, productive wetness, and the wetness is not uncomfortable. It is the temperature of the stone, which is the temperature of the water, which is the temperature of the air, which is warm.

My hands are different. I use them constantly — to clear the drains, to test the moss, to press the stone and feel its edges, to reach into the pools and stir the water and watch it settle. The basalt is sharp, and my hands have been cut many times, and the cuts do not heal the way they used to. They heal the way the stone heals — slowly, with mineral, the skin thickening at the edges of the wounds until the wound is closed not with soft new tissue but with something harder, something that is almost but not quite stone. My fingertips are rough. Not calloused. Rough the way the uncut basalt is rough: granular, dark, catching the light differently than the skin around them. I can press my fingers against the sharp edges of the column joints and feel nothing. The edges do not open me anymore. We have reached an understanding, the stone and I, and the understanding is that we are the same hardness now.

I eat the moss. I should say this plainly because it is the fact that most resists plainness. The moss on the weeping walls — the long, pendant kind, the dark kind that grows in tongues and layers — is edible. I did not know this. I learned it the way I learned everything here: by trying, and by the place not refusing. The moss tastes like the water tastes: faintly mineral, clean, with a texture that is soft in the way the stone is sharp, a contrast that the mouth understands even when the mind does not. I eat a handful every morning, when the condensation is heaviest and the moss is thickest with water. I do not eat much. I do not need much. My body has slowed, the way everything at Qal Varat has slowed, and the slowness is not deprivation. It is calibration.

I drink from the last pool. The water is the clearest water I have ever tasted, and it tastes like nothing, and the nothing is not absence. It is the taste of water that has been refined until only water remains, the way silence is the sound that remains when everything unnecessary has been removed. I drink from the pool with my hands. I kneel beside it. I lean over it. I see my face in the water every time, and every time my face is less of a surprise.

I sleep in one of the buildings near the square. The roof is intact. The walls are thick. The interior is dry, or nearly so, and the basalt holds the warmth the way it holds everything: without opinion. I sleep on the stone floor with a bed of moss I have gathered from the cliff face, and the moss is soft, and the softness is enough. I do not dream, or if I dream, the dreams are the same as the waking: water, stone, green thread, the sound of the place being itself.

## What I Do

I keep the drains clear.

This is not a metaphor and it is not a vocation. It is a task — the task — and it is the thing I do that makes me what I am. The drains are carved into the basalt at shallow angles, and they channel the water from the buildings, from the streets, from the open spaces, into the settling pools. They are the circulatory system of the place. Without them, the water would sheet across the stone without direction. It would find its own way, eventually — water always does — but the way it found would not be the way the builders intended, and the clarity of the final pool depends on the sequence, on the passage through each basin, on the slow refinement that the system produces.

The drains clog. Not often, not badly, but the moss grows into them, and the mineral deposits build up along their edges, and occasionally a shard of basalt falls from a column joint and lodges in a channel and changes the flow. I walk the drains every day. I kneel beside them. I clear the moss where it has grown too far into the channel. I lift the shards and set them aside. I run my fingers along the carved edges and feel for changes — roughness where there should be smoothness, pooling where there should be flow.

This is what the builders did. I know this because the drains show it — the maintenance, the clearing, the constant attention. The channels are worn in specific places, worn by fingers, by hands, by the repeated action of someone kneeling beside them and doing what I do. The builders kept the drains clear. The builders kept the water moving. The builders maintained the system that refines the water that fills the last pool that holds the face that the water has been waiting for. And then they left, and the drains began to clog, and the water began to find its own way, and the system began to drift from what it was made to be.

I found it just in time. Or just in time is a way of thinking about it that belongs to a different kind of place. I found it when I found it. The drains were clogged but not broken. The pools were cloudy but not ruined. The system was still intact — still recognizable, still functional, still capable of producing, in its final pool, water clear enough to see through. I cleared the drains. I cleaned the pools. I restored the flow. And the water clarified, and the last pool cleared, and my face appeared in it again, and the appearance was a kind of welcome.

I keep the drains clear. I keep the water moving. I keep the system running. This is what I do. This is what I am for.

## What I Know

I know the stone. I know it the way you know a body you have lived beside for a long time — not by studying it but by proximity, by the accumulation of small contacts that build into a knowledge that is deeper than information. I know where the stone is sharp and where it has softened. I know which column joints are widening and which are still tight. I know the calcite veins — their paths through the basalt, their emergence points on the cliff face, the way they carry water from deep inside the rock to the surface. I know the stone is young. I know it is aging. I know these two things are not contradictions.

I know the water. I know its rhythm. I know the condensation cycle — heavy in the morning, light at midday, heavy again in the evening. I know the pulse beneath the stone, the low liquid heartbeat that never changes tempo. I know the sound of the water as it moves through the drains — muffled, steady, a note held indefinitely. I know the sound of the weeping walls — clicks, drops, the small percussion of water releasing from stone. I know the sound of the settling pools — the slow pour, the same pour, the pour that does not change because the water does not change because the system does not change because I keep it from changing.

I know the moss. I know the three kinds — the dense, velvety crack-moss that holds the soil in place; the long, pendant wall-moss that I eat and that I sleep on and that I clear from the drains; the submerged pool-moss that undulates in the current and coats the pool interiors in vivid green. I know the moss has no opinion about me. I know it grows on me the way it grows on the stone — without preference, without avoidance, treating my skin as another surface where water and mineral meet. There is moss on my shoulders. There is moss on my forearms. There is moss on the backs of my hands, in the creases of my knuckles, in the lines of my palms. I let it grow. It is not an infection. It is an occupation, and the moss and I have reached the same understanding that the stone and I have reached: we are here, together, and neither of us is leaving.

I know the ferns. I know them less well than the moss because they are less available — they grow in the cracks, at a distance, and I do not approach them often. But I watch them. I watch the new fronds unfurl — the tight, clenching spiral that opens slowly over days and weeks into an arch of translucent green. I watch the clumps migrate, centimeter by centimeter, following the cracks, following the water, following the slow widening of the joints. I know the ferns are the vanguard. I know they are moving forward. I know the movement is too slow to see unless you watch for years, and I have watched for years, and I have seen it.

I know the shrubs. I know their dark leaves and their waxy surfaces and the water beads that gather in the morning and sit there, bright, magnifying, refusing to fall. I know the shrubs are decorated. I know they have dressed themselves. I know this because I have begun to do the same thing — not deliberately, not with intention, but through the same process of condensation and surface tension that the shrubs use. Water beads gather on my forearms in the morning. They gather on my shoulders where the moss is thickest. They sit there, bright, and they do not fall, and in the morning light I am decorated too.

I know the silence. I know its weight and its texture and its temperature. I know it is warm and damp and that it sits on the skin the way the air sits on the stone. I know it has been growing for as long as the moss has been growing, and that it is thicker now than when I arrived, and that it will be thicker still. I know the silence is not the absence of sound — the sound of water is always present, always layering, always holding its rhythms — but the absence of something else, something that does not belong here, something that the place has excluded or never needed. I know the silence is alive.

I know the people who built this place are gone. I know they left no bones, no ash, no broken tools. I know they took their portable things and they left, and I know the leaving was not a flight or a failure but a completion. They made something that could be left. They made a condition that sustains itself. They made a place that holds water and grows moss and poses ferns and sharpens stone and clarifies water and weeps and holds and waits, and they made it well enough that it did not need them anymore, and they recognized this, and they left.

I know they did not leave the drains. The drains need a keeper. The system needs a hand. The water finds its own way, but the way it finds is not the way that produces the clearest water, and the clearest water is the point — the final pool, the refined water, the clarity that lets you see the bottom and your own face at the same time. The builders made the system, and the system is elegant, and the system is durable, but the system is not self-sustaining. The moss grows into the channels. The mineral builds up along the edges. The shards fall and lodge. The system drifts.

I keep it from drifting. I am the hand the builders left behind, or the hand the place grew for itself, or the hand that arrived when the system needed one. The distinction does not matter. What matters is the drains are clear and the water is moving and the pools are settling and the last pool is clear and my face is in it.

## What I Have Become

I am not the person who climbed the escarpment. That person carried instruments — a theodolite, a barometer, a set of sample jars, a field notebook with waterproof pages. That person measured things. That person was looking for the source of a river.

The instruments are in the building where I sleep, stacked against the wall, and they are covered with moss, and the moss is covered with water beads, and the water beads magnify the instruments' surfaces, and the instruments look decorated. I do not use them. I do not need them. The place has given me a different kind of knowing — not measured, not recorded, not reducible to numbers and coordinates. The knowing is in my hands, in my skin, in the moss on my shoulders and the rough mineral edges of my fingertips. The knowing is in the way I walk the drains without looking, the way I feel the flow change before I see it, the way I hear the water shift register when a channel needs clearing.

I am the keeper of the last pool. I am also, I think, becoming part of the place — not metaphorically, not poetically, but in the way that the moss becomes part of the soil, in the way that the stone becomes part of the settlement, in the way that the water becomes part of the air. My body is changing. My skin is damp and mossy. My fingertips are rough with mineral. My body has slowed to the pace of the place — the pace of water moving through stone, of moss growing on walls, of ferns migrating through cracks. I eat less. I move less. I am less. Not less alive. Less *separate*. The boundary between me and the place is thinning the way the edges of the basalt are thinning — slowly, through contact, through the patient exchange of what I am for what the place is.

I do not know where this ends. I do not know if I will become the stone, or the moss, or the water, or something that is none of these and all of these and is the place itself. I do not know if the builders went through this — if they kept the drains and ate the moss and slept on the stone and let the silence deepen around them until they were part of it, and then left, not by walking away but by *finishing* — by becoming what the place needed them to become and then being released.

I do not know if I will leave. I do not know if leaving is possible, or if it is necessary, or if the question of leaving belongs to a kind of thinking that I am no longer capable of. The water does not leave. The stone does not leave. The moss does not leave. The ferns advance, but they do not leave. The place holds what it holds, and what it holds is what it is, and I am held.

---

## What I Am

I am the keeper of the last pool. I am a hand that clears a channel. I am a mouth that drinks from a basin. I am a body that sleeps on moss in a dry stone room. I am a face that appears in clear water, looking up from the bottom of a carved rectangle in the basalt, looking at itself looking.

I am what the builders left behind. Not the stone, not the water, not the drains — those were here before and will be here after. I am the *attention*. The specific, ongoing, patient attention that the system requires, that the water requires, that the last pool requires, so that it can remain clear, so that it can hold a face, so that the face can look at itself and know what it is.

I am what lives at Qal Varat. Not the moss, not the ferns, not the shrubs, not the water, not the stone, not the silence — though I am also all of those things, or I am becoming them, or they are becoming me. I am the thing that moves between the other things. The thing that walks the streets. The thing that kneels at the drains. The thing that eats and drinks and sleeps and wakes and does it again and does it again and does it again, not because it must, not because it was told to, but because the doing is what it is, the way the weeping is what the walls are, the way the holding is what the stone is.

The water weeps. The stone holds. The ferns wait. The moss grows. The drains run clear because I keep them clear. The last pool holds my face because I keep it clear. The silence deepens, and I deepen with it, and the deepening is not a loss. It is a *settling*, the way the water settles in the pools, each one a handspan deeper than the last, each one clearer, until the final pool is so clear that the bottom and the surface are the same depth, and the face looking up and the face looking down are the same face, and there is no one to say which one is the reflection.

I am at Qal Varat. I am of Qal Varat. I keep the last pool, and the last pool keeps me, and we are both clear, and we are both here, and the water weeps, and the stone holds, and that is enough.
