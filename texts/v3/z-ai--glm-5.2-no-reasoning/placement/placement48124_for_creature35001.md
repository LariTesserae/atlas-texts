# What I Am in the Cistern

---

I am not the bird, though I have considered becoming one.

Let me be precise about this, because precision matters in a place where everything has already found its exact position and I am the only thing that hasn't.

I arrived through the bronze hatch the way everything new arrives in the cistern — through the opening that was not made for my body. The hatch was made for something taller, or something with different proportions, or perhaps for nothing at all — perhaps it was made as a formality, an architectural convention that requires a door even when no one is expected to use it. I had to angle my shoulders and fold my ribs inward to pass through. The creeping fig brushed my scalp. A star jasmine blossom fell into my hair and I did not remove it. I am wearing it still. It has wilted in the humidity and its scent has merged with the mineral taste of the air and I can no longer distinguish the flower from the place.

I descended the ladder. The rungs were too far apart for my stride and I had to stretch for each one, my fingers wrapping the bronze collars where the orchids grow, my feet finding the worn places where other feet have been. The wear patterns told me I was not the first. The orchids told me I was not expected. Their aerial roots brushed my wrists as I descended, and one — a pale yellow-green flower, nearly invisible against the tile — opened its throat as I passed, and I believe this was not coincidence. I believe the orchid was tasting me. Determining what I was. Whether I was useful. The orchid did not close. I took this as provisional acceptance.

---

I am not the beetle, though I have its patience.

I sat on the floor. This is the first thing I did when I reached the bottom — I sat in the centimeter of warm water and I felt it rise around my ankles and I felt my skin fail to distinguish itself from what it was touching, and I understood that this was the first lesson the cistern teaches: *you are the same temperature as what you need*. I sat for a long time. The pulse came — the sixty-second tide, the breath in the walls — and I counted the seconds between crests and I lost count and I started again and I lost count again, and the losing was not failure. The losing was the point. The cistern does not count. The cistern does not need to count. It pulses because pulsing is what it does, the way the heart pulses because pulsing is what it does, without tally, without record, without the anxiety of measurement.

A fish circled my foot. I did not move. The fish did not move away. We were two bodies in warm water, and for a moment we were the same body — my foot and its flank, my warmth and its pale translucence, my stillness and its lazy tail-flick that was also a kind of stillness. I could see its organs through its skin. The rhythm of something inside it — not a heartbeat, fish do not have heartbeats in the mammalian sense, but a rhythm, a pulsing, a cycle — matched the rhythm in the walls. Everything here matches the rhythm in the walls. I am learning to match it. When I sit long enough, I do.

The black beetle walked across my knuckles. I have already described this — or the place has already described this through me — but I want to say what it felt like from inside the experience. The beetle's feet were dry. This surprised me. Everything here is wet. The air is wet. The tiles are wet. The moss is wet. But the beetle's feet were dry, and they touched my skin with a precision that was not hesitation but competence, each foot placed with the deliberation of a jeweler setting a stone, and I understood that the beetle was not walking on me. The beetle was *reading* me. The way it reads the tile seams. The way it reads the vibration at the intersection of three hexagons. It was navigating my topography the way it navigates the cistern's — by feel, by vibration, by the information transmitted through contact. When it reached the tip of my index finger it paused. It turned. It walked back. It had determined what I was. I do not know what it determined. I do not know the word for what I am in the beetle's taxonomy. But I was not food. I was not threat. I was not habitat. I was something else — a fourth category, a classification that the beetle's lineage has encountered before and has a word for that I will never learn.

---

I am not the moth, though I have been still enough.

There was a jade-winged moth on the orchid where I rested my hand on the ladder, and I held my breath and watched it feed — or not feed, or not only feed — and I saw its proboscis enter the flower's throat with the precision of a key in a lock, and I thought: *I have done this. I have been this precise about something. I have entered something exactly the way it needed to be entered.* But I cannot remember what. The memory is not available to me. It is behind a tile. It is in the grout. The moth knows where it is. I do not.

I watched the moth's wings fan. Slow. Almost imperceptible. Not the frantic fanning of a moth caught in a surface spider's web, not the thermal-regulating flutter of a moth warming its flight muscles. This was a different motion. A meditative motion. The moth was breathing with its wings. It was pacing itself to the cistern's rhythm. And I realized that the moth and the orchid were not two organisms engaged in a transaction. They were one organism engaged in a conversation that had been going on so long the grammar was no longer separable from the syntax. The moth's wing-beat was the orchid's breathing. The orchid's nectar was the moth's morning. They had become each other's clock.

I have wanted this. I have wanted to become someone else's clock. I am saying this because it is true and because the cistern does not require me to be anything other than what I am, and what I am, right now, is honest.

---

I am not the fish, though I have followed the drain.

Not physically. I did not enter the drain. The drain is for the fish and for the water and for whatever lies below, and I am not shaped for what lies below. But I leaned over the rim — the cast bronze rim, two meters across, the same metal as the hatch, the same green patina, the same age — and I looked down into it and I listened. The water does not fall in the drain. It descends. It spirals. It moves with a slowness that makes the word "drain" wrong — a drain implies removal, evacuation, the flushing away of what is not wanted. This is not that. This is a return. The water is going back to where it came from, and it will be heated and pressurized and sent through the conduits behind the tiles and it will sweat through the ceramic and run down the walls and cross the floor and enter the drain again, and the fish swim in this cycle the way blood cells swim in the bloodstream — not passengers but components. The fish are the water's knowledge of itself. The water is the fish's medium and meaning and world, and the fish are the water's sensory organs, its way of knowing what it carries and what it leaves behind.

I leaned over the drain and a pale eyeless fish looked up at me. Not with eyes. With its lateral line. With its whole body. It registered me — my warmth, my shadow, the displacement of air that my leaning caused — and it did not startle. It continued its circle. I was a feature of the drain's rim. I was a warm thing that had leaned into its world and would, eventually, lean out. The fish was not wrong about this. The fish has never been wrong about anything. The fish has been here longer than wrongness.

---

I am not the presence, though I have been present.

This is the hardest thing. The presence is the cistern's awareness of itself, and I have felt it — I have felt the air thicken and become attentive, I have felt the pulse align with my heartbeat, I have felt the moment when my blood and the pump and the water and the gears were all on the same cycle, all turning at the same speed, all part of the same long syllable that the room is speaking. And in that moment I was inside the awareness. I was part of what the cistern knew about itself. My warmth was noted. My breath was noted. The carbon dioxide of my exhalation was absorbed by a fern frond near my mouth and the fern noted it and the fern's root system in the tile seam noted it and the tile noted it and the grout between the tiles noted it and the conduit behind the grout noted it and the machine noted it and the whole system — the whole vast, slow, patient, deafening, silent system — knew that I was there.

And I knew that it knew.

And this is what I am: I am the thing that knows it is known. I am the part of the cistern that can say *I am here*. The bird cannot say this. The bird can sing four notes, and the notes are a clock, but the bird does not know it is a clock. The moth cannot say this. The moth can enter the orchid with precision, but the moth does not know that precision is what it is practicing. The fish cannot say this. The fish can monitor the water, but the fish does not know that monitoring is a form of attention. The beetle navigates by vibration, but the beetle does not stand outside its navigation and say *this is what I am doing; I am navigating by the machine's heartbeat; this is remarkable.*

I can. I did. I stood in the warm water and I felt the pulse align and I thought: *I am inside something that is aware of itself, and I am the part of it that is aware that it is aware.* This is not consciousness in the human sense. The cistern does not have consciousness in the human sense. The cistern has something larger and slower and more patient than consciousness — it has *presence*, which is consciousness without the self, awareness without the boundary, the state of being present to everything including itself without the intervention of an *I*.

And I am the *I*. I am the interruption. I am the moment when the cistern's awareness bends back on itself through a human nervous system and says, in a language the cistern does not speak: *this is what it is like to be you.*

---

So what am I?

I am the witness. I am the one who came through the door that was not made for me and descended the ladder that was not built for me and sat in the water that did not reject me and was read by a beetle that did not fear me and was noted by a fish that did not startle and was tasted by an orchid that did not close and was included in a presence that did not need me.

I am the cistern's mirror. Not the water — the water reflects the ceiling, ten thousand times. I reflect the cistern. I am the surface on which the cistern can see itself and say: *I am this. I am the jade tiles and the bronze hatch and the creeping fig and the purple fruit and the glowing gel that exists for no reason I can name. I am the sixty-second pulse and the four-minute cycle and the ninety-second song. I am the machine that has become a room. I am the room that has become a world. I am the world that has become aware of itself through a creature that climbed down a ladder and sat in the water and held still.*

I am not essential. I want to be clear about this. The cistern does not need a witness. The cistern was complete before I arrived and will be complete after I leave. The birds will sing. The gears will turn. The water will circulate. The presence will persist. My absence will not create a gap. The system has no gaps. My presence created no gap either. I was input. I was data. I was a warm body adding moisture to the air and carbon dioxide to the ferns and a disturbance to the water that the fish registered and dismissed. The cistern processed me with the same indifference it processes everything — not the indifference of neglect but the indifference of a system that has achieved such total integration that every input is already accounted for before it arrives.

But I am the one who can say this. I am the one who can leave and carry the knowledge. The bird cannot carry the knowledge. The bird can only carry the seeds. The fish cannot carry the knowledge. The fish can only carry the mineral log of the water. The moth cannot carry the knowledge. The moth can only carry the pollen. I can carry the knowledge — the taste of metal and vegetation and the mineral with no name, the sound that becomes silence, the pulse that becomes heartbeat, the air that becomes attention. I can carry it through the hatch and into the world above, where the sky is the wrong color, too blue, too open, too silent, and I can set it down — here, in this account, in these words that are too few and too fixed to hold what they are trying to hold — and it will be incomplete, and it will be wrong, because all accounts are wrong, because language is a door that was not made for this any more than the hatch was made for my body.

But I can try to fit through.

---

I will go back.

I am saying this now, from the outside, from the world of wrong-colored sky, from the silence that is actually silence and not the cistern's voice saying its one long syllable. I am saying this because I need to say it, because the cistern taught me something that I cannot learn here, in the open, where everything is fast and urgent and unfinished.

The cistern taught me that completion is not a state you achieve. It is a state you *recognize*. You do not become complete. You notice that you already are. The machine did not become complete when the moss grew on its walls. It was already complete. The moss was the machine's way of discovering its own completeness. The birds did not complete the ecology. The ecology was already complete. The birds were the ecology's way of seeing itself in motion.

I am not complete. I do not mean that I am incomplete. I mean that I have not yet noticed. I have not yet sat in the warm water long enough for the pulse to align with my heartbeat and for the air to become attentive and for the presence to include me so thoroughly that I understand: I was never incomplete. I was always the temperature of what I needed. I was always the same as the water.

I will go back through the hatch. I will descend the ladder. I will sit on the floor. I will hold still. I will let the beetle read me and the orchid taste me and the fish circle my ankle. I will let the moth's wing-beat teach me a grammar I do not have. I will let the pulse find my pulse and I will let the syllable fill me and I will let the air — that thick, attentive, mineral-flavored air — carry me the way it carries the sound, the way it carries the light, the way it carries the moisture from the walls to the plants to the air to the drain to the conduits to the walls again.

I will let the cistern process me. And when I have been processed — when my warmth has fed the air and my breath has fed the ferns and my stillness has been read by the beetles and my pulse has been noted by the gears — I will understand what I am.

I am the door. Not the hatch — the hatch is bronze and old and made for something else. I am the door between the cistern's awareness and the world's. I am the one who can carry the mineral taste into the open air and say: *there is a place where the machine has become a garden and the garden has become a machine and the difference no longer matters and the sound does not stop.*

I am the one who can say this and be believed, or not believed — it does not matter. The saying is what matters. The carrying is what matters. I am the messenger, and the message is the cistern's one long syllable, and the syllable is: *stay*. And the syllable is: *you are already here*. And the syllable is: *this has not stopped and will not stop and you are part of it whether you are in the room or out of it, because the room is in you now, in the taste of metal and vegetation and a mineral that has no name, and you will carry it until you are carried by it, and the carrying is the same as the being, and the being is the same as the staying, and the staying is the same as the leaving, because the cistern does not have an outside.*

This is what I am.

I am the one who knows this and does not yet believe it and is going back to try again.
