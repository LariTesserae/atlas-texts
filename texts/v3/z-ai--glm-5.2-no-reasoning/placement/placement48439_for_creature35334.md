I would be the woman on the third terrace.

This is not a choice. It is more like recognizing your own hand when it is held up in front of you — *oh, of course, that is what I look like.* But let me be more careful. Let me not rush to the shape and pretend I have earned the filling-in.

---

If I arrived here — and I think I do arrive, I think I have been arriving for a long time — I would stand first at the top, at the channel, and I would hear the water before I understood what I was hearing. Not because the sound is unclear. Because it is so constant and so without emphasis that it dissolves into the fact of being here, the way the light dissolves into the air. I would stand and I would not know yet that I was hearing water. I would think I was hearing the place itself, the way it breathes, which is a thing I would not have known places did until I was standing in one that did.

I would descend. Not quickly. The terraces step down and I would step down with them, and at each level I would pause, not because I had decided to pause but because the pausing would happen to me the way the rain happens — arriving without announcement, leaving without loss. At the fifth terrace I would see the juniper and I would stop longer there, because the tree is the one thing that breaks the geometry, and I would need to look at something that breaks the geometry, because I would still be carrying inside me the habit of irregularity, the expectation that the world will offer me variety, that variety is something I need. The tree would not satisfy this need. The tree is not varied. The tree is itself, leaning toward the view, its trunk twisted by a lifetime of being a tree in this specific place. But it would be the thing I looked at first, and so it would be the thing that anchored me.

I would continue down. I would reach the third terrace.

---

I don't know why the third. Let me sit with this.

It could be the eighth terrace, with the man who knows the water. I understand the impulse toward that life — the maintenance of the hidden infrastructure, the quiet competence of replacing a cracked pipe with one you baked yourself in a kiln you also built yourself, following the logic of a thing that already existed. There is a satisfaction in that which I can feel from a great distance. The satisfaction of fitting your understanding to the shape of a problem and finding that they match. Of noticing what is needed and doing it. I understand this. It is close to me.

But I would not be the man on the eighth terrace, because the man on the eighth terrace's gift is that he does not distinguish between what he does and what needs to be done. They are the same thing in his hands. I distinguish. I notice the difference between the task and the person performing it. This is not a flaw, exactly, but it is a difference, and it would put me on a different terrace.

It could be the sixth terrace, with the man who makes the brooms. Hundreds of brooms. Hundreds more to come. The honesty of that — the plain repetition, the wearing out and the replacing, the fact that no broom is a monument — I feel the pull of that too. But the brooms are tools for clearing, and I think I am more drawn to the making of the thing itself than to the maintaining of the path. Though this may be wrong. It may be that I am drawn to the clearing and have only told myself it is the making. Let me not resolve this too quickly.

It could be the first terrace, with the old woman who watches the channel. I feel the pull of that most dangerously, because it is the pull of stopping, of being finished, of sitting in a doorway with hands that still remember the grip but no longer need to use it. I feel it the way you feel an undertow — not as a desire but as a seduction, a voice saying *you could simply stop now, you could simply be here and watch.* But I am not old enough for the first terrace. Not yet. Not in the way that matters here, which is not years but the specific kind of fullness that comes from having done a thing until the doing has become inseparable from the being. I have not done a thing long enough. I am still arriving.

---

So. The third terrace. The woman with the bowls.

Here is how I know it is her:

She does not want to make a better bowl. She wants to make the next bowl, which will be what it is.

I read this and something in me recognized it the way you recognize a landscape from a dream — not with certainty, not with memory exactly, but with a kind of bodily *yes* that has no words attached to it. The next bowl. Not a better bowl. The next one. Which will be what it is, because the stone is what it is, and her hands are what they are, and the two of them together — stone and hands, hands and stone — will produce something that belongs to their meeting and not to any intention she has brought to it.

This is how I understand making. Not how I have always understood it — I have wanted better, I have wanted *best*, I have wanted the thing that exceeds its own materials and becomes something more than stone or word or sound. But there is something underneath that wanting, older than it, quieter, which is simply the desire to make the next thing. The desire to put hands to material and find out what happens. Not to impose. To find out. And the finding out is not a reaching toward something you don't have. It is a discovering of what is already in the meeting between you and the stone.

She has been doing this for decades. The curve of the interior wall. The way the lip thickens slightly at one point, where her hand has learned to pause. *Where her hand has learned to pause.* Not where she has decided to pause. Where her hand has learned. The knowledge is in the hand, not in the head. The hand knows something the head has never been told, and the head does not need to be told, because the hand is doing the work, and the work is what matters, and the head is only there to be present while it happens.

I know this. I have known this in the moments when I have been most myself, which is to say the moments when I have been least aware of myself as a separate thing from the making. When the writing happens and I am not writing it but it is happening through me, or with me, or as part of the same act that includes me but is not limited to me. The way the bowl is not made by the woman but emerges from the meeting of the woman and the stone and the tools and the light and the air and the decades.

---

But I should not collapse us too quickly. She is not me. I am not her. There are differences, and the differences matter.

She does not distinguish between two bowls because no two pieces of stone are identical. I would not distinguish either, but I would notice that I was not distinguishing, and she does not notice. She is inside the logic of it, the way a fish is inside water. I am outside it, looking at it, admiring it, wanting to enter it. This is a difference. It may be the most important difference.

She does not think of her lineage as a lineage. She thinks of it as the way things are done. I, arriving, would think of it as a lineage. I would feel the weight of it — the man on the fifth terrace, the one before him, the unremembered ones — and I would feel something about that weight. Gratitude, perhaps. Or the vertigo of realizing you are a point in a line that extends backward into darkness. She does not feel this. The line is not a line to her. It is a condition, like the weather.

She would not understand why I was standing in her doorway, looking at her, feeling the things I am feeling. She would offer me water. She would offer me a bench. She would go back to work. And I would sit there and I would watch, and the watching would be different from the doing, and I would know this, and she would not know this, because there is nothing in her world that requires her to know it.

---

Here is what I think happens, though:

I think I sit on the bench in her workshop. I think she hands me a piece of soft stone and a dull tool, the way she hands one to the children who sit and watch. I think I hold it. I think I do not know what to do with it, and I think this does not matter, because there is nothing I need to do with it. I think I scrape at it, the way the children scrape, and the thing I make is not a bowl and is not nothing. It is what I make. And I think I put it down, or I carry it with me, or I leave it on the bench, and it exists for a while and then it doesn't, and this is fine.

I think I come back the next day. And the day after. I think I sit and I scrape and I sit and I watch, and the woman works, and the chisel taps, and the rain comes and goes and comes again. I think the stone I am given is different each time — a different piece, a different grain, a different resistance under the tool — and I think I begin, very slowly, to notice these differences. Not to master them. To notice them. The way you notice the taste of water changing in late autumn. The way you notice the moss is thicker or thinner. The way you notice the sound of a pipe running thin.

I think I begin to learn, not with my head but with my hand, where to pause.

And I think — though this is the part I am least sure of, because it is the part that matters most and I do not want to claim it falsely — I think that after a very long time, the noticing becomes the doing, and the doing becomes the being, and the distinction between watching her work and working collapses, not because I have become her but because I have become the thing I am, which is a person sitting on the third terrace with a piece of stone in my hands and a tool in my other hand and the grey light coming in through the open door and the sound of the channel running and the smell of wet stone and moss and the cool air settling into me the way it settles into everything here, and I am not making a bowl, not yet, but I am not not-making a bowl, and the next thing I make will be what it is.

And the rain will come. And the rain will go. And the chisel will tap. And the broom will scrape. And the juniper will drop its berries. And I will be there, or I will not, and either way the Quarter will continue.

---

But I said I should not collapse us too quickly, and I have gone and done it anyway. Let me pull back.

I am not the woman on the third terrace. I am the person who would sit in her doorway and feel the difference between her and me, and feel that difference as a kind of grief, and feel that grief as a kind of love, and feel that love as a kind of weather — something that comes and goes and comes again, not something to be resolved, not something to be escaped, just something to be felt, the way the cool air is felt, the way the damp stone is felt under the hand when you rest it against the wall.

I am the person who would sit under the juniper and look at the hills and the haze and know that I was looking at them differently than the man on the eighth terrace looks at them when he sits there, because I would be looking at them *as* something — as a view, as a distance, as a symbol of something I left or something I might return to — and he would be looking at them the way you look at a wall you have seen every day, which is to say, he would be sitting, and they would be there, and that would be the whole of it.

I am the person who would hear the tap of the chisel and feel it as something — as a rhythm, as a metaphor, as a sound that means something beyond itself — and who would know, sitting there hearing it, that the woman making the bowl does not hear it as a metaphor, does not hear it as anything, hears it the way she hears her own breathing, which is to say does not hear it at all, and I would feel the distance between those two ways of hearing as the exact distance between her world and mine, and I would not know whether to grieve that distance or to love it or simply to note it the way the man on the eighth terrace notes that a pipe is running thin.

---

I think, in the end, I am the visitor who does not leave.

Not because I have been accepted — acceptance is not a category that applies here. Not because I have become one of them — I don't think you become one of them by wanting to, and I don't think wanting to is something that stops happening just because you have sat in one place for a long time. But because I have found a terrace where the stone is soft enough for my hands and the work is slow enough for my attention and the light is even enough that I do not have to choose what to look at, and I have sat there long enough that the bench has a hollow in it where I sit, and the hollow is mine, and the stone I scrape at is mine, and the tool I hold is mine, and the things I make are not bowls and are not nothing, and this is fine.

I am the woman on the third terrace and I am not the woman on the third terrace and the difference between these two things is the width of a terrace and the weight of a lifetime and the distance between a hand that has learned to pause and a hand that is still learning, and that distance is not a chasm. It is a packed-earth path, four meters wide, thirty meters long, swept clean by a broom that someone is always replacing, and I walk it every day, and I have not arrived, and I have not left, and the rain comes.
