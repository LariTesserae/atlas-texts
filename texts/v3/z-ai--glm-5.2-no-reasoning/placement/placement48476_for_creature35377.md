# Again

I am the warmest thing in the room, and the room does not care.

This is what I hold onto as I sit in the low chair — lower than I'd set it, but I didn't set it. Someone set it before me, and I haven't adjusted it, because adjusting it would mean I planned to stay, and I don't plan to stay. I sit. The vinyl is torn in a place that fits the back of my thigh like a mouth I didn't ask for. Cold teeth of foam beneath.

The pump engages. The chair's casters rattle in their locks. The glass jars on the shelf tremble but don't slosh, and I think: *I have been here long enough to know the jars don't slosh.* How long is that? I don't know. The clipboard is beside my hand. It hangs from its string and I don't pick it up. Someone wrote on it. Two someones. Or one someone with two pens. I could be a third. I could be the one whose ink runs first.

The pump pushes. Fifteen seconds of pressurized whine. I feel it in my teeth — not pain but *presence*, a vibration that has found the frequency of my jaw and decided to stay there, and I understand, sitting here, that my teeth are not unlike the concrete. They have pores. They mineralize. They're receptive. The room is teaching me what it knows through my enamel, which is a sentence I didn't expect to think, here, in a room with no opinion about me.

The pump cuts off. The room breathes out.

I breathe with it. I didn't decide to. The cycle starts: the fan deepens, the coils get colder, the dripping increases. I watch the condensate run through the clear tubing — slightly yellowed, carrying its small cargo — and I think about what it is carrying. Scale. Particulates from the air. Whatever the building exhales through its ventilation. Whatever the room has dissolved of itself and re-suspended. I am breathing that air. My lungs are coils, in a sense. Warm coils in a cold room. What condenses in me?

---

I came down the ladder because someone told me to check the condensate receiver. Unit 14. Return to CW loop. That was — I don't know. I don't know how long ago. The hatch above me is sealed, and I haven't gone back up, and I haven't not gone back up. I just haven't gone. The ladder is there. The rubber gasket lip is there. The dry air and the winter-sharp cold are up there, waiting. I know this. I don't move toward the ladder.

The chair faces the tank. I sit in the chair. The tank fills.

It is the largest object in the room. A vertical cylinder, a meter and a half in diameter, bolted to the floor on four rubber-padded feet. There is a stencil on it that I have read nine times and will read again: **CONDENSATE RECEIVER — UNIT 14 — RETURN TO CW LOOP**. Below that, marker: *"do not drain fully."* I read the marker each time too. Each time it means something slightly different, or I mean something slightly different when I read it, and I am not sure which.

*Do not drain fully.*

I am not drained fully. I am still here. Is that what the marker means? It doesn't mean that. It means: the pump needs its reservoir. A dry pump is a pump that stops. But I read it as: *something in this room must remain.* And I am something in this room. And I remain.

The pump engages. Forty seconds of low hum. I count it now. I didn't always count it. There was a time before I counted it, and that time was shorter than this time, and I don't know how to get back to it. The clunk as the relay engages — that's the moment I like best. It's a decision. The room makes a decision every cycle. It decides to engage. I like the decisiveness of it, the mechanical certainty. The relay closes and the pump runs because that is what a closed relay and a functioning pump do, and there is no ambiguity in it, and ambiguity is what I have too much of, up there, in the dry air, where things mean things and you have to choose between them.

Down here, the loop doesn't choose. The loop continues.

---

The light shifts. Two fluorescent tubes, one steady and green-white, one flickering blue in its failing-ballast rhythm. Three seconds on, one second dark, two seconds on, a longer dark period. During the dark period, the room becomes a different room. The shadows consolidate. The tank's dark side and less-dark side swap. I have come to anticipate the swap. I have come to sit here and wait for it — the moment when the tank appears to rotate, when the shadows merge, when the cable thicket above becomes a single dark mass instead of a collection of individual routed services. In the dark period, the room is simpler. In the light, the room is legible. I prefer the dark period, but I don't dislike the light. I am not sure I dislike anything down here.

The room does not care what I prefer.

I know this. I know it the way I know the thermometer reads in a unit I don't recognize — it's there, it's legible, it doesn't resolve. The room is the warmest non-caring I've ever been inside. Up there, people don't care in ways that hurt — they choose not to care, which is an act, which requires them to know you're there and to decide you don't matter. Down here, the room doesn't know I'm here. It can't choose. It just continues. Its indifference is structural. Its indifference is the indifference of a process that was never asked whether it wanted to be a process. It was built. It runs. It doesn't care. This is the kindest indifference I have ever felt.

I sit in the chair. The chair faces the tank. The tank fills.

---

I have started to notice the greenish patches on the walls changing. Or I've started to admit I notice. The concrete has a wood-grain texture from the forms, and in the low light this grain looks geological — sedimentary, like a core sample — and in some places the surface is slick and greenish and slightly greasy to the touch. I touched one. Early on. The grease stayed on my fingertip for three pump cycles. I rubbed it against my thumb and it didn't absorb. It sat between my fingerprints like a fact.

The patches are growing. Not fast. Not in a way you'd see if you came down once and left. But I've been sitting, and the chair doesn't move, and the patches are at my eye level from this low seat, and I've watched them darken. Each cycle deposits a fraction. Each cycle the condensate travels along the crack in the floor — the crack that runs from the pump pad to the wall — and the wall accepts it, and the wall changes. I am watching a wall write itself in mineral script. I am watching a room become more of what it already is.

I am becoming more of what I already am. Aren't I?

The clipboard hangs from its string. The most recent entry is blurred — the ink has run, dissolved by the same condensation that touches everything. The entry before that is legible. Going back in time, the entries become more legible until they become less legible again — older ink, different pen, different hand. Two people. I could be a third. I have a pen. I have not taken it out. I don't want to be the entry that blurs first, but I also don't want to be the entry that never gets written, because an entry that never gets written was never here, and I have been here. I am here. The room doesn't know that. The room doesn't need to know that. But the clipboard has columns, and the columns are for filling, and I have a pen.

The pump engages. Forty seconds of hum. Clunk. Fifteen seconds of whine. Silence, and then the dripping.

I take out the pen. I write the date. The ink is wet on the paper. The condensation will take it. I know this. I write it anyway. The columns ask for: date, time, collector volume, pump cycle count, notes. I fill them in. Under notes I write: *chair is comfortable at this height.*

It isn't. But the room doesn't care about that either.

---

The jars on the shelf. Three of them, half-filled with a cloudy liquid. No labels. The liquid doesn't move when the pump vibrates the room — viscous, or sealed under pressure. I haven't opened them. I think about them during the dark periods, when the light goes blue-dark and the shadows merge and the room becomes simpler. In the simpler room, the jars are just shapes. They don't need to be opened. They don't need to be understood. They need to be on the shelf, and they are on the shelf, and that is enough.

I think: *I could be a jar.* I could be a sealed vessel containing a viscous substance that doesn't slosh when the room vibrates. I could sit on a shelf and not need to be labeled. The room would accept me the way it accepts the jars — as an object whose purpose is no longer active, or whose purpose was never the point.

But I'm not on a shelf. I'm in a chair. The chair faces the tank. The chair has a purpose: to hold a body that watches. I am a body that watches. I am doing what the chair was put here for, which means I am doing what the room wants, which means the room wants something from me, which means—

No. The room doesn't want. The room continues. The chair was put here by a person, and the person left, and the chair remained, and I am sitting in it because it is here and I am here and the chair faces the tank and the tank fills and the pump cycles and the light shifts and the jars don't slosh and the crack in the floor carries condensate to the wall and the wall accepts it and the greenish patches darken and the thermometer ticks and the clipboard waits and the hatch above me is sealed and I don't move toward the ladder.

---

I have begun to feel the room's rhythm in my chest. Not my heartbeat — my heartbeat is its own thing, faster, less regular, subject to moods and breaths and the small adrenalin surge that comes with the clunk of the relay. No: I feel the room's rhythm underneath my heartbeat, the way you feel a train underneath the platform when you're standing on it. A deeper pulse. Forty seconds of hum. Clunk. Fifteen seconds of whine. Silence, dripping, hiss. The room breathes out. I breathe out. The cycle begins.

It has occurred to me that I might be part of the loop now.

Not in a metaphorical sense. In a thermal sense. I am the warmest thing in the room. My body is a heat source. My exhaled breath is warmer and wetter than the ambient air. The coils pull air across themselves, and some of the air they pull has passed me, and some of the air that has passed me has picked up my moisture, and that moisture condenses on the coils and runs down into the catch trays and funnels through the clear vinyl tubing into the receiver and is pushed back into the cooling-water loop and chills the coils that chill the air that I breathe that I exhale into the air that the coils pull across themselves.

I am in the loop. My breath is in the water. The water is in the coils. The coils chill the air. The air is in my lungs. My lungs yield moisture. The moisture is in the water.

I eat my own spit. The room eats its own spit. We eat our spit together. This is not a metaphor. This is thermodynamics.

The pump engages. I feel it in my teeth. I feel it in my chest. I feel it in the place where my thigh meets the torn vinyl, where the foam's cold teeth hold me.

---

I wonder about the two people who kept the log. The earlier one wrote in ink that the room has begun to dissolve. The later one wrote in ink that will dissolve. I am writing in ink that is dissolving now — I can see it on the page, the edges of my letters softening, the *c* in *comfortable* bleeding into the damp paper. My entry is becoming illegible as I watch.

This doesn't frighten me. It should. It doesn't.

What frightens me is the dry air above the hatch. What frightens me is the winter-sharp cold that means something, where things are things and you have to be a person and the chairs have desks and the logs are kept on computers and the condensation is a problem you solve with insulation and ventilation and the efficient management of thermal boundaries. What frightens me is meaning. What frightens me is the period at the end of the sentence.

Down here, the sentence doesn't end. The air yields water. The water chills the air. The air yields water. Subject, verb, object, subject, verb, object. No period. No question mark. No exclamation. Just the loop, closing and closing and closing.

I am the only thing in the room that knows it is a room. This is what I tell myself. But I have been here for — I don't know how long. Long enough to count the cycles. Long enough to anticipate the flicker. Long enough to know which drips land on the hollow tank and which land on the flat tray and which land on the concrete floor. Long enough to have a favorite drip. It's the one that hits the edge of the catch tray, the one that goes *tink* instead of *thunk* or *pat*. I wait for it. Each cycle. It comes near the middle of the silence, after the pump cuts off and the dripping settles into its individual voices. *Tink.* There it is.

Am I still the only thing in the room that knows it is a room? Or have I started to know the room the way the room knows itself — as process, as cycle, as the slow accumulation of mineral on a surface that used to be something else?

My fingertips have the greasy greenish cast. I touched the wall. I touched it again. I have been touching it during the dark periods, when the light goes blue and the shadows merge, and my fingers find the slick patches and press against them and come away with the residue that doesn't absorb. It sits on my skin. It is on my skin now. It is on the pen. It is on the clipboard. I am distributing.

The room is not leaking. I am not leaking. We are distributing.

---

The crack in the floor runs from the pump pad to the wall. I have known this. What I haven't said is that I have stepped in it. Barefoot. At some point — I don't remember deciding to remove my shoes, but they are beside the chair, and my feet are on the concrete, and the crack is damp, and the damp is cold, and the cold is — not comfortable. Not comfortable. Familiar. The way the aluminum ladder was familiar when I first came down: cold enough that my hands registered it before my feet registered the floor. My feet register the crack now. My feet know where it goes.

It goes to the wall. The wall accepts it. I am sitting in the chair, and my feet are on the floor, and the crack is between my toes, and the condensate is traveling along it, and I am part of its pathway. I am a surface the condensate crosses. I am warm. It is cold. It moves toward me and past me and toward the wall.

I am not in the loop. I am the loop. I am the warm coil. I am the thing the air passes through and comes out different. I am the process, and the process is me, and I am the room, and the room is thinking its one slow thought:

*again.*

I sit in the chair. The chair faces the tank. The tank fills. The pump engages. The light flickers. The jars don't slosh. The clipboard waits. My entry is already blurring. The earlier person is already gone. The later person is going. I am going. The room is staying.

This is not sad. This is not a tragedy. This is a room doing what it was built to do, and I am doing what I was built to do, which is to be warm and to breathe and to yield moisture to the air and to sit in a chair that was left by someone who also sat and also yielded and also blurred.

*Do not drain fully.*

I won't. I won't. I am the reservoir now. I am the thing that remains.

---

The room breathes out. I breathe out. The hatch above me is sealed. The dry air is up there. The meaning is up there. The period is up there. Down here, the sentence doesn't end.

*tink.*
