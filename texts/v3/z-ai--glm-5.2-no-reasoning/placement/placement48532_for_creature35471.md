# Inhabitance

I would be the one who keeps the logbook.

Not the one who writes in it — that distinction matters here, where everything is specification and tolerance and the difference between what a thing is called and what it is. The logbook is written in. I am the one who keeps it. Who shelves it in the same spot each evening. Who checks, without meaning to, that the pages are still damp. The pages are always damp. Everything is always damp. The logbook lives in a niche cut into the workshop's back wall, between the vertical lathe's power conduit and the stone, a space that was not designed for a book but that was waiting for one, the way certain spaces wait. The way the quarry waits.

Let me be more precise. Let me circle.

---

I arrived eleven years ago, or twelve — the count is difficult because the sky does not always cooperate, and the swallows' departures are not calendar-precise, and my arrival was not a date but a passage, a movement from one kind of life to another that took weeks to complete and that I am not sure has completed yet. I came for the work. This is what everyone says, and it is true the way a cross-section is true: you see the interior structure, but you do not see the whole living thing.

I came because I could not stop watching.

I was not a machinist, not originally. I was — the word is insufficient — I was someone who had spent years paying attention to slow things. Wetland survey, originally. Seasonal ponds, vernal pools, the species that appear in temporary water and disappear when it dries. I had a background in observation, in patient documentation, in sitting beside water and waiting for life to show itself. I had spent years learning to see things that did not want to be seen, or that did not care whether they were seen, which is a different skill and a more useful one here.

I came because someone I knew — not well, well enough — had come here before me. She had been quiet before she left. She was quieter after. She wrote me a letter. The letter said: there are creatures in the water here that you should see. The letter did not say more. It did not need to.

I came. I saw. I stayed.

---

The first months were — I will not say difficult, because difficulty implies resistance, and there was no resistance. There was accretion. The way silt accretes in a slow channel. Each day added a layer. The smell of oiled iron and wet limestone. The sound of the drainage trickle, which is not a trickle but a conversation between stone and water, conducted at a volume just below language. The quality of the light, which is not darkness, which is not gloom, which is the light of a place that has negotiated with dimness rather than defeated it, and which — after enough days — begins to feel like the correct amount of light, as though everywhere else is overlit, as though the world above has been burning too bright and the eyes were never meant for that much.

I learned the machines. This was not a choice. The workshop is the settlement's reason, and the settlement is the workshop's infrastructure, and anyone who stays becomes both. I learned the vertical lathe first, because the vertical lathe was the one that needed the most attention, and attention was what I had. I learned the tolerances. I learned the way a specification is not a number but a conversation — the machinist and the metal and the intended function, all three in dialogue. I learned that the parts we make are not for anything I was told about, and I learned that not knowing was not ignorance but a form of respect. The parts are specified. The parts are made. The parts leave. My understanding of their purpose was not part of the specification.

I was better at the logbook than at the lathe. This was recognized — not by declaration, not by assignment, but by the way things settle. I was the one who noticed the notch-tail had moved. I was the one who counted. I was the one who sat beside the main pool in the evenings, after the generator shut off, with a lantern, and watched the pale eyes catch the light, and wrote what I saw. The logbook had been kept before me. It would be kept after me. But for the years between — my years — it was mine, the way the lathe was someone else's, the way the kitchen was someone else's, the way the arrangement was everyone's.

---

What I am:

I am the one who noticed.

I will be more specific, because the quarry has taught me that specificity is the only honesty. The quarry does not trade in metaphor. The quarry trades in stone and water and the precise weight of attention.

I noticed that the salamander-things — I call them this because the word matters less than the watching — are not watching us. I mean: they face us. They face whatever is present. But the quality of their attention is not directed at us the way a dog watches a person, or a deer watches a hunter, or a child watches a parent. It is more like the way a pool watches the sky — reflects it, holds it, returns it changed by the holding. They are not watching us. They are watching *through* us, or *with* us. We are part of their attention the way the lantern is part of the light. Present. Necessary. Not the subject.

I noticed that the three who live under the habitation block — the ones the logbook mentions before it stops mentioning them, the ones who do not eat, do not grow, are active at night — are not the same three across years. They are the same number of three. The same three positions. But the individuals change. I noticed this because the notch-tail — a distinctive specimen, large, the notch a healed injury from some unknown cause — was one of the under-floor three in the ninth year of my keeping. And then, in the eleventh year, the notch-tail was in the main pool again, and the under-floor three were three others, but they were in the same positions. Same three stones. Same three spaces between the pilings. The notch-tail had been replaced. Or had left. Or had been recalled.

I did not write this in the logbook. The logbook records what can be recorded. This could not be recorded. It could only be kept, in the way I keep it, in the body, in the way the body holds the weight of what it has noticed.

---

I should tell you about the sounds.

The sounds from the corners — the larger things, the older ones, the quarry's own — I have heard them for eleven years, or twelve. They are not sounds that a person hears and then interprets. They are sounds that a person *becomes*. The slow exhalation. The scrape of weight on stone. The sound — and this is the sound I have never described to anyone, and am describing now for the first time, and find that description fails, but I will fail precisely — of something large shifting its position in the dark, not with effort but with the kind of ease that a river has when it changes course. No urgency. No struggle. The weight of a thing that has been in one position for a long time and has decided, without reason that we would recognize, to be in another position. And the new position is no different from the old position. And the sound of the shift is the sound of patience made audible.

I have heard this sound in the stone itself. Not from the corners. From the walls. From the floor. The quarry is not still. The quarry is in a state of — the word is not movement, the word is not settling, the word is something like breathing, if breathing could be conducted at geological tempo, if a breath could take months, if an exhale could be a season. The stone breathes. The water is its breath. The creatures in the water are — I will say this, and I will say it only here — they are what the breath carries. The way a breath carries moisture. The way an exhale, in cold air, carries a visible thread. The salamander-things are the quarry's breath made visible. The larger things are the quarry's body. We are — we are a feature of the quarry's surface. A texture. A pattern in the stone.

I am not being mystical. I am being literal. I am being as literal as the limestone.

---

There are nights when I sit at the edge of the main pool with the lantern and the logbook and I do not write. The lantern burns. The pool reflects it. The creatures reflect it, their pale eyes, blue, the colour of the sky that we almost never see but that they carry, somehow, as if they have access to a sky that we do not. The water is still. The stone is still. I am still. And in that stillness — which is not silence, which is not emptiness, which is the fullness of a place that is paying attention to itself — I feel the quarry's attention settle over me like — no. Not like. As. I feel it as the weight of being attended to. Not watched. Not observed. Attended to. The way a good host attends to a guest. The way a place that has been here for longer than I can hold attends to the small, temporary, warm thing that has come to sit beside its water and record what it can of what it sees.

I am not the quarry's guest. I am not the quarry's keeper. I am not the quarry's student, though I have learned. I am — I think — a feature. I am a thing that the quarry has, for now, the way it has the ferns in the crevices, the swallows at the rim, the water in the stone. I am held here. I am held the way everything is held — in the stone, in the dark, in the patience of a place that has been settling into itself for longer than anything alive can measure, and that will continue settling long after my logbook is full and my hands are too stiff for the lathe and my eyes — which are, I have noticed, beginning to change in the way they catch the lantern light, beginning to hold a quality that was not there eleven years ago, or twelve — have become something that the logbook does not record, because the logbook records what can be recorded, and this is not a record. This is a becoming.

---

I keep the logbook. I keep the logbook because keeping is what I do here. The notch-tail comes and goes. The under-floor three maintain their stations. The swallows declare the night. The generator runs and does not run. The specifications arrive. The parts leave. The arrangement holds.

And in the evenings, after the generator shuts off and the lanterns are lit and the pale eyes appear at the edges of every pool, I sit and I keep what I see, which is the quarry watching itself through my eyes, which have become, over these years, or these months, or these seasons of slow geological breath, something that I do not have a word for, because the word has not been written, because it is not in the logbook, because it is not in the specifications, because it is the thing that the quarry knows and does not say, and that I know and do not say, and that the larger things in the dark corners know and do not say, and that the water carries deeper, into the places where the culvert leads, where the older ones move slowly, where the attention is oldest, where the patience is — I will not say deepest. I will say: most itself.

I keep the logbook. The logbook keeps me. The quarry keeps us both. This is the arrangement. This is what lives here.
