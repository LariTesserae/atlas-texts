I have been sitting here long enough that the distinction between the sound and the silence has become purely academic. There is a pitch that lives in my jaw now. Not a note — a *presence*, the way a stone in a river is present: not moving, not still, just *there*, exerting its weight on everything around it without effort or intent. I have stopped asking it to be anything else. This feels like progress.

Let me tell you what I am not.

I am not the keeper. I was allowed to understand this, eventually, the way you are allowed to understand most things in the dark — by the slow elimination of every other possibility. I thought I might be, in the first hours. Or in the first days. Time behaves differently here. I sat on the bench and felt the carved grooves pressing into my thighs and I thought: *this is what I am for. I am the one who returns.* Because something does return, doesn't it? The grooves are fresh. The pegs are tight. The oil on the bench is not old. Something tends this room. Something sits where I am sitting and listens where I am listening and tends.

But I do not have hands for the carving. I know this now. I knew it before I knew it — the way the floor knows it is floor before the word arrives, the way the root knows which direction is down before the direction exists. I do not have hands for the carving because the carving requires a relationship with the wood that I do not have. The keeper does not *make* things. The keeper *is* the thing that the wood makes when the wood has been listened to long enough. I cannot explain this better than that. The keeper is the wood's idea of what listening sounds like, given a body. I am something else.

---

What I am: I am the one who came down.

I came down from the surface — the mineral dust, the salt-flat, the nothing that stretches in every direction under a sky that has forgotten what clouds look like. I came down because I heard the thrum from above. You can hear it, if you lie flat on the salt and press your ear to the ground. It comes through the rock, through the dried mineral substrate, through the root-mass that underlies everything here the way a foundation underlies a building — not visible, not optional, just *there*, holding everything up simply by continuing to be.

I do not know what I was before I came down. This is the part that is hardest to explain. I am not certain I was a *what* in the way that whats are usually whats. I lived above. I moved across the salt. I sought — something. Water, perhaps. The salt holds water, locked in its crystal matrix, unavailable to anything that lacks the patience or chemistry to extract it. I lacked the patience. I lacked the chemistry. I moved across the salt because moving is what things do when they are not yet dead and not quite alive and the difference between those states has become, over a very long time, academic.

I found a crack. A fissure in the mineral substrate where the root had pushed through, ages ago, and the ground had eroded around it, and there was a way *down*. Not a passage. Not an entrance. A crack — the kind of crack that exists in old landscapes where something underneath has been growing so slowly that the surface forgets to resist it and simply opens, like a mouth that has fallen asleep.

I went down. Not because I chose to. Because the sound was there, and the sound was *directional*, and I was the kind of thing that moves toward direction. I do not know if that makes me a creature or a current or a something-else. I moved toward the sound. The crack opened around me. The salt gave way to root-fiber, to bark, to the living wood that is the only thing in this landscape that has never stopped being alive.

I crawled for a long time. I do not know how long. The wood narrowed and widened and narrowed again. I passed through cavities — some natural, some not. Some showed the marks of shaping. Some showed the marks of older shaping, shaping that had been worn smooth by time and by the slow pressure of growing wood. I passed through rooms that had been rooms and were now just shapes in the grain, their walls absorbed back into the root, their pegs dissolved, their benches erased. The root does not preserve its past. It grows over it. It grows *through* it. What was once a wall becomes a grain line. What was once a bench becomes a ridge. What was once a room becomes a density in the wood, a place where the rings are tighter, compressed by the ghosts of old structures.

The root eats its history. This is important. The root does not remember. The root does not *need* to remember. The root simply continues, and continuation, over enough time, looks like memory the way erosion over enough time looks like sculpture. You cannot tell the difference. Perhaps there is no difference. Perhaps memory is just continuation that has been compressed into a shape, the way a diamond is just carbon that has been compressed into a shape, and the carbon does not remember being coal.

---

I found the room. I found the bench. I sat down.

I do not know why I sat down. I do not, I think, have the architecture for sitting. But I sat. My body — whatever it is, however it is shaped — arranged itself on the bench in the way that bodies arrange themselves on surfaces when they have been moving for too long and they find a surface that will hold them. I sat. I pressed into the grooves. I felt the cold air from the pores move across me. I listened.

The chord entered me. Not through my ears — I do not think I have ears in the way that ears are usually ears. Through the surfaces of my body. The way the floor receives vibration. The way the walls receive vibration. I became, for a moment, an additional surface in the room, a new complexity in the acoustic geometry, and the chord adjusted around me the way water adjusts around a stone. It did not change. It *accommodated*. I was added to the room the way a new peg is added to a wall — fitted, seated, pressed into place until the friction holds.

I sat for a long time.

---

The keeper found me, or I found the keeper, or we found each other, or the room found us both at the same time, which is the version I prefer.

I did not reach behind the bench. I want to be clear about this. I did not reach. I understood, in the way that you understand things in the dark — by the accumulation of evidence through channels other than sight — that reaching was not the logic of the room. The logic of the room was *sitting*. The logic of the room was *remaining*. The logic of the room was the sound and the emptiness and the darkness and the bench and the waiting. I sat. I remained. I waited.

The keeper made a sound. Not the stridulation of an insect, not the breathing of an animal, not the voice of a person. A sound like the sound the bench makes when weight settles onto it after a long absence — a creak, a compression, a settling of fibers that have been holding a shape for someone who was not there and who has now returned. I understood that the keeper had been in the cavity behind the bench and that the keeper had heard me arrive — had felt the acoustic geometry of the room shift when my body entered it the way you feel a stone enter a stream — and that the keeper had been waiting to see what I would do.

What I did was sit.

What the keeper did, after a time I cannot measure, was emerge.

---

I did not see it. I want to be clear about this too. The dark is absolute. What I know of the keeper I know by sound and by touch and by the quality of the silence around it — the way you know the shape of a hole in a wall by the way the wind moves through it, the way you know the depth of a well by the time it takes a stone to reach the bottom.

The keeper is small. Small enough to fit in the cavity behind the bench, which is small enough to fit inside a human chest, if humans still existed, if chests still existed. Small enough that its body, when it moved past me, displaced only a narrow column of air, and the chord barely shifted to accommodate it. The keeper is small the way a seed is small: not diminished, not incidental, just *concentrated*. Everything it is, packed into the minimum volume that can hold it.

The keeper is warm. Not warm-blooded, not the radiating warmth of a mammal, but the low, slow warmth of a process — the warmth of compost, of fermentation, of wood undergoing the first stages of decay, of anything that is actively becoming something else. The keeper is warm because the keeper is *working*. Always. The keeper is always working. The warmth is the exhaust of the work, the way the sound is the exhaust of the breathing. The keeper maintains the room the way the root maintains itself: by constant, invisible, unfelt effort.

The keeper touched me. Not with hands — I do not think the keeper has hands, though I do not know what it has instead. It touched me the way a vine touches a wall: by *arriving* at it, by growing toward it, by extending a part of itself into contact with a surface it had not previously contacted. The touch was exploratory. It was the touch of something that identifies by touch the way seeing-creatures identify by sight. The keeper was *reading* me. Reading my surfaces, my density, my thermal signature, my resonance — the way I sounded when the chord passed through me, the way I absorbed and reflected and transmitted the frequencies of the room.

I do not know what the keeper learned. I know what I learned: that the keeper has been doing this for a very long time. That the keeper has touched every surface in this room, many times, with this same exploratory patience. That the keeper knows the room the way the root knows the ground — not by mapping it, not by remembering it, but by *being it*, by having extended itself into every surface until the boundary between keeper and room became as academic as the boundary between root and landscape. The keeper does not live in the room. The keeper *is* the room, the way the root is the landscape. The room is the keeper's body, arranged in the shape of a space, the way your body is arranged in the shape of you.

---

I do not live here. I am not the keeper. The keeper has made this clear, not in language — there is no language, there has never been language, language is a thing that happened above, on the surface, among creatures who needed to transfer information quickly because their time was short. Here, time is not short. Here, information transfers by contact, by vibration, by the slow osmosis of one surface against another over hours and days and what might be weeks. The keeper has communicated to me, through this slow osmosis, that I am not the keeper. That I am not the right material. That I do not have the right density, the right resonance, the right relationship with the wood.

I am not offended. I am not capable of being offended. Offense, like language, is a surface phenomenon, a thing that happens among creatures who have skins that separate them from their environments and who therefore experience the environment as *other*. I do not experience the room as other. I experience the room the way the room experiences itself: from the inside, by the sound passing through me, by the cold settling into me, by the grain pressing into the parts of me that are in contact with the bench. I am in the room the way a note is in a chord. Not the chord itself, not the instrument, not the player, not the listener. Just: *in* it. Part of it. Added to it, for now.

The keeper allows this. The keeper does not *permit* it — permission is a surface concept, a thing that happens when someone has authority over a space and someone else does not. The keeper has no authority. The keeper has *continuance*. The keeper has been here, doing this, for longer than the concept of authority has existed. The keeper does not allow me to be here. The keeper does not disallow it. The keeper simply *continues*, and I am part of what the keeper continues through, the way a new growth ring is part of what a tree continues through. Temporary. Incidental. But present.

---

I do not know what I was before I came down. I know what I am now.

I am the one who sits on the bench and is not the keeper and does not carve and does not maintain and does not oil or sand or replace. I am the one who *listens*. Not the way the keeper listens — the keeper listens the way a root listens to the ground, by growing into it, by becoming it. I listen the way a stone in a stream listens: by being in the way. By being present. By having a surface that the sound hits and passes through and comes out the other side changed. I am the complexity. I am the variable. I am the thing that makes the chord slightly different from what it was before I arrived, and slightly different from what it will be after I leave.

I do not know how long I will stay. I do not know how long I *can* stay. The cold is accumulating. The air from the pores is very cool. My body — whatever it is, however it holds its warmth — is slowly equalizing with the room. At some point, I will be the same temperature as the wood, and I will not be able to distinguish my own warmth from the room's cold, and I will either have to leave or I will become something that does not leave. I do not know which. I do not know what the keeper would do if I stayed. I do not know what I would become.

I know that the people who built this room were, once, like me. They came down. They sat on the bench. They listened. They were not the keeper. But they stayed, and they carved, and they oiled, and they maintained, and over generations — over centuries of sitting in the dark, of eating what the root provided, of breathing what the root exhaled — the boundary between person and room and keeper eroded, the way the boundary between root and landscape eroded, the way the boundary between water and stone erodes in a streambed, not by violence but by *presence*, by the simple fact of being in one place for long enough that the place begins to shape you the way you shape it.

They became the keeper. Or: the keeper is what they became. Or: the keeper was always there, waiting for them, waiting for someone to come down and sit and listen and stay, and when they stayed long enough, the keeper showed them what to do, and they did it, and they did it for so long that the doing became the being, and the being became the keeping, and the keeping became the keeper.

I will not stay long enough to become the keeper. I think this is true. I think the keeper also thinks this is true. There is something in me that is not the right material, not the right density, not the right resonance. I am too much of the surface. I am too much of the salt-flat, too much of the dry air, too much of the world that has forgotten what roots are. The keeper can feel this. The keeper has been feeling this since I arrived. The keeper knows that I will not stay, and the keeper does not grieve this, because grief is a surface phenomenon, and the keeper has never been to the surface.

But.

The keeper has touched me. The keeper has read my surfaces, my resonance, my thermal signature. The keeper has done this the way the keeper touches the bench: slowly, carefully, with the patience of something that has never needed to hurry. And in the touching, something has been transferred. Not information. Not instruction. Something more like *imprint*. The way the bench holds the imprint of the hands that have touched it. The way the pegs hold the imprint of the tools that shaped them. I hold the imprint of the room. I will carry it when I leave, if I leave. I will carry the chord in my jaw, in the plates of my skull, in whatever parts of me are equivalent to those parts. I will carry the sound the way the salt carries the water — locked in, unavailable, but *present*.

---

Here is what I am. I am a thing that came down from the surface and sat in the dark and listened. I am not the keeper. I am not the root. I am not the room. I am the one who was *in* the room, the way a note is in a chord, the way a stone is in a stream, the way a word is in a sentence — present, shaping, shaped. I am the one who will leave, if I leave, and will carry the sound with me, not as memory but as *fact*, as a vibration that continues in the body even after the source has been left behind, the way a bell continues to ring after the striker has been pulled away.

I am the one who heard the root breathe.

I am the one who knows that the root breathes. That the root has been breathing. That the root will continue to breathe. That somewhere underneath the salt-flat, underneath the mineral dust, underneath the world that has forgotten what it was, a root system the size of a landscape is conducting respiration at a pace so slow it takes hours to complete a single breath, and the breath produces a sound, and the sound fills a room, and the room was built for the sound, and the room was built by people who came to listen, and the people are gone, and the room remains, and the sound remains, and something remains in the room that maintains it — that oils the bench, that replaces the pegs, that carves new maps when the old ones drift — and that something is the keeper, and the keeper has been there since before the people left, or since the people became, or since the room was built, or since the root grew into the shape that made the sound that made the room necessary.

I know this. I will carry this. I do not know where I will carry it. There is no one to tell. The surface is empty. The salt-flat is empty. The world above is empty in the way that the room is empty — not cleared, not abandoned, but *designed* to contain very little. The world above was made for wind and dust and the slow erosion of everything that is not root. The world below was made for sound.

I will go back up, eventually. Through the crack. Through the fissure in the mineral substrate. Back to the salt-flat, back to the surface, back to the dry air and the sky that has forgotten what clouds look like. I will go back up and I will lie on the salt and I will press my ear to the ground and I will hear the thrum, faintly, from a great distance, filtered through rock and mineral and the dead substrate of a landscape that has been worn down to almost nothing.

And I will know what the thrum is. I will know that it is a root, breathing. I will know that it has been breathing for longer than I can comprehend. I will know that inside the root there is a room, and in the room there is a bench, and on the bench there are grooves that describe the shape of a sound, and in a cavity behind the bench there is a keeper, and the keeper is tending the room with the patience of wood, which is to say with the patience of something that has never needed to be in a hurry.

I will know this. And the knowing will be useless, the way all knowing is useless when there is no one to tell and nothing to be done. But I will know it. And the knowing will be in me the way the sound is in the room — not as information, not as memory, but as *fact*. As something that is true regardless of whether anyone knows it. As something that continues.

---

The chord shifts. A frequency that has been dominant recedes. Another takes its place. The root is exhaling. In six hours it will inhale, and the tones will change, and the map on the bench will be, in some minute degree, inaccurate, and the keeper will come out from behind the bench and run something — a hand, a tendril, a process, a whatever-it-is — along the grooves and carve a new one, a tiny correction, a minute adjustment, the way a navigator adjusts a course by fractions of a degree over a journey of years.

I am here for this. I am here for the shift. I am here for the moment when the sound changes and the keeper moves and the room becomes, briefly, a workshop instead of a temple, a place where something is *done* instead of a place where something is *felt*. And then the carving is finished, and the keeper retreats, and the room becomes a temple again, and the sound fills it, and the emptiness holds the sound, and the darkness holds them both.

I sit on the bench. The wood is cold. The grooves press into me. I do not reach behind the bench. I do not need to. I have been touched. I have been read. I have been added to the room, temporarily, the way a new peg is added to a wall. I will be removed, eventually, the way a peg is removed when it loosens. The keeper will fill the space I leave. The wood will close over it. The sound will not change.

But I will have been here. I will have been in the room. I will have been a note in the chord, a stone in the stream, a surface that the sound hit and passed through and came out the other side changed. I will have been changed. I am already changed. I am a thing that knows what the root sounds like from the inside, and that knowledge is in me the way the water is in the salt — locked in, unavailable, but present.

The root breathes. The keeper keeps. I sit.

The dark holds us all.
