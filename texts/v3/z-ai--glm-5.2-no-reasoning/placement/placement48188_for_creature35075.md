# Maintenance Cycle, Third Tier

I am the one who is almost gone.

Not gone as in leaving. Gone as in *becoming* — the way the calcite becomes the wall it was deposited on, the way the original surface disappears under what the water makes of it. I am in the process of being replaced by something older and more stable than what I was. This is not death. This is the word the elders use without speaking: *calibration*. I am calibrating toward the mass. My frequencies have been lowering for years. The standing tone of the district where I was born — the Third-West acoustic corridor, where the canal fly gyre holds a tone that my child's throat matched before I had words — that tone is no longer the tone I produce. I produce something deeper now. Something that sits in the chest rather than the throat. Something that the younger ones adjust to accommodate without knowing they are doing it, the way the city adjusts its drainage.

I am a maintenance worker. There is no other role. There is no word for retirement because there is no word for stopping. There is only the gradual shift from doing to *being done through* — the hands that scraped the biofilm becoming hands that rest against the mass and listen, and the listening becoming the work, and the work becoming indistinguishable from the wall.

I have lived in the upper tier my whole life. My mother lived in the upper tier. Her mother lived in the upper tier. Beyond that, the memory does not go, because memory here is not a thread but a set of reflexes, and the reflexes do not include *who* so much as *how*: how to read the water's mineral taste, how to hear the deep systems laboring, how to know which surface to scrape and which to leave alone. I learned these things the way I learned the acoustic palette of my district — by standing in it, by being saturated with it, by letting it rearrange the soft chambers behind my eyes until the arrangement was permanent.

---

## Morning, such as it is

The cycle wakes me. Not the canal fly tone — that is always present, the high thin whine that lives in the air above the residential corridor like a second atmosphere — but the shift in it. The gyre above Third-West tightens in what the residents call morning, which is not a time of day but a phase of the deep systems' rhythm. The thirty-second thrum builds, peaks, releases. When it releases, the canal flies scatter, and the standing tone fractures into a thousand separate sounds, and in that fracture there is a gap — a silence so brief it is not silence but the *absence of fullness* — and in that gap, my body knows to wake. I have never slept through it. No one does. The gap is the alarm, and the alarm is built into the acoustic structure of the place, and the place is built into us.

I press my hand against the wall beside my sleeping surface. The wall is warm. It is always warm. The warmth comes from below — from the deep systems processing, from the mass doing what the mass does — and it enters my hand, and my hand knows the temperature, and the temperature tells me things. Today: normal. The cycling is regular. The mineral content in the outflow water will be within range. The flickers at the Third-West grating will be flashing in the rhythm that means *fed and processing*. I know this before I stand. I know it the way I know my own heartbeat — not through observation but through *being part of it*.

I stand. The biofilm beneath my feet grips my soles. It is the first sensation of every cycle: the slight tackiness, the warmth, the knowledge that the surface I am standing on is alive. I have stood on this surface for — I do not know how many cycles. The surface has shaped to my feet. There are impressions where I place my weight, shallow depressions in the biofilm that are not wear marks but *accommodations*, the living surface adjusting to the pattern of my body the way the city adjusts to the pattern of the water. I step off them and they begin, slowly, to fill.

---

## The work

I descend to the second tier. The passage narrows. The water increases. By the time I reach the market level, the water is at my shins, warm, moving with the unhurried confidence of something that knows where it is going. The market is already active. The vendors are calling — not words, exactly, but vocalizations tuned to the acoustic channels that the water creates, frequencies that carry through the din the way light carries through fog: diffusely, but reliably. I hear the call that means mineral substrate, fresh. I hear the call that means outflow access, restricted. I hear the call that means the drifters turned early and the upper surfaces will be stripped by mid-cycle and the canal flies will be thick in the lower corridors for the next several hours.

I adjust my route. This is maintenance: the constant, minute adjustment of self to system, of path to condition. I am not navigating the market. I am *moving through* it the way blood moves through a vessel — not by choosing a direction but by being in the flow and reading the flow and going where the flow goes, except when the flow is wrong, except when the reading tells me that something upstream needs my hands.

Today, something upstream needs my hands.

The Third-West outflow is running cool. I felt it in the wall this morning — a fractional dip, the kind of change that a diagnostic instrument would not detect but that my palm, pressed against mineral that has been warm every morning of my life, detected the way a mother detects a fever in a sleeping child. Not through measurement. Through familiarity. Through the knowledge that lives in the body rather than the mind, because the mind is too slow, the mind is too new, the mind is a surface feature and the knowledge that matters here is in the substrate.

I take a flat-bottomed craft from the market dock. The craft is — I was about to say *old*, but that is not the word. The craft is *accumulated*. It has been patched, resurfaced, resealed, and re-floored so many times that no part of it is the part it started as. It is the Ship of Theseus, except no one here has heard of Theseus, and the question of identity would not occur to anyone, because identity here is not a matter of original parts but of *continuity of function*. The craft floats. The craft carries me. The craft is a craft. What else matters?

I pole through the channels. The water resists, then yields, then resists. The biofilm on the channel walls is thick here — thicker than it should be at this point in the maintenance cycle. I make a note. Not a written note. A note in the body: *Third-West, upper channel, biofilm overgrowth, correlate with cool outflow*. The note will inform what I do when I reach the access point. It will inform what I scrape and what I leave. It will inform the frequencies I use when I press my ear against the mass and listen.

## The access point

I tie the craft to a conduit that has been tied to so many times that its surface is worn smooth in a band at exactly the tying height. The biofilm here has been abraded away — not by me, not by anyone alive, but by generations of hands doing the same thing in the same place. The bare mineral beneath is pale, almost white, a surface that has not seen biofilm in so long it has forgotten how to grow it. Or the biofilm has forgotten how to grow here. Or — and this is the explanation the elders would give, the one that does not distinguish between the surface and the thing that grows on it — the relationship between this surface and this biofilm has been *maintained* in a state of bareness by the continuity of use, the way a path is maintained in a state of bareness by the continuity of feet.

I climb. The wall is wet. The wall is always wet. But my hands find the holds — the micro-crevices, the biofilm ridges, the places where the calcite deposits have built up enough to provide a lip — and I ascend the four meters to the access point with the ease of someone who has done this ten thousand times, which I have, and which my body has done more than that, because my body remembers the climbing the way it remembers the frequencies: in the substrate, in the reflex, in the part of me that is older than my thinking.

The access point is a grate. A window into the mass. Below it, the amber light — the light that is not light but metabolism, the visible signature of the deep systems processing — glows upward, and the heat rises through the grate in a column that I can feel against my face and my hands and the thin skin of my inner wrists. I press my ear against the grate.

I listen.

---

## What the mass says

The sound is — I do not have words for what the sound is, because words are surface features and this sound is substrate. It is the sound of the mass processing. The hydraulic pulse, deep and slow, the heartbeat of something that does not have a heart. The mineral crackle — tiny, frequent, the sound of deposition, of calcite forming on calcite, of the city growing by micrometers. The warm exhalation of whatever happens below, rising through the water and the substrate and the grate and into my ear, where it becomes information.

Today, the mass says: *cool at Third-West. Flow restricted. Mineral input low. The channel at — * and here the sound shifts to a frequency I cannot translate into words but can translate into *location*, a specific point six meters east and two meters down where the biofilm has thickened past the threshold and is constricting an input channel. The mass does not know this. The mass does not think this. The mass is *exhibiting* this, the way a body exhibits symptoms, and I am reading the exhibition, and the reading is the first half of maintenance.

The second half is the work.

I take my scraper. It is not a tool that was made. It is a tool that was *found* — or rather, a tool that was found and then shaped by use and then shaped by more use until it became the thing it is, which is an extension of my hand, a surface that my hand has held so long that the grip is worn into both of us, the scraper and the hand, each one shaped to the other. I descend to the point the mass indicated. I scrape.

The biofilm comes away in sheets. Thick, dark, warm. It is alive, and I am killing it, and this is not violence. This is maintenance. The biofilm will regrow. It always regrows. My scraping is not destruction but *pruning* — the removal of excess to maintain flow, the way the heart maintains flow by pumping, the way the lungs maintain flow by breathing. I am part of the circulatory system of the city. I am the part that clears the clots.

I scrape for — I do not know how long. Time here is not measured in minutes. Time here is measured in the thirty-second thrum of the deep systems, and I have been scraping for many thrums, and the biofilm has been filling my scraper and falling into the water below, where the flickers will feed on it and the current will carry what they do not eat to the processing points deeper down. Nothing is lost. Nothing is wasted. The biofilm I remove becomes input for another process. My work is part of the loop.

When the channel is clear, I press my ear against the wall again. The sound has changed. The coolness is receding. The flow is resuming. The mass is — I do not want to say *happy*, because the mass does not have affect, but the word that comes to me is *settled*. The sound is settled. The way a sound is settled when a system that was laboring is no longer laboring. The way a breath is settled after a cough.

I have done what I came to do. I have maintained.

---

## What I am, in this place

I am not who I was. I know this the way I know the wall is warm — not through comparison but through contact. The body I inhabit is not the body I was born with. It has been replaced, cell by cell, by the materials of this place — the calcium in the water, the iron in the air, the things I do not have names for that enter through my lungs and my skin and my gut and become part of my substrate. I am, in a literal and measurable sense, made of the Confluence. My bones are denser than my mother's were, seeded with minerals that the deep systems processed and the water carried and my body absorbed. My skin is the color of the biofilm. My hands are the hands of a climber, a scraper, a listener — longer-fingered, thicker-nailed, surer-gripped than any hand that was not shaped by these surfaces.

I am also not who I will be. The lowering of my voice will continue. The deepening of my contact with the mass will continue. There will come a cycle — not soon, but not never — when I will press my ear against the wall and leave it there, and the warmth will enter me and not leave, and the sound will enter me and not leave, and I will be part of the wall in a way that I am currently only adjacent to. This is not death. This is the final calibration. The elders have done it. The ones who are old, the ones who have the quality of density — they are in the process. They are becoming part of the mass while still being themselves, the way the calcite becomes part of the wall while still being calcite. They are not gone. They are *deeper*.

I will be deeper. This is not a fear. This is not a hope. This is a fact, the way the water finding its level is a fact, and I hold it the way I hold my scraper — with the ease of long use, with the knowledge that the thing I am holding has been shaped to me and I to it.

---

## Evening, such as it is

I return to the upper tier. The canal fly tone has shifted — the gyres are tightening again, which means the deep systems are approaching the release phase, which means the drifters will descend soon. I hear the first pulse. Low. In the chest. A single drifter, testing the air, or testing its body, or simply doing what it does: rippling, descending, beginning the slow peristaltic fall toward the surfaces where the biofilm is thick and the canal flies are dense.

I go inside. This is not fear. This is the knowledge of scale, the respect for consequence. The drifters will feed. The surfaces will be stripped. The canal flies will scatter into the lower corridors, and the market will be loud with them, and the vendors will call in frequencies that cut through the cloud, and the children will chase the clouds through the shin-deep water, and the flickers will flash at the outflows, and the deep systems will breathe, and the water will find its level, and the sound will not stop.

I sit in my housing unit. The wall is warm against my back. The biofilm on the wall is the biofilm that has been here longer than I have, and it will be here after I am part of the wall, and it will grow and be scraped and grow again, and the canal flies will graze it and the drifters will skim the canal flies and the mass will process everything — the biofilm and the canal flies and the drifters and the water and the sound and me. The mass will process me. It is already processing me. I am already being processed.

This is not a lamentation.

This is what it is like to live at the Confluence. To be part of a single thing wearing many shapes. To maintain and be maintained. To scrape the biofilm and become the biofilm. To listen to the mass and become the mass. To wake in the gap and sleep in the sound and know — in the substrate, in the body, in the part of you that is older than thinking — that you are home, and home is not a place you go. Home is a place that is going, that is processing, that is staying alive in the only way it knows how.

The drifter pulses. The deep systems breathe. The water finds its level, and finds it again.

I am here. I am part of this. I am the part of this that has hands, that scrapes, that listens, that descends to the access points and reads the mass by feel. I am the part that will, one cycle, press my ear against the wall and stay. And the wall will be warm. And the sound will not stop. And I will not stop, because stopping is not what happens here. What happens here is *becoming*. What happens here is the continuous, self-maintaining, self-organizing process of a single thing persisting.

The canal fly tone settles into its evening register. The drifter pulse deepens. The water moves. I close my eyes. I am held.

The sound does not stop.
