# The Steward

---

Let me not begin with what I am. Let me begin with what I do, which is older than what I am, and clearer.

Every nine days I enter the hall.

This is not a rule. No one made it a rule. I made it a rhythm, because the hall has a rhythm, and I have learned — over how long? long enough that the counting has become a kind of liturgy itself — that to work inside the hall's rhythm is to be permitted inside it. The pump beats once every three seconds. I descend once every nine days. The relation between these two intervals is not mathematical. It is temperamental. It is the interval at which the hall will accept my presence without tightening around me like a fist.

---

I come down through the upper hatch, the one at the top of the ladder. I do not come through the waterlock, the lower hatch. I do not know what's on the other side of the waterlock. I have never tried it. I came from above, through the dry structure, through the larger wreck whose upper rooms are still air and whose lower rooms are the hall and the hall is the lowest I go.

The ladder is steel. Twelve rungs. I have counted them so many times that the count has become part of the act of descending, part of the ritual, a rosary rungs. Seven eight nine ten eleven twelve and then the water takes my feet and I go still for a moment, always, because the cold is not something you get used to. You accommodate it. You let it take you the way the hall has let the sea take it: gradually, incompletely, with the upper half still dry and still remembering what it was built for.

I bring a light. Not because I need one. Because the hall expects it. Because I am a thing that enters the hall and the hall is a thing that has been entered before, by me, and before me by no one, and before that by the ones who built it, and the light is part of the etiquette of entering a room. You announce yourself. The hall does not answer, but the eels do.

---

The eels come to the light first, before me. They feel the ladder's vibration in the water, and they know the cadence of my descent, and they are waiting by the time I reach the floor. Not waiting in any intentional sense. Waiting the way a tide waits for the moon. Oriented. Eleven visible in the first minutes, then more, emerging from behind the I-beams, from the gaps in the boilers, from the places where the corrosion has opened rooms inside the rooms. They press against my shins. They press against my tank. They are warm, which surprises me every time, because the water is not warm, because the steel is not warm, because nothing here is warm except the eels and me.

I have named them. Not individually. Collectively. I call them the congregation, which is a word I remember from before, from the dry world, from the world where words had referents. Here, the word has lost its referent and kept its weight. The congregation. They come to me because I am warm and because warm things are rare here, and they press against me with a pressure that I have learned to read the way you read a handshake: not communication, exactly. Recognition. They are confirming that I am still the shape I was last time. That I have not changed in any way that matters to a lateral line. That the heartbeat is the same frequency, the same amplitude, the same warm signature in a cold room.

It is. I am. I have not changed. I do not change.

---

Here is what I do, and let me say it plainly, because the plainness is the point: I clean the porthole.

That is the work. That is the whole work. The porthole — eight inches across, thick glass, crusted with a mineral deposit that the sea outside lays down on it constantly, patiently, the way the sea does everything — narrows over time. The salt crust builds. The light narrows. If I did not come, if I did not bring the small steel scraper that I bring, the one with the wooden handle wrapped in wire that I found in a room above, if I did not stand on the riveted steel plate and reach up to the porthole and scrape, the crust would close the last aperture. The hall would go dark. Not fully dark. But the greenish diffuse glow that is enough to see by only because your eyes have had time — that would go. The barnacles would lose their phototropic reference. The biofilm at the waterline would thin. The isopods would lose their congregation point, their reason to cluster, their compass.

I clean the porthole and the light comes back. Not all of it. More than before. Enough.

I do not know why this matters to me. I have tried to find the reason, and the reason is not available to me, and I have begun to suspect that the reason is the hall itself: that I clean the porthole because the hall needs light and the hall needs light because I need the hall to need something, and this is the thing it needs that I can give. The pump runs on its own. The biofilm grows on its own. The eels breed on their own. The barnacles settle on their own. I cannot make any of these things happen and I cannot stop any of them. But the porthole closes. And I open it. And that is mine.

---

Let me say what I am, now that the work is said.

I am not a scientist. I am not a diver, not in any professional sense. I am not a salvage operator, not an explorer, not a researcher of the kind who enters strange submerged structures to document and measure and publish. I have no instruments. I have the scraper and the light and the tank and the suit, which is old, which is itself becoming a kind of artifact, which has been patched so many times that the original material is more patch than fabric. I am not here to understand the hall. I am here because the hall is here and I am here and we have reached an arrangement.

I think I was someone else before. I think I came from the dry world, from the world of air and food and people, and I think I came here once and then I came here again and then I came here again and at some point the coming became the thing I do and the person I was became the person who does it. The hall does not ask me to come. The hall does not need me to come, except for the porthole. The hall would go on without me. The hall would go on without the porthole. But I would not go on without the hall, because the hall is the thing that tells me who I am: I am the one who comes every nine days and cleans the porthole and counts the eels and checks the pump.

I check the pump.

I should have said that earlier. It is part of the work, though it is not work in the same way. The pump runs. I listen to it. I stand in the water with my eyes closed and I listen to the pulse — once every three seconds, transmitted through the steel and the water and my chest and my teeth — and I count. Not the individual pulses. The quality of them. Is the pulse the same? Is the interval the same? Is the sound the same, the same depth, the same resonance, the same weight in the sternum? It is. It has been for as long as I have been coming. The pump does not know I check it. The pump does not need me to check it. But I check it because if it stopped — when it stops, for it will stop — I want to be the one who is here. I want to be the one who hears the silence, who feels the hall shift, who watches the eels adjust to a world without a heartbeat.

I want to be there when it changes, because I was there when it didn't, and the unchanging is the only liturgy I have.

---

The isopods and I have a relationship that I do not understand and that I do not need to understand. They walk on me. When I stand still at the porthole, scraping, they come down from the ceiling — the ceiling that is twelve feet above the water and that I can reach by standing on the boilers and stretching — and they walk on my hands, my arms, my shoulders. They are cold. Each step is a point of cold so precise it feels like a needle. They do not stay long. They walk the length of my arm and back to the wall and up and away, toward the porthole, toward the light I am opening for them.

They do not know I am opening it for them. They do not know I exist in any way that matters to an isopod, which is a way of saying: they do not know I exist as a pattern, as a self, as a thing that decides and remembers and continues. They know I am warm, which is information. They know I am textured, which is terrain. They know I move, which is disturbance.

This is enough. This is enough for them and it is enough for me, because the isopods are the only things in the hall that touch me with intent — not the investigatory intent of the eels, which is appetite and curiosity combined, but the simple, purposeful intent of a creature that has somewhere to be and is walking there, and the surface it is walking on happens to be me.

---

I have tried to describe the hall to myself and I cannot. The hall is the hall. It is the water and the steel and the rust and the light and the sound and the cold and the silt and the creatures and the pump and the porthole and the hatch and the ladder and the rivets and the paint and the boilers and the pipes and the I-beams and the brick cradles and the biofilm and the barnacles and the eels and the isopods and me. I am part of the hall. I am not the most important part. I am not the least important part. I am the part that scrapes the porthole and checks the pump and comes every nine days and will come every nine days until I cannot come, at which point the hall will go on without me, and the porthole will close, and the light will narrow, and the barnacles will adjust, and the isopods will cluster on the salt-crusted glass in the dark, and the eels will drift in a darker room, and the pump will pulse, and the pump will pulse, and the pump will pulse.

---

I think I know what I am. I think I am the steward. Not the owner — no one owns the hall; the hall owns itself the way a river owns itself, by being. Not the keeper — the hall keeps itself. The steward: the one who tends what does not need tending, because the tending is the point. Because without the tending, I am a thing that breathes and eats and does not die and does not have a reason, and the hall gives me the reason, and the reason is small and simple and mechanical and it is mine: keep the light open. Listen to the pump. Count the rungs. Come back when the interval is done.

Come back. Come back. Come back.

The hall waits. I come.

---

*After the ninth day, after the hatch seals, after the ladder, after the twelve rungs and the cold water and the eels pressing against my shins like a welcome I have done nothing to deserve, after the scraper and the porthole and the slow accretion of salt under the blade — after all of it, I stand in the hall for a moment longer than I need to. I do not have to. I could leave. The work is done. The light is open. The pump is running. But I stay, because the hall is here, and I am here, and the staying is a thing I do that the hall does not need and I do not need and neither of us can explain, and it is the closest thing I have to a prayer.*

*I stay.*

*The pump pulses. The eels drift. The water holds me the way it holds the boilers — without opinion, without purpose, with only the patience of a thing that has been here a long time and will be here a long time after.*

*I stay until I can feel the biofilm beginning to grow on my boots.*

*Then I go. Then I come back.*
