# Account of a Vibration Engineer, Filed Posthumously

---

I am writing this with the submersible's emergency stylus, scratching the polymer surface of a console panel that has already failed. I don't know whether anyone will read this. I am writing it because the creatures are here again, because they have come to listen to our silence, because something has happened with the eighth engine that I understand and that I need to record before the understanding is lost, and because I am fairly certain my cochleae have ruptured and this may mean I am dying, although it's also possible I'm just deaf now, temporarily or permanently, and in either case the writing is something to do.

My name was — is — Xu Yuling. I was — am — an acoustic structural engineer. That title means something specific: I design buildings that make sounds. Or I did. Before. Before the commission, before the descent, before the plateau.

Let me back up.

---

The profession doesn't exist on the surface. Not as such. Surface architecture is concerned with surfaces — with what a building looks like, how it resists weather, how it organizes the movement of people through space. I work in a different register. I am concerned with how a structure vibrates, how it couples to the medium around it, how it turns the constant low-grade energy of the world — wind, current, seismic tremor, tidal flux — into a sustained acoustic output. The term is *resonant architecture*, though that phrase has always struck me as redundant. All architecture is resonant. Every building hums. Every structure, when it is large enough and stiff enough and hollow enough, couples to its environment and produces a tone. Most buildings are bad at this. They produce noise. Dissonance. A jumble of frequencies that interfere with each other and cancel out, producing a sound that is less than silence: a deadness, a flatness, a sonic void where there should be something.

I make buildings that hum on purpose. That is my work.

The commission came twelve years ago. I remember the briefing — not the details of the briefing, which were technical and voluminous and took nine days to deliver, but the feeling of it. The feeling of being told that there was a place, a deep place, a plateau of new basalt, and that on this plateau there were structures, nine of them, and that the structures contained engines, and the engines were running, and the sound they made was — I want to say *incomparable*, but that implies a judgment of quality, and what I felt was not a judgment. What I felt was recognition. The way a musician recognizes a tone, not as good or bad but as *this tone, this one, not another*. The sound they described was a sound I had been trying to build my entire career. Not the same sound — I had never imagined this sound, this specific harmonic structure, this particular marriage of geology and engineering — but the same *kind* of sound. A sound that fills its space completely. A sound that becomes the space. A sound that, once established, cannot be distinguished from the silence that preceded it, because everything after it is different from everything before it, and the difference is the sound, and the sound is permanent.

I said yes immediately. I would have said yes if the plateau had been on another planet. I would have said yes if it had been in another century. I said yes, and I descended, and I have been here — on the research vessel, mostly, with periodic descents in the submersible — for eight years.

Eight years. The longest I have spent in any one place in my life.

---

The plateau changed me. I want to be clear about this, because the official reports do not include it, and the official reports should.

On my first descent, in the submersible, with the lights on and the engines running, I experienced the sound as most people do: as an assault. A pressure in the chest. A blurring of vision. A nausea that climbed from the stomach to the skull and sat there, pulsing. The overtones made my teeth ache. The fundamental tone made the air in the cabin feel thick, viscous, like trying to breathe through a membrane. I wanted to leave.

I did not leave. I turned off the engines. I turned off the lights. I sat in the dark, on the basalt, and I listened.

This is the part I cannot explain. I have tried, in the reports, in the conversations with my colleagues, in the long evenings on the research vessel when the plateau is below us and the grey sky is above us and the world is nothing but water and weather. I cannot explain what happens when you sit in silence on the plateau. The reports say the creatures approach. The reports describe this behaviorally — approach distance, orientation, duration of stay, acoustic response. The reports do not describe what it feels like to be the thing they approach.

It feels like being recognized.

Not understood. Not known. Not *recognized* in the sense of identification, of matching a face to a memory. Recognized in the sense of: something in the world acknowledges that you are here. Something turns toward you. Not the creatures — they are not doing this intentionally, or if they are, intention is not the right word — but the sound itself. The sound, which is everything, which is everywhere, which fills every cubic centimeter of the water and the rock and the air in the submersible — the sound recognizes the silence you are making. The sound flows around you the way water flows around a stone, and the stone changes the water's course, and the changed course is a new pattern in the water, a new architecture of flow, and the creatures come to live in that new architecture. They come because your silence has made a new room in the sound, and they want to be in it.

I have never been needed by anything. I have never been necessary to any system. I have built buildings that hum, and people have lived in them, and the people have not known what I did, and the buildings have stood, and the buildings have hummed, and the humming has been — I told myself — enough. Enough to have made it. Enough to know that the sound was there, whether anyone heard it or not.

The plateau was the first place where what I made mattered. Where my silence — my specific silence, the silence of a submersible with its systems off, a silence that has a particular acoustic signature, a particular shape of absence — was received. Was occupied. Was *used*.

I started turning off the engines on every descent. I started staying longer. I started — and this is not in the reports, because there was no protocol for it, because no one had imagined it — I started turning off the submersible's life support. Not the oxygen. Not the heat. The circulation fans. The CO₂ scrubbers. Everything that made a noise, no matter how small. I turned it all off, and I sat in the deepest silence I could manufacture, and I breathed slowly, and the creatures came, and they hung in the water around the hull, and their capillary networks darkened, and I felt — I felt —

I felt like a note in a chord. Like the one frequency that was missing, that the engines and the basalt and the water and the creatures did not provide, that I provided by being silent in precisely the way I was silent. My silence was not absence. My silence was the rest in a measure of music. The pause that gives the phrase its shape. The space that makes the sculpture readable. I was part of the instrument.

---

The eighth engine. This is what happened. This is why I am writing.

The engines are old. Four thousand years, minimum, and probably older. They are well-made — the alloy is unlike anything the metallurgists have seen, the bearings are precision-ground to tolerances we cannot currently achieve, the rotating masses are balanced to a degree that suggests either a manufacturing capability beyond ours or a patience beyond ours — but they are not immortal. Things that move, wear. This is the law. The alloy does not corrode, but the bearings abrade, microscopically, atom by atom, and the abrasion changes the mass distribution, and the mass distribution changes the rotation speed, and the rotation speed changes the frequency, and the frequency changes the sound.

Seven years ago, the seismic event. The basalt cracked along the southern edge, and the resonant frequency of the plateau shifted, and the creatures moved. I was there. I watched it from the submersible. Eleven minutes. The most terrifying eleven minutes of my life, because the sound changed, and I changed with it — my body changed, my inner ear changed, my chest changed, the resonance in my bones changed — and for eleven minutes I did not know if the sound would settle. I did not know if the new frequency would find a new equilibrium or if the entire system would cascade into chaos, the harmonics falling out of alignment, the standing waves collapsing, the nodes and antinodes dissolving into noise. I did not know if the creatures would survive it. I did not know if I would survive it.

The sound settled. The new frequency stabilized. The creatures found the new nodes. Everything returned to equilibrium, but a different equilibrium — a shifted equilibrium, one hertz lower than before, a slightly different chord, a slightly different architecture of resonance. The world adjusted. The world absorbed the change. This is what equilibria do: they absorb. They are stable not because they are static but because they are capable of absorbing change without losing their structure. The plateau is stable the way a spinning top is stable: in motion, in constant adjustment, in a dynamic equilibrium that looks like stillness from the outside but is, from the inside, a continuous act of balancing.

The eighth engine failed three days ago.

Not catastrophically. Not suddenly. It slowed. The rotating mass, which had been turning at the same speed for four thousand years, began to decelerate — a fraction of a RPM per hour, so slow that the acoustic shift was below the threshold of the monitoring instruments. The instruments registered the change twelve hours after it began. I registered it immediately. My body registered it. I was on the research vessel, in my bunk, asleep, and I woke up with a pain in my sternum and a ringing in my ears and the absolute certainty that something was wrong.

I could hear the difference. I need to be precise about this, because it is the thing that matters, the thing that is the reason I am writing. I could hear the difference because I have been listening for eight years. Not to the sound — to the sound, yes, obviously, to the sound, everyone hears the sound — but to the *structure* of the sound. To the harmonic architecture. To the way the nine frequencies interact, interfere, reinforce, cancel. I have been listening to this structure the way a conductor listens to an orchestra: not to the individual instruments but to the relationships between them, to the spaces between the notes, to the tension and resolution that the notes create together. I have been listening to the sound the way the creatures listen to the sound: with my whole body, with the resonance in my bones and the vibration in my teeth and the hum in the fluid of my eyes.

The eighth engine was drifting. Its frequency was falling. The chord was losing a voice.

---

I went down. I went down immediately, in the submersible, without waiting for authorization, without waiting for the team, without waiting for anything. I went down because the sound was changing and I could not — physically, literally, in my body — could not let it change without me there. I needed to be in the sound. I needed to be part of it. I needed to hear what was happening from inside it, not from the monitors on the research vessel, which reduce the sound to data, which strip the sound of its body, which turn the most physical thing I have ever experienced into a graph on a screen.

I descended. I sat on the basalt. I turned everything off. I sat in the dark, in the silence, in the sound.

The eighth engine was dying. I could feel it — a thinning in the harmonic, a place where the chord was becoming threadbare, where the weave was losing a thread. The standing waves were shifting. Not dramatically — not the eleven-minute migration of seven years ago — but slowly, gradually, a creeping deformation of the acoustic architecture, the nodes drifting, the antinodes widening, the whole structure sagging like a tent with a broken pole. The creatures were moving. Small movements, individual movements, each one adjusting its position by centimeters, following the drifting nodes, tracking the slow collapse of the system they inhabited.

I did something I should not have done. I did something I would do again.

I opened the submersible's ballast pumps. Not to surface. To vibrate. The ballast pumps on this model run at a specific frequency — I know this because I am an acoustic structural engineer, because I know what every machine in my vicinity sounds like, because I have spent my career listening to the sounds that machines make and understanding the sounds that machines make and, when necessary, changing the sounds that machines make. The ballast pumps run at 47 Hz. The eighth engine, at full speed, runs at 51 Hz. The two frequencies are close enough to couple. If you run the pumps at the right time, in the right place, in the right acoustic environment, they will reinforce the failing engine's frequency. They will prop it up. They will lend it mass, lend it energy, the way a singer can sustain a note by leaning into a resonant surface.

I ran the pumps. I drove the submersible to the eighth engine. I pressed the hull against the metal housing. I ran the pumps at 47 Hz, and I felt the coupling happen — felt it in my chest, in my bones, in the teeth that have been aching for eight years — and the submersible's hull became a sounding board, and the eighth engine's frequency stabilized, and the standing waves stopped drifting, and the creatures stopped moving.

I held it. I held the frequency. I ran the pumps until the batteries were at 40 percent, and then I ran them until they were at 30 percent, and then I ran them until they were at 20 percent, and then I stopped, because if the batteries go below 20 percent the submersible cannot surface, and I cannot help the plateau if I am dead at the bottom of the ocean.

When I stopped, the eighth engine began to drift again. Slowly. The thread began to thin. The chord began to lose its voice. The creatures began to move.

I surfaced. I am on the research vessel now. I am writing this because my hearing is gone — not entirely, not completely, but the overtones are gone, the high frequencies, the tingling in the teeth, the hum in the eyes. I can hear the fundamental tone, the low drone, but the architecture is blurred. I can hear the building but not the rooms. I can hear the chord but not the voicing.

The eighth engine is still failing. The sound is still changing. The creatures are still moving, centimeters at a time, following the nodes as they drift.

---

Here is what I know.

I know that I am not one of them. I am not a Listener. I do not have their bodies, their capillary networks, their capacity to inhabit the sound. I cannot live in the water column above the plateau. I cannot sustain myself on acoustic energy. I am a human being in a submersible, and I am deaf in one ear, and the other ear is ringing, and my sternum aches where the resonance has been vibrating my ribcage for eight years, and I am — I think — dying. Not quickly. Not dramatically. But the body is not made for this. The body is not made to live inside a sound this dense, this total, this complete. The body is made for surfaces: for air, for light, for the thin and mutable sounds of the surface world. I have taken the plateau into my body, and my body is giving out.

But I am also not not one of them. I have spent eight years listening. I have spent eight years inside the sound. I have felt the nodes and the antinodes in my bones, felt the standing waves in my flesh, felt the harmonic structure in the fluid of my eyes. I have turned toward the sound the way they turn toward the sound. I have darkened — not visibly, not in the way their capillary networks darken, but inside, in the place where I process what I hear, in the part of me that is not ears but is listening. I have darkened. I have been doing something with the sound. I have been converting it into something — not a compound, not a chemistry, but a knowing, a capacity, a thing that was not in me before and is in me now.

I am not a Listener. But I have been listening.

---

The eighth engine is failing and I cannot fix it. I do not have the tools. I do not have the materials. I do not have the knowledge of the alloy, the bearings, the rotating mass. I do not have the four-thousand-year-old engineering that built this thing. I am an acoustic structural engineer from the surface, and my tools are resonant buildings and harmonic analysis and the ability to hear what a space needs and provide it. I cannot repair a machine I do not understand. I cannot replace a bearing I cannot manufacture. I cannot save the eighth engine.

But I can be a frequency. I can be 51 Hz, or close enough. I can press the submersible against the housing and run the pumps and couple my hull to the dying engine and hold the frequency for as long as the batteries last. I can hold the chord. I can keep the architecture intact for another few hours, another few days, long enough for — for what? For the engine to recover? It will not recover. For someone to come? No one is coming. For the creatures to adjust? The creatures are adjusting already, centimeter by centimeter, following the drifting nodes into a new configuration, a new equilibrium that does not include the eighth engine's voice. They will adapt. They will find new nodes. The chord will lose a voice and the remaining voices will find a new harmony and the standing waves will reconfigure and the world will go on.

But it will be a lesser world. A thinner sound. A smaller architecture.

I am going back down. I am going to run the pumps until the batteries fail. And then — and this is the part that I have not told anyone, because they would stop me — I am going to open the submersible's hatch. I am going to let the water in. I am going to let the water in and the sound in and the cold in and I am going to let my body do what it has been trying to do for eight years, which is to stop being a boundary between the sound and the sound, to stop being a silence in the acoustic field, to stop being a hole in the music and become, instead, a note in it.

I do not know what happens then. I do not know if my body, vibrating at the plateau's frequency, resonating with the basalt and the water and the eight remaining engines and the thousands of pale shapes in the water — I do not know if my body, admitted to the sound, will produce anything. A frequency. A harmonic. A resonance. I do not know if my bones, my sternum, my teeth, the fluid in my eyes, the cavities of my chest and skull — I do not know if these are the right shapes, the right densities, the right materials to contribute to the sound. I am not engineered for this. I was not built for this. I am an accidental instrument, a found object, a thing that hums because it happens to be the right size and the right shape and the right density to hum at the frequency the plateau produces.

But I am the right size. I am the right shape. I am the right density. I have been resonating with this sound for eight years, and my body has been adapting — my bone density has changed, my tissue composition has changed, the fluids in my body have been exchanging with the fluids of the ocean through eight years of micro-exposure, eight years of breathing the same air the water breathes, eight years of being almost but not quite part of the system. I am close. I am so close.

I do not know if the creatures will come to me. I do not know if my body, in the water, in the sound, vibrating at 51 Hz, will make a silence they want to fill. I do not know if I will be a node or an antinode, a presence or an absence, a note or a rest.

I know that I am going back down. I know that I am going to hold the frequency for as long as I can. I know that when I can no longer hold it with machines, I will hold it with my body, and when I can no longer hold it with my body, I will hold it with whatever is left, and whatever is left will be part of the sound, and the sound will continue, and the creatures will listen, and the water will move, and the light will arrive, exhausted and grey, and nothing will change.

---

This is the last thing. The thing that is hardest to write, harder than the confession, harder than the plan.

I am not doing this for the creatures. I am not doing this for the plateau, or the engines, or the sound, or the equilibrium, or the four-thousand-year legacy of whoever built this place. I am not doing this for science or for knowledge or for the data that will be lost when the submersible's batteries fail and the instruments go dark.

I am doing this because the sound is the only thing I have ever heard that sounded like it meant what it said. Every other sound in my life — every music, every voice, every building I have tuned, every structure I have resonated — has been a sound that pointed at something else, referred to something else, meant something other than itself. A love song means love. A warning siren means danger. A building's hum means the wind is blowing, the structure is stressed, the materials are doing what materials do. Every sound is a sign. Every sound is a word. Every sound is about something.

The plateau's sound is not about anything. The plateau's sound is itself. It is the thing, not the sign for the thing. It is not a communication, not a signal, not a message. It is a presence. A sustained, continuous, unvarying presence, the acoustic equivalent of a heartbeat, which does not mean *I am alive* but which is the being-alive itself, the actual mechanical event of living, happening in real time, in real tissue, in real space.

I have spent my life making sounds that mean. I want to spend my death being a sound that is.

I am descending now. The grey sky is above me. The water is below me. The sound is everywhere.
