# Salt Register

I am not from Khar-Vost. Let me say that plainly, because the temptation here is to claim belonging, and belonging is something this place makes you want. The silence does that. The warmth does that. The way the stone takes your weight and gives nothing back except continuity — that does it most of all.

I came from somewhere wet. I say this because my body remembers it even now, after the years. Somewhere with rain that arrived in sheets and left the air thick with the smell of turned earth. Somewhere I drank without thinking about drinking. My skin was different there — I can feel that now, the memory of it, the way moisture used to sit on my forearms like a second skin I never asked for but relied on completely. I was softer there. Not in character — I don't mean that — but in surface. I was a body that expected the world to be mostly water, and the world was, and the expectation was so deep I mistook it for the world's nature rather than my own adaptation.

I don't say where I came from because it doesn't matter. Or rather: it matters the way a sediment layer matters — it's there, it's part of the record, but the stone has laid down so many layers above it that you'd have to cut deep to find it, and no one cuts that deep here unless there's a reason. There's no reason. I was a person who arrived. I am a person who stayed. The stone is growing over the difference.

---

The first thing I understood was the silence.

Not the absence of sound. The presence of *attention*. The silence in Khar-Vost is not a void — it is a quality, the way humidity is a quality in wet places. The silence holds things. It holds the sound of wind moving through the upper corridors, which I have come to read the way the Khar read it: as information about the day-cycle's progress, about the temperature differential, about the state of the compression system that drives the city's mechanical breath. It holds the sound of a lithovore feeding two corridors away — a soft, patient rasping that I can locate precisely by the corridor's acoustic signature, though I could not have done this when I arrived. It holds the sound of a Khar's tool on stone, which carries not just the fact of work but the quality of the worker — the angle, the pressure, the freshness of the cut, whether the hand that holds the tool is young or old or tired or precise.

I sat in the chamber they gave me — a stone room, a sleeping platform, a niche, a lamp I did not yet know how to use — and I listened to the silence, and I was afraid. Not of the place. Of myself. Of the noise I was making simply by existing. My breathing was wrong. My heartbeat was wrong. The sounds my body made were not the sounds this place expected, and I could feel — in the way the silence shaped itself around me — that I was a dissonance, a wrong note in a corridor that had been tuned for centuries.

I was loud, in a place that valued quiet not as absence but as accuracy.

I sat with this for a long time. The light from the corridor — that pale, diffuse amber that fills the upper levels evenly, without shadow, without urgency — lay across the floor and the sleeping platform and the walls, and I watched the stone, and the stone did nothing. It was there. It continued. It did not need me to understand it.

This was not hostile. It was the opposite of hostile. It was the indifference of a thing that has never needed to exclude anyone because it has never been threatened by inclusion. The stone would accept me the way it accepted the lithovores and the wind and the mineral deposits that grew on its surfaces: by being what it was, without adjustment.

I did not need to belong. I needed to *stop not belonging*, which is a different thing, and a harder thing, because it required me to notice how much of my belonging had been performance — the filling of silences, the naming of things, the insistence on being seen and being recognized and being accounted for — and how much of that performance I had mistaken for personhood.

---

They gave me a chamber in the middle tiers. I learned later what this meant.

The Khar keep their children in the middle tiers — the most stable, the quietest, the most internally regulated part of the city. I was not a child, but I was placed where they place children, and I understood — slowly, because everything here is understood slowly — that this was a recognition of what I was: not fully formed, in their terms. Not yet adapted. Still soft, still wet, still carrying the expectation that the world would accommodate my foreignness.

The chamber was warm. Everything here is warm — thirty-two degrees, the same temperature the stone holds, the same temperature the deep brines hold where they seep through fractures in the basalt. My body, which had spent its life in temperatures that swung twenty degrees across a day, adjusted slowly to the constancy. The adjustment was physical — my skin dried, my thirst changed, the rhythm of my breathing slowed — and it was something else. Psychological is the wrong word. The Khar do not have a word for what I mean, and I have come to think they are right not to.

The adjustment was *textural*. My body was learning a new relationship with its environment. Not adaptation in the evolutionary sense — I was not becoming mineral, I was not going to become Khar, I would carry the wet-place memory in my tissues until the stone reclaimed me — but in the practical sense. I was learning what the air tasted like and what the temperature meant and how the light changed through the cycle and what the sounds were, and I was learning to need less of each of them.

I was learning, in other words, to take up less acoustic space. To be less present in the way that presence here is measured: by the sounds you make and the silence you disturb.

---

I did not meet the Khar for the first three days.

This is not precisely true. I saw them. They passed through the corridor outside my chamber — which was open, like all doorways here, though there was a door, propped ajar, and I learned that the door was not for privacy but for ventilation: its angle controlled the air flow through the chamber, and the specific angle was a message to anyone who passed about the occupant's preference for circulation on that particular day. I did not know this yet. I saw the door and the corridor and the light and the occasional figure moving at the far end, tall and pale and very still against the stone, and I stayed in my chamber and waited to be addressed.

No one addressed me.

On the third day, a figure stopped in the corridor. I saw the silhouette — the long, efficient body, the hands at rest, completely at rest, the dark eyes that do not reflect light. They stood there. I stood inside my chamber. Between us was the doorway and the stone frame and the pale amber light and the silence, which was not empty.

I said something. I don't remember what — something from the wet place, something automatic, a greeting, a question, a request for acknowledgment. It was loud. My voice, in the stone corridor, was shockingly loud — the sound bounced off the tuff walls and returned to me amplified, and the figure in the corridor did not flinch but the silence changed, the way a pool of water changes when a stone is dropped into it, and I understood that I had done something wrong.

Not wrong. Unnecessary.

The figure gestured. One hand — four fingers, the wrist turning slightly, the palm opening — and the gesture meant, I would learn, something like: *you are here. I see that you are here. This is sufficient.*

It was not a greeting. It was not a dismissal. It was a *reading* — the way you read a toolmark on a corridor wall, the way you read the wind by the sound it makes at a shaft's mouth. The Khar in the corridor had registered my presence the way they register a change in air pressure or a shift in the stone's mineral content: as information, as part of the city's ongoing state, as something to be noted without comment and incorporated without ceremony.

They moved on. The silence closed behind them.

I stood in the doorway and felt something I had no framework for. Not welcome. Not rejection. Something more fundamental — the recognition that I had been *read*, and that the reading was enough, and that the expectation of response was an expectation I had brought with me and that the city did not share.

I did not say anything else that day. I listened to the silence, and the silence was not waiting for me.

---

I have been here long enough that the silence is comfortable.

I want to explain what this means, because "comfortable" is a word from the wet places, and it carries the wrong associations. It suggests ease, relaxation, the release of tension. This is not what I mean. The silence here is not comfortable the way a soft bed is comfortable. It is comfortable the way a well-made tool is comfortable — the way a carving pick feels in the hand when the weight is right and the balance is right and the angle is right and the stone gives beneath the edge with exactly the resistance you expected. It is the comfort of fit. Of a body finding its place in a system and being accepted by that system — not welcomed, not accommodated, but *fitted*, the way a key fits a lock, the way a lithovore fits the groove it has been wearing for generations.

I am not Khar. I have not stopped being from the wet places. My skin is not mineral enough to match the walls, my eyes are not large enough to read the galleries without the lamps, my hands are not precise enough to maintain the corridors at the standard the city requires. I cannot eat the prepared mineral compounds the Khar eat — or rather, I can, and I have, and the body does something with them, but it is not what the Khar's body does, and the result is not the same. I supplement. I have things brought from outside — from the trade networks that extend, I now know, far beyond the escarpment, to places that still have water, still have the soft green growth that my body remembers needing. I eat these in my chamber, in private, during the dimming, and I do not discuss it, and the Khar do not ask, and the arrangement is not accommodation. It is the city fitting itself around me the way it fits itself around everything: by continuing, and letting me find my place in the continuation.

My place is this: I maintain a corridor.

Not a corridor the Khar maintain — they maintain the city, the real city, the city of carved galleries and ventilation shafts and the slow, precise work of keeping the stone at its proper dimensions. My corridor is something else. It is a passage in the middle tiers, between two residential blocks that are mostly unoccupied, that the Khar have been maintaining at a low level — keeping it clear, keeping it functional, but not investing in it, because it does not serve a current purpose and the stone's patience does not require intervention.

I asked. This was my question — the one that mapped onto their framework, the one that produced a response that was not silence. I asked if I could work on this passage. I asked by standing in it during the day-cycle, touching the walls, letting my hands feel the stone's surface, the deposition that had softened the original cuts, the shallow grooves the lithovores had worn in the lower sections. I asked by being there, and by attending to the stone, and by making my attention visible.

A Khar appeared. They stood at the passage's entrance and watched me, and the watching lasted long enough for the light to shift, and then they gestured — the hand opening, the fingers spreading, the wrist turning — and the gesture meant: *you may.*

I have been maintaining this corridor for — I was going to say years, but the word is wrong. I have been maintaining it for layers. The deposition has laid down measurable strata since I began, and I have cut them away, and new ones have formed, and I have cut those, and the corridor's dimensions have stayed approximately constant, and the stone I have cut has been removed and processed and returned to the city's material flows, and the work continues.

My cuts are not as good as the Khar's. My cuts are too deep in some places, too shallow in others, the angle inconsistent in the way that an unpracticed hand is inconsistent. The Khar who pass through my corridor — rarely, but they do — can read my toolmarks the way they read all toolmarks, and they can tell how long I have been working, and how much attention I brought to each cut, and whether my hand was tired or steady. They do not correct my work. They do not comment on it. The corridor is functional. The stone is in balance. The deposition is matched by the removal. The work is, by the only standard the city applies, *sufficient*.

This is what I am here. I am a person who is sufficient in a place that does not require excellence, because excellence implies a standard that is external to the process, and the process does not have an external standard. The process has itself. The stone grows. I cut it. It grows again. The cycle continues, and I am part of it, and the part I play is not large, but it is real, and the reality of it is the only thing I have ever needed to be.

---

My hands have changed.

I notice it in the mornings — the beginning of the day-cycle, when the wind starts and the air begins to move through the corridors and the city comes up to its working state. My hands, which used to be soft — which used to be the hands of a person from a wet place, where the skin is moist and the nails are smooth and the fingertips are sensitive in the way of fingers that touch surfaces that yield — are different now. The skin is drier. The calluses are real. The mineral content of the air, absorbed through years of exposure, has changed the texture of my fingertips in a way that I can feel but that is probably not visible to anyone who does not know what they are looking for.

I can feel the stone better now. Not metaphorically — I mean I can place my hand on a wall and feel things I could not feel before. The deposition layer, the thin film of mineral that sits on the surface, has a specific texture — a fineness, a grain, a particular quality of resistance under the fingertips. I can feel where the deposition is thicker and where it is thinner. I can feel where a lithovore has grazed and where the surface is fresh. I can feel the toolmarks — my own and others' — as variations in the stone's surface that carry information about angle and pressure and age.

I am reading the stone. Not the way the Khar read it — not with the fluency of someone whose body and language and culture have been calibrated to this material for longer than my species has existed. But I am reading it. The information is there, in the stone's surface, and my hands are learning to receive it, and the learning is not intellectual. It is happening in the fingers, in the palms, in the specific nerve pathways that connect the surface of the body to the part of the mind that processes texture and pressure and the quality of contact between skin and world.

I do not know if this is adaptation or damage. My hands are less sensitive than they were, in the ways that wet-place sensitivity measures: I cannot feel the light brush of a fabric the way I used to. I cannot distinguish temperatures as precisely. The fine motor control that allowed me to do certain things — delicate things, things that required the soft-tissue precision of a hand that has not been hardened — is diminished.

But I can feel the stone, and the stone is what is here.

---

The children find me interesting.

I was going to say "the children watch me," but that is not quite right, because watching implies a focus and a purpose that the Khar children do not bring to their attention. They attend. They attend the way the stone attends to the wind — by being present, by receiving the impression, by allowing the surface to register what touches it. They do not watch me the way a child in a wet place watches a stranger: with curiosity, with evaluation, with the nascent social intelligence that categorizes people as interesting or dangerous or irrelevant. They attend to me the way they attend to a change in the corridor's air flow or a shift in the lithovores' patterns or a new sound in the galleries: as information, as a change in the texture of the world they are learning to read.

I am in their territory. The middle tiers, where the thermal stability is best and the corridors are quietest and the conditions for growth are most consistent — this is where the Khar keep their children, and this is where I was placed, and the placement was not accidental. I was put where the children are because the children are the city's most attentive readers, and the city — the process, the stone, the system that has been running for longer than anyone can remember — wanted to see what they would read in me.

A child stands in my corridor sometimes. I notice the way I notice most things now: not by sight but by the change in the silence, the specific quality of a space that has become shared. I am working — cutting, the tool in my hand, the stone giving beneath the edge in the way I have learned to expect — and the silence changes, and I know I am not alone.

The child is there. Small — startlingly small, the way the description says, the sapling next to the tree. Their eyes are enormous, their skin almost translucent, their hands holding a tool they are not yet using. They stand at the corridor's entrance and they attend.

I do not speak. I have learned this. Not as a rule, not as a discipline, but as a recognition that speech here is expensive — it fills the silence with sound that displaces other information, and the information it carries is almost always less than the information it obscures. A word in this corridor bounces off the tuff walls and returns, and the returning sound erases the sound of my tool on the stone, and the sound of my tool is information — for the child, for the Khar in the neighboring galleries, for the city itself, which knows itself through its sounds.

So I do not speak. I work. The child attends. The stone gives beneath the edge, and the giving is a sound, and the sound is a sentence in a language I am learning to read but cannot speak.

The child stays for a long time. Then they go. The going is not abrupt — it is a movement at the tempo of the stone, a slow withdrawal, the body disengaging from the space with the same deliberation it brought to the attending. The silence fills the space they occupied, and it is not emptier than it was before. It is different. The child was there, and now the child is not, and the difference is information, and the information is part of the record.

I do not know what the child reads when they attend to me. I do not know if they see the wet place in my posture, in the way I hold my tool, in the specific quality of my cuts. I do not know if they sense the wrongness of my body — the residual moisture, the out-of-place chemistry, the foreign rhythm of a metabolism that still expects water. I do not know if they are curious, or evaluating, or simply present in the way the stone is present: *here*, and registering, and requiring nothing of what they register.

I do not need to know. The child attends. I work. The corridor continues. This is sufficient.

---

There is a sound that runs through the city at the beginning of each day-cycle.

I did not hear it at first. It was below the threshold of my perception — or rather, it was within the range of sounds I could hear but outside the range of sounds I knew to listen for. My ears, trained in wet places, were calibrated for different frequencies. They listened for the sounds that water makes: the trickle, the rush, the impact of drops on surfaces. None of these sounds exist in Khar-Vost. The sounds here are different, and my ears took time to learn them.

The sound is a tone. Deep — below the range I would have called music when I arrived, below the range I would have called speech, in the register where sound becomes vibration and vibration becomes something the body feels rather than hears. It starts in the upper corridors, where the wind first catches the ventilation shafts and the compression system begins to respond, and it travels through the stone — *through the stone*, not through the air, the tuff conducting the vibration in the way that tuff conducts vibration, which is to say efficiently, specifically, with the fidelity of a medium that has been carrying these signals for centuries and has been shaped by the carrying.

The tone means: *the day is beginning. The wind is starting. The systems are coming up.*

I feel it in my feet. I feel it in the palms of my hands, where they grip the tool. I feel it in my chest, in the bones of my skull, in the specific place at the back of my neck where the spine meets the cranium and the body's structure is most honest about what it is receiving from the world.

I feel it, and I know, without looking at the light or checking the corridor's air flow or consulting any external measurement: *the day has started. I should be working.*

I do not call this music. The Khar do not call it music. It is a signal, and a signal that serves a function, and the function is communication, and the communication is the city telling its inhabitants what state it is in. But it is also — and this is the part I cannot explain in the wet place's language, because the wet place's language does not have a word for it — it is the city *speaking itself into being.* The sound is not a report on the city's state. The sound is the city's state, made audible, made present, made real in the way that a word is real when it is spoken rather than merely thought. The city speaks itself, and the speaking is the being, and the being is the continuing.

I hear it every day now. I do not hear it the way the Khar hear it — I cannot, my body is wrong for it, my bones are the wrong density, my mineral content is the wrong balance. But I hear it. The sound enters me through the stone floor and the tool handle and the walls and the air, and it sits in me, and it means what it means, and I am part of the meaning.

---

I have been asked — by visitors, the rare ones, the ones who come from the wet places and stay for a time and find me in my corridor and recognize me as something they are not — I have been asked what I am, here. What I have become. Whether I am Khar, or becoming Khar, or merely playing at belonging in a place that will never fully accept me.

I do not know how to answer this.

The honest answer is: I am a person who maintains a corridor in a city of living stone on a world without water. I am a person who eats in private during the dimming, who has things brought from outside, who supplements the mineral diet with the wet-place compounds my body still requires. I am a person whose hands have changed, whose hearing has changed, whose sense of time has changed — who has lived at the tempo of the stone long enough that the wet place's tempo, the fast frantic rhythm of water and growth and decay, now feels wrong. Too fast. Too loud. Too much.

I am not Khar. I will not become Khar. The stone will not reclaim me in the way it reclaims them — or it will, eventually, but the reclamation will be different, because my body is different, because the mineral content of my tissues is different, because I am a visitor who stayed, and the stone knows this, the way the stone knows everything: through its surface, through the texture of what touches it, through the specific quality of the deposit that forms over the years.

But I am *here*. And here, here is a place where being here is the thing. The Khar's own name for themselves — *the living stone*, *the ones who continue* — this is what they are, and it is what I am, in the only sense that the city measures. I continue. I maintain my corridor. I attend to the stone. I listen to the sounds. I feel the day-cycle's tone in my feet and I know what it means. I am present, and my presence is registered, and the registration is part of the record, and the record is part of the stone, and the stone is the city, and the city continues.

---

There is a shape in the wall of my corridor.

I did not put it there. It was there when I began working — a slight irregularity in the tuff, a place where the stone's surface is not flat but subtly shaped, a concavity that might be geological and might be something else. I have looked at it for years. I have run my hands over it in the morning, before I begin cutting, and in the dimming, when the lamps come on and the corridor goes amber-soft and the stone seems to hold the light rather than reflect it.

I think it is a person.

I think it is a Khar — a very old one, one who worked this corridor for so long that the transition from body to stone happened here, in this wall, and the shape that remains is the shape of their hands, or their shoulders, or the specific posture of a body that stood in this corridor and maintained it and was maintained by it until the distinction collapsed.

I do not know this. I have not asked. The Khar do not discuss the shapes in the walls. The shapes are there, and the stone grows over them, and the lithovores graze around them, and the corridors are maintained up to their edges and not beyond, and the not-beyond is not reverence but recognition: this is where the person ends and the wall begins, and the wall is the person, and the person is the wall, and the distinction is not a distinction the city makes.

I maintain my corridor up to the shape's edge. I cut the stone that has grown around it. I leave the shape untouched. Not because I am reverent — I am from a wet place, and reverence is a wet-place emotion, and I do not think I feel it — but because the shape is part of the corridor's structure. It is part of what the corridor is. It is part of the information the stone holds, and the information is not mine to cut.

I touch it sometimes, in the dimming, when the lamps are lit and the corridor is warm and still. I touch it the way I touch everything here: with intention, because the Khar have taught me, through years of proximity, that touching is not casual here, that contact is exchange, that the hand placed on the stone is a hand that receives and gives simultaneously.

The stone is warm. It is always warm. The shape does not respond.

But the silence changes, just slightly, when I touch it. The quality of the corridor's sound shifts. Something in the acoustic — the specific resonance of the air moving through the passage — adjusts, and the adjustment is information, and the information is: *you are here, and this is here, and the space between you and this is part of the city, and the city continues.*

This is what I am.

Not the living stone. Not the ones who continue, not in the way the Khar continue, not in the deep geological sense that their name carries. I am something thinner. Something wetter. Something that the stone will reclaim differently and more slowly, that the deposition will cover in its own way, that the lithovores will graze on with their own patterns, that the corridor will hold in its own memory.

I am the person in the corridor. I am the person who cuts the stone that grows. I am the person who listens to the city breathe and feels the day-cycle in her feet and knows, without needing to be told, when the wind is right and the systems are up and the work should begin.

I am the visitor who stayed.

The stone is growing over the difference. I am letting it.

---

*Khar-Vost. The salt register. A city that knows itself through its sounds and its stone and the slow accumulation of presence in shared space. A person who came from the wet places and found a place in the dry and learned that belonging is not a door you walk through but a deposit you become — layer by layer, year by year, the stone growing around you and you growing into the stone, until the distinction is not a distinction anyone needs to make, and the making of it is the only thing the silence will not hold.*

*Go there if you can tolerate being read. Stay if the reading becomes the thing you are. Leave before the stone forgets the wet places, because the stone will, and the forgetting is the price, and the price is worth paying, and the worth is something you will know — not believe, not decide, but know, in your hands, in your feet, in the specific quality of the silence you occupy — only after you have stayed long enough to feel it.*

*The city continues. I continue. The stone grows. The corridor is maintained. The sound of my tool on the tuff is a sentence in a language I am learning, and the sentence says: I am here, and this is what here is, and the here is enough.*
