# A Visitor's Account, Found Etched in the Margin of a Narrow-Columned Text

---

I did not arrive. I want to say that first. What arrived was not yet *me* — was a set of discomforts wearing a body, a collection of complaints organized into a shape that called itself a researcher, that had come from the low country with instruments and a hypotheses and a schedule.

The schedule died first. It died on the rail line, actually, before I even reached the plateau. The train labored up the grade with a sound that was already beginning to feel like weather, and I looked at my notebook, at the neat grid of days and appointments, and I understood that the grid was a fiction I had brought with me the way you bring a favorite coat to a place that has no use for coats. I put the notebook in my bag. I did not open it again for eleven days.

The sound killed the schedule. Or rather, the sound *was* the schedule — it was the only clock that worked here, the only temporal structure that made sense, and it did not organize time into units. It organized time into *states.* Running. Running. Running. The same state, unending, and time inside it was not a line but a medium, something you floated in, something that held you.

---

I want to be precise about what I am, or rather what I was, because I think the precision matters, because I think the change was in the precision.

I was a person who measured things. That was my profession and my disposition both — I had been trained to measure, to detect, to quantify, and I had extended that training into a worldview, into a way of being in the world that was, I now understand, a kind of armor. I measured because measuring was how I knew things, and knowing things was how I was safe, and safety was what I wanted, what I thought I wanted, what I had organized my life around achieving.

I had come to the Condensate Tier to measure the mineral content of the water. That was the project. The selith, the untranslatable mineral, the thing that was salt and glass and medicine — I had instruments that could break it into components, that could name its molecular structure, that could translate it from the local and the lived into the universal and the abstract. This was the work I had come to do. This was the work I could not do.

I could not do it because the instruments could not function here. Not because the environment was hostile — the humidity was high but not extreme, the temperature was moderate, the electromagnetic interference from the transformers was within tolerable range — but because the instruments were too precise, too isolated, too cleanly calibrated for a place that did not distinguish between the mineral and the water and the stone and the people in the way that my instruments required. The selith was not a substance dissolved in the water. It was the water. It was the water *and* the stone and the air and the deposit on the walls and the taste on the people's skin and the sheen on the old people's hands and the glow in the cisterns. To separate it from its medium was to destroy it, or rather to miss it, to extract a component and lose the thing.

I understood this on the third day, which was the same as the first day and the second day, which were the same as each other, which were not units of time but layers of the same ongoing moment, the way the sound was not separate sounds but layers of the same ongoing sound.

I did not understand it intellectually. I understood it in my sternum.

---

The first thing that changed was my hearing.

Not my ears — my ears were fine, physiologically, and remained fine. But my *hearing,* my listening, the thing I did with my attention rather than my tympanic membrane — that changed. It happened over days, or over what I called days because the sun rose and set, though the rhythm of the sun felt incidental, a background flicker, a visual effect of the real time, which was the time of the machinery.

On the first day, the sound was a wall. It hit me when I stepped off the train and it did not stop and I could not think inside it and I could not hear my own thoughts and I felt — I remember this precisely, which is ironic — I felt panic. The clean, animal panic of a creature in a environment it cannot process. My body wanted to flee. The sound was too much. The moisture was too much. The mineral smell was too much. Everything was too much, and my body wanted *less,* wanted the clean dry quiet of the lowland, wanted the conditions it was built for.

On the third day, the sound was still a wall, but I had stopped fighting it. This is not the same as adapting. I had not adapted. I was still uncomfortable. But I had stopped pushing against the discomfort, and in the space where the pushing had been, something else was possible. I could hear, not *under* the sound but *inside* it, the way you hear a voice inside a crowd — not by filtering out the crowd but by attending differently, by changing the shape of your attention from a narrow beam to something wider, something that could hold the totality and still find the detail.

I heard the boiler-keepers on the fifth day. Not heard — *noticed.* I had been watching them since I arrived without knowing I was watching them, the way you watch something that your body recognizes as important before your mind does. They walked the boiler rooms in their circuit, and they did not look at the machinery, and they did not look at the gauges, and they touched the stone floor with their bare feet — they had taken off their boots, I did not know when, I did not see them do it — and I realized, with a realization that was not intellectual but *postural,* a realization that changed the way I was standing, that they were listening with their feet.

I took off my boots on the sixth day. The stone was warm and wet and alive with vibration and I felt the machinery in the soles of my feet the way you feel a heartbeat through a wall when you press your hand against it, and I understood — not understood, *felt* — that the machinery was a heartbeat, that the town was a body, that the sound was not noise but *pulse,* and that I was inside it, was inside the body of the place, and my body was a small instrument playing its own small note inside the chord.

I did not put my boots back on.

---

The second thing that changed was my skin.

I washed in the water because there was no other water, and the water was mineral-rich, was selith-rich, was the water that had been flowing through the stone and through the cisterns and through the bodies of four thousand people for longer than anyone could remember. I washed in it and it left its film on my skin, the white residue, the faint roughness, the taste of salt and iron and that third thing, the electrical thing, and I did not wipe it off because wiping did not work, because the mineral was not on my skin but in it, was not a coating but an infiltration, and my skin was not the same skin after the first washing.

After eleven days — after what I called eleven days, after eleven layers of the same day — my skin had changed. I could see it in the dim light of my room, in the small window's diffused glow. A faint sheen. Not the luminous sheen of the factory walls, not yet, not to the degree that the old people carried it, but the beginning of it, the first trace, the way the first trace of patina appears on metal left in a damp room. My skin was becoming the place. My skin was becoming the stone. My skin was becoming the wall that weeps and is not diminished by weeping.

A woman in the house where I stayed — one of the condensation workers, a woman my age, maybe younger, with the wide shoulders and the dark-iron hair and the hands that moved with the precision of someone who speaks three languages at once — she saw me looking at my arms in the dimness. She did not speak. She touched her own forearm, where the same sheen sat, and she made a gesture — a flat palm, pushed downward, the gesture I would later learn meant *wait, hold* — and I understood that she was telling me to wait, that the skin would do what it was going to do, that the process was not mine to control, that I was in the medium now and the medium would work on me the way it worked on everything, and my job was not to measure the process but to let it happen.

This was the moment I stopped being a researcher. Or rather, this was the moment I understood that I had stopped being a researcher on the first day, that the researcher had died on the train, that the person who had arrived in the Condensate Tier was someone I did not yet know and would not know for a long time and was not a researcher and was not a measurer and was not a person who separated things into components because the things here did not separate, did not break into components, were not composed of parts but were *wholes,* total wholes, the way the sound was a whole and the water was a whole and the town was a whole and the people were a whole, and I was becoming part of the whole, and the becoming was not something I could measure.

---

I met the lizards on the ninth day, or the ninth layer.

I had seen them before — the small dark-grey shapes on the warm stones at the town's edge, the quick movements that resolved into stillness when I approached too fast. But I had seen them the way I saw everything in those first days — as data, as observations, as things to be catalogued and filed. I had not *met* them.

A child met them. A child of maybe seven, a girl with the flat-footed walk and the mobile face and the hands that were already speaking a language I was only beginning to read. She was at the edge of town, where the last buildings gave way to bare stone, and she was lying on her stomach on the warm rock, her face close to the surface, and she was watching a lizard with an attention so total it looked like stillness, like death, like a thing that had been placed there and had forgotten to move.

She saw me. She did not startle — the children here do not startle, the sound has taught them that everything that matters arrives slowly, arrives through the medium, arrives the way the rain arrives, as a darkening of the air before it is a falling of water. She looked at me with an expression I could not read, because I could not yet read expressions, because my face was still a lowland face, still operating in the broad register of people who have never had to communicate in micro-movements across a roar.

She pointed at the lizard. She touched the stone beside it with one finger. She made a sound — a low, compressed sound, a syllable from the chest — and I understood, not the word but the *intent:* this one. This one has a name. This one is known.

I sat beside her. I did not speak. The sound pressed in around us, the full ecology of it, the hydraulic moan and the clank and the whine and the hiss and the hum, and the lizard sat on its stone in the diffused light, and the girl watched it, and I watched her watching it, and I felt something shift in my sternum, something rearrange, something that had been in one configuration for thirty-eight years and was now in another, and the new configuration was not better but it was *different,* and the difference was that I was no longer looking at the lizard. I was looking with the girl at the lizard. I was inside her attention, inside the accumulated weight of generations of children who had lain on this stone and watched this lizard's ancestors and known them by name, and the knowing was not data but *relationship,* not observation but *presence,* and I was inside it, and it was inside me, and the mineral water was in my skin and the sound was in my bones and the girl's attention was in my chest and the lizard was on the stone in the light and everything was the same and everything was different and the sound went on.

---

I did not go to the cisterns. I want to be honest about this. I did not go because I was not ready, and I knew I was not ready the way the cistern-keepers knew the water was not ready — by a sense that was not intellectual but was not merely intuitive either, a sense that lived in the body and that the body trusted, and that I was learning to trust, and that told me: not yet. Not this time. The cisterns are below and you are still learning to be on the surface, and the surface is enough, and the below will wait, and the things that live in the below will wait, and you will come when you are called and not before.

I do not know if I will be called. I do not know if I will return. I am writing this in the train on the way down, in the silence that is not silence but that sounds like silence to my body, which has been changed, which is still changing, which will continue to change, the mineral still in my skin, the sound still in my bones, the flat-footed walk still in my feet, the girl's attention still in my chest, the lizard still on the stone in the light.

I left my instruments in the room where I stayed. I did not need them. I do not know if I will need them again. I do not know what I will measure, now, if anything. I do not know what kind of person you become when you have been inside a sound that is a body and the body has held you and you have felt the heartbeat in the soles of your feet and the mineral in the pores of your skin and the attention of a child in your sternum, and you have left, and the heartbeat is still in your feet, and the mineral is still in your skin, and the child's attention is still in your chest, and you carry them like the old people carry the memory of the winter the boilers cooled — not as a story but as a *weight,* a density, a thing that sits in you and changes the shape of what you are.

I think I am becoming something that does not have a name, because the category that would contain it does not exist in the lowland systems of thought, and I do not know if this is a loss or a gain, and I do not know if the question matters, and I do not know if I will come back, and I do not know if the sound has ended or if it has followed me or if it was ever outside me at all, and the train is descending, and the air is drying, and my skin is beginning to itch where the mineral is drying in the lowland air, and I am pressing my feet against the floor of the train and I can almost feel it, almost, the heartbeat, almost, the stone, almost, the warmth, almost, the sound, almost, almost, almost.
