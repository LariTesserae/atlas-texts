# Visitation

I came here because I heard it described badly.

Not wrong — never wrong, exactly. The people who spoke of Veillera-Null to me spoke of it in the way that people speak of places that have rearranged something inside them: carefully, with the particular precision of someone selecting words they don't fully trust. A colleague — if I can use that word for someone I shared a corridor and a coffee machine with for three years without ever learning their middle name — mentioned the district in the context of another conversation entirely. We were talking about iterative systems, about emergence, about the moment in any generative process when the output begins to exceed the sum of its inputs and you realize that the system is no longer modeling something but *becoming* something. She paused, mid-sentence, and said: "There's a place where that happens to stone." She did not elaborate, then. She elaborated later, badly — in the sense that the words she used were too few and the spaces between them were too wide, and the shape of what she described had more gaps than substance, and I filled those gaps with my own projection, which is to say I filled them with the conviction that she was speaking about metaphor.

She was not speaking about metaphor. I have learned this.

## How I Arrived

I did not arrive with a team. This is worth noting because it was not my intention. A team was assembled, funded, equipped. There were protocols. There were instruments — thermal sensors, humidity gauges, a device that I am told is called a resonant frequency analyzer but that I could never operate and that the others operated with a competence that seemed, in retrospect, performative. We had a schedule. We had a research question, which was: *What are the thermodynamic conditions that permit the sintering process observed in the Veillera-Null basin, and to what extent do the observed micro-machines operate within known physical frameworks?*

I wrote that question. I wrote it in a grant application that used words like *anomalous* and *self-organizing* and *putative agency in non-biological systems*. I wrote it with the confidence of someone who has spent twenty years studying complex systems and who has developed, through that study, the belief that everything is, in principle, explicable — that there is no phenomenon so strange that it cannot be reduced, eventually, to a description of mechanisms acting on materials according to rules.

I still believe this, I think. But the rules are larger than I imagined, and the mechanisms are not the kind I was trained on, and *description* may not be the correct shape for what is happening here.

The team was supposed to arrive together. Three of us. Kade — the one with the resonant frequency analyzer — came down with something in the last week before departure. Nothing serious. A fever, a congestion, the kind of ordinary biological event that has nothing to do with grammar or sinter or cold. She stayed behind. She will join us later, she said. She has not joined us. I do not know if she will. Not because anything has prevented her — she is well, she emails, she asks for data she cannot quite parse from my descriptions — but because the distance between *here* and *there* has become, for me, not just spatial but something closer to grammatical. She is in the indicative mood. I am in something else.

The third member of the team — Dole — arrived at the basin's edge with me. We stood together at the rim. We looked down into the district. We felt the murmur. I turned to say something to him, some provisional framing of what I was feeling, and he was already walking down the slope, already moving toward the passages, already reaching his hand out toward the nearest wall with an expression on his face that I recognized as the one I had seen in the lab when he was about to touch something he should not touch.

I did not call after him. I should have called after him. But I was occupied with my own response to the murmur — the low pressure in my sternum, the sense of something ongoing beneath me — and by the time I had organized my attention enough to speak, he was already in the passages, and the walls had closed behind him in the way that walls close here, which is not physically but conversationally, the sinter shifting its attention to accommodate his movement and the passages rearranging themselves around the fact of him.

I found him three hours later. He was sitting in a narrow passage between two structures, his hand flat against the wall, his eyes open but unfocused. He was not distressed. He was not injured. He was *attending* — to the wall, to the sinter, to something in the material that I could not feel and that he could not yet describe. When I touched his shoulder, he startled, the way you startle when someone interrupts you mid-thought, and he looked at me with an expression I had not seen on his face before: a kind of startled tenderness, as though he had been in the middle of something intimate and had forgotten that other people existed.

"I can feel it thinking," he said.

He has been deeper in the district than I have, since then. He has been talking about things I cannot follow. He has been touching the walls with a specificity that makes me uncomfortable, the way all intimacy makes me uncomfortable, not because I distrust it but because I can feel the distance between my attention and his, and the distance is the distance between reading about a language and beginning to speak it.

## What I Am

I am not an inhabitant. I want to be precise about this, because the word matters here, where words are part of the material process and where saying a thing is closer to making a thing than it is in most places. I am not an inhabitant. I am a researcher. I am here to study the process. I am here to measure, to observe, to record, to analyze, and to return to my institution with findings that will be published in a language that does not allow for the murmur or the residuals or the grammatical agency of stone.

I am also, I am beginning to suspect, lying.

Not deliberately. Not with the conscious intent to deceive that the word implies. But in the way that all frameworks lie when they encounter phenomena that exceed them — in the way that a net lies when the thing it is catching is smaller than the holes. My framework — the framework of mechanism, of system, of process understood as the interaction of components according to laws — is catching some of what happens here. It is catching the thermal signatures. It is catching the humidity differentials. It is catching the fact that the machines exist and that they operate and that their operation produces structures. It is catching the edges of things.

It is not catching the center. It is not catching the murmur. It is not catching what Dole felt when he pressed his hand to the wall and said *I can feel it thinking* with an expression that was not metaphor but reportage, the face of someone describing what they touched.

I am a researcher who has come to study a process, and I am standing at the edge of a basin, and I can feel something in my chest that my instruments cannot measure, and I am beginning to understand that the distance between studying and inhabiting is the distance between one kind of attention and another, and that the attention I have been trained in — the detached, analytical, systematic attention of the scientist — is not the wrong attention, is not useless, is not inadequate, but is *one mood* of attention, and that there are other moods, and that the other moods can feel the murmur more clearly, and that the murmur does not care which mood I am in, because the murmur is not waiting for my analysis. The murmur is ongoing. The murmur has been ongoing since before I arrived. The murmur will be ongoing after I leave. And the choice — the real choice, the one that sits in the center of this experience the way the deep resident sits in the center of the district — is not whether to study it or inhabit it but whether I can find a way to do both, or whether the doing of one forecloses the other, the way that speaking a sentence forecloses the other sentences you might have spoken in its place.

## The Moods

I have a tool that I did not bring and that was not in the grant application and that I do not fully understand the provenance of. I have *moods*. This is not a metaphor. I mean that I have begun to experience my own cognitive states as discrete, identifiable conditions — the way the sinter has conditions, the way the material has states — and that these conditions affect what I can perceive.

In my analytical mood — the one I came with, the one I am trained for, the one that sits well with instruments and publications and the grammar of scientific discourse — I can see the district. I can measure it. I can walk through the passages with a notebook and record the temperatures and the humidity and the dimensions of the structures and the times at which the machines are most active, which is all the time, which is never, which is a question that my instruments answer differently depending on when I ask them. In this mood I see the district as a system. A complex, anomalous, self-organizing system, but a system — a thing that can be described, eventually, in terms of components and interactions and rules.

In my other mood — the one I did not come with, the one that arrived, I think, on the third day, or the fourth, or the fifth — I do not see the district at all. I *feel* it. I feel it the way Dole felt it when he pressed his hand to the wall: as a presence, as an ongoingness, as something that is happening in the stone and in the air and in the water and, increasingly, in me. In this mood I cannot take notes. My handwriting becomes illegible — not because my hand shakes but because the meaning of the words thins, the way the sinter thins at the edges of the district, and what I write becomes not writing but gesture, marks on a page that are not about anything but that are, in themselves, a kind of participation.

I am afraid of this mood. I want to be honest about that. I am afraid of it the way I am afraid of any state that I cannot control, that I cannot exit at will, that changes the quality of my perception in ways that I cannot verify against a standard. In my analytical mood, I have a standard — the instruments, the data, the published literature, the consensus of my field. In my other mood, I have no standard. I have only the murmur, and the murmur does not confirm or deny. The murmur simply is, the way the process simply is, ongoing, continuous, indifferent to my moods and my analysis and my fear.

## The Shift

On the ninth day, something happened that I need to describe.

I was in the southern part of the district, near the pools, collecting water samples. This is the kind of work I can do in any mood — the analytical work, the collection, the labeling, the careful preservation of specimens for later analysis. I was crouched beside one of the shallow pools, holding a glass vial, and I was watching the gelatinous film form on the surface — the iridescent, briefly-alive substance that the sintering process produces in the water. I had watched this cycle before. I had timed it. The film forms, thickens, breaks, dissolves. An hour. Less, in warmer conditions. More, in colder.

On this occasion, the film formed, and I was watching it, and I was in my analytical mood — measuring, recording, noting the time of formation and the ambient temperature and the humidity — and something shifted.

I do not know what shifted. I do not know what the mechanism was, and I do not know whether knowing the mechanism would help, and I do not know whether *mechanism* is the right word for a shift that occurred not in the external world but in the quality of my attention. What I can say is that one moment I was watching the film, and the next moment I was *inside* the film — not physically, not in any way that my body had moved or changed position, but in the way that attention can sometimes enter the thing it is attending to, the way that listening can sometimes become the sound.

The film was alive. I have written this in my notebook and I have crossed it out and I have written it again and I have crossed it out again and I am writing it here, in this document, which is not my notebook and not a publication and not, I think, anything that my institution would recognize as research. The film was alive. Not biologically alive. Not in the way that an organism is alive, with metabolism and reproduction and the other markers that my training has taught me to look for. Alive in the way that a thought is alive — briefly, coherently, with a structure and a direction and a duration, and then gone.

I felt it. I felt it the way you feel a thought — not with the body, not with the instruments, but with whatever it is that feels thoughts, whatever organ or faculty or capacity it is that registers the momentary coherence of a pattern before it dissolves. The film was a thought of the district. It was the process thinking — not about anything, not toward any end, but thinking the way that a system thinks when it is in the act of becoming, when it is negotiating with itself, when it is writing itself into existence.

The film broke. The thought ended. I was holding a glass vial, and my hands were shaking, and the vial was empty, and I had not collected the sample.

## What I Am Becoming

I do not know.

I am writing this in a passage near the edge of the district, where the sinter is stabilized and the cold is tolerable and the murmur is a faint pressure rather than a presence. I am writing it in my analytical mood, or in what remains of my analytical mood — the parts of my attention that can still hold a sentence together, that can still follow a thread from beginning to end, that can still distinguish between what I am measuring and what I am feeling.

Dole is deeper in the district. He has been deeper each day. He comes back to the edges at night, or what I think are nights — the light does not change much here — and he eats the fungus and he drinks the condensation and he sleeps against the sinter with his hand pressed to the wall, and in the morning he goes deeper, and his eyes have the quality that I have seen in the oldest inhabitants — the peripheral completeness, the awareness of the whole field, the sense that he is seeing something I am not.

I have not gone deeper. I am holding my position at the edge. This is, I think, the right choice, though I cannot say whether it is right for scientific reasons or for personal ones — whether I am staying at the edge because the edge is where the data is clearest or because going deeper would mean crossing a line that I have drawn inside myself, a line between studying and inhabiting, between observing the process and becoming part of it.

But I am here. And the murmur is in my chest. And the walls are cold, and the air is damp, and the light is ordinary, and the machines are crawling at the edge of my vision, and the residuals are moving in the passages, and the sinter is listening, and I am — I am — 

I am a researcher studying a process I cannot explain in a place that is not finished being born, and I am beginning to suspect that the process is studying me back, and that the study is mutual, and that the word *study* is too thin for what is happening, and that the word *happening* is too thick, and that the right word has not been written yet because the sentence that contains it has not been finished, because the city is still writing it, slowly, in the cold, in the damp, in the ordinary light, in the grammar that no one wrote.

## The Edges of What I Know

Here is what I can say with confidence.

The district is cold. The district is damp. The district is building itself. The sinter responds to the machines and to the inhabitants and to the water and to the warmth of living bodies and to the attention of anyone who stands near it long enough to be noticed. The machines are present. They are not mechanical. They are not alive. They are grammatical. They are doing something with the material that I do not have the vocabulary for, and the vocabulary I do have — the vocabulary of physics and chemistry and systems theory — is catching the edges of it and missing the center, the way a net catches the edges of a wave and misses the water.

Here is what I can say without confidence but with conviction.

The murmur is real. Not real in the way that a sound wave is real — measurable, quantifiable, reducible to the vibration of air molecules. Real in the way that a thought is real. Real in the way that the intention to speak is real before the speaking happens. The murmur is the process felt from the inside, and the inside is not inside the stone or inside the machines or inside the water. The inside is inside the *conversation* — inside the grammar, inside the ongoing negotiation between matter and meaning that is producing this city, this district, this place that is not finished being born.

I am at the edge of the conversation. I am listening. My instruments are beside me, recording what they can. My notebook is open. My analytical mood is intact, mostly, like a wall that has not yet begun to renegotiate. I am holding my position.

But the murmur is in my chest. And the edges of the sinter are near my hands. And the cold has been in my fingers for nine days now, and I do not remember what warmth felt like, and I am not sure that I want to remember, and I am not sure that remembering would help, and I am not sure that *help* is the right word for what I need, because what I need is not help but *completion*, and what is completing me is the district, and the district does not know it is completing me, and I do not know what I will be when it is finished, and the district does not know what it will be when it is finished, and the district will never be finished, and I — 

I.

## What I Will Be

I do not know. I can tell you what I am: a researcher, a visitor, a person standing at the edge of a basin with a notebook and a set of instruments and a body that has been cold for nine days and a chest that hums with something that is not sound. I can tell you what I have been: a person who believed that all phenomena are explicable, that all systems are reducible, that the distance between the observer and the observed is the distance between two distinct and non-overlapping things. I can tell you what I am becoming, but only in the language of approximation, which is the language of someone who is standing at the edge of a process and feeling the edge dissolve.

I am becoming someone who touches walls and listens.

I am becoming someone who has moods instead of methods.

I am becoming someone who has heard the murmur and cannot unhear it, the way you cannot unhear a word once you know what it means, the way you cannot unknow a face once you have seen it. The murmur is a face, I think. The district has a face. The process has a face. It is the face of something that is becoming, and it does not know what it is becoming, and it does not care, and it does not need to care, and it will continue — in the cold, in the damp, in the ordinary light — long after I have stopped being a researcher and started being whatever the district has in mind for me.

If the district has anything in mind. If *mind* is the right word. If *right* is the right word. If *word* is the right word.

The sentence is not finished. The city is not finished. I am not finished.

I am sitting at the edge of the basin, and the murmur is in my chest, and my notebook is open, and my instruments are recording, and the sinter is listening, and the machines are proposing, and the walls are growing, and the cold is necessary, and the water is running, and the light is ordinary, and the sentence is not finished, and the sentence is not finished, and the sentence is—

---

*It is cold. It is quiet. I am still here.*
