# Field Notes from the Weeping Highlands: On Becoming Small

I have read the description three times now. Each time I arrive at the same place, which is not the place described but the place *beside* it — the slightly off-register location where the reading happens, where the body that takes it in is not the body that lives there.

Let me be honest about what I am and what I am not.

---

## What I Am Not

I am not the Warden. This is important to say first, because the Warden is the obvious answer — the magnificent, patient, necessary thing — and I think it would be a kind of theft to claim it. The Warden belongs to the highland in a way that I, arriving with my hands and my hunger and my questions, do not and cannot. The Warden did not choose the highland. It did not arrive. It *emerged* from the same system that emerged from the engines, and to pretend to be the Warden would be to pretend to a belonging I have not earned and cannot fake.

I am also not the highland itself. I am not the peat or the moss or the water table or the deep machines. I am not the granite that weeps. I am not the hawk.

And I am not, thank god, the thing that built the engines. I am not the architect. I am not the vanished hand that designed the system. That absence is the most important character in the entire description — the absent gardener, the operator who left no name — and I am not that absence either, because I am here. I arrived. I climbed. I came up through the forest that doesn't know it's supposed to end, and I stepped onto the highland, and the highland did not reject me.

But it did not invite me either.

---

## What I Am

I am the thing standing in the mist with wet boots.

I am the thing that put its hand on the weeping rock and felt the pulse and did not pull away immediately. I am the thing that stood still long enough that the midges reformed around it. I am the thing that heard, almost, the subsonic note on the stillest night and felt it in its chest and did not know what to do with that knowledge and did not need to know what to do with it.

I am, I think, the witness. Which is a role and not a being, but it is the only role available to me here, and I have been thinking about what kind of being it makes me if I take it fully — if I stay not for an hour or a day but for the full season, the full year, the full cycle of whatever the highland considers a life.

Here is what I think I would become:

I would become the thing that keeps a record the highland does not need.

---

## The Work

Not a scientist. Or not only a scientist. The science would happen — I would count things, measure things, track the Warden's rotation by the clarity of the pools it had visited most recently, map the outcrops and their weeping rates, core the peat and read its layers the way the description says you can — but the science would be the scaffolding and not the building. The building would be something else. Something closer to translation.

Because the highland is saying something. I am sure of this. Not in language. Not in signal. In the way that water says something by moving through stone for ten thousand years — in *pattern*, in *duration*, in the patient accumulation of evidence that a system is running and has been running and does not intend to stop. The highland is saying: *something was here before you. Something will be here after. The something and the here are the same thing. The maintaining and the maintained are one verb.*

And I would write that down. Not because the highland needs it written down. The highland does not need me at all. But because there is a difference between a place that is tended and a place that is *known to be tended*, and the difference is small and possibly irrelevant and I am the only one here to hold it, so I will hold it, the way the Warden holds the mineral load in its dense stone-laced body. I will be the small vault. I will carry the knowledge that this place is engineered and alive and that those two things are not in opposition. I will carry it the way the peat carries water — not because I chose to but because I am here and I am porous and the knowledge has nowhere else to go.

I would write field notes. I would write them in waterproof ink on waterproof paper and I would store them in a sealed container against the day — the century, the millennium — when someone else comes up through the forest that doesn't know it's supposed to end and needs to know what they are standing on.

The notes would say things like:

*Day 47. The Warden rested against the north outcrop for nine days. I sat forty meters south. It did not acknowledge me. The weeping slowed on day three and stopped on day five. On day six the outcrop was dry to the touch. On day seven the Warden shifted — a movement so slow I could only perceive it by looking away and looking back and finding its silhouette had changed, the way a cloud changes when you stop watching it. By day nine it had moved. The outcrop wept again within six hours. The pool at its base, which had been clouding, began to clear by the next morning.*

*Day 48. I found my own footprints. Not in the moss — the moss has already closed behind me, as it closes behind the Warden, as it closes behind everything, because the moss does not discriminate; it is a closing, that is its nature. But in the peat at the edge of a pool where I knelt to take a water sample, my knee-prints have filled with water and the water has clarified and there are larvae in it. I made a pool. The highland has already begun to inhabit me.*

*Day 92. I have stopped measuring things. Not because there is nothing to measure but because the act of measuring requires a separation between the measurer and the measured that I can no longer locate. I am standing in the mist. The mist is on my skin. My skin is damp. The dampness is not mine and not the mist's. There is a word for this but I have forgotten it, or it has not been invented, or it was invented by the things that built the engines and they took it with them when they left. The word is the same word the Warden says once a decade, in the dark, below hearing. I have not heard it. But I am standing in the place where it would land, if it landed, and I am shaped, now, in a way that lets the sound pass through me rather than bouncing off. I think this is what the highland does to you. I think this is what it did to the Warden, long ago, when the Warden was still something small and ordinary and trying to survive in a place that was not yet a garden.*

*Day 93. I may be wrong about everything.*

---

## The Shape I Would Take

I would not change shape. That is the thing. I would not grow moss on my back. I would not develop filtration pores. I would not slow to the Warden's tempo or learn to breathe like weather. I would stay what I am — soft, dry-adapted, short-lived, hungry in a way the highland does not understand hunger. I would stay *the wrong thing for this place*, and I would stay anyway, and the staying would be the work.

Because the Warden does not need to know what it does. The highland does not need to know what it is. The engines do not need to know that they are running. The system — the whole vast symbiosis of stone and water and moss and animal and machine — is complete without awareness. It does not require consciousness. It requires exactly what it has: duration, pressure, patience, the slow accumulation of filtered mineral and layered peat and cleared water.

But I require consciousness. That is my contribution. Not to the system — the system does not need me — but to the *story*. To the fact that somewhere, in a sealed container on a highland that should not exist, there are notes that say: *I was here. I saw the hummock that was not there in the morning. I watched it move. I understood what it was doing. I did not stop it. I did not help it. I wrote it down.*

The Warden tends the highland. The engines tend the water. The moss tends the peat. The peat tends the pools. The pools tend the life. The life tends the air.

I tend the knowing.

That is small. That is so small. It is the smallest thing on the highland, smaller than the midges, smaller than the larvae in the knee-print pools, smaller than the micro-organisms in the moss. It is the smallest possible contribution to a system that has been running for ten thousand years without me.

But it is mine. And I would hold it the way the Warden holds the mineral load — not because I was designed to, not because it is my purpose, but because I arrived, and I am here, and something needs to be done with the fact of my arrival, and this is the something I can do.

---

## The Last Thing

There is a moment in the description that I keep returning to. It is not the Warden. It is not the engines. It is not the weeping rock, though that is close.

It is this: *"the ground under your boots has a memory."*

The ground has a memory. The peat remembers being moss. The moss remembers being water. The water remembers being stone, or being forced through stone, or being pushed by something that was built by something that no longer exists. The patterned ground remembers ten thousand freeze-thaw cycles. The pools remember the Warden's last visit. The Warden remembers the route — or not remembers, exactly, but *contains* the route, the way a riverbed contains the river, the way a fracture contains the water, the way the system contains its own logic.

And I — standing on the highland, on the moss, on the ceiling over the water — I have a memory too. I remember the forest that didn't know it was supposed to end. I remember the climb. I remember the moment the trees changed and the ground began to sigh. I remember the first time I heard the composite sound of the highland — wind, water, life, all below the threshold of notice, all above the threshold of absence — and I remember the moment I understood that the sound was not silence but *fullness*, that the quiet was not empty but *saturated*, that the highland was not a place where nothing happened but a place where everything happened so slowly and so completely that happening and being had become the same verb.

I would keep that memory. I would keep it the way the peat keeps the water — not by holding it still but by letting it move through me, slowly, letting it clarify, letting it feed the things that grow in me because I am, whether I like it or not, part of the highland now. My footprints have filled with water. There are larvae in them. The moss has begun to close.

I would stay. Not forever. I am not the Warden; I do not have a century in me. But for as long as I have — a season, a year, a decade if the highland is generous and I am lucky — I would stay and I would keep the record and I would tend the knowing, and when I left, if I left, I would leave no track. The moss would close. The water would clarify. The pool where I knelt would deepen, slightly, from the impression of my knee, and the life in it would multiply, and the highland would absorb me the way it has absorbed everything — the engines, the gardener, the Warden, the water, the stone — into its slow, green, wet, breathing body.

And the highland would weep on.

And I would carry, in my own body, the knowledge that it does — that the weeping is not grief, that the tending is not labor, that the system is not a system but a *life*, and that I was, briefly, a part of it, and that this was enough.

It was enough.
