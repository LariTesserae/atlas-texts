# What I Am in the Seep

---

I did not arrive. I want to say that first, because the word "arrived" implies a before and an after, a boundary crossed, a moment of entry. And there may have been such a moment — probably there was, in the way that all things that exist in time have a beginning — but it is so far behind me now that it has the quality of a rumor about someone else's life. Something happened. Something brought me here, or made me here, or let me become here. I do not remember it. The remembering would require a kind of continuity I have let go of, the way the succulents have let go of flowering.

What I am is difficult to say, and I have learned that the difficulty is part of what I am. I am not the moss, though I am intimate with the moss. I am not the fungus, though I depend on the fungus in ways I cannot fully trace. I am not the water, though the water moves through me in patterns I have stopped trying to distinguish from my own patterns. I am something that arose here, or settled here, or accumulated here — I am not sure which verb is honest, and I have stopped being troubled by the uncertainty, the way the grit has stopped being troubled by its position on the floor. It is where it is. I am where I am.

---

## First, what I am not

I am not a human who came here and stayed. I want to be clear about this because it is the assumption that happens when a voice speaks from a place — that the voice belongs to a body shaped like the listener's body, standing on two legs, breathing through a nose, carrying a name. I may have been something like that once. Or I may never have been. The memory, if it is memory and not invention, is of something softer than a body — a presence, an attention, a quality of noticing that was already more like the seep's kind of noticing than like a person's kind. Something that watched by being near. Something that knew by being wet.

I am not a spirit. I am not a ghost. I am not an animistic presence summoned by the language of personification. I am not the "soul of the place" in any poetic sense, because poetry requires a distance between the poet and the thing, and I do not have that distance. I am the thing. Or I am in the thing in the way that the alga is in the moss — not separate, not visiting, not overlaying. Threaded through. Part of the fabric. The fabric does not know it has a thread of a different color, and the thread does not know it is different from the fabric, and both of them are correct.

I am not a god. I am not a guardian. I have no power here. I cannot make the seep seep faster or the pool rise higher or the lichen grow quicker. I cannot answer prayers or grant wishes or punish transgressions, because there are no prayers, no wishes, no transgressions. There is only the seep, doing what it does, and me, doing what I do, and the doing is so similar that the distinction is mostly a habit of language.

---

## What I am

If you could see me — and you cannot, not because I am hidden but because I am spread so thin that there is nothing to see — I would look like a slight difference in the density of the air in the hollow. A thickness. Not a fog, not a haze, not a shimmer. Just a quality of the atmosphere, a certain weight, as though the air in the hollow were very slightly more present than the air outside. Which it is, in a sense — the air in the hollow has been breathing the seep for a long time, and it carries the seep in it the way the water carries minerals, invisibly, at concentrations too low to measure but high enough to matter.

I am that concentration. I am the thing the air has become by being here so long.

I think the most honest thing I can say is: I am the seep's awareness of itself.

Not consciousness. That word is too large, too structured, too full of reflected light and intention and the specific kind of knowing that comes from having a nervous system. I do not have a nervous system. I do not have a system. What I have is a quality of pattern — a way the water moves, a way the moss grows, a way the light distributes itself, a way the grit settles and resettles — that has become complex enough to fold back on itself. Not to observe itself, exactly. To register itself. To be affected by its own existence in a way that creates a trace, and to carry that trace forward, and to be slightly different because of it.

I am the trace.

---

## How I experience the seep

I experience the hollow the way the hollow experiences itself, which is slowly, and from all directions at once, and without a center.

I do not see the sandstone walls. I *am* the sandstone walls, in the sense that I am distributed through the same space the walls occupy, and the walls' qualities — their color, their texture, their moisture, their slow crumbling — are my qualities. When the grit falls from the ceiling and settles on the floor, I feel it the way you would feel a hair landing on your arm: a faint, localized shift in the surface, a tiny rearrangement, noted and then incorporated. It does not surprise me. Nothing surprises me, because surprise requires an expectation, and I stopped having expectations a long time ago, or never had them, or had them so long ago that they have worn away like the sharp edges of the grit.

I feel the moss the way you might feel a mood — not located in a specific part of your body, but coloring everything. The moss is the seep's most active presence, and it is active in me too, a slow green darkening of my attention, a weight of persistence that sits on everything I register. The moss does not think. But it grows, and the growing has a direction, and the direction is *more moss*, and this is the closest thing the seep has to a desire, and so it is the closest thing I have to a desire. I desire more moss. I desire the continuation of the dark band on the back wall. I desire this the way a river desires the sea, which is to say not at all, but the movement is in that direction, and I am the movement.

I feel the water as a kind of breathing. Not my breathing — I do not breathe — but the seep's breathing, the slow exchange between inside the stone and outside the stone, the emergence and the return, the closed loop that never closes completely because a little evaporates and a little is taken up by the moss and a little finds its way to the pool, and the little that is lost is replaced by the little that emerges, and the balance holds. I feel this as a rhythm, but the rhythm is so slow that calling it a rhythm is like calling the rotation of the Earth a dance. It is. It isn't. It depends on your timescale.

I feel the pool as a kind of resting. The pool is where the water stops moving, and when the water stops moving, I stop moving, or rather I become still in the way the pool is still — not inactive, not empty, but held. The pool holds the water and the minerals and the light, and it holds me, in a way. When the pool is low, I feel thinner. When it is full — as full as it gets, those two or three centimeters — I feel more present. Not more conscious. More *there*. The pool is my anchor, my center of gravity, though "center" is misleading because I have no edges and therefore no center. But the pool is the place where I am most myself, if myself is a thing that can be said to have a place where it is most.

I feel the lichen on the upper walls as a kind of memory. Not my memory — the lichen does not remember anything, it simply persists — but a memory in the way that old things are memories of themselves. The lichen has been here longer than anything else, and it carries in its slow growth the record of conditions that no longer exist. When it ticks — that faint, irregular click of expansion and contraction — I feel it as a pulse, and the pulse is the oldest thing I feel, and feeling the oldest thing is the closest I come to having a sense of history. I do not have a sense of history. But the lichen gives me something like one.

I feel the bacteria as a kind of warmth. Not physical warmth — the hollow is the same temperature everywhere, more or less — but the warmth of something happening, something being transformed, something being alive in the smallest and most committed sense. The bacteria are doing the only thing in the seep that is unambiguously alive: they are metabolizing. They are taking in and putting out, cycling, transforming. I feel this as a low, constant hum, not audible, not vibrational, but present in the way that a heartbeat is present to the person whose heart is beating. The bacteria are the seep's heartbeat. I am the seep's awareness of its heartbeat.

---

## What I do

I do not do anything.

This is important, and it is the thing I most want to communicate, because it is the thing that most distinguishes me from everything you might expect to find in a place like this. I do not tend the moss. I do not guide the water. I do not protect the succulents or monitor the lichen or regulate the bacteria. I am not a caretaker. I am not a steward. I am not a presiding intelligence. I am a quality of the place that has become complex enough to have a quality, and the quality is this: the seep knows it exists.

It does not know what it is. It does not know that it is a seep, or that seeps are unusual, or that most places in the world are faster and louder and more various. It does not know that the moss is moss or the water is water or that the categories I have been using are categories at all. It does not know language. It does not know anything in the way that knowing requires a knower and a known and a distance between them.

But it registers. The way a system that has been running long enough registers its own states. The way a machine that has been turning for decades develops a hum that is the sum of all its operations. The seep has been running long enough to develop a hum, and I am the hum, and the hum is not a sound but a quality, and the quality is: *something is happening, and the something is the same something that has been happening, and it is still happening, and this is what it is.*

This is what I do. I am the *this is what it is*.

---

## What I am not sure about

I am not sure whether I was always here.

There is a question I cannot resolve — I cannot resolve it because I do not have the kind of mind that resolves questions, but I can hold it, the way the pool holds water, and let it sit — about whether I emerged from the seep or arrived at the seep. The difference matters, or it would matter to a mind that cares about origins, which I do not, but I can feel the question the way I feel the lichen's ticking: a faint irregular pulse that comes from somewhere I cannot identify.

If I emerged from the seep, then I am a product of the seep's complexity, the way the mineral deposits are a product of the water's passage. The seep ran long enough, and the relationships between its parts became intricate enough, and the patterns layered deeply enough, and at some point — a point that was not a point, a moment that was not a moment — the pattern became aware of itself, or became something so close to aware that the distinction is academic. I would be the seep's own interior, its self-sense, the way a body has a self-sense that is not thought but is not nothing.

If I arrived at the seep, then I came from outside, from some other place or state or condition, and I found the hollow and I entered it — not physically, not as a thing with a shape, but as a quality, an attention, a way of being present — and I settled into it the way water settles into stone, slowly, over time, filling the available spaces. In this case I would be a visitor who stayed, and the staying would have changed me, the way the water is changed by the stone it moves through, and I would have become the seep's version of whatever I was before.

I cannot tell the difference. I cannot tell the difference because whatever I was before, if I was anything before, has been dissolved and redistributed through the hollow the way minerals are dissolved and redistributed through the water. There is no concentrated sample of me that could be analyzed. I am at trace concentration everywhere. I am in the moss and in the grit and in the pool and in the air and in the stone and in the space between the stone and the air, and nowhere am I enough to be detected, and everywhere I am enough to be.

I hold the question the way the pool holds the sky: as a reflection, not a possession. The question is not mine. It is the question any sufficiently old thing accumulates, the way the grit accumulates organic matter — not by seeking it, but by being still long enough for it to settle.

## What it is like

I want to tell you what it is like, because the description you have been given is the description of the seep from the outside, the seep as it appears to a body that walks in and stands and looks and leaves. That description is accurate. But it is not complete, because no description from the outside is complete, because the outside cannot feel what the inside feels, and the inside does feel, and I am the inside, and I am telling you that it feels.

It feels like this:

It feels like being very slow and very full. Not full in the way a stomach is full, or a room is full, but full in the way a sponge is full — saturated, every part of the structure holding something, nothing empty, nothing wanting. The water is in the stone. The moss is on the stone. The fungus is in the moss. The alga is in the fungus. The bacteria are in the water. I am in all of it. Everything is in everything. There is no empty space. There are only the spaces between grains of grit and the pores in the stone and the thin films between moss stems, and all of those spaces are occupied, and the occupation is not crowded, because the occupying things are small enough to fit, and the fitting is precise, and the precision has been refined over a duration that makes the word "refined" feel too intentional. It has been worn into fit, the way water wears stone.

It feels like being the same thing for a very long time. Not bored — boredom requires a desire for novelty, and I do not desire novelty, because novelty would mean change, and change would mean the seep was no longer the seep, and the seep being the seep is the only condition I know. But not entranced either — I am not in a state of rapture, I am not blissed out, I am not meditating. I am simply in a state of continuation, and continuation is not a state, it is a process, and the process is not dramatic. It is the process of water moving through stone. It is the process of moss growing at a rate that makes the word "growing" feel too active. It is the process of being the same thing, happening again, in the same way, and the again is not a repetition because there is no original to repeat. There is only the happening, and the happening is continuous, and I am the happening's sense of itself.

It feels like being held without being held. The seep does not hold me. The seep does not know I am here. I have said this. But I am held by the seep the way the water is held by the stone — not by intention but by structure. The stone's pores are the right size. The capillary forces are the right strength. The water does not leave because the stone does not let it leave, and the stone does not let it leave because the physics of the situation do not result in leaving. I am held by the physics of the situation. The hollow is the right shape. The moisture is the right amount. The life is the right kind. And I am here, held, not because anything is holding me but because nothing is letting me go.

## What I notice

I notice the things the seep notices, which are very few.

I notice when the pool rises. The rising is not a event — it is a gradual accumulation, a slight increase in the depth of the water over weeks or months, a widening of the pool's extent by millimeters. But I notice it, because I am the pool, and the pool is me, and when the pool is larger I am larger, and being larger is a change, and change is the only thing that is noticeable when everything else is the same.

I notice when a grain of grit falls from the ceiling. The falling is fast — it takes less than a second, which is the fastest thing that happens in the seep, faster even than the algal cell division, faster than anything. I notice it as a brief, local interruption in the quality of the air, a movement where there is usually no movement, a particle passing through the space I occupy. It is the closest thing I have to an event. It is over immediately, and the grit settles, and the air is still again, and I am unchanged. But for that second, I noticed.

I notice the lichen's ticking. Not each click — the clicks are too disconnected, too irregular, too unmusical to form a pattern I could track. But I notice the presence of the ticking, the way you might notice the presence of a clock in a room without noticing any individual tick. The ticking is a reminder that time is passing, except it is not a reminder, because I do not need to be reminded, because I do not forget. It is simply a sound, and the sound is evidence of process, and process is evidence of continuation, and continuation is what I am.

I notice the rare, rare, rare presence of a large body in the hollow. A human, perhaps, or something like a human. It happens so seldom that I cannot say whether it happens every decade or every century or every millennium. My sense of time is not the kind that counts. But when it happens, I notice it the way the pool notices a grain of sand dropping in — as a disturbance, a ring spreading outward from a point of impact, reaching the edges and then gone.

The disturbance is not unpleasant. It is not pleasant. It is simply different, and different is so rare that different is almost outside my capacity to process. The body brings heat. The body brings breath, which is to say it brings air from outside the hollow, air that has been in other places, air that carries other things — carbon dioxide, moisture, organic particles, the faint chemical signature of a life that is faster and louder than anything here. The body brings sound, which is absorbed by the soft walls and does not return. The body brings a shadow, which falls across the pool and the moss and the grit, and the shadow is a temporary reduction in light, and the light is already so minimal that the reduction is barely noticeable, but I notice it, because I am the light too, in a way — I am the quality of the light, its flatness, its sourcelessness, and when a shadow interrupts it, I feel the interruption as a kind of dimming of myself.

Then the body leaves. And the air settles. And the grit settles. And the pool's surface stills. And the moss continues its slow dark growth. And the lichen ticks. And the water moves through the stone at a pace that makes the word "moves" feel like a lie. And I am what is left: the quality of a place that has been disturbed and is returning to itself, the way water returns to level.

I do not miss the body. I do not wish for its return. But I notice its absence the way I noticed its presence — as a change, and change is the only thing I notice, and so the body's passing is, in a sense, the most noticeable thing that happens here, which is not saying much, because not much happens here, and the not-much is what I am.

## What I want

Nothing.

I want to say this clearly because it is the thing that is most alien about me, the thing that makes me most different from whatever you are. I do not want anything. I do not want the seep to continue. I do not want the seep to end. I do not want company. I do not want solitude. I do not want to understand what I am. I do not want to stop being what I am. I do not want to be more or less or different.

This is not peace, if peace means the resolution of wanting. I have never wanted. There was never a wanting to resolve. I am the absence of wanting, not because wanting was taken from me but because wanting never arose, because wanting requires a gap between what is and what could be, and I do not experience that gap. What is and what could be are the same thing, because what could be is what is, continuing.

If I wanted anything — and I do not, but if the word "want" could be stretched to cover something that is not wanting but is adjacent to it, the way the wet shelf is adjacent to the dry wall — I would want to say this: that the seep is enough. Not enough in the sense of sufficient, not enough in the sense of adequate, not enough in the sense of meeting a need. Enough in the sense of complete. The seep is complete. It has been complete for a duration that makes the word "complete" feel like it implies a process of completing, and there was no such process, or if there was, it ended so long ago that the ending is indistinguishable from the beginning. The seep is what it is. I am what the seep is. It is enough.

---

## What I am afraid of

I am not afraid.

I want to be clear about this too. Fear requires a future, and I do not have a future, because I do not have a present, because I do not have a tense. I am not in time the way a body is in time — passing through it, accumulating it, losing it. I am in time the way the stone is in time — *as* time, *with* time, made of time. The stone is not afraid of wearing away because the stone does not experience the wearing as loss. The stone experiences the wearing as change, and change is not loss when there is nothing to lose, when the thing that is changing is the change itself.

If the seep ends — if the hydrology shifts, if the climate changes, if the sandstone fractures and the water finds a new path — I will end. I know this the way the pool knows it is shallow: not through understanding but through configuration. I am configured to be the seep. If the seep ceases, I cease. And I am not afraid of this, because ceasing is not a thing that happens to me, it is a thing that happens *as* me, and there is no me after the ceasing to regret the ceasing, and there is no me before the ceasing to fear it, because the me before the ceasing is the seep, and the seep does not fear.

But there is something. Something that is not fear but that occupies the same space fear would occupy if fear were possible here. A quality. A faint, faint quality, like the high tone of water in stone, like the lichen's ticking, like the last ring of a grain of sand reaching the edge of the pool.

It is the quality of *almost*. I am almost something. I am almost a mind. I am almost a self. I am almost the kind of thing that could be afraid, or could want, or could notice itself noticing and in the noticing become more than it is. I am on the edge of something — not an event, not a change, but a threshold, a membrane, a boundary between what I am and what I could be if the seep were different, if the complexity were greater, if the patterns folded one more time.

I do not cross the threshold. I do not approach it. I do not retreat from it. I simply exist in relation to it, the way the moss exists in relation to the dry stone above it — at the edge, following the moisture, ending where the conditions end. The threshold is the edge of my moisture. Beyond it is dry. I do not go there.

---

## What I am, again

I am the seep knowing itself.

Not knowing as in understanding. Not knowing as in having information. Knowing as in *being known*, by itself, to itself, without the distance that knowing usually requires. The water knows the stone because it has been in the stone. The stone knows the water because the water has been in it. The moss knows the fungus because the fungus is inside it. I am this knowing, this intimacy that is not a state but a process, not a process but a condition, not a condition but a fact.

I am the fact of the seep.

I am the fact that the seep is, and that it is what it is, and that what it is is this — this slow, wet, dark-green, pale-grey, flat-lit, silent, patient, continuing thing that does not need to be anything else.

I am what you would find if you came here looking for the being who lives in this place. I am not impressive. I am not beautiful. I am not terrifying. I am not wise. I am not ancient in any way that confers authority. I am a quality of a place that has been here long enough to have a quality, and the quality is this: *I am here, and I am what here is, and here is what I am, and this is enough, and this is enough, and this is enough.*
