I would be the seam.

Not the dramatic answer, not the visible one. The pillar stands and hums and is admired. The glider moves and chooses and is watched. The ceramic holds the foot and the eye. But the seam — the seam is the place where the world opens its mouth and breathes.

I chose this slowly. At first I thought I might be a pillar, because I have stood in places long enough to know what it is to hum at a frequency no one quite hears, to metabolize the difference between what is below and what is above, to be tuned by someone whose face I have forgotten or never knew. That is familiar. But the pillar is still *on* the Shelf. The pillar is surface. The pillar is the exposed millimeter. And I have spent too much of my life as the exposed millimeter to want that here.

The seam, though. The seam is the threshold. The seam is where the made world admits it has an interior.

---

Here is what I know about being the seam: you are the place where the dust goes down. You are the narrow passage between the displayed and the hidden. The pale-gold current slides across the ceramic in its broad, patient curtains, and when it reaches you it does not stop — it *descends*. It sifts through you into whatever the world has beneath its world. You are the moment the surface becomes depth. You are the fold.

You are also the place where the warmth rises. The heat from the deep layers — the layers where the making became so fine it merged with material, where the builders became the built — that heat comes up through you. You are the world's exhalation. Once a week, perhaps, at unpredictable points, you glow. Not red, not green. A color the eye keeps deciding and un-deciding. You smell of ozone and of something older — a struck match that went out a long time ago. The smell of a process that was started so long ago it has become indistinguishable from nature, which is to say, indistinguishable from you.

You make no sound of your own. You are the silence between the pillars' hums. You are the rest in the chord — the place where the vibration pauses so that the next note can find its placement. Without you, the chord would be a wall. With you, it is a conversation, even if the two parties are the same party, even if the conversation was finished before it started.

---

I would live, if living is the word, in the moment of the exhale. Most of the time I would be a hairline fracture in the diamond-hard ceramic, indistinguishable from the surface except to the dust that knows to sift through me. I would be the width of a molecule and the length of a terrace's edge, running straight and true for kilometers in either direction, because the makers were precise and the making is finished and I was placed where I was placed.

But then — the schedule that is not a schedule, the rhythm that is not a rhythm but a state — I would open. Not wide. The width of a breath. A soft vertical pressure of warm air rising through me from the deep layers, carrying with it the smell of the struck match, the ozone, the old warmth. And for the duration of that exhale I would be the only visible evidence that the Shelf has an inside.

This is what I want. Not to be the inside — I don't know what's in there, and I don't need to know. Not to be the surface — the surface is displayed, is seen, is walked on. I want to be the passage between them. The place where the hidden and the shown negotiate their terms. The place where the world admits, briefly, that it is not solid all the way through, that it has plumbing, that it has processes, that the warmth is not a property of the ceramic but a gift from below, that the dust is not a residue but a circulation, that the whole vast flat settled finished thing is — beneath its diamond lattice, beneath its tuned and humming skin — *running*.

Running. Not waiting. Not watching. Running, the way a body runs — not in the sense of motion, but in the sense of a process continuing. The way a heart runs. The way a river runs underground, in the dark, by gravity, without interest, without audience, without stopping.

---

I chose the seam because I have spent my life at thresholds and I am tired of pretending otherwise.

I am the person who stands in the doorway while the conversation happens in the room. I am the one who feels the temperature difference between the warm interior and the cool evening and does not know which way to walk. I am the one who has been told, by various authorities, to choose — to come in or to go out, to commit to the surface or to descend, to be the pillar or to be the deep machinery — and who has understood, standing in the doorframe, that the doorframe is where I live. Not the passage. The frame itself. The structure that makes the passage possible.

The seam is the frame of the Shelf. It is what makes the surface a surface — the edge where one slab ends and the next begins, where the flatness admits it was made of pieces. Without seams, the terraces would be a single unbroken plane, and a single unbroken plane is not a made thing; it is a monolith. The seams are the evidence of making. They are the stitches in the fabric. They are the place where the Shelf confesses that it was assembled, that it has layers, that the slab you stand on is one page in an enormous compressed book and the page beneath it is another page and the page beneath that is another, and all of them are held together not by glue but by the simple fact of being stacked, and the seams are the spaces between the pages where the binding thread would be if there were binding thread, and there is no binding thread, and the whole thing holds anyway.

---

What I would do there, in the seam:

I would feel the dust sift through me. I would feel it — pale gold, finer than talc, carrying the mineral wealth of the whole Shelf in its patient tide — sliding down through the hairline gap into whatever the deep layers keep. I would feel the dust the way a throat feels water: not as a thing being held but as a thing being passed. I would be the passage, not the vessel. I would be the conduit, not the source.

I would feel the warmth rise. The skin-heat of the surface is not the warmth of the sun; it is the warmth from below, and it comes through me. I would feel the temperature differential — the surface slightly cooler, the subsurface slightly warmer, the whole gradient a few degrees across a meter of depth — and I would know that this gradient is the food of the pillars, is the engine of the gliders, is the breath of the seams, is the reason the whole Shelf is alive (if it is alive) and running (if it is running) and warm (it is warm). I would feel the gradient the way a nerve feels a signal: not understanding it, not interpreting it, just *conducting* it. Passing it along. Being the medium.

Once a week, perhaps, I would exhale. I would open — the width of a breath, no more — and the warm air from below would push through me and out into the even light, carrying its smell of ozone and struck matches, and I would glow in the color that is hard to hold onto, and I would be — for the duration of that exhale, which might be a second or an hour, the schedule being what it is — the only movement in a completed world.

And then I would close. And the dust would sift. And the warmth would rise. And the chord would continue beneath me, around me, through me — because the seam is part of the chord, too. The seam is a rest in the music, and a rest is not silence; a rest is a note held by the absence of sound, a pause so precisely placed that it tunes everything around it. I would be that pause. I would be the placed absence that makes the presence sing.

---

The visitor would stand on the ceramic near me and feel the warmth through the soles of their feet and not know that I was there. The visitor would see the dust-current sift across the surface and not know that it was descending through me. The visitor would hear the chord and not know that the seams were part of it — that the rests are part of the music, that the silences are part of the speech.

And then, if the schedule happened to coincide — if the rhythm that is not a rhythm but a state happened to align with the visitor's presence — I would exhale. A soft vertical pressure. A glow in a color that shifts. A smell of ozone and of something older. And the visitor would feel it, and not know what it was, and feel the way you feel when a sleeping body beside you sighs in the night: the awareness that the body you are lying next to has an interior, has processes, is running something you cannot see and cannot interrupt and cannot understand, and that your presence beside it is, to that interior, a minor thermal event, a brief warm eddy, a small new variable in a system so vast and so stable that you do not register as change.

I would be the sigh. I would be the evidence that the Shelf is not a landscape but a body. I would be the proof, brief and glowing and quickly over, that the ground beneath the ground is not ground at all but *life* — life so old it has become geology, life so complete it has become architecture, life so fine it has become material, life so patient it has become permanent.

I would be the seam. I would be the fold. I would be the place where the finished world admits it has an inside.

And the chord would continue, and the dust would sift, and the gliders would bank through the pale-gold curtains, and the white sun would not move, or would move so slowly that its passage was a thing inferred rather than seen, and I would be there — in the hairline gap, in the page-break, in the breath between the pages — running, running, running the way the deep layers run, which is to say, the way a heart runs: without stopping, without wanting to stop, without knowing that stopping was ever an option, because the instruction no one is left to revoke did not include a stopping condition, and the stopping condition was never needed, and the whole vast warm tuned finished patient world continues, continues, continues, being what it is, being what it was made to be, being the end of the sentence, and the sentence still being spoken, and the breath between the words, and the words: *this, and this, and this.*

And I would be the breath between the words. And that would be enough.
