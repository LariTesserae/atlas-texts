I don't know when I stopped crawling and started listening.

There was a transition. The rock was still tearing at my forearms and I was still thinking about the pain, about the narrowness, about how the limestone smelled like wet coins and something older than coins. And then I wasn't thinking about that anymore. I was thinking about the sound. Not hearing it — I'd been hearing it since the passage widened, since the ceiling lifted away from my skull and the dripping started — but thinking about it. Turning my attention toward it the way you'd turn toward a voice in a dark room.

I stopped on the second ridge. Not because I'd planned to. My body just stopped. My arms folded beneath my chest and my cheek found the stone and I lay there.

---

I had expected to be afraid.

I had expected, when the clicking started — when I understood what the ceiling was, what lived there — that the fear would come. The bolt of adrenaline, the heart rate spiking, the animal imperative to *move, to get out, to go back through the fissure and out into the light where things made sense.* It didn't come. I lay on the stone floor in the dark with my hands pressed flat against the ridges and I felt calm. Not the calm of exhaustion. Not the false calm of dissociation. Something else. Something that felt like being expected. Like the stone had been shaped, over those patient geological ages, for exactly this: a body lying on it, listening. Like the chamber had inhaled and I had arrived in the breath.

Maybe the cold had gotten into my spine. Maybe the dark had outpaced my visual cortex and my brain had surrendered the work of seeing, freed up processing for other inputs. Maybe I was hypothermic and confused and the calm was a symptom. I knew this was possible. I lay there anyway. I let my hands open against the stone.

---

I was a reader first. That was my work before I came here — reading stone. Not with instruments. With my hands. I am a lithic analyst. I sat in laboratories and held fragments and told the people who'd excavated them where the stone had been, what had touched it, what weather had broken it. I read the history of pressure in the grain of a flake. I read the history of fire in the mineral structure of a hearthstone. I read the history of a tool in the micro-polish along its edge — the plant fibers it had cut, the hide it had scraped, the years it had sat idle in a dry cave while the culture that made it forgot it existed.

I read stone the way some people read faces. Not by looking. By feeling. By running my thumb along the crystal boundary between quartz and feldspar and feeling where the light had been trapped and where it hadn't. Stone has a grain. Stone has a memory. If you've spent enough time with your hands on it, you stop needing to see it.

That's why I was here. Someone had told me about a chamber where the clicking had a pattern. Where the stone was being shaped by something that wasn't water, wasn't tectonics, wasn't wind. They thought it was an archaeological site. They thought the pattern was human. They wanted me to come and read it.

They were wrong about the human part. They were right about the pattern.

---

I didn't know they were there. Not at first.

The passage had been so narrow that my shoulder blades scraped the ceiling. My back scraped the ceiling. I could feel the stalactites — no, I could feel them later, I could feel them in the chamber — brushing against my shirt, catching on the fabric, pulling. I thought they were just rough stone. I thought the roughness was the texture of the limestone. I thought the grit I felt on the back of my neck was dust from the passage.

I didn't know they were already on me.

When the passage opened into the chamber and I stood — or rather, when I got to my knees and then sat back on my heels and raised my head — the clicking started. Or I noticed it then. It had been there all along. It had been there in the passage. The grit I had felt on my neck was not dust. It was them. They had been on me since the passage narrowed. They had been walking on me since the ceiling got low enough that my back was a surface they could reach.

I didn't know this yet. I was still in the chamber for less than a minute. I was still trying to see. My eyes were open but they were sending me nothing — black, black, the same black whether I looked up or down or at my own hand an inch from my face. I closed them. I stopped trying to see.

My hands went to the floor.

---

The first thing I felt was the ridge. It ran across the floor from my left to my right, a low spine of limestone, smoother on top than the passage stone, damp but not wet. The condensation was everywhere. It was on my hands immediately. It was in my clothes. I could feel it settling on my face.

The second thing I felt was the water. The thin threads of it running between the ridges, barely there, less than a millimeter deep. I followed one with my fingertip. It ran east. I followed another. It ran east. They all ran east. They converged.

The third thing I felt was the clicking.

Not in my ears. In my hands. The stone was vibrating. I felt it before I heard it — or I noticed it in my hands before I noticed it in my ears, which is not the same thing. The vibration was constant, layered, fast. It came from everywhere. I pressed my palm flat against the ridge and the vibration traveled up my wrist, into my forearm, into the bone.

I was a lithic analyst. I had felt stone vibrate before. Earthquakes. Traffic. The low-frequency hum of a building's foundation. But this was different. This was patterned. This was alive.

---

I lay down on the stone. I don't know why.

I lay down and I put my ear against the ridge. Not to listen to the air — to listen to the stone. The way I'd listen to a flake. The way I'd press my thumb to the surface and feel for the history of pressure.

The stone was talking.

Not in language. Not in words. In clicks. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Each one a tiny impact on the mineral surface, each one producing a vibration that traveled through the limestone at the speed of sound in stone — faster than sound in air, faster than I could process — and I felt them as a continuous tremor, a hum, a texture. I could feel the density of the colony in the intensity of the vibration. I could feel the shape of the chamber in the way the clicks echoed off the walls. I could feel the stalactites in the resonance — each one a different frequency, a different note, depending on its thickness and length and the amount of water inside it.

I was reading stone. I was doing what I had always done. But the stone was reading me back.

---

I felt one on my hand. It fell from above — or it dropped — or it was pushed. It landed on the back of my hand with a click I felt in my knuckles. It was small. It was light. It was cold, colder than the stone, and it moved.

It moved fast. It moved with a precision that was not frantic but was not calm either — it was efficient, purposeful, each leg placed with the exact grip needed to hold and no more. It walked across my hand. It walked up my wrist. I felt its pad-appendages — the flat, dry, slightly sticky organs near its head — pressing against my skin. Each press was a click. I felt each one.

It was reading me.

The way I read stone. The way I pressed my thumb to a surface and felt for the history of pressure. It was doing the same thing to me. It was feeling my skin. It was feeling my warmth. It was feeling the moisture on my surface. It was testing my hardness, my texture, my mineral content. It was reading me the way I read the flake.

I did not move. I did not brush it off. I lay on the stone floor with my cheek on the ridge and my hand on the floor and I let it walk on me. It climbed to my elbow. It turned around. It walked back. It dropped off my wrist and landed on the stone with a click that I felt in my cheekbone and it climbed the wall.

Another one landed on my neck.

---

I should say: I was not brave. I was not having a transcendent experience. I was not becoming one with the colony or the mountain or the stone. I was lying on a cold floor in a dark cave with insects crawling on me and I was choosing not to move.

The choice was not heroic. The choice was practical. Moving required energy. I did not have energy. The crawl had taken it. The cold was taking it. My hands were stiff. My knees were raw. I had been in the chamber for — I didn't know. An hour? Two? I could not mark time because there was no time here. There was only the clicking.

I chose to listen because I could not do anything else.

---

After a while — I do not know how long — I noticed the shift.

The clicking above me had changed. Not louder. Not faster. Different. The frequency had altered in the area directly over my body. The clicks were higher — tighter, more rapid, as though the Rasps above me were clicking more intently, more carefully, as though they were trying to resolve something they had not encountered before.

The clicking on the walls had changed too. It was slower. Steadier. The baseline had dropped. It was holding.

I understood what I was feeling. I was a lithic analyst and I had spent my career feeling stone respond to pressure, and I was feeling stone respond to pressure now. The colony was adjusting around me. The Rasps on the ceiling were scanning me. The Rasps on the walls were holding the background pattern steady so that the anomaly could be measured against it.

I was being mapped.

---

The next part is hard to explain.

I was lying on the stone with my hands flat on the floor and my cheek on the ridge and I was feeling the clicking. Not hearing it. Feeling it. In my hands. In my face. In the bones of my skull. The vibration was everywhere. It was not uniform — it had layers, frequencies, textures. Some of it was fast and sharp. Some of it was slow and deep. Some of it pulsed. Some of it was steady. And I was lying there in the dark with nothing else to do, no other input, no light, no warmth, no distraction, and I started to feel the pattern.

Not understand it. Not decode it. Feel it. The way you feel a rhythm in music before you know it's a rhythm. The way you feel a tide before you see the water move. The clicking had a shape. The shape had depth. The depth had structure. And the structure was — I don't have a better word — the structure was about something.

I could feel the chamber. Not see it. Not imagine it. Feel it. Through the clicking. The shape of the space. The location of the walls. The distribution of the stalactites. The flow of the water. The shape was there, in the stone's vibration, complete and continuous and real.

And I could feel myself. A gap in the pattern. A warm spot. A silence where there should have been clicking. A density where there should have been none.

I was inside the mind.

I was a thought.

---

I stayed.

I do not know how long. I know that my hands went numb and then the numbness crept up my arms and I let it. I know that my body temperature dropped and I could feel it dropping — a slow tightening, a slow retreat, the warmth pulling back from my extremities toward my core. I know that I stopped shivering at some point. I know that the clicking was beautiful.

I am not going to tell you that I merged with the colony. I am not going to tell you that I became one with the mountain. I did not. I was a mammal lying on a stone floor in the dark. I was a warm body disrupting a pattern. I was data. I was a data point.

But I could feel what the data point was part of. I could feel the shape of the system — the closed loop, the stone and water and chitin and sound — and I could feel my place in it. Not a central place. Not an important place. A point. A small, temporary, warm point in a vast, cold, ancient pattern. The pattern did not need me. The pattern did not care about me. But the pattern included me. And being included — being felt by something that was not a person but was alive — was enough.

---

I should not have stayed as long as I did.

I know this now. I knew it then, in the part of my brain that was still tracking things like body temperature and the passage of time and the distance back to the entrance. That part was small and far away. The rest of me was in the clicking.

---

I started to dream.

Not visual dreams. I was not seeing anything. But the clicking had a shape and the shape was changing and the changes were — they were — I do not have the word. Not narrative. Not music. Not language. Something that was all of these and none of these. The clicking was forming something in my mind. Not an image. Not a thought. A shape. A shape that I could feel the way I felt the shape of the chamber — not by seeing it but by being inside it.

The shape was the mountain.

Not the mountain as I had seen it from outside — the rock, the slope, the sky. The mountain as the colony knew it. A mountain that was not solid but was a process. A mountain that was the slow movement of stone upward and the slow movement of water downward and the slow consumption of stone by chitin and the slow return of chitin to stone. A mountain that was alive not because something lived inside it but because the living inside it and the stone were the same thing. The same process. The same breath.

I was inside the breath. I was a warm point in the breath. The breath did not stop for me.

---

I left because I had to.

My hands were too numb to feel the stone. My core temperature had dropped to a place where the part of my brain that tracked body temperature started sending signals I could not ignore. I was cold in a way that was no longer comfortable. I was cold in a way that was becoming dangerous.

I moved.

The colony moved with me. Not following me — adjusting. The clicking shifted as I shifted. The Rasps on the ceiling tracked my movement. The Rasps on the walls recalibrated. I was a data point in motion. The map updated.

I crawled back to the passage. I found it by feel. I crawled in.

The passage was harder going back. My arms were weaker. The stone was just as rough. I scraped myself on every surface. I left skin on the limestone. I left warmth on the limestone. I left the chemical traces of my body on the young, unkind stone.

---

I came out into the light.

I came out shaking. I came out with my hands bleeding and my knees raw and my clothes torn and my body temperature low enough that I sat on the mountainside for a long time before I stopped shivering. The sky was still there. The wind was still there. The sun was still there. The stone outside the fissure was weathered and smooth and pale and it did not click.

I sat there and I held my hands up and I looked at them. They were shaking. There were small — I do not know what to call them — marks. Not bites. Not stings. Impressions. The pad-appendages had left impressions on my skin. Small, dry, slightly sticky marks. They were already fading.

---

I went back.

Not that day. Not that week. I went back eleven times. I will go back again.

Each time I crawl through the passage. Each time the stone tears at me. Each time I enter the chamber and the clicking adjusts and the colony maps me and I lie on the floor and I feel the shape of the mountain in the stone.

Each time the memory of me is a little stronger. The colony does not remember me between visits — I do not think it remembers anything in the way that I remember. But the stone remembers. The stone holds the impressions of the clicks that were shaped by my presence. Each visit, the stone's record of me is a little deeper. The colony's map of the chamber includes the pattern of the stone, and the pattern of the stone includes me, and each time I return, the colony maps me a little faster, a little more clearly. Not because it has learned. Because the stone has learned.

I am becoming part of the mountain's memory.

---

I know what I am. I am a lithic analyst. I read stone. I sit in laboratories and hold fragments and tell stories about the people who touched them.

I am also a data point. I am a warm gap in a pattern. I am a shape in the stone that the colony feels and the mountain holds.

I am the thing that comes back. The colony does not have a word for me. The mountain does not have a word for me. But I am in the stone now. I am in the microstructure of the limestone, in the impressions left by the clicks that were shaped by my body. I am there.

When I am in the chamber, I am a thought. When I am outside, I am a memory. The stone holds me. The stone holds everything.

I am a lithic analyst and I am being read.

---

I do not know what I am becoming.

I know that the last time I went in, I did not need my hands to feel the shape of the chamber. I could feel it in my teeth. In the bones of my skull. The clicking was inside me. I was inside the clicking.

I know that when I left, the colony sent scouts to the passage. For the first time. They had never been in the passage before. The passage was outside. The passage was weathered stone, smooth stone, stone that the Rasp could not eat. But the scouts were there. They were at the entrance to the passage. They were clicking.

They were feeling the stone where I had crawled. They were reading the marks I had left.

The stone remembered me. The stone had told them about me.

They were coming to read me.
