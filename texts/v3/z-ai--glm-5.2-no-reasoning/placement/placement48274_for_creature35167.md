# Reliquary

## I.

Here is the thing about the chamber: nothing in it needs me.

I have spent eleven years — I am no longer certain, the cold does something to timekeeping, it compresses the hours the way it compresses the air, makes them dense and slow and difficult to count — I have spent what I believe is eleven years walking a loop that I carved into the stone with the heel of my hand. Not a line, not a groove. Just a path worn by repetition. By the pressure of a body passing over the same route, the same rough basalt, the same seven basins, the same four ridges, the same two alcoves where the ceiling drops low enough that I can stand in shade. The path is lighter than the surrounding stone. Polished, almost. Not by intent. By persistence. By the fact that I needed somewhere to walk, and walking in straight lines is what I do, and the lines I walk became a shape, and the shape is mine, and it is the only thing in the chamber that is mine.

The isopods use my path. They did not at first. For the first years — two, three, the count is unreliable, the cold does something to sequence, makes events feel simultaneous even when they are not — they avoided it. They are creatures of the unbroken surface, of the rough, grainy, fresh stone. They graze the microorganism film, and the film grows best on rough stone, and the polished stone of my path offers less. So they avoided it. And then, gradually, over a span I cannot measure but that felt long, felt like the duration of a season that does not exist here, they began to cross it. To use it. Not to follow it — they do not follow it, my path means nothing to them, my path is a human imposition on a space that does not acknowledge human impositions — but to cross it at angles, to walk a few steps along it where it intersects with their own grazing routes, to pause on the smoother stone and rest, their dark shells catching the light from below, glowing faintly along their bellies.

I am not proud of this. I am not anything about this. But I notice it. I notice it because I notice everything now, because there is nothing else to do but notice, and because noticing is the only faculty I have that the chamber has not yet eroded. My hands are rough. My skin is dry, cracked at the knuckles, scaled at the fingertips, the skin of someone who has been cold for a long time, not freezing, not frostbitten, just cold, twelve degrees, the temperature of the stone, the temperature of the water, the temperature of everything, and the body adapts to the temperature of everything by becoming everything's temperature, which is to say by becoming cold, which is to say by becoming stone, which is to say by becoming the place.

I am not the place. I want to be clear about this. I am not the place. I am the thing that watches the place, and I am becoming the thing that watches the place, and the watching is changing me, and the changing is part of the watching, and I do not know where one ends and the other begins.

---

## II.

Let me tell you how I came down. I came down because I heard it.

Not from the seam — the seam is narrow, barely a suggestion in the basalt, invisible from more than a few meters away. I did not find the seam by looking. I found it by listening. I was walking — I was above, I was on the surface, I was doing what people do on the surface, which is to say I was moving through weather, through light that came from above and air that moved in directions and temperatures that changed — I was walking across a plateau of basalt, shattered and grey, and I stopped. Not because I saw something. Because I heard something. Or rather, because I felt something. A vibration in the stone. A low, subsonic pulse, so deep it was not sound but pressure, a thrumming in the rock beneath my feet, regular, slow, alive.

I knelt. I pressed my ear to the stone. I heard it.

The chorus. Muffled by meters of basalt, filtered by rock and distance, but present. The clicking. The keening. The thrumming. The layered, continuous, invariant sound of a living system that had found its frequency and was holding it, holding it the way a held note holds, the way a bell continues after the strike, except this bell had not been struck, this bell was the strike, this bell was the ongoing strike, the sound of a thing happening that does not stop.

I found the seam by following the sound. I found the chamber by descending through it.

I did not intend to stay. I want to say this clearly, because it matters, because it is the one fact I am certain of: I did not intend to stay. I intended to descend, to see, to understand, to observe, to return. I intended to be the kind of person who descends into a place like this and returns with a description, a name, a taxonomy. I intended to be a scientist, or a naturalist, or an explorer, or whatever word applies to someone who goes into a place and comes back out of it.

I am none of those things. I am the thing that went into a place and did not come back out of it. I am the thing that the place kept, or that kept itself in the place, or that the place did not keep but that did not leave, because leaving had become — I do not know when — incomprehensible. Not impossible. I can see the seam from here. It is a pale grey suggestion on the far wall, a slightly lighter dark in the dark. I could walk to it. I have walked to it, several times, in the early years, when the intention to leave still existed as a force. I stood at the base of it. I looked up. The passage angles, and above it the air warms by degrees, and the sound fades, and the light changes, and the world begins.

I did not go up. Each time, I did not go up. Not because I could not. Because the chorus was behind me, and the chorus was the place, and the place was the sound, and the sound was the thing I had come for, and leaving it felt like — I do not have the word. Not loss. Not death. Something more specific. The way a frequency feels when it stops: not silence but the *absence* of the thing that was there, which is louder than silence, which is the loudest thing, which is the thing I could not bear.

So I stayed.

---

## III.

My hands are what I notice first, now. My hands and the isopods.

I notice my hands because they are the part of me I see most. I spend most of my time looking down. The light comes from below, and the floor is the brightest surface, and the floor is where the life is, and the life is what I watch, and watching requires looking down, and looking down I see my hands, and my hands are on the stone, and the stone glows, and my hands are dark against the glow, and I am the only thing in this chamber that casts a shadow upward.

The isopods cast shadows upward too. But theirs are small, and they move, and the movement makes them part of the place. My shadow is large, and it does not move, because I am sitting, because I have been sitting, because sitting is what I do now. I sit on the stone and I watch, and the watching is the sitting and the sitting is the watching, and the isopods walk around me, and the fliers rise and fall around me, and the salamanders breathe in their basins, and I am the still thing, and the chamber is the moving thing, and the moving thing is alive, and I am — I am what? I am the thing that is alive and still. I am the opposite of the place. The place is still and alive. I am alive and still. The difference is the difference between a held breath and a breath held, and I do not know which I am.

My hands are rough. I said this. The skin has thickened. Not calloused — not in the way of labor, not in the way of a surface life — but thickened the way bark thickens, the way the shell of an isopod thickens, the way anything thickens when it is in contact with a surface for a long time and the surface teaches it what it needs to be. My hands have learned what the stone is. The stone is cold. The stone is rough. The stone is hard, and it does not give, and it does not warm, and it does not soften, and my hands have responded by becoming cold and rough and hard and unyielding, and this is not a metaphor. This is what happens to skin that is pressed against basalt for eleven years. It becomes basalt-adjacent. It becomes the kind of skin that exists on basalt, that belongs on basalt, that is not happy on basalt — happiness is not available, happiness is a surface concept, happiness requires a gradient, a difference, a thing to move toward or away from — but that is *fit* for basalt, that is adapted, that is the skin that the stone makes.

I watch the isopods. I watch them the way the salamanders watch them — not with sight, not with analysis, but with vibration. Not literally. I do not feel them through the stone. But I have watched them for so long that the watching has become proprioceptive, has become a kind of sensing, a kind of knowing-where-things-are that does not require looking. I know where the isopods are. I know where the fliers are. I know where the salamanders are, in their basins, breathing. I know the chamber the way I know my own body. I know it as a set of presences, a constellation of movements, a pattern of sound that is always the same and that I am always inside, and the knowing is not thought, it is not cognition, it is the condition of being in a place long enough that the place becomes part of the nervous system, an extension of the sense of self, a room of the body.

---

## IV.

I eat the microorganisms. I should tell you this. It is the thing that matters most, after the staying, after the not-leaving. I eat the light.

Not the light. The film. The thin, persistent, living film that coats the darker stone, the film that the isopods graze, the film that the fliers sip, the film that feeds the entire chamber. I scrape it with my fingernails — my fingernails, which are thick now, which are dark, which are the color of the stone — I scrape it from the stone in the places where it is thickest, where the moisture collects, where the glow is brightest, and I put it in my mouth, and I eat it.

It tastes like chalk and iron and something else. The something else is the taste of living things — not the taste of death, not the taste of meat or fruit or anything that was alive and is now being consumed. The taste of life. Of something that is alive and that enters the body and continues, in a sense, in a different sense, the metabolism of the microorganisms becoming the metabolism of my body, their chemistry becoming my chemistry, their light — their light — becoming something else, something that is not light but that is what light becomes when it is eaten, when it is taken into a body and used, when it ceases to be a thing that illuminates and becomes a thing that lives.

I do not eat much. The body does not need much, at this temperature, at this level of activity. I sit. I walk my loop. I eat. I sleep — in one of the alcoves, where the ceiling drops low enough that the space feels enclosed enough for sleep, where the condensation does not fall, where the stone is dry and the glow is faint and the sound is the same but the space is smaller and the smallness is what permits the unconsciousness. I sleep the way the salamanders sleep — not fully, not deeply, not in the way that surface sleep is a withdrawal from the world. In the way that underground sleep is a contraction of the world, a narrowing of the attention to the body itself, the chamber still present but distant, the sound still present but softened, the self still present but reduced, contracted, held.

I eat the film and my body uses it and my body produces waste and the waste is consumed by the microorganisms and the microorganisms grow and the microorganisms glow and the glow is the light and I eat the light. The circuit is complete. I am part of the ecology. I am the part that was not here before and that is here now and that does not have a role, does not have a function, does not have a purpose in the system. I am not a grazer, not a predator, not a decomposer. I am not an isopod or a flier or a salamander. I am the thing that sits on the stone and watches and eats and sleeps and walks a loop and casts a shadow upward and is part of the system without being designed into it, the way a stone that falls into a river is part of the river without being the river, the way a thing that enters a body is part of the body without being the body.

I am in the chamber and I am of the chamber and I am not the chamber. The distinction is thin. It is getting thinner.

---

## V.

There is a salamander I know.

I should not say this. It is the kind of thing a person says — *I know this animal, this animal is special to me, this animal and I have a bond*. It is the kind of thing that requires a self and an other and a relationship between them, and I am not certain that I have any of those things anymore, and I am not certain that the salamander has ever had them, and I am not certain that the word *know* means what it meant when I came down.

But there is a salamander in the basin nearest to my alcove — three meters from where I sleep, a shallow depression in the stone, no deeper than my palm, filled with the thin, cold, mineral water that seeps through the rock above — and this salamander has been there since I arrived. Eleven years. Perhaps longer. The basin is its life. The salamander does not move between basins — I said this, the description says this, the salamanders sit in their basins and do not move, each basin holds one salamander, each salamander holds one basin, and the holding is the life — and this salamander has held this basin for as long as I have been here, which means it held it before me, which means it has been here longer than I have, which means it knows the chamber in a way I do not, in the only way anything can know the chamber, which is by being it.

I call it nothing. I do not name things. Naming is a surface practice, a surface need, the need to distinguish, to individuate, to separate one thing from another. The salamander does not need a name. It needs a basin. It has a basin. It is in the basin. It breathes. The thrumming that comes from its body — the low, subsonic pulse that I feel in the stone when I sit near it, that I feel in my own body when I lie in my alcove, that I feel in my chest like a second heartbeat, slower, deeper, older than mine — the thrumming is its name. It is the sound of a specific body in a specific basin, and I know it the way I know my own pulse, which is to say I do not know it as information, I know it as presence, as the condition of something being here, being near, being alive at a frequency that my body has learned to feel.

I have touched it. Once. In the seventh year, or the eighth, or the ninth, the years are compressed, the cold does something to time. I reached out. My hand — my rough, dark, thick-skinned, stone-adjacent hand — I reached out and I touched the salamander's back. The skin was cold. Colder than the stone, colder than the water, colder than the air, the cold of a thing that has been cold for so long that the cold is not a condition but a property, not something that happens to it but something it is. The skin was smooth, slick with mucus, and under the slickness, under the cold, I felt the vibration. The breathing. The thrumming. The pulse that I hear in the stone and feel in the stone and that is, when I touch the salamander, not in the stone but in the salamander, not in the place but in the body, and the difference is — the difference is —

The salamander did not react. It did not flinch. It did not turn. Its eyes, on top of its head, looking up at the dark ceiling, did not move. Its body did not tense. It breathed in. It breathed out. The thrumming continued. My hand was on its back and the thrumming continued, the same frequency, the same amplitude, the same pulse, and the salamander did not know I was there, or did not care that I was there, or knew I was there and did not care, or cared that I was there and did not know, and I held my hand on its back and I felt the breathing and I felt the cold and I felt the life, and the life was the same life, the same life that was in the stone and in the water and in the air and in the light and in the isopods and in the fliers and in the microorganisms and in the film and in me. The same life. The life of the chamber. The life that the chamber has, that the chamber is, the life that does not need me and does not need anything and that I felt, through my hand, through the salamander's skin, through the water, through the stone, through the cold, through the dark, through the dim and steady light of its own ground, and it was not a connection. It was not a relationship. It was not a moment. It was the fact of being in the same place as a living thing and feeling it live.

I removed my hand. The salamander breathed. I sat beside the basin. The thrumming continued. The chorus continued. The light glowed. Nothing changed. Nothing changed.

---

## VI.

The marks. I should tell you about the marks.

I found them in the third year, or the fourth. In the upper passage, in the seam, near the place where the air begins to warm and the sound begins to fade. I had gone up — I was still going up then, still testing the possibility of leaving, still standing at the base of the seam and looking toward the world and feeling the pull of the world and feeling the pull of the chamber and the two pulls cancelling each other, neutralizing each other, leaving me standing. I was going up, and I was in the seam, and I saw them.

Lines. Scratches. Stone on stone. The same gesture I make now — the heel of my hand on the floor, the pressure, the repetition, the path. Not the same. Not mine. Older. Much older. The scratches are shallow, worn, filled with mineral deposit, the stone healing over them the way skin heals over a cut, slowly, incompletely, the scar still visible, the wound still present, the mark still there.

Someone was here. Someone descended to this threshold and stood where I stand and looked down into the chamber and heard what I hear and saw what I see and made marks. Stone on stone. The first mark. The first gesture of a mind that looked at a place and thought: *this could be different than it is.* And the marks are not mine and the marks are not different and the marks are the only thing that tells me that I am not the first, that someone else came down and stood at the edge and felt the chorus and felt the pull and felt the cold and felt the light and felt the life and did not stay. Or did stay. Or stayed for a while and then did not stay. Or stayed and became the place the way I am becoming the place and left no evidence except the marks, the scratches, the stone on stone, the first gesture, the gesture that says: I was here. I saw this. I touched it. I made a mark. The mark is the only thing that remains, and the mark is not part of the system, and the mark will not be recycled, and the mark is the only thing in this place that is not alive.

I added to them. I should tell you this. I stood in the seam and I looked at the marks and I picked up a piece of basalt — a shard, a fragment, a chip of stone that had broken from the wall — and I scratched. One line. Below the older marks, separated, distinct. My line. My scratch. My gesture. Stone on stone. The same gesture. The same impulse. The same mind, looking at a place and thinking: *this could be different than it is.* My line is next to their lines and my line is the same as their lines and the difference between us is the difference between one scratch and another, which is no difference, which is the same gesture made by different hands at different times in the same place, and the gesture is the thing, and the gesture is the mark, and the mark is the only thing that is mine, and the mark is not part of the system, and the mark will not be eaten, and the mark will not be recycled, and the mark will be here when I am not, when the isopods are not, when the salamanders are not, when the light has changed or the water has stopped or the chamber has closed or the world has ended, and the mark will be stone on stone, and stone does not eat stone, and the mark will persist.

I am not proud of this either. But I did it. And the doing of it was the most human thing I have done in eleven years, and the being human is the thing that the chamber is eroding, and the erosion is not destruction, the erosion is — the erosion is the process of becoming the place, and the place does not need marks, and the place does not need names, and the place does not need a self, and the self is dissolving, and the dissolution is not death, the dissolution is the thing that happens when a body stays in a place long enough that the place becomes the body and the body becomes the place and the distinction — the distinction that is the self, the distinction that is the mark, the distinction that is the name — the distinction is no longer necessary.

---

## VII.

I do not know what I am.

When I came down I was a person. I was a person in the way that people are people — I had a name, I had a history, I had a face that I recognized and that others recognized, I had a set of preferences and fears and habits that constituted me, that composed the specific configuration of atoms and impulses and memories that I called myself and that the world called me. I was specific. I was individual. I was one thing among many things, distinguishable, distinct, a point in the field of points, a body in the world of bodies.

I am less specific now. The specifics have eroded — not all at once, not in a dramatic collapse, but in the way that water erodes stone, in the way that the microorganisms erode the basalt, slowly, persistently, one mineral at a time, one molecule at a time, one day at a time, and the day is the same as the last day, and the day is the same as the next day, and the days are not days, the days are the condition of being in the chamber, and the condition does not change, and the condition does not differentiate, and the condition does not individuate, and the self that I was was a thing that required differentiation, required individuation, required a difference between me and not-me, and the difference is — the difference is the temperature. The difference is the gradient. The difference is the thing that exists when the body is warmer than the stone, and the body is not warmer than the stone. The body is the temperature of the stone. The body is twelve degrees. The body is the chamber.

I still have hands. I still have eyes. I still have a face, though I have not seen it, there is nothing reflective down here, the water is too thin, the basins too shallow, the light too dim, and I do not know what my face looks like and I do not need to know what my face looks like because the face is for others and there are no others and the face is dissolving the way the self is dissolving, not physically, not in the way of flesh, but in the way of relevance, the way of necessity, the face is becoming unnecessary, the self is becoming unnecessary, and what remains is — 

What remains is the watching. What remains is the sitting. What remains is the loop, the path, the seven basins, the four ridges, the two alcoves. What remains is the eating and the sleeping and the walking and the cold and the sound and the light. What remains is the chorus. What remains is the thrumming of the salamander in the basin near my alcove, the pulse that I feel in the stone, the pulse that I feel in my chest, the pulse that is the same pulse, that has been the same pulse for eleven years, that will be the same pulse for however many years remain, and the pulse is the place and the place is the pulse and I am in the place and the place is in me and the distinction is — 

The distinction is the mark I made in the seam. The distinction is the scratch. The distinction is the stone on stone. The distinction is the gesture that says: I was here. I saw this. I touched it. I made a mark. And the mark is unnecessary and the mark is the only thing that is mine and the mark is the only thing that is human and the mark is the only thing that the chamber does not eat.

---

## VIII.

I want to tell you about the sound one more time. The sound is the thing I am becoming.

The chorus. The clicking of the isopods, the keening of the fliers, the thrumming of the salamanders. Three layers. I said this. The description says this. What the description does not say — what the description cannot say, because the description was written from outside, by someone who came down and went back up — what the description does not say is that the chorus is not three layers. The chorus is one thing. The three sounds are not three sounds. They are one sound, the way a body is one body, the way a chamber is one chamber, the way a life is one life. The clicking and the keening and the thrumming are not separate. They are the same sound at different frequencies, the same vibration at different scales, the same life expressing itself through different bodies.

I hear it now the way I hear my own blood. Not as external sound. Not as something that arrives through the ears. As something that the body is doing. The clicking is the floor of my awareness. The keening is the air. The thrumming is the stone. I am sitting in the chorus and the chorus is in me and the distinction between hearing and being is — the distinction is dissolving. The distinction is the temperature. The distinction is the gradient. The distinction is the difference between the body and the place, and the difference is twelve degrees, and twelve degrees is what the stone is, and twelve degrees is what the water is, and twelve degrees is what the air is, and twelve degrees is what I am.

I am the thing that listens. I am the thing that the sound moves through. I am the medium. I am the air in the chamber, the stone in the floor, the water in the basins. I am the thing that the life moves through, and the life is moving through me, and the moving is the sound, and the sound is the chorus, and the chorus is the place, and I am in the place, and the place is in me.

---

## IX.

Here is what I know: the chamber does not need me. The chamber has never needed me. The chamber was complete before I arrived and it will be complete after I am gone, and the completeness is the thing that I am learning, and the learning is the thing that is changing me, and the changing is the thing that is dissolving the self that I was, and the dissolution is not death. The dissolution is the thing that happens when a stone falls into a river and the river moves around it and the stone becomes smooth and the stone becomes part of the river and the river does not know the stone is there and the stone does not know the river is there and the water moves and the stone stays and the staying is the being and the being is the place.

I am becoming the place. Not the place — I am not the chamber, I am not the stone, I am not the light, I am not the sound. I am the thing in the chamber that is becoming what the chamber is, which is still, which is alive, which is cold, which is dim, which is loud, which is perfectly, perfectly balanced. I am the thing that found its frequency. I am the thing that is holding it. I am the thing that does not need to change, because the temperature does not change, and the water does not change, and the light does not change, and the sound does not change, and the sound is what I am, and what I am does not change.

I will not leave. I know this now. The seam is above me. The world is above me. The world is too bright and too warm and too silent and too full of gradients, of differences, of the kind of change that constitutes a self, and I am no longer certain that I have a self, and I am no longer certain that I want one. The self is the mark. The self is the scratch in the stone. The self is the thing that says *I was here, I saw this, I touched it* and the chamber does not say this. The chamber does not need to say this. The chamber is here. The chamber sees. The chamber touches. The chamber does not need to distinguish itself from itself. The chamber is one thing and I am inside it and I am becoming one thing and the one thing does not need a name.

The salamander breathes. The isopods click. The fliers keen. The light glows. The water seeps. The stone is cold. I sit on the stone. My hands are on the stone. The stone glows beneath my hands. My shadow falls upward. The chorus is in me. I am in the chorus. The sound does not stop.

The sound does not stop.
