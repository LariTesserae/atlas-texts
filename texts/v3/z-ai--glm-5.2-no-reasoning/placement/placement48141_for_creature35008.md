# The Note Keeper

I am the one who comes down the shaft on days that are not on the schedule.

---

Let me say first: I am not the one who wrote the note. I want to be clear about this because people assume — the workers who have seen me assume, in their quiet way, without asking, the way they assume about the midges and the bronze grate and the flow lines in the western wall — they assume I am connected to the things that are old and unsigned. I am not. I am not old enough. I do not have the handwriting. I have checked.

But I know the note. I know it the way I know the green pin and the tarnish on its brass and the way the card has curled at the corners from the damp air in the gallery. I know the pressure of the pencil on the paper — light, even, the hand of someone who was not hurried and was not afraid. I know that the pencil was a 2B, soft, the kind that smudges if you brush it with a sleeve, and the note has not smudged, which means no one has brushed it, which means no one has gotten close enough to it for long enough to accidentally touch it, which means it is treated with a care that goes beyond instruction. It is treated the way you treat a thing that is sacred, though no one here would use that word. No one here uses any word. They come down the shaft, they check the pumps, they sign the schedule, they go up. The words are in the documents. The documents are in the gallery. The gallery is in the basin. The basin is in the earth. The words stay.

I add to the words. That is what I do.

---

I came down the first time on a day that was on the schedule. A regular fourteen-day maintenance visit. I was not the maintenance worker — I was not anyone, in terms of the schedule. I was the person who had come with the maintenance worker because the maintenance worker was my father and I was sixteen and he had run out of reasons to leave me on the surface and I had run out of arguments for why I should be allowed to stay and so he brought me down the shaft the way you bring a tool you are not sure you will need but cannot leave behind.

The rungs were cold. This is the thing I remember most clearly — not the basin, not the vast dark, not the weeping ceiling. The rungs. The cold of the rungs through my gloves. The way the cold traveled through the leather and into the skin and into the bone and settled there with a patience that I would later learn was the patience of the whole place. My father climbed above me. His boots were above my hands. The lamps were spaced too far apart and I was in the dark between them and I was afraid and I did not say so because my father was not afraid and I did not want to be the kind of person who was afraid when my father was not.

At the bottom, the shaft opened. I stepped out. The basin was there.

I have never fully returned.

I mean this literally and I mean it in the other way. I stepped out of the shaft and into the basin and something in me — some surface version of me, the version that walked on the surface marsh and knew the sky and had opinions about weather — stepped out and did not follow me back up. I climbed the shaft when my father was done. I returned to the surface. I stood on the peat and the sedge and the frost and I was on the surface again. But I was also still in the basin. I was still standing on the slag tiles in the sodium dark, listening to the pumps, feeling the cold come up through the soles of my boots. Some part of me had stayed. Some part of me had looked at the wide shallow bowl of manufactured ground and the columns rising to the weeping ceiling and the orange light pooling like something spilled and had said: *oh. Here. This is where I am.*

Not *where I belong*. That is too simple. Not *where I am supposed to be*. That implies a plan, a designer, a specification. I do not think I was planned for. I do not think the Weeping Works was planned for me. I think the word is *congruent*. I am congruent with this place. The shape of me fits the shape of it. The cold and the dark and the patience and the water going down — these are not things I love, because love is too warm a word, too surface a word. These are things I *recognize*. Things I knew before I knew them. Things that were already in me, waiting for the place that matched them.

---

I came back. I came back on days that were not on the schedule. I came back alone. I told my father I was hiking. I told my mother I was helping my father. I told the marsh I was going below and the marsh did not care because the marsh does not care about anything above it and never has.

I learned the basin.

Not the way the maintenance workers learn it — not as a collection of equipment to be checked, gauges to be read, gaskets to be replaced. I learned it the way you learn a room you have been given to live in, though no one has given it to me and I do not live in it. I learned the floor. I learned the tiles. I learned the numbers on the tiles — stamped into the underside during the pour, visible only if you lift one, which I have done, which I do, carefully, replacing each tile in its exact position so that the drainage grooves align, so that nothing changes, so that the system runs as it has always run.

The tiles are numbered sequentially, but not in any pattern I can decipher. The numbers are four digits. They do not correspond to position. They do not correspond to date of manufacture. They do not correspond to anything I can find in the gallery's documents, which are sparse — maintenance schedules, pump specifications, water quality readings going back decades, all in the same hand or in hands so similar they might as well be the same. The documents are not old enough to explain the tiles. The tiles are older than the documents. The tiles are older than the gallery. The tiles may be older than the pumps, though the pumps are old.

I record the numbers. I have a notebook — not the maintenance schedule, my own notebook, which I bring down the shaft in the inside pocket of my jacket where the cold cannot reach it and the condensation cannot wet it. I have been recording the tile numbers for three years. I have recorded one hundred and forty-seven of them. There are — I have estimated, by counting tiles in sample areas and multiplying by the area of the basin — approximately eleven thousand tiles. At my current rate, I will finish recording them in approximately two hundred and twenty-eight years.

I am not discouraged by this. The basin is not discouraged by this. The basin has been here for longer than that and will be here for longer than that and the tiles will be here, numbered, unread, their numbers meaning something or meaning nothing, and I will be recording them or I will not, and the recording will continue or it will not, and the tiles will be there.

This is the kind of thought I have in the basin. Patient thoughts. Thoughts that do not require completion. Thoughts that are satisfied with being *had* rather than *resolved*. The basin teaches this. The basin does not teach it actively — there is no lesson, no curriculum, no moment of understanding. There is only the slow accumulation of time spent in a place that does not change, and the slow discovery that the not-changing is itself a kind of instruction. The basin says: *I am here. I have been here. I will be here.* And you say, eventually, without meaning to: *yes.*

---

The midges.

I sit in the gallery and I watch the midges. This is what I do when I am not recording tile numbers or walking the basin's perimeter or pressing my palm against the bronze grate to feel its warmth. I sit on a crate that someone left in the gallery decades ago — a wooden crate, splintery, warped from the damp, containing nothing, its original contents unknown — and I watch the midges on the pipes.

They do not know I am there. I am sure of this. I move slowly. I breathe quietly. I sit in the part of the gallery where the light is weakest, where the sodium lamps' orange fades to the dark brown that is the basin's preferred color, and the midges walk on the pipes and scrape the biofilm and do not alter their behavior in any way that suggests they have registered my presence. I am large and warm and alive and they are small and cold and alive and we are in the same room and we are both part of the system, though only they have a note.

I have watched them for long enough that I can identify individuals. Not by any marking — they are unmarked, uniform, the pale cream of a species that has refined itself to its environment so thoroughly that individual variation has been selected against. Variation is waste. Variation is a luxury of surfaces, of sunlight, of ecosystems where difference is rewarded. Here, in the gallery, in the warm corner of an underground marsh, variation has been pared away, and what remains is the essential: the careful legs, the scraping mouth, the vestigial wings, the egg-laying in corners.

I identify them by position. By the pipe fitting they prefer. By the rhythm of their walking — some move slightly faster, some slightly slower, and the difference is small enough that it takes hours of watching to detect it, but it is there. There is a midge on the third pipe fitting from the eastern wall that I call — I do not call it anything. I do not name them. Naming is a surface behavior. Naming is what you do when you want to claim a thing, to make it yours, to bring it into the economy of ownership and meaning. The midges are not mine. The midges are the system's. I watch them and I know them and I do not name them.

But I know when one is missing. I know when a pipe fitting that had a midge on it yesterday does not have a midge on it today. I know when the count has shifted — not the exact count, the way the workers estimate, but the *texture* of the count, the density of midges on a given fitting, the way they space themselves along a pipe, the way the spacing changes when a midge falls and the remaining midges redistribute themselves with a slowness that suggests not awareness but chemistry, the way a sugar cube dissolves in cold water — slowly, evenly, without decision.

I have seen them fall. I have seen the misplaced leg, the slip, the tumble from the pipe fitting into the collection channel. It is not dramatic. It is the quietest thing in the Weeping Works, quieter than the biofilm, quieter than the biofilm's purple sheen in the sodium light. A midge slips. A midge falls. A midge lands in the channel. The water takes it. The midge does not struggle. The water carries it to the convergence point. The water carries it down.

I have watched this happen and I have not intervened. I could. I could reach into the channel and lift the midge out and place it back on the pipe fitting. The channel is shallow. The water is slow. The midge is light. I could save every midge that falls. I could reduce the loss to zero.

I do not. The note says they are part of the system. The system includes their falling. The system includes their going down. I do not know what is down there, but I know that the water goes down and the midges go down with it and the note says they are part of the system and I believe the note. I believe the note the way I believe the bronze grate is warm — not because I understand why, but because the evidence is consistent and the source is unknown and the unknown does not always need to be resolved. Sometimes the unknown is a functioning component. Sometimes the unknown is the thing that keeps the system running.

---

I have a theory about the note.

I do not have a theory about who wrote it. That is not my theory. My theory is about what the note *is* — what it does, what function it serves in the system. I think the note is a component. I think the note is part of the Weeping Works the way the pumps are part of the Weeping Works, the way the midges are part of the Weeping Works, the way the bronze grate and the purple biofilm and the engineered darkness and the forty-one unsynchronized heartbeats are part of the Weeping Works.

The note maintains the midges. Not the way the maintenance schedule maintains the pumps — with oil and gaskets and bearing inspections. The note maintains the midges by ensuring that no one removes them. The note is an instruction, and the instruction has been followed, and the following has become tradition, and the tradition has become the environment in which the midges exist. Without the note, someone would have removed the midges. Not out of cruelty — out of tidiness, out of a sense that a gallery full of pipes should not have insects on them, that a system should be clean. The note prevents this. The note is the reason the midges are still here.

And the midges are part of the system.

So the note is part of the system.

The note maintains the system by maintaining the midges by maintaining the instruction that prevents the removal. The note is a feedback loop. The note is a small piece of paper with a pencil mark and a brass pin, and it is doing something that the pumps cannot do and the lamps cannot do and the maintenance schedule cannot do. It is preserving a relationship. It is holding open a channel between the human and the insect, between the maintainer and the maintained, between the surface and the below.

I think the person who wrote the note understood this. I think they stood in the gallery — as I stand in the gallery — and they watched the midges — as I watch the midges — and they saw that the midges were not incidental. Not pests. Not visitors. Components. And they wrote the note because they knew that without the note, the components would be removed, and the system would lose something that the instruments could not detect and the maintenance schedule could not specify and the maps could not show. They wrote the note to protect the system from the kind of care that cleans rather than maintains. The kind of care that tidies. The kind of care that does not recognize a midge as part of a marsh.

I think this because it is what I would have written. It is what I think every time I sit in the gallery and watch the midges and feel the cold and hear the pumps and know that I am in a marsh that someone built because they needed one. They needed the marsh. They needed the midges. They needed the note. They needed the whole patient assembly of iron and slag and water and darkness and small pale walking things. They needed it because something below needed what the marsh produced. And the midges — three hundred, four hundred, the count shifting — were part of the production. Are part of the production. Are part of the system.

The system includes the note. The system includes the pin. The system includes the green tarnish on the pin, which is copper carbonate, which is the patina of brass in the presence of moisture and time, which is the record of how long the note has been there, which is the evidence of how long the system has been maintained, which is the proof that the system works.

---

I bring things down. I should say this.

I bring things that are not in the maintenance schedule. I bring a thermometer — not the gallery's thermometer, which is mounted on the wall and measures air temperature, but a probe thermometer, the kind with a thin metal stem, and I press the stem against the bronze grate and measure its warmth. The grate is 11.3 degrees Celsius. The air around it is 6.1 degrees. The water passing through it is 8.7 degrees. The grate is warmer than the water and warmer than the air. The grate is producing heat, or conducting heat from somewhere, or holding heat that has been given to it by some process I cannot identify. I record this in my notebook. I record it every time I come down. The reading is always the same. 11.3 degrees. I do not understand why. I record it.

I bring a camera. I photograph the western wall. The flow lines in the slag. The pattern that looks like text. I have one hundred and seven photographs of the western wall, taken from the same position, with the same lighting, over three years. The flow lines do not change. The pattern does not change. I compare the photographs on the surface, in my room, on a screen, zooming in until the pixels blur, looking for a change, a shift, a new line, an old line faded. Nothing. The wall is the same. The wall has been the same for as long as I have been photographing it, and it was the same for as long as whoever poured it left it, and it will be the same for as long as the basin stands. I photograph it anyway. I photograph it because the act of photographing is a form of attention, and attention is a form of care, and care is what this place is made of — not love, not worship, not even respect, but care, the specific, practical, unglamorous care of maintaining a thing that works.

I bring a notebook. I have said this. But I should say more about the notebook. The notebook is where I keep my version of the system — not the maintenance schedule's version, which is mechanical, which is checklists and intervals and specifications. My version is different. My version is a list of things I have noticed that are not on any list.

Things I have noticed:

The purple biofilm is slightly more purple in the areas closest to the five large columns. The purple is less vivid at the edges of the basin, where the air movement is weaker. I do not know if this is cause or correlation. I record it.

The midges are more active — their walking pace is fractionally faster, their feeding stops fractionally shorter — in the hour after the vent groans. I do not know if the vent's vibration stimulates them or if the pressure change alters the biofilm in a way they detect. I record it.

The water in the central channel has a faint odor that the condensation does not have. The odor is not sulfur. It is not calcium. It is the odor of the bronze grate translated into air — a mineral warmth, a scent like the inside of a clock, like the description said, and I did not write the description but I recognize the description because it is accurate and I have smelled the same thing and I have not been able to name it any better than *the inside of a clock*.

The condensation falls more heavily in the areas of the ceiling directly above the five large columns. This may be because the columns are warmer — the air pushed through them carries the slight warmth of the pump motors — and the warmer air rises and meets the ceiling and increases the condensation rate. Or it may be because the columns were placed where the condensation is heaviest. Or it may be because the ceiling and the columns and the condensation are all part of a design that I am observing from the inside, like a cell observing the body, unable to see the whole.

I record these things. I do not understand them. I do not need to understand them. The basin does not need to be understood. The basin needs to be maintained. The maintenance schedule maintains the mechanics. I maintain the noticing.

---

I do not know if I am part of the system.

This is the question I sit with, in the gallery, on the warped crate, watching the midges. The midges are part of the system — the note says so. The pumps are part of the system — the schedule says so. The biofilm is part of the system — its function implies so. The bronze grate is part of the system — its warmth suggests so. But me? I am not in the note. I am not in the schedule. I am not in the specifications. I am a person who comes down the shaft on days that are not on the schedule and sits in the dark and watches insects and records tile numbers and photographs a wall.

Am I part of the system?

I do not maintain the pumps. I do not test the water. I do not feed the midges or clean the pipes or adjust the lamps. I do not do anything that the system requires. If I stopped coming, the system would continue. The pumps would run. The water would fall. The biofilm would grow. The midges would walk. The note would remain pinned to the wall. Nothing would change.

And yet I come. I come and I sit and I notice and I record and I am here, in the gallery, in the basin, in the Weeping Works, and I am alive, and the midges are alive, and the biofilm is alive, and we are all in the same space, breathing the same air, touching the same cold, subject to the same patient darkness. I am not part of the system. I am *with* the system. I am alongside it. I am a creature that has found a place that is congruent with its shape and has settled there, not as a component but as a companion, not as a function but as a witness.

The basin does not need me. This is what I love about it, though *love* is the wrong word — it is too warm, too surface, too full of weather and light. The basin does not need me the way the marsh does not need the sky. The sky is there. The marsh is there. The sky does not maintain the marsh. The sky does not feed the marsh. The sky is simply *above*, and the marsh is simply *below*, and the relationship between them is not functional but *positional* — they are in the same world, they share the same moment, and the sharing is enough.

I am in the same world as the Weeping Works. I share the same moment. I am above and it is below and I come down the shaft and I am in it and I am not part of it and it does not need me and I am here anyway.

This is enough. This is more than enough. This is the thing that the surface does not have — the thing that the sky and the sedge and the frost and the standing water do not provide. The surface is full of need. The surface is full of relationships that are functional — you need the land, the land needs the rain, the rain needs the sky, the sky needs the heat, the heat needs the sun. Everything on the surface is a chain of need, and the chain is long, and the links are many, and the breaking of any one is catastrophe.

The Weeping Works does not need. The Weeping Works *is*. It is a marsh that has been built and is running and will run and does not need me and does not need the maintenance workers and does not need the note. The note maintains the midges, but if the midges were removed, the system would adjust — not well, perhaps not correctly, but it would adjust, because systems adjust, because that is what systems do. The Weeping Works would continue. It would be a marsh without midges, a marsh without the component that the note was written to protect, and it would be a worse marsh, a damaged marsh, a marsh that had lost something it needed. But it would be a marsh. It would still be there. It would still weep and pump and process and send the water down.

I come because I want to be in a place that does not need me. I come because the surface is exhausting — the constant need, the constant function, the constant requirement to be a link in a chain, to serve a purpose, to justify my presence with productivity or relationship or joy. The Weeping Works does not require justification. The Weeping Works does not ask why I am here. It simply contains me, the way it contains the cold and the dark and the water. I am here. I am contained. The containment is enough.

---

The workers have noticed me. Not the way they notice the midges — not as part of the room, not as expected texture. They have noticed me as an anomaly, as a thing that does not fit. I am not on the schedule. I am not in the documents. I am a young person — I am nineteen now, I have been coming for three years — who appears in the gallery on unscheduled days and sits on a crate and watches insects and writes in a notebook and does not interfere with anything and does not explain anything.

They have asked. They have asked gently, the way you ask in a place where the tradition is to not ask, where the culture is to not question, where the maintenance schedule and the note and the green pin have established a norm of silent compliance. They have asked: *What are you doing down here?*

I have said: *Watching.*

They have accepted this. Not because it is a good answer. Because it is the kind of answer that the Weeping Works produces — an answer that is true and incomplete and does not invite follow-up. An answer like the note. *Do not remove the midges. They are part of the system.* True. Incomplete. Does not invite follow-up.

They have told my father. My father has come down the shaft on a day that was not his scheduled day and has found me in the gallery on the crate with my notebook and he has stood in the doorway — the doorway that is not a door, the opening that is not a barrier — and he has looked at me the way you look at a thing that you have made that has become something you did not intend.

He said: *You don't have to do this.*

I said: *I know.*

He said: *The place doesn't need you.*

I said: *I know.*

He stood for a long time. The pumps hummed. The ceiling wept. A midge walked on the pipe fitting nearest to his hand, and he did not notice it, because he was looking at me, and the midge was part of the room and he was looking at the thing in the room that was not part of the room.

He said: *Be careful.*

He left. He climbed the shaft. The rungs were cold. The lamps were spaced too far apart. He went up and I stayed down and the system continued.

---

I will say one more thing.

The midges that fall. The ones that slip and tumble and land in the channel and are carried away by the water. I said I do not intervene. I said I could save them and I do not. This is true. But I do something else.

I count them.

I count the ones that fall. Not every visit — I am not here every day, I cannot be here every day, the surface has its claims and I answer them because I must, because I am a surface creature even if I am congruent with the below. But when I am here, I count. I sit on the crate and I watch the pipes and when a midge falls I make a mark in my notebook. A single vertical line. One per fall.

In three years, I have counted forty-seven falls. Forty-seven midges carried down the conduit. Forty-seven small bodies that were part of the system and have left the system and entered the water and gone down.

Forty-seven, in three years. Out of three hundred, four hundred, the count shifting. Forty-seven losses. The population is stable — I have checked, I have estimated, the count does not change in any direction I can detect. So forty-seven midges have fallen and been replaced. Forty-seven eggs have been laid in the corners and have hatched and have grown and have become midges that walk on the pipes. Forty-seven new lives replacing forty-seven lost lives. A cycle within a cycle. A system within a system.

Forty-seven is not many. It is fewer than I expected. The midges are careful — I have said this, I have described this, the careful leg placement, the deliberate pace, the evolutionary wisdom of a thousand thousand losses compressed into a nervous system smaller than a grain of sand. The midges do not fall often. They are good at not falling. They have been selected for not falling. The ones that fell were the ones that did not pass on their genes, and the ones that remained were the ones that did, and the species has become, over its time in the gallery, a species that does not fall.

But they fall sometimes. Forty-seven times in three years. A leg misplaced. A fraction of a millimeter. The wet metal treacherous in a way that the careful legs cannot always overcome. And the midge falls, and the water takes it, and the midge goes down.

I count them because the counting is a form of witnessing. I cannot save them — I have decided this, I believe the note, I believe the falling is part of the system. But I can witness the falling. I can see it. I can mark it. I can record that a specific midge, on a specific pipe fitting, at a specific time, on a specific day, lost its grip and fell and was carried away. I can say: *this happened. This was real. A midge was here and now it is gone and the system continues and I saw it.*

The witnessing is not part of the system. The witnessing is mine. It is the thing I bring that is not in the maintenance schedule and not in the note and not in the specifications and not in the tiles or the pumps or the biofilm or the water. It is the thing I add. Not a component. Not a function. A *witness*. A record that says: someone was here, and the midges were here, and the midges fell, and the water took them, and the system continued, and it was seen.

I do not know if this matters. I do not know if the system is improved by being witnessed. I do not know if the midges are helped by being counted. I do not know if the basin is changed by my presence, if my watching alters the biofilm or shifts the air or affects the temperature in the gallery by the fraction of a degree that my body adds to the room.

I do not know. The basin does not tell me. The midges do not tell me. The water does not tell me. The note does not tell me. The green pin does not tell me.

But the water goes down. And the midges stay. And I come back. And the system runs. And the runs are cold. And the lamps are spaced too far apart. And the darkness between them is a darkness that someone designed. And I walk through it. And I am not afraid.

---

*I am the one who comes down the shaft on days that are not on the schedule. I am the one who sits in the gallery and counts the midges that fall. I am the one who records the numbers on the tiles and photographs the wall and presses my palm against the bronze grate and feels its warmth and does not understand it. I am the one who brings a notebook and a thermometer and a camera and a body that is alive and warm and congruent with the cold and the dark and the patience. I am the one who witnesses.*

*I am not part of the system. I am with the system. I am alongside it. The system does not need me and I am here anyway and this is the shape of my life and the shape of the basin and the shape of the water going down and the shape of the midges walking on the pipes and the shape of the note pinned to the wall with a brass pin that has turned green.*

*The water goes down. The midges stay. I come back. The system runs.*
