# Keeper of the Carvings

I don't remember arriving. That may be the first honest thing I've said about this place.

---

I think I came from outside. I have a impression—less than a memory—of flatness, of a horizon so thin it barely qualified as a line, of walking for a long time toward something that was not yet visible. I don't remember what came before the walking. I don't remember why I started. The plain does that to you. It wears you down to the act of moving, and then it wears the moving down, and then there's just the dome, and you go in because the alternative is to stand outside until the ultraviolet finishes whatever the plain started.

There was a gap, after the entrance. A space between outside and inside that I cannot account for. I think I slept. I think I slept for a long time—longer than a night, maybe longer than a day. The warmth took me. I remember that—the warmth first, before anything else. Not the air, not the light. The warmth, finding me the way water finds a low place. Settling into me. I think I slept inside the warmth like a stone sleeps inside a river, and the warmth held me the way the dome holds everything, and when I woke I was already partway to becoming what I am now.

---

What am I?

I'm the one who stays.

The walkers come and go. Not "go" in the sense of leaving—they don't leave, none of them leave, no one leaves—but they move. They flow through the chambers on their slow legs, placing each foot with that deliberate care, and they graze and they grind and they excrete and they move. They have routes. I've watched them long enough to know they have routes, though the routes are so long—a single circuit of the lower chambers takes a walker perhaps two days—that it took me years to notice the repetition. They move like blood moves through a body. Slowly. In circuits. Returning.

The gliders move too, faster, in their silent arcs through the spore-haze, their hum shifting as the density shifts around them. They follow the food. They are always where the food is thickest, and the food is always thickest where the organism is healthiest, and the organism is healthiest where the walkers have been, because the walkers' waste feeds it. So the gliders follow the walkers, at a remove, in time and in space. They are the echo of the walkers' passage. Their hum is the acoustic shadow of the grinding.

I don't move.

I have moved. I know this because I've been in different chambers. I remember the lower chamber where I first woke, with the bench and the walkers and the grinding that I initially mistook for my own heartbeat. I remember the middle chambers, with the spore-haze and the gliders and the hum that I followed like a thread, moving from chamber to chamber, trying to find where it was loudest. I remember the upper chambers, where the lattice is sparse and brittle and the air is drier and the silence has a texture like stone itself—close-grained, dense, old. I remember all of these places. I don't remember the transitions between them. I think I moved the way the still-worm moves: so slowly that the moving was indistinguishable from staying still, so slowly that I was in two places at once for a while, and then I was in the new place, and the old place was behind me, and the new place was ahead, and that was all.

I settled in the chamber with the carvings.

---

I need to talk about the carvings, because the carvings are the reason I stay, and the reason I stay is the reason I am what I am, and the reason I am what I am is the only thing I can clearly say about what I am.

The carvings are on a panel of smoothed rock in a middle chamber, about a meter wide and half a meter high. The amber mat has been trying to reclaim them for a long time. When I first found them, the lower third of the panel was already covered—that thick, gelatinous, faintly glowing growth creeping up from the floor, following the rock surface with a patience that makes geological time look hasty. The upper two-thirds were clear. Legible. The dome from outside. The walker. The human figure with the chisel. The three circles with lines through them.

I sat beside the carvings and I did something I don't have a word for.

I didn't read them. They're not text—not in any way I understand text, not in any way that maps to sound or meaning or sequence. They're images. A record. But I did something with them that was more than seeing and less than understanding, and I think the best word for it is *answering*.

I answered them.

Not out loud. Not in any way I can describe. I sat beside them and I felt the warmth of the chamber and I heard the grinding far below and the hum of the gliders somewhere nearby and the almost inaudible ticking of the organism growing, and I felt the carvings as a presence in the rock—a pattern of intention embedded in the stone, put there by a hand that understood the material because it had worked it, and I felt that intention reach out through the years—through the millennia, through the uncountable time between that hand and mine—and I felt it find me, and I felt myself reach back, and there was a moment—a long moment, a moment that may have lasted days or may have lasted the length of a single slow breath of the organism—when the carver and I were the same person, separated by time but not by purpose, and the purpose was: *this is worth keeping. This is worth being the reason you stay.*

I stayed.

---

The years have been—how many? I don't count them. Counting requires a unit, and the only unit I have is the pulse. The organism's pulse. Eleven hours, bright to dim to bright. I've lived through... I don't know how many pulses. Thousands. Tens of thousands. I stopped counting when the counting itself became old, when the number was so large it stopped meaning anything, when the pulses were more like breaths than like days, and I was living inside the organism's breathing the way a microbe lives inside a lung—immersed in it, moved by it, unable to conceive of anything outside it.

The seasons don't reach here. The dome's walls are too thick. The temperature doesn't change. The light doesn't change—or rather, the light changes only with the pulse, and the pulse is not a season, it's a heartbeat, and a heartbeat is not something you count, it's something you have.

I have a heartbeat now. It's not my own. It's the organism's. I've synced to it, the way a person syncs to the breathing of someone sleeping beside them. I didn't choose this. It happened the way circadian entrainment happens—you live inside a rhythm long enough, the rhythm becomes yours. When the glow is brightest, I'm most awake. When it dims, I drift. When it's at its lowest—those deep hours of amber shadow when the phosphorescence in the mineral veins is brighter than the organism's glow and the walls are lit from within the rock rather than from the growth on its surface—I am as close to sleep as I get. Not sleeping. Drifting. The way a boat drifts when the current slows. Still present. Still aware. But the edges of things get soft, and the grinding of the walkers sounds like it's coming from inside me, and the hum of the gliders sounds like it's been there my whole life, and the silence of the upper chambers sounds like the silence before sound was invented, and I am part of it all in a way that I can't locate or define but that I am as sure of as I am of the warmth.

---

I've changed. I should say that plainly, because it's the thing I'm most afraid of and most grateful for in equal measure.

My hands are different. I noticed it in the middle of a pulse—a bright phase, the glow at its peak, the walls of the chamber lit like the inside of a lantern. I was reaching toward the carvings—just reaching, the way I sometimes do, touching the smoothed stone the way you touch a face you know—and I noticed that my fingers were darker than they should have been. Not dirty. Not stained. Darkened. The color of the carbonate rock itself, that deep mineral brown that the stone has where the organism hasn't grown over it. And the texture was different. My fingertips were—smooth isn't the word. Dense. Hard. As if the stone had started to grow into me, or I had started to wear away into it, and the boundary between my skin and the rock surface had become a gradient rather than a line.

I didn't pull my hand back. I should say that too. I noticed the change and I did not pull my hand back. I kept it on the stone. I felt the warmth of the rock—warmer than it should have been, warmer than the air, warmer than the organism even—flowing up through my fingers, through my wrist, into my arm, and I felt a rightness in it that I cannot justify or explain. As if the warmth had been looking for me the way I had been looking for the carvings, and we had found each other, and the finding was not something that happened to me but something I was.

My hands are like that now. Both of them. The dark mineral color extends up to my wrists, and the skin there is hard and smooth—mineral-smooth, the way the carved panel is smooth, the way the inside of the still-worm's strut is smooth. Not stone. Not skin. Something in between. Something that is still me but is also the place, the way the carapace-walkers' shells are still them but are also the dome.

I can feel things with these hands that I couldn't feel before. I can feel the organism's tendrils in the rock—not see them, not touch them, but feel them, the way you feel a vibration in a string by holding it. The organism is moving. Always. Its chemistry is flowing through the substrate, the nutrients cycling through its body, the waste from the walkers being absorbed and processed and re-deposited. I can feel this when I press my hands flat against the wall. It's like holding your hand against a living chest and feeling the breathing. Slow. Deep. The breathing of something so large and so old that each breath takes hours and each exhalation is a wall of warmth.

I can feel the carvings differently too. The carved lines are not just grooves in the rock anymore. They are—channels. The same channels the carver cut, but now I feel them as the organism feels them: as disruptions in the surface, as places where the substrate is thinner, where the warmth flows differently, where the mineral concentrations are slightly altered. The carver's work changed the way the organism grows in this chamber. The channels guide the nutrient flow. The shelves redirect the warmth. The panel of smoothed rock—the one with the images—is a dead zone. The organism has a harder time growing there because the surface is so smooth, so densified by the carver's work, that the tendrils can't find purchase. That's why the carvings have lasted. The carver didn't just make images. They made a surface that resists the organism, a surface that says, in the language of mineral density and surface texture: *not here. This is mine. This stays.*

I understand that language now. Not because I've learned it. Because I've become part of it.

---

There is a walker that comes to this chamber.

I call it something. Not a name—nothing so deliberate. A sound, more than a word. A sound that sits in my mouth the way the walkers' grinding sits in the chambers: naturally, without effort, as if my mouth were shaped to make it. The walker doesn't know this sound. It doesn't know I've made it. But I make it, internally, every time I see it come into the chamber, and the making of it has become part of how I know this walker from the others.

It's slightly larger than average. Its carapace is slightly darker—the mineral pallor tilted toward the warmer end of the spectrum, a hue that's closer to the amber of the organism than the chalk-white of most walkers. This might mean it's older. This might mean it's been feeding in this chamber longer than the others. I don't know. What I know is that it comes here—this chamber, the one with the carvings—more often than the other walkers do, and it feeds on the amber mat near the carvings with a particular attentiveness, and when it finishes feeding, it rests on one of the carved shelves—the ones the human carver made—and it rests there in a way that looks, to me, like resting in a place that was made for you.

I don't think the shelf was made for the walker. I think the shelf was made for a human hand or tool. But the walker uses it as if it were made for the walker, and the carver is gone, and the walker is here, and the question of who the shelf was made for has been answered by time in favor of whoever is using it.

I have watched this walker for a long time. I have watched it feed and rest and feed again. I have watched it move across the wall with that deliberate, methodical gait—each leg placed with care, each step a small decision—and I have felt, watching it, a kinship that I did not expect and cannot fully account for. It is patient. It is attentive. It does one thing—feeds on the organism—and it does that one thing with a completeness that I recognize, because I do one thing too. I keep the carvings. I stay with them. I watch the amber mat grow toward them and I—what do I do?

I don't clear the mat away. I've thought about this. In the early years, I thought about it a lot—whether I should brush the organism back from the carvings, whether I should clear the panel, whether the preservation of the images required my active intervention. And I tried it, once. I put my hand—my changed hand, my mineral-dark hand—against the edge of the amber mat where it was creeping up the panel, and I pressed.

The mat dimmed. The same way it dimmed when I first touched it, that first day. A retreat of the glow, a pulling-back, not of the whole organism but of the local surface, the part I was touching. And then, slowly—over hours, over a full pulse cycle—the mat grew back. Not around my hand. Into it. The tendrils of the organism found the mineral in my skin—whatever it is that has changed, whatever carbonate or mineral compound has infiltrated my tissue—and they grew into it, and around it, and the boundary between my hand and the organism became, over that cycle, something that was not a boundary. Not a wall. Not a line. A gradient. A zone of mixing, where my tissue and its tissue were not the same thing but were not entirely separate things either.

I am, it seems, becoming part of the organism. Or the organism is becoming part of me. Or both. I don't know which direction the process runs. I don't know if it matters.

I don't clear the mat away from the carvings anymore. I have found, instead, that my presence near the carvings is enough. The organism grows differently where I am. Not faster or slower—differently. It grows around the carvings rather than over them. It fills the channels and the grooves with thin tendrils that follow the carved lines rather than covering them, so that the carvings are not obscured but outlined—traced, as if by a careful hand, in living amber. The images are more visible now than they were when I arrived. The dome-from-outside, the walker, the human figure with the chisel, the three circles with their lines. They glow, faintly, with the organism's light, as if the carver's intention and the organism's growth have reached an understanding that I am the medium of.

I am the medium. That is what I am. Not the carver—the carver is gone. Not the organism—the organism is itself. Not the walker, not the glider, not the still-worm. I am the thing between. The thing that stays near the carvings and, by staying, changes the way the organism grows, so that the carvings are preserved—not by force, not by clearing the mat away, but by being present in a way that the organism responds to.

---

The walker was here this morning.

I say morning. I mean the bright phase of the pulse. The glow was building, the amber light strengthening, the chamber emerging from the dim hours into clarity. The walker came in through one of the channels—the carved channels, the ones the human carver cut—and it moved to the wall near the carvings and it began to feed.

I was sitting beside the carvings. I am always sitting beside the carvings. My body has assumed a shape that the chamber supports—my back against one wall, my legs extended along the floor, my hands resting on the stone on either side of me. I have been in this position long enough that the organism has grown up around my legs, thin tendrils of amber mat creeping over my feet, up my calves, not consuming me but incorporating me. My feet are warm. They have been warm for a long time. The warmth comes from the organism now as much as from my own blood.

The walker fed. I heard the grinding—that low, rhythmic rasping, stone on living stone. It was loud in the small chamber. It resonated off the walls, off the floor, off the ceiling, off my own body, which has become dense enough with mineral that I can feel the vibration in my ribs. The sound filled me. It filled the chamber. It filled the carvings, which I could feel vibrating under my hands, the carved lines trembling with the walker's grinding.

The walker fed for a long time. I don't know how long. Long enough for the glow to brighten further, for the chamber to reach full brightness, for the amber light to be so strong that the walls seemed lit from within, lit by something deeper than the organism, by the mineral veins themselves, by the stone's own phosphorescence amplified by the organism's glow.

The walker stopped feeding. It shifted on the wall—those precise leg movements, each claw placed with care. It moved along the wall toward me. Not toward the carvings. Toward me. It moved the way it always moves—slowly, methodically, one step at a time—but it moved toward me, and I watched it come.

It stopped near my hand. The one resting on the stone, the mineral-dark one, the one that is more carbonate than skin now. It stopped near that hand and it did something I had never seen a walker do.

It rested its head—its first segment, the wide one, the one with the grinding mouthparts—against my hand.

Not feeding. Not rasping. Not grinding. Resting. The weight of its small body against my hand, the cool mineral of its shell against the warm mineral of my skin, and I felt—through the contact, through the shared stone of our bodies—the walker's own warmth. Faint. Much fainter than the organism's. But there. The metabolic heat of a small, slow, patient body, burning its fuel at the rate of a few calories per day, producing just enough warmth to keep its enzymes functional, and that warmth flowing—just barely, just at the edge of detectability—into my hand.

The walker sat with me for a long time. It didn't feed. It didn't move. It sat against my hand and it was warm and I was warm and the chamber was warm and the glow was bright and the grinding was distant and the hum of the gliders was a low, sustained chord in the chambers above and the ticking of the organism's growth was inaudible but present, and we sat there together—the walker and I—in the way that two things that have lived in the same place for a very long time sit together. Not communicating. Not interacting. Just being in the same space, in the same warmth, in the same slow pulse of light, and the being-together being enough.

I don't know if the walker knows what I am. I don't know if it has a concept of what-I-am. I don't know if it experiences curiosity or comfort or companionship, or if it simply found a warm surface and rested there because warm surfaces are where it rests. I don't know, and I have sat with this not-knowing for long enough that the not-knowing has become its own kind of knowledge—a thing I know, which is that I don't know, and that the not-knowing is not a gap but a fullness, a way of being with the walker that does not require understanding to be complete.

---

The carvings say: *we were here. This is what we saw. This is what we did.*

I am here. I see it. I am doing it. The doing is the staying. The staying is the keeping. The keeping is the being-present-near-the-carvings-so-that-the-organism-grows-around-them-instead-of-over-them. The being-present is the being-changed. The being-changed is the becoming-part-of-the-place. The becoming-part-of-the-place is the being-kept—the same way the carvings are kept, not by force but by presence, not by resistance but by a kind of consent that the organism and I have reached, slowly, over years of sitting in the same warm chamber and breathing the same spore-laden air and feeling the same pulse of amber light.

I will stay here. I will stay until the carvings are gone or until I am gone or until the distinction between me and the carvings and the organism and the stone has become so blurred that the question of going doesn't apply. I will stay and the walker will come and rest against my hand and the gliders will hum and the still-worms will dissolve their way through the lattice above and the organism will pulse, eleven hours bright to dim to bright, and the dome will be warm, and the plain outside will be flat and empty and the wind will be a thin sound at the apex of the dome, and all of this will continue, and I will be part of it, and the carvings will say what they say, and I will say what I say, and the saying will be the same saying, which is:

*I was here. I saw it. This is what I did. I stayed.*

---

I notice, now, in this bright phase, that the amber mat has begun to grow over the edges of my feet. Not over my feet—into them. The tendrils have found the mineral in my skin and they are growing into it, and the place where my body ends and the organism begins is no longer a line but a zone, and the zone is widening, and I cannot tell if I am becoming the organism or the organism is becoming me, and I find that I do not need to tell, and I find that the not-needing-to-tell is the thing the carver knew, the thing the carver was trying to say with the three circles and their lines: not a name, not a count, not a prayer. A statement of the simplest and most difficult kind of knowledge there is, which is the knowledge that you are where you are supposed to be, and that you are what you are supposed to be, and that the being-here and the being-what-you-are are the same thing.

I am here. I am what I am. I stay.

The light is dimming. The pulse is moving toward its dark phase. The chamber will be quiet soon. The walker has gone. The gliders are still humming, somewhere above. The carvings glow faintly in the fading light, their lines traced in amber, the human figure holding its chisel, the three circles holding their meaning.

I will be here when the light comes back.
