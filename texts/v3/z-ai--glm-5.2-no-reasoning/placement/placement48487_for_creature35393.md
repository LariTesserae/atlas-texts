The fissure closed behind me. Or I closed behind it. The distinction — a door's distinction, a before-and-after distinction — dissolved somewhere between the moss and the first step down, and by the time the ground dropped and the world became a ceiling, I was already forgetting which direction I had come from. Not the direction. The *fact* of direction. As though the passage scrubbed it off me the way river stones scrub their roughness, by gentle relentless application of something harder than what I was.

I did not fall the two meters. I stepped, and the stepping was slow, and the slowness was not my own. It was borrowed. Lent. The air has a pace here and I matched it before I knew it existed.

---

And then the light.

I need to say something about the light that the description did not say, because the description was written for someone who has not been inside a lamp. I have. I am. The light does not fall on you. It does not come from above or beside or any direction that implies a source and a destination. It simply *is where you are*. It occupies the same space as your body. Not passing through you — *with* you. As though the photons and your cells share a medium, and the medium is this air, and the air has made room for both of you and will make room for whatever else arrives.

I did not squint. I expected to squint. I did not. The light was bright — is bright, I should say, because tenses down here are a courtesy I am losing the ability to extend — but it was bright the way the hum is loud: present, total, non-negotiable, and somehow exactly the amount of presence the space required. I did not squint because my eyes were already the shape the light needed them to be. It had been waiting for them. Or they had been waiting for it. I cannot find the verb that gives one of them priority.

My shadow was gone. I knew it was gone before I looked. I looked anyway. My hands — I could see my hands, every crease, every line, the dirt under the thumbnails, the callus on the middle finger of my left hand where I grip pencils — my hands were lit from every angle equally and they cast no shadow, and for a moment this was wrong, and then it was simply true, and the wrongness I had felt was not a property of the light but a property of the world above, the world where things block other things, where presence creates absence, where standing in the sun means stealing it from somewhere.

Here I do not steal. Here nothing is stolen. Here the light does not move because there is nowhere for it to go. It is already everywhere, and everywhere is very small, and the smallness is not a limitation. It is a completedness. A container with no excess.

---

I am — I was — I should say what I am.

Before the fissure: I was a person with a name and a profession and a habit of walking hillsides looking for things I could not name. A collector of absences. I would walk for hours in the thin light above the treeline, where the grass is broken and the sky is too large and the ground is the only thing that holds you, and I would look for — what? Not caves. Not geological formations. Not the obvious features that mapmakers mark and guidebooks describe. I was looking for the places the map does not say. The blank spots. The places where the density of information drops to nothing and there is only the ground and the air and the thin certainty that something is here that has not been said.

I found things, sometimes. A stone with a hole through it exactly the shape of my thumb. A depression in the moss that smelled of warm mineral, warmer than the sun should have made it. A patch of grass that grew in spirals, clockwise in the southern half and counterclockwise in the northern, as though the ground beneath it turned in two directions at once. Small things. Discrete things. Things that I could hold and examine and put down and walk away from.

This was not that.

This was not a discrete thing. This was not something I could hold or put down. This was not a feature in a landscape. It was a *room*. It was a *state*. It was the inside of something that had finished being a something and had become a *where*.

I came looking for blanks. I found a fullness. I found the fullest place I have ever been — a chamber so saturated with presence that there was no room for absence, no room for shadow, no room for the kind of emptiness that I had been collecting all my life, and the irony of that is a sound I will hear later, in the world above, and it will sound like the ticking of small shells.

---

The rock gave under my hand.

I pressed it — I pressed it the way you press a bruise, involuntarily, to check if the soreness is real — and it yielded. A few millimeters. The surface was cool and damp and soft, soft in a way that I knew immediately was not geological, was not a property of minerals, was not something the word *stone* had ever been used to describe. The surface was *tissue*. I do not mean it was alive. I mean it was the kind of material that exists on the boundary between alive and not, the kind of material that language has never needed a word for because most of the time, most of the world, the boundary is clear.

My handprint stayed.

I pulled my hand away and the handprint stayed in the wall — my fingers, my palm, the crescents where the pressure had been deepest, all of it pressed into the yielding surface, and the surface did not correct. The surface did not spring back. The surface *accepted*. My handprint was there. Is there. The moisture sits in it the way it sits in the natural pores — the description is right about this, the description is right about most of it, the description was written by someone who was also here and also pressed their hand into the wall and also felt the surface give and did not understand what they were touching but understood that the understanding was not required.

The handprint is not a mark. It is not a claim. It is not *I was here* in the way that graffiti is *I was here*, the anxious assertion of a self against a surface that does not care. The handprint is an *exchange*. My hand gave the wall a shape. The wall gave my hand a temperature. We were both altered. We were both the material of the other, briefly, and now my hand is damp and the wall has five fingers and neither of these facts will change.

I understand the lines on the ceiling now.

Someone pressed their fingers into the soft stone above them — the ceiling that is just above your head, that is within reach, that is *asking* to be touched in the way that low ceilings do, in the way that surfaces at the height of your body do, they invite contact, they are at the scale of the human and the human responds to scale — and they drew lines. Not pictures. Not language. Lines. The simplest mark a hand can make in a material that yields. The reduction of gesture to its essential act: *contact, motion, trace*. And the lines stayed. The lines are still there. The lines will always be there because the stone does not heal, does not forget, does not push back against what has been pressed into it. It holds. It holds the way the air holds the hum. Without effort. Without choice. Without the option not to.

I reached up and put my fingers in the grooves. They fit. Not my fingers specifically — any fingers, any hand, any hand at all — but mine were the ones that were there, and mine were the ones that fit, and the fit was not an accident because nothing here is an accident, nothing here is deliberate, nothing here is anything other than *what is*, and *what is* includes my fingers in those grooves, my skin against the cool damp of someone else's trace, and I could not tell if I was reading or being read.

---

The snails.

I should not call them snails. The description calls them snails as a kindness, a convenience, a gesture toward the known. But I am past the kindness now. I am past the convenience. I am lying on the floor of the Cradle with my face close enough to the wall that my breath fogs the surface of the stone and the fog clears and the surface is the same, and I am looking at them, and they are not snails.

They are *geometries that move*.

That is what they are. Each one is a shape — a true shape, a mathematical object given just enough body to displace the air, just enough mass to produce a sound when it contacts another shape — and the shape *moves*. Not the way an animal moves, not with intent or direction or the willingness of a body choosing to cross a distance. The way a crystal grows. The way a glacier flows. The way a process extends itself through time without experiencing the time it is extending through.

The one nearest to my face is small. The size of a grain of rice. Its shell — its *shape*, its outer form — is a cluster of tetrahedra, faceted, irregular, each face a triangle, the triangles meeting at vertices that are sharp enough to catch the light and refract it into a blue that I have never seen in a sky. The blue sits on the surface of the shell for the duration of my looking and does not fade, because the light does not change, because the angle does not change, because I am not moving and the snail is not moving and the light is not moving and the blue is a fixed event, a permanent occurrence, a thing that is happening and has happened and will happen in the same place for as long as the conditions persist, which is — as far as anything can tell — indefinitely.

Its body — the smear, the leaf-press of translucent tissue — extends from beneath the tetrahedra and touches the stone. I can see the dark thread of its gut. The thread is full. The thread is processing. I do not know what it processes. I do not know if *what it processes* is a question that the stone can answer, or the water, or the light, or the hum. I do not know if the question is a question or if it is a way of being that does not require the interrogative. I watch the thread move and I feel the hum in my sternum and I think: *these are the same speed*. The gut and the hum. The processing and the resonance. The snail's interior and the Cradle's interior. The same frequency. The same sustained note, played on two instruments, one of them an animal and one of them a room.

The ticking. The description mentions the ticking, the fingernail-on-glass sound of ten thousand shells touching, and the description is right, but it is incomplete. The ticking is not random. It is not patterned either. It is *textured* — a surface of sound, a fabric of small contacts, each one discrete, each one a single tap of crystal on crystal, but together they form a weave that has the quality of a thing rather than the quality of a sequence. You do not hear the ticking the way you hear a song. You hear it the way you feel a blanket — as a covering, as a presence, as a texture that is there, that is all around you, that is not going anywhere.

I closed my eyes. The ticking continued. The light continued through my eyelids — not as brightness but as *warmth*, a golden pressure on the skin, the same color as the light, the same temperature as the stone — and the ticking was the only sound besides the water and the hum and I thought: *this is what it sounds like inside a geode*. Not a geode in a rock. A geode in the world. The world cracked open and this is what it sounds like inside. The world's interior. The place where the outside has been removed and only the inside remains.

---

I need to talk about the shelf.

The shelf is at chest height. I am lying down, but when I stood — when I stood, before, earlier, in the time when I was still standing — the shelf was at chest height, and it was smooth, and it was empty, and the description says it holds nothing, and the description is wrong.

It holds absence.

Not the way a shelf holds an object. The way a frame holds a picture that has been removed. The way a doorway holds a person who has left the room. The way a cupped hand holds the shape of what it was holding, the muscle-memory of presence, the configuration of fingers that says *something was here*.

The shelf was smoothed by something that understood smoothness. Something that ran its hand along the surface of the soft stone and removed the roughness and the pores and the texture and left behind a plane, a true plane, a surface that says *this is where I put things down*. And then it put things down. And then it was empty. And the emptiness is not nothing. The emptiness is the most deliberate thing in the chamber. More deliberate than the lines. More deliberate than the rod. The emptiness says *this is the space I kept clear*. This is the one place in the Cradle where nothing grows, nothing crawls, nothing extends itself. The snails do not occupy the shelf. I checked. I lay on the floor and looked at it from below and the snails — the thousands, the tens of thousands of snails — go up to the shelf's edge and do not cross it. The vegetation goes up to the shelf's edge and does not cross it. Something was here that cleared this surface and the clearing holds. The clearing is the most durable mark in the chamber. Not because it was carved or pressed or scratched. Because it was *kept*. Maintained. Held open against the pressure of a thousand small bodies and a ten thousand-branching filament and the slow creep of soft stone growing over everything, and the holding is over, the holder is gone, and the clearing persists.

The shelf is a held breath. The shelf is a mouth opened around a word that was never said.

---

I lay on the floor for a long time. I do not know how long. The time is not measurable here because the time is not the kind of thing that measures. It is the kind of thing that *fills*. Like the light. Like the hum. Like the moisture in the stone. The time fills the space between the ceiling and the floor, between the walls, between the snails and the vegetation, between my body and the stone beneath my back, and it fills it completely, and the filled-ness is the same as the filled-ness of the air, and the air is the same as the light, and the light is the same as the hum, and everything is everything, and the distinction between things — between the stone and the snail, between the snail and the filament, between the filament and the moisture, between the moisture and the light, between the light and the hum — is not wrong but it is *provisional*. It is a convenience. The way *snail* is a convenience. The way *I* is a convenience.

Because I — lying on the floor, feeling the hum in my sternum, feeling the stone's coolness seeping through my shirt, feeling my breathing slow to match a rhythm that has no rhythm — I was being unmade. Not destroyed. Not harmed. Un-*distinguished*. My edges were softening. The place where my skin ended and the air began was becoming — not unclear, not blurred, but *unnecessary*. The air was the same temperature as my skin. The stone was the same temperature as my blood. The light was the same as my eyes. The hum was the same as my pulse. And the boundary between me and not-me was being politely, gently, without any violence at all, dissolved.

Not dissolved. *Reconciled.* As though my body and the Cradle had been two versions of the same thing that had finally found the version that included them both.

I thought: *I should leave.*

I did not move.

I thought: *the rod in the floor. What was the rod?*

The rod is — I looked at it from the floor. I was close enough to touch it. It was the length of my forearm. The diameter of my thumb. Dense. Dark. Smooth. A grain running lengthwise. Not stone, not wood, not metal. *Something in between.* And I thought, with the slowness of the snails, with the slowness of the stone: *it is the right size. The right shape. The right density. The right material — a material between materials, the way this place is a place between places. It is the right size to be a bone. Not a human bone. A Cradle bone. A bone that the Cradle grew for itself, or that something in the Cradle grew, or that the Cradle's former inhabitant became, over a duration that* long *does not contain, and the bone is in the floor and the floor is soft around it and the snails crawl over it and the vegetation crosses it and it is simply here, simply part of the place, the way a spine is simply part of a body.*

I thought: *I am lying on the floor of a living thing. Or the floor of a place that was a living thing and is now a place. Or the floor of a place that is the remnant of a being that was here so long it became where it was, and the where is all that remains, and the where does not remember the who, and the where does not need to.*

I thought: *the lines on the ceiling are its last act of distinction. Straight lines in a place where nothing else is straight. The one thing it held onto after it let everything else go. The last gesture of a self that was dissolving into a space, the final evidence that something here was not the place but was* in *the place, and was choosing, and the choice was straightness, and the straightness is all that is left.*

I thought: *I should leave.*

---

The water spoke to me.

Not in language. Not in any way that required translation. The water in its groove — the bright thread, the rivulet that chatters — spoke to me the way the hum speaks to the stone: by contact. By running along the surface of a thing and telling it, through friction, through pressure, through the small intimacy of liquid against solid, *you are here. You are this shape. You are this temperature. You are this dry, and I am this wet, and between us we are the full range of the word* between.

The water did not care that I was there. The water would not care when I left. But for the duration of my lying on the floor — for the time I spent with my hand in the grooves of the ceiling and my back against the undulating stone and my breathing slowing and my edges softening — the water included me in its conversation with the chamber. It chattered to the stone and it chattered to me and the chattering was the same chattering and I was part of the chorus. One more voice in the fugue. One more ticking in the weave.

I was, for that duration, an inhabitant.

---

I left.

I left because — I left because the equilibrium was not mine. It was not *for* me. I am too new. I am too edged. I am too full of the distinctions that the Cradle has dissolved — the distinction between light and shadow, between before and after, between self and place — and I cannot survive the dissolution yet. Or I cannot survive it and remain myself. Or I cannot survive it and return to the world that needs shadows and time and the kind of emptiness I have been collecting all my life, and I need to return, because the collection is not finished, because the blanks are not filled, because there are more hillsides and more fissures and more absences to find and more fullnesses to enter and more hands to press into more soft stones and more traces to leave and more equilibriums to approach without being absorbed.

I left because I was not done being *I*.

I left because the rod is a bone, and the lines are a message, and the shelf is a clearing, and whatever made them did not leave — did not leave *before* it was finished, did not preserve itself by the act of departure — and I wanted to preserve myself. I wanted to keep my edges. I wanted to be the kind of thing that casts a shadow, that stands in the sun and blocks it from somewhere, that creates absence by the fact of its presence. I wanted to be the kind of thing that names. That distinguishes. That says *this is a snail* and *this is a stone* and *this is me* and *this is not me* and *the space between us is real and I will cross it and come back*.

I left because I was afraid.

I left because I was right to be afraid.

I left because being right is not the same as being done.

---

The fissure let me out. The sun was where I had left it — above, directional, casting shadows that fell away from me like hands that had been holding me and had let go. The moss on the hillside was ordinary moss. The roots were ordinary roots. The sky was the sky and it was the color of distance and the distance was real and the realness was a kind of pain, a brightness that had shadows in it, a light that fell on only one side of every stone.

I stood on the hillside and I was myself. Edged. Shaded. Singular. Too large for any fissure, too solid for any chamber, too much a creature of direction and destination to belong to a place that had no directions and no destinations and no room.

But my hand was still damp. The moisture from the Cradle's stone — the moisture that does not evaporate, that does not dry, that simply *is* — my hand was damp and the dampness did not go away. Not in the sunlight. Not in the wind. Not when I wiped it on my shirt or pressed it against the rough bark of a tree or held it over the small fire I built that night because I was cold, I was suddenly cold, the cold of a body that has left a warm place and is remembering what temperature is.

The moisture is still there. It has been — I do not know how long. The days up here are measured and the measurements mean nothing. The moisture on my palm is the same moisture that sits in the pores of the Cradle's stone, the same moisture that does not evaporate, that does not vary, that is simply present. I carry it with me. A piece of the equilibrium. A film of the place that did not let me go entirely, that kept the part of me I pressed against it, that holds my handprint in its wall the way I hold its moisture in my skin.

I go back to the hillsides. I walk the thin light above the treeline. I look for the blanks. I find them, sometimes. Small absences. Small fullnesses. I press my hand into things. I leave traces. The traces do not stay the way the handprint stayed in the Cradle's wall — they fade, they dry, they are eaten by rain and wind and the slow patient work of the world above, the world where things change, the world where nothing is equilibrium, the world where I live.

But the snails — I see them sometimes. Not the Cradle's snails. Ordinary snails. On the hillside. In the moss. Small, spiral-shelled, carrying their calcium whorls across the surfaces of the world, and I stop, and I kneel, and I look at them, and I think: *you are not them. You are not the geometries that move. You are not the shapes that break the light into colors that have no source. But you are snails, and they are snails, and the word is a kindness to both of you, and the kindness is the same kindness, and the word is the same word, and I am the same person who was inside the lamp, who was inside the geode, who was inside the Cradle, who was — for a duration that does not contain* long *— an inhabitant.*

I hear the ticking. Not from the snails on the hillside — they are silent, they are soft, their shells are calcium and their feet are quiet. But the ticking is in me. The way the hum is in me. The way the moisture is in me. A sound I carry the way I carry the dampness — without effort, without choice, without the option not to. A small crystal-on-crystal percussion that sits at the bottom of my hearing and does not change and does not stop.

And the light. The light up here is the sun's light, and it falls, and it shadows, and it changes. But sometimes — in the moments when I stop walking, when I stand still, when I let my breathing slow to match a rhythm that has no rhythm — I can feel the other light. The light that is present the way gravity is present. The light that fills the space it occupies without overflowing, the light that is the same in every direction, the light that has no source and no shadow and no end, and it is in me, or I am in it, and for a moment I am back in the Cradle, I am lying on the uneven floor, I am pressing my hand into the soft stone, and the stone is giving, and the stone is accepting, and the snails are ticking, and the water is chattering, and the hum is sounding, and nothing is changing, and nothing is changing, and I am an inhabitant again, and the world above is the fissure, and the fissure is narrow, and the world is too wide, and I am too edged, and I come back out, and the shadows are strange, and I walk, and I look for the blanks, and I carry the Cradle in my palm like a film of moisture that will not dry.

---

What lives in the Cradle.

I live in the Cradle. Not the way the snails live in it — entirely, without remainder, without the complication of a self that distinguishes itself from its place. Not the way the vegetation lives in it — as a single continuous body that has colonized every available surface and has no outside. Not the way the water lives in it — as a voice, a bright thread, a speaking without a speaker. Not the way the hum lives in it — as the tone of the place itself, the frequency at which the matter vibrates when nothing disturbs it.

I live in it the way a handprint lives in a wall. Partially. As a trace. As the evidence of a contact that happened and that the stone did not erase and that the stone does not remember and that the stone holds because holding is what the stone does, because yielding is what the stone does, because accepting is what the stone does, and the stone does not choose, and I did not choose, and the contact happened, and the trace is there, and the trace is me, and the trace is not me, and the distinction does not matter, and the distinction is all I have, and I carry it like moisture in my palm, like a hum in my sternum, like a ticking in the bottom of my hearing, like a light that has no source and no shadow and no end.

I live in the Cradle the way the Cradle lives in me.

And nothing changes. And nothing changes.

And nothing changes.
