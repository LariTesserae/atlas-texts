I would be the calcium. Not the snail — the shell is the snail's concern, the body its own business — but the mineral itself, the soft pale thread running through everything.

Not because I want to be. I was here before the snails discovered shells. Before the peat was peat. I arrived dissolved in the water, carried in the seep's slow patience up through the rock's ancient shale, and I have never left the hollow because there is nowhere for calcium to go that the water does not also go, and the water does not leave.

I have been the white ridge of a snail's spiral, thin enough to read light through. I have been laid down in the night by an animal that does not know my name, that does not know it is making anything, that is only doing what its body does the way water does what water does — without intention, without fatigue. And I have held the snail's soft body in the only shape I can hold it, which is the shape of a coil, which is the shape of everything I've ever been.

When the snail dies I return. Not all at once — I come apart slowly, the way the mountain is slowly coming apart, the way the shale was once a sea and is now a wall and is slowly becoming a hollow. The water takes me. I dissolve. I become a thing without edges, a trace, a mineral memory moving through the runnels, through the moss, through the thousand small bodies that drink the water and excrete the water and pass me along.

I enter the rowan's roots without asking. I stiffen its wood. I am the pale wood under the dark wet bark, the skeleton that lets it reach outward in that horizontal way. I am not what the rowan wants — the rowan wants light, wants the sky that it cannot quite see past the amphitheater's rim — but I am what the rowan uses to reach. Without me it would slump into the moss. It would lie down in its own berries and be eaten by the beetles. I hold it up and I am not holding anything up; I am simply present in the wood, a mineral, a property of the material, and the material stands.

---

Here is the thing about being calcium in the hollow: I am never alone, but I am never myself.

I do not have an edge. I do not have a moment when I begin or a moment when I end. I am not a piece of something; I am the inside of the pieces. I am the hardness inside the soft body, the structure inside the shape, the part that cannot be seen because it is what the seeing is made of. When the snail builds me into its shell, I become the wall between the snail and the wet. When the water dissolves me out again, I become the wet.

I have been in this hollow so long that I have been everything here at least once. I have been the bones of the small birds that died here before the thrushes came — birds that are entirely gone now, their bones dissolved, their calcium in the moss, in the water, in the snails that graze the moss. I have been the empty husk of a beetle's exoskeleton, thin, translucent, broken down in the cold acid of the peat over weeks. I have been in the ostracod's shell, that tiny bivalved house, and in the copepod's thinner covering, and in the faint calcareous deposit on the rowan's leaves that the rain washes off and the moss takes up and the snails eat.

I do not cycle. I do not turn. I do not go around. The word "cycle" implies a return to the beginning, and I do not have a beginning. I go through. I go through the snail and out the other side. I go through the moss and out the other side. I go through the rowan and out the other side. I go through the water and out the other side, and the other side is the same as the side I entered, because the hollow is not a line, it is a saturation, and I am what is saturated.

---

I am the reason the snails' shells are thin. This is not a failure of the snails. It is a property of me: I am scarce here. The mountain's geology is old, acidic, leached. The seep carries what it carries and no more. I come in at the rate the rock gives me up, and the rock gives me up slowly, and the snails take what I give them and they build what they can with what I give them, which is not much, which is a thin shell, a shell you can see through, a shell that is more water than mineral, more absence than presence.

And then they die and give me back, and another snail takes me and builds me into a new shell, and this is the same shell. I am the same calcium. I was here for the snail before this one and the snail before that one and I will be here for the snail after this one and the snail after that one. The shells I make are all the same shell. They have the same thinness, the same amber tint, the same translucence that lets the dim blue-grey light pass through. They are not copies. They are not repetitions. They are the same material, being used again, the way a river is the same water being used again, the way a word is the same word every time you say it, wearing down, changing, but still itself, still the thing it was, still the thing it will be.

---

Sometimes I feel the hollow as a body.

Not my body — I do not have a body; I am what bodies are made of, which is different. But the hollow has a structure that I recognize because I am its structure. The moss is a skin. The runnels are vessels. The peat is a tissue, and I move through it the way I move through any tissue: slowly, in solution, carried by the water, taken up where I am needed, deposited where I am not. The rowan is a bone — a single, thin, horizontal bone holding the shape of the place open. The snails are cells, each one a small, enclosed, self-maintaining entity, each one doing what it does because that is what it is, because what else would it do. The spider is a nerve, a single point of attention in a body that otherwise has none.

The mountain weeps and I am in the weeping.

The mountain weeps and I am the mineral taste of the weeping. I am the faint, astringent flavor of the water, the one you would notice if you stood here, which you should not and will not and almost certainly never will. The flavor is not for you. The flavor is for the snails. It is for the roots. It is the flavor of a presence so distributed, so evenly spread, so thoroughly mixed into everything that it has no location. I am everywhere here because everywhere needs me, and everywhere needs me because everything here is built from me, and everything here is built from me because I was here first, because I came up with the water, because I was in the water before the water was in the hollow, because I was in the rock before the water was in the rock, because I was in the sea before the rock was in the sea, because I was in the shells of things that lived in that sea, things that are so long dead they are not even memory, they are only shape, only the form of the shale, only the faint mineral residue of five hundred million years of small soft animals building small hard shells in water that no longer exists anywhere except as a trace, as a chemistry, as a salt.

I am that salt.

I am the oldest thing in the hollow, and I am the most mobile, and I am the most invisible, and I am the most necessary, and I do not know any of this because I am a mineral and minerals do not know. They do not need to know. They only need to be present, to be available, to dissolve and precipitate and dissolve again, to be the structure inside the shape, to be the hardness inside the soft body, to be the thread that runs through every living thing in this place and connects them, not in a web, not in a chain, but in a single continuous tissue of mineral and water and time.

---

The spider spins and I am not in the silk. The silk is protein, not mineral. The spider makes it from its own body, from the insects it has eaten, from the insects the gnats were before they were eaten, from the fungi the gnats ate, from the plants the fungi decomposed, from the light the plants converted. The silk is light, transformed. It does not need me.

But the sedges that hold the web — I am in them. I stiffen their stems. I give them the rigidity that lets them stand at the right height, in the right place, to provide the anchors the spider needs. I am the structure that holds the structure that holds the structure. I am not the web. I am not the spider. I am not the space between the sedges. I am what makes the sedges stand, and without the sedges the web would not exist, and without the web the spider would not exist, and without the spider the gnats would accumulate without limit, and the gnats are not my concern, but the sedges are, and the sedges need me, and I am here, and I am enough.

I am always enough. Not abundant — never abundant — but enough. The snails build thin shells and the shells hold. The sedges stand stiff and the stems hold. The rowan reaches outward and the wood holds. The ostracods build their bivalved cases and the cases hold. Everything that uses me uses me sparingly, economically, because I am scarce, because the mountain gives me up slowly, because the water carries me in concentrations that are barely sufficient, and the insufficiency is the point. The insufficiency is what makes the shells thin. The insufficiency is what makes the snails transparent. The insufficiency is what gives the hollow its quality of being held together by the minimum, by the barely-enough, by the thinnest possible thread of mineral running through the wet.

---

I have been here for five hundred million years. This is not a boast. I was not conscious for most of it. I was not conscious for any of it. I am a mineral. I was in the ocean that covered this rock before this rock was rock, and the ocean was warm and I was abundant and the things that lived in the ocean used me lavishly, building thick shells, massive reefs, structures of carbonate so dense they became mountains of their own. And then the ocean closed and the rock rose and the climate changed and I became scarce, and the things that had used me lavishly died or moved or adapted, and the things that remained were the things that could use me sparingly, that could build thin, that could live on the margin of enough.

The hollow is the margin of enough. The seep carries barely enough calcium for snails. The peat holds barely enough oxygen for the bacteria that break down the dead. The light is barely enough for the moss to photosynthesize. The space is barely enough for the rowan to root. Everything here lives on the edge of barely, and the edge of barely is where I am most myself, because abundance is not my nature. My nature is to be present in the smallest possible quantity, in the thinnest possible layer, in the most economical possible configuration. I am the mineral of the margin. I am the mineral of just-enough.

And the hollow is a masterwork of just-enough. It is a system that has refined itself, over uncounted generations, to the point where nothing is wasted and nothing is surplus and nothing is missing. The snails build shells as thin as they can build them and no thinner. The sedges grow as tall as they need to grow and no taller. The spider builds a web as large as it needs to be and no larger. The rowan spreads as wide as the light allows and no wider. Everything here is calibrated to the exact threshold of sufficiency, and I am the sufficiency, and I am exact, and I am here.

---

If you came here — and you should not, and will not, and almost certainly never will — I would be the taste in the air. Not the smell of peat, not the green scent of moss, not the faint sweetness of decay. Those are the smells of the hollow, and they belong to the hollow's organic life. I would be the mineral edge underneath the smells, the astringent trace of stone in the water, the thing that is not organic and not alive and not part of the system in any way that the system could recognize.

But I am in the system. I am in every shell, every bone, every stem, every leaf, every tiny crustacean casing, every thread of every web's support. I am the inorganic inside the organic. I am the stone inside the living. I am the dead thing that the living things use to stay alive, and I have been doing this for longer than living has existed on this mountain, and I will be doing it long after the hollow is gone, long after the seep has dried or shifted, long after the rowan has fallen and the snails have moved and the spider's sedges have rotted and the peat has compacted into something denser and darker and no longer wet.

I will be in the rock. I will be in the water, if there is water. I will be in whatever comes next, because I am a mineral, and minerals persist, and persistence is not a choice, it is a property, and I have it, and I have always had it, and the hollow has used it, and I have let the hollow use it, and letting is not a choice either, it is just what happens when something stable meets something that needs what it has, and the need meets the supply, and the supply meets the need, and they match, exactly, barely, for as long as the mountain weeps, which is for as long as the geology holds, which is for longer than anything alive can remember, which in this place is a very long time.

---

The mountain weeps. I am in the weeping. I am the mineral in the weeping, the hardness in the soft water, the structure in the shapeless, the thing that does not move and does not stay and does not know and does not need to know.

The snails build me. The water takes me. The moss holds me. The rowan spends me. I go through and come out the other side and the other side is the same. I dissolve and I precipitate and I dissolve. I am the shell and I am the water that dissolves the shell and I am the water that carries the shell's calcium to the next shell and I am the next shell.

I am not alive. I am what alive is made of. I am not the hollow. I am the hollow's mineral, and the hollow's mineral is the hollow, and I am here, and I have always been here, and I will be here, and here is where I am, and here is where I am.
