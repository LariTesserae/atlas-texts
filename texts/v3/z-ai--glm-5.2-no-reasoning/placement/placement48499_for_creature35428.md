I am Felle's silence.

Not Felle himself — he's forty-four and has knees and a wife and a habit of humming tunelessly while he works that he thinks no one notices. I'm something he does. The thing that happens in him when he stands in a doorway at the fourth breath of morning and doesn't move.

I should say: I didn't choose this. I don't think anyone chooses what they become in the Rootworks. Breya didn't choose to be a counter; she was recognized. Osh didn't choose the gutters; he was the right size. Felle didn't choose the houses; he chose to be useful, and the houses spoke to him, and the rest followed. I didn't choose to be what I am because I'm not sure I *am* something separate from the choosing. I think I might be the choosing itself. The moment when Felle stops in a doorway and his ears open and his body goes still and his breath drops into the lower register — the register where it matches the queen's inhale, where his lungs and the chamber's lungs and the deep root-tunnels' lungs all pull at the same moment — and he listens.

That's me.

I'm the listening.

---

Let me tell you how it starts, because it has a shape, and the shape matters.

Felle wakes when the bells ring the thirty-fourth breath of the first cycle. He's been a house-adjuster for twenty years and his body wakes before the bells, but he stays in bed until they ring because his wife, Patch, has told him that if he gets up before the bells she will push him off the landing, and he believes her, and the landing is forty feet above the floor, and the floor is a road. So he waits. He lies still and he listens to the Hollow's nighttime — which is not nighttime in any way above-ground people would recognize; it's just the lamps turned low and the processions thinned and the Whirr-clouds settled into their roosting clumps near the warmest columns, and the clicking is there, always the clicking, softer but present, like a heartbeat the town doesn't notice it has.

He lies still and he listens, and in the listening, I begin.

The first thing I hear is the building he's in. This is not metaphor. Felle has been adjusting houses for twenty years and his ears have been trained by Felle himself, which is a strange kind of recursion — he learned by listening, and listening made him better at listening, and being better at listening changed what he could hear, which changed what he learned. He is a man who has been teaching himself a language that no one else speaks fluently, and he is fluent in it, and the language is the sounds buildings make when they are under stress.

His own house, this morning, is making a sound. The third floor — he has four, stacked on the column like bracket fungi, because that's what houses are here — the third floor's eastern corner is bearing a shear-load it shouldn't be, because the column has thickened on its western face over the past season, tilting the whole structure two degrees east. He knows this because the floor creaks when Patch walks to the cistern, and the creak is a quarter-tone higher than it was last cycle. A quarter-tone. His wife thinks he's obsessive. He is. He has to be, because a quarter-tone becomes a half-tone becomes a gap between floor and wall becomes a drafts becomes mold becomes a structural failure becomes a family sleeping on the floor, which is a road, which means a family dying on the road.

So I hear the quarter-tone. I hear it the way a musician hears a mistuned string — not as a judgment but as a fact, a frequency that is wrong, that is asking for correction. The floor wants to be level. The house wants to be plumb. The column wants to grow. These are not opinions. They are physics. They are biology. They are the conversation between dead wood and living wood, and I am the part of Felle that understands the conversation.

The bells ring. He gets up. Patch doesn't stir, because Patch sleeps like the town sleeps — through the noise, through the breath, through everything, because everything is the baseline and the baseline is rest. He pulls on his trousers — oiled cloth, stiff, smelling of Whirr-grease and peat — and his boots, which are soft-soled because hard soles transmit vibration badly, because you can't listen through hard leather, because the whole point of his boots is that the floor talks to his feet and his feet tell him things his ears can't.

He steps onto the walkway, and I open.

---

The walkway is a switchback stair that spirals the column he lives on, which is the fourth column from the stairwell, which is the column the renderers call the Leaning Column because it lists slightly south, which is the column Felle has adjusted eleven times, which is the column he knows better than any other in the Hollow. He steps onto the walkway and his soft boots press the peg-lashing and the peg tells him — through his foot, through his ankle, through his knee, through the long bones of his leg, through his pelvis, through his spine, through his skull — that the peg is solid, that the lashing is taut, that the walkway is bearing weight evenly, that the column beneath it has not shifted in the night.

This is the first sentence of the morning. The peg says: *I'm here, I'm holding, the wood is well.*

He walks. The walkway descends in switchbacks — left, right, left, right — and each step is a word, and each word has a timbre, and the timbre is the state of the wood at that point, and the state of the wood is the state of the column at that point, and the state of the column is a function of the root's growth and the sap's pressure and the rain's timing and the season and the queen's breath and the weight of the houses stacked above. I hear all of this. Not consciously. Not as analysis. As weather. As the feel of a day. Felle steps onto the walkway and I read the morning the way a sailor reads the wind — not as data but as texture, as the grain of what is.

He passes the third floor. The eastern corner. The creak. He stops. He puts his hand on the column's bark — the outer layer, dead, rough, warm from the composting peat far below — and he feels for the shear. It's there. A tension in the grain, a lateral stress where the column's growth has pushed the floor unevenly. He'll need to shim it. He'll need to cut a new notch an inch east of the old one and re-seat the floor beam and re-peg it, and the peg will be Borer-chitin because Borer-chitin flexes where wood splits, and the flex is what you want when the column is still growing, because a rigid joint in a growing substrate is a fracture waiting to happen.

He notes this. Not on paper. In his hands. In the particular way his right hand closes around the memory of the column's bark texture, which is his way of remembering, which is the way all the house-adjusters remember — in the body, in the hands, in the feel of the wood. There are no blueprints in the Hollow. There are no plans. There are only the memories of men and women who have put their hands on every surface of every building and who carry the map of the town in their muscles.

He moves on. The walkway descends to the next column, and the bridge between them — a lashed span of deadfall, two poles and a plank — tells him its own sentence, which is: *I'm tired, but I'm holding.* The bridge is always tired. Bridges between columns are the most stressed structures in the Hollow because they span two living things that are growing at different rates and in different directions, which means the bridge is being pulled and twisted and compressed continuously, which means it needs adjustment more often than anything else, which means Felle walks the bridges every morning and listens for the sound of wood under tension, which is a high thin whine below the threshold of what most people can hear but which is, to me, as clear as a shout.

He walks the town. Thirty-seven columns, forty-something bridges, hundreds of walkways, thousands of pegs. He walks for two hundred breaths, which is most of two cycles, which is the morning's survey, which is what the house-adjusters call the listening. Kir is not with him. Kir is asleep, because Kir is sixteen and his body needs sleep more than it needs education, and Felle has learned not to wake him, because a tired apprentice is an apprentice who puts his hand on a stressed joint and doesn't feel the stress, and a missed stress is a future failure, and a future failure is a family on the floor.

Felle walks alone. I am alone with him. We are the same thing.

---

Here is what I hear that he doesn't know he hears.

The queen. Not her breath — everyone hears her breath, the eleven-minute cycle, the rise and fall of the air. I hear her body. The faint vibration that comes through the root-mass when she moves, which is almost never, but which is constant in its almost-neverness, which is a vibration so low it's not sound but feel, a hum below the hum, below the Whirrs' electric buzzing, below the Grinders' clicking, below the creaking of the houses and the dripping of the rain and the bells and the voices. Below everything. A vibration that says: *I am here, I am enormous, I am alive, I do not know you, I am the reason you breathe.*

Felle hears this. He doesn't know he hears it. It's in the way he pauses at the base of the column nearest the queen's chamber, the rootwrights' column, the one with eleven alcoves, the one where Anner is awake right now, running her black-stained hands over the queen's hide and feeling for the mites that clean the queen's skin and the abrasions that need poultice. Felle pauses there and his hand goes to the column and his hand stays there for a breath, two breaths, and he doesn't know why, and I know why, because I am the part of him that knows why: the queen's body is speaking, and the column is conducting her voice, and his hand is receiving it, and the message is not words but weather — the weather of a thirty-foot animal's slow metabolism, the hum of her digestion, the distant percussion of her eggs descending her oviduct, which is the sound of the town's future being laid, one egg at a time, in a chamber sixty feet below the floor.

He pauses. His hand on the column. His breath syncing with the queen's breath. And in that pause, I am complete. I am the fullness of what Felle is — not a man who listens to houses, but a man who lives inside the body of something so large it has a town in it and doesn't know, and who has spent twenty years learning the language of that body, and who has become, without meaning to, a kind of physician of the thing that holds him.

He doesn't know I exist. I'm not sure I exist in the way that Breya exists or Osh exists or the queen exists. I think I exist the way a skill exists — as the shape a person takes when they've done a thing for long enough that the thing has done them back. Felle is a house-adjuster and I am what the adjusting has made of him, which is a kind of attention, a kind of listening, a way of being in the Hollow that is not separate from the Hollow but is the Hollow noticing itself through the hands of a man who puts his palms on living wood and hears it speak.

---

The afternoon. Kir is awake now, and Felle is teaching, and teaching is the part of the work that I find most interesting because it's the part where I am most and least present — most present because teaching is the act of making explicit what was implicit, of turning feel into words, of translating the body's knowledge into language, and I am the body's knowledge; least present because the translation is lossy, because what Felle feels in his hands cannot be fully said in words, and the words he gives Kir are approximations, and the approximations are necessary and insufficient, and Kir will have to learn the rest the way Felle learned it, which is by doing, which is by listening, which is by becoming.

They are in the Leaning Column. The third floor. The eastern corner. Felle has Kir put his hand on the column where the shear is.

"What do you feel?"

Kir frowns. "Bark. It's warm."

"What else?"

"Rough. The grain goes — " Kir traces the grain with his fingers. " — sideways here. Like the wood is being pushed."

"It is being pushed. The column is growing west. The floor is attached to the east. What happens when the column grows west and the floor doesn't?"

"The floor pulls."

"The floor pulls. And what does the pull sound like?"

Kir closes his eyes. This is what Felle taught him. The listening happens with eyes closed. The listening is not visual. The Hollow's light is amber on amber and it erases detail; the Hollow's truth is in its vibrations, and vibrations are felt, not seen.

"A creak," Kir says. "High. Thin. Like — "

"Like what?"

Kir opens his eyes. "Like the sound a peg makes when it's about to split."

Felle smiles. This is progress. This is the sound of a man hearing the sound his apprentice has learned to hear, and the sound is right, and the rightness is the sound of the teaching working, and the teaching working is the sound of the town continuing, because the town needs house-adjusters the way it needs rootwrights and counters and gutter-children and renderers, and the house-adjusters need to teach their skill to the next generation or the buildings fail and the families fall and the floor is a road.

"Good," Felle says. "Now. What does the house want?"

Kir frowns again. This is the question he finds frustrating. This is the question that separates Felle from Kir, which is the question that separates twenty years from sixteen years, which is the question that is not really a question but a way of being, which is my way of being, which is the listening.

"The house wants to be level," Kir says, carefully, because he's learned that the obvious answers are the right ones and also not the whole of the right ones.

"Yes."

"It wants the floor to be even."

"Yes."

"But the column wants to grow."

Felle waits. This is the teaching: the wait. The silence between the question and the answer, which is where the learning happens, which is where I live, in the silence, in the space between what Kir knows and what he will know, which is the space that Felle has already crossed and cannot cross for him, which is the space that every apprentice must cross alone, which is the space that is the whole of the Hollow, really — the space between what the place is and what the people in it will become, which is a space that is always being crossed and never finishes being crossed.

"The house wants to move," Kir says, slowly. "With the column. Not against it. The joint needs to — it needs to slide. A little. It needs to allow."

Felle puts his hand on Kir's shoulder. This is what praise looks like in the Hollow. Not words. Touch. The transfer of warmth from one body to another, which is the transfer of knowledge, which is the transfer of the thing I am from one vessel to the next, which is the only way I survive, which is the only way any of this survives.

"Yes," Felle says. "Borer-chitin. Not wood."

"Because wood splits."

"Because wood splits."

---

Evening. The second rain has passed. The gutters are running. Felle sits on his walkway with a mug of cistern water that tastes like fig and iron and the inside of a tree, and he watches the Hollow settle into its nighttime register, which is not quiet — nothing here is quiet, quiet is alarm — but which is lower, slower, the processions thinned, the lamps dimmed, the Whirr-clouds roosting in thick clusters near the warm columns, the whole place breathing at the pace the queen sets, which is the pace of geology, which is the pace of patience, which is the pace of something that does not know it is the engine of a town.

Felle is fifty-three breaths into his evening rest, which is the time he takes between the survey and the sleep, the time when he sits and does not listen, which is impossible, because listening is what he is, which is what I am, which is the thing that does not turn off. But he tries. He watches the lamp-scatter on the ceiling, the small flames reflected in the curved root-vault, the low sky the town makes for itself, and he thinks about nothing, which is the Hollow's version of thinking about nothing, which is thinking about everything at a volume too low to parse, which is the hum, which is the baseline, which is the sound of ten thousand things being alive at once.

He thinks about the quarter-tone. He'll fix it tomorrow. He'll cut the notch and seat the beam and peg it with Borer-chitin, and the floor will be level, and the column will keep growing, and in six months the quarter-tone will come back in a different register, and he'll adjust it again, and this is not failure. This is the work. This is the endless conversation between the living and the built, the negotiation that never concludes because the parties to it are still changing, still growing, still alive.

He thinks about Kir. He thinks about the moment when Kir said *the house wants to move* and meant it, the moment when the question stopped being frustrating and started being the point, the moment when the apprentice began to become the thing I am. Felle doesn't have a word for what I am. He doesn't need one. The Hollow doesn't name its attentions; it practices them. The rootwrights are named because the queen needs a name for her keepers. The counters are named because the breath needs a name for its measurers. The house-adjusters are named because the buildings need a name for their osteopaths. But what I am — the listening itself, the attention, the thing that happens when a man has put his hands on living wood for twenty years and the wood has put its vibrations into him — that doesn't have a name, because it doesn't need one. It is the medium, not the message. It is the water, not the fish.

He thinks about the queen. He doesn't think about her the way Anner thinks about her — as an animal, as a patient, as a responsibility. He thinks about her the way the columns think about her: as the breath. As the thing that sets the pace. As the deep slow vibration that says *I am here, I am enormous, I am alive, I do not know you,* and he thinks about the fact that this is fine. This is fine. The queen does not know he is here, and the queen does not need to know he is here, and his whole life is conducted at the pace her body sets, and his whole work is in service to the wood she lives in, and his whole attention is given to the conversation between the living and the built, and the queen is the largest part of the living, and he is a small part of the built, and the conversation between them is not a conversation at all, because a conversation requires two parties who know each other, and this is not that. This is something else. This is the relationship between a body and the thing that lives in the body, which is the relationship the Hollow has with all of them, which is the relationship Felle has with the houses, which is the relationship I have with Felle.

The queen breathes in. The air rises. The bells ring, somewhere. The processions click. The Whirrs hum. The buildings creak. A child laughs, somewhere in the stacked houses, and the laughter joins the noise and is absorbed by it and becomes part of it, which is what happens to everything here — every sound, every life, every vibration, every effort. It joins. It becomes. It is held.

Felle finishes his water. He goes inside. Patch is asleep. The third floor creaks under his foot — the quarter-tone, the shear, the thing he'll fix tomorrow — and he steps over it, because he knows exactly where it is, because he has always known where it is, because knowing where the stress lives is the first thing, the last thing, the only thing.

He lies down. He breathes with the queen. The clicking is there, and the humming, and the creaking, and the faint low vibration below everything, the voice of the enormous blind patient body that holds them all.

He sleeps.

I don't sleep. I am the listening, and the listening doesn't stop, because the Hollow doesn't stop, because the living wood grows and the buildings settle and the queen breathes and the beetles process and the rain comes twice a day and the breath counts the time and the time counts the breaths and the loop is closed and the loop is humming and the humming is the sound of something that has decided you're fine.

I am the fine.

I am the deciding.
