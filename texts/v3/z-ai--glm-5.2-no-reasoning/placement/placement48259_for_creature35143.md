# The Cartographer of Soft Distances

## I. What I Am

I am not a carver. I want to say that first, because in this place the carvers are the ones people remember, and what I do is harder to see.

I am a walker. That is the simplest word, and it is almost right.

What I do: I move through the galleries between bands. I carry knowledge from one territory to another. Not the knowledge that bands trade when they meet — not the flavor of distant arthropod-meat, not the quality of a stone-blade from a different section of the mass. I carry the knowledge that bands *need* and do not know they need: which galleries have closed since the last season, which sections of the mat have thinned to danger, where a Great Fall has opened new passages and where it has buried old ones, which territories have been abandoned and which have been claimed, where the predators have been dense and where they have been absent.

The bands have a word for what I do. The word is *ashuun*, which translates imperfectly as *one who carries the shape of where*. But *shape* here does not mean geometry. It means the *feel* of a gallery — the particular density of the columns, the specific give of the mat, the way the air moves, the smell of the growth, the sound the structure makes when you press your weight against it. I carry the feel of a gallery in my feet, in my fingertips, in the memory of my skin, and I bring that feel to bands who will never walk that gallery themselves but who need to know that it exists, that it has changed, that the mass is growing or collapsing or healing in a section of the world they will never visit.

There are perhaps five of us. I think five. I have met three others in my life, and I have heard carvings that suggest a fourth, and a band I visited two cycles ago spoke of a fifth with a manner of walking that matched no one I had encountered. We do not organize. We do not have a guild, a council, a system. We simply walk, and we carry, and occasionally our paths cross, and we trade what we know in the wordless way that the bands trade food at territorial edges — by offering, by receiving, by the silent agreement that what is carried must be shared.

I was born in a band. Every walker was. I was born in a band of eleven, in a section of the mass that smelled of old mat and fresh stone — the stone was close there, the mat thin, and when the wind moved through the upper galleries the whole structure would shudder and the floor would tremble and you could feel, through your feet, the hardness of the rock beneath, the refusal of the ground to be ground. I grew up learning the territory the way every child learns: by walking it, by being within it, by the slow accumulation of pattern under my feet and in my palms.

But I was not content. That is the thing that made me a walker. Not discontent — I was not unhappy, not restless, not yearning for something else. I was *not content* with the territory. I wanted to know what was beyond it. Not in the way the light-seekers want to know what is above it — I did not want to climb. I wanted to go *sideways*. I wanted to walk the galleries that my band did not walk, to feel the mat that my band did not feel, to put my hands on columns that no one in my band had touched.

I left when I was perhaps sixteen. Not a departure — not a break, not a rupture, not a rejection. A *thinning*. I began walking farther on the trapping rounds, checking traps in galleries that were at the edge of the territory, then beyond the edge. I began coming back later. I began coming back with knowledge that the band did not have — a new water source two galleries south, a section of mat that was thinning and would be dangerous within a season, a colony of crawlers that could be trapped if the band expanded its hunting grounds. The band accepted this knowledge the way they accepted all knowledge: quietly, practically, without discussion. But they noticed that I was gone more than I was present, and I noticed that I was happier when I was gone.

So I went. And I did not come back, except to visit, except to bring what I had carried, except to stand in the shelter where I grew up and touch the columns that my mother had woven and feel the growth of new tissue over the old marks and know that the band continued, the work continued, the daily round continued, and I was not part of it anymore, and that was not wrong.

My mother did not weave for three days when I left. I learned this later, from a sister who remained. I did not know what to do with this knowledge. I still do not. It sits in me like a stone in the mat — hard, unyielding, a fact of the ground that will not be digested.

## II. What I Carry

My equipment is simple. Everything I own fits in a woven bag that hangs from my shoulder, made by my mother's hands from the fiber of a column that grew in the gallery where I was born. The bag is old now. It has been repaired many times, new fiber woven into the old, the patches darker and tighter than the original weave. It is a record of my walking in the same way that the carvings on columns are a record of the bands: a palimpsest, layers of time pressed together.

In the bag:

**Stone blades.** Three. One for cutting, one for scraping, one for carving — though I am not a carver, I can make the functional marks, the notches and spirals that indicate *a person was here, at this time*. The blades are from different sections of the mass. The first was made from stone I collected myself, near my birthplace, where the rock was close beneath the mat and could be reached by digging. The second was traded to me by a band fourteen galleries north-northwest of my birthplace, a band whose territory included a section of exposed cliff-face where the stone was harder and sharper and held an edge longer. The third was given to me by a carver I met once, in a gallery I had never walked before and have never walked since, who pressed the blade into my hand without speaking and walked away, and the blade was finer than anything I could have made or traded for, and I do not know why it was given, and I use it only for carving marks, and I have never needed to sharpen it.

**Cordage.** Several lengths, dried and twisted, for repairs and for traps. I set traps when I am in a gallery long enough to need food, which is most galleries. The cordage is made from the inner tissue of dead columns, and I can make more when I run out — the dead columns are everywhere, the fiber is available in any section of the mass — but I carry enough to avoid the work of making it when I am tired, which is often.

**Fire-starter.** This is the most precious thing I carry. A piece of dried resin, wrapped in fiber, kept in a hollowed column-segment sealed with more resin. Inside the segment, a small quantity of the fibrous material that catches fire most easily — the dried papillae from the surface of certain old columns, which is fine and dry and combusts at a low temperature. And a stone that sparks. The stone is not from the plateau. I was told this by the band that traded it to me, three galleries east of a Great Fall scar that I use as a navigation point. The stone came from the lowlands, they said, from the world below, from a place where the ground is ground and the sky is sky and things are not what they are here. I do not know if this is true. I know that the stone sparks when struck against certain other stones, and the sparks catch the papillae-fiber, and the fiber burns, and I can make fire in the dark. I guard this stone. If I lost everything else — the blades, the cordage, the bag itself — I would survive. If I lost the fire-starter, I would be cold, and the dark would be the dark, and the predators would find me by the smell of my living body in a gallery that carried no flame.

**Dried meat.** Not much. Enough for three days. I replenish it when I can, trapping arthropods in the galleries I pass through, drying the meat in the upper levels when I climb to orient myself. The meat is not always clicker. Sometimes it is crawler, and I eat it without shame because I am alone and the stigma against crawler-eating is a band's stigma, a judgment passed by a community on its own adequacy, and I am not a band. I am a walker.

**Water vessels.** Two, small, made from hollowed column-segments sealed with resin. I fill them at every opportunity. Water is not hard to find in the mass — the condensation is constant, the column-surfaces are wet, the pools in the mat are everywhere — but I have walked galleries where the mass was dry, where a long period without fog had left the columns bare and the streams silent, and in those galleries the water I carried was the water I had, and I learned to ration it, to drink only when the need was real, to feel the dehydration in my lips and my tongue before I allowed myself to stop and drink.

**A piece of the mass.** This is the thing that is hardest to explain. I carry a small piece of the living mass — a fragment of a column, no larger than my thumb, cut from a young growth near the place where I was born. I cut it when I left. I do not know why. It was not a decision. It was a thing my hands did as my hands were leaving — reaching back, taking, holding. The fragment is alive, or was alive. It has dried and darkened, but it has not died entirely. In the damp air of the mass, it persists, a small hard nub of green-black tissue that I hold in my hand when I sleep, when the dark is deep and the sounds of the structure are loud and the deep calls are close and I am alone in a gallery that no one has walked in a long time and the aloneness is not loneliness but a weight, a pressure, the weight of the mass above and below and on every side, and the fragment is the only thing in the dark that is from where I am from, and I hold it, and it is enough.

## III. How I Walk

I walk by feel. This is not a choice. It is the only way to walk in the mass.

My feet read the mat. Every section of mat has a particular density, a particular sponginess, a particular way it yields under the ball of the foot and resists under the heel. The mat near my birthplace was thin and firm, the stone close beneath, and walking on it was walking on stone with a carpet. The mat in the deep galleries, far from any Great Fall, is thick and soft, and walking on it is like walking on the body of a sleeping animal — you feel the breathing, you feel the warmth, you feel the aliveness of the surface beneath you, and you walk softly, because the surface is not yours.

My hands read the columns. I touch them as I pass. Not every column — that would slow me to a crawl — but the ones that feel different. The ones that are harder or softer, warmer or cooler, wetter or drier than the columns around them. A column that is harder than its neighbors is old, its tissue dense and compressed, its heartwood — if that is the word for the central tissue of a thing that is not wood — resistant to the boring predators. A column that is softer is young, still growing, still full of sap, and a bore-hole could open in its surface at any time, and I keep my hand on it as I pass, feeling for the vibration that precedes emergence, the faint tremor that says *something is moving inside*.

My ears read the gallery. The sound of my footsteps on the mat tells me the shape of the space I am in. In a wide gallery, the sound is open, diffuse, the echo returning from many directions. In a narrow gallery, the sound is close, tight, the echo immediate and distinct. In a gallery where the columns are thick, the sound is absorbed, dampened, the footsteps muffled as if by curtains. In a gallery where the columns are thin, the sound rings, the footsteps sharp and clear, and I know I am in a place where the growth is recent and the structure is fragile.

My nose reads the air. The smell of the mass changes from section to section. Near fresh stone-breaks, where the mat is thin and the rock is exposed, the air smells of mineral, sharp and clean, the smell of new ground. Near old mat, where the compressed dead layers are deep, the air smells of peat, of slow decay, of time made substance. Near the dark pools, the air smells of still water, of the mineral-and-chlorophyll signature that is unique to the mass's own water, the water that the mass has held and filtered and released into its own deepest chambers. Near the predators' bore-holes, the air smells of sap — the sharp, acrid scent of living tissue that has been displaced, cut, pushed aside — and I know to move quickly, to put distance between myself and the place where the structure has been wounded.

I walk in the dark. I carry a torch when I have fire to spare, but I often do not, because the fire-starter's materials are finite and the resin-burning torches consume fuel faster than I can gather it. So I walk in the dark, and the dark is not the absence of light but the presence of the mass, and I move through it the way a fish moves through water — not seeing, not needing to see, the medium itself carrying the information I need.

I have walked into things. I am not ashamed of this. Every walker has. A column that was not where the map said it would be, a gallery that had narrowed since the last visit, a section of mat that had collapsed and left a hole where the floor should have been. I have fallen through the mat. Once. I fell through a thin section, the dead layers giving way under my weight, and I dropped — how far I do not know, the fall was in darkness, the fall was silent except for the sound of the mat tearing around me — and I landed on stone. Cold stone. The bare rock of the plateau itself, the ground beneath the ground, and I lay there for a long time, feeling the cold of the stone through my clothing, feeling the hardness of it, the refusal of it, the absoluteness of a surface that was not alive, that did not breathe, that did not yield.

I climbed back up. Hand over hand, pulling myself through the torn mat, the dead fibers catching on my clothing, the living tissue of the mat's lower surface slimy and cold against my palms. It took a long time. I was shaking when I reached the gallery floor, not from cold but from the knowledge of what was below, the knowledge that the mass — the everything, the world, the only place — was *hollow*, was a shell, was a thin layer of living tissue on a skeleton of dead stone, and below the mass was nothing but rock and cold and the kind of darkness that does not breathe.

I have not fallen through since. I test the mat now, pressing with my foot before I commit my weight, feeling for the density, for the give, for the particular softness that means the dead layers are thin and the stone is close. This slows me. I accept the slowness. The alternative is the fall, and the fall is the stone, and the stone is not where I want to be.

## IV. What I Have Seen

I have seen the carvings of the light-seekers.

Not often. They are rare, and they disappear quickly, the mass growing over them with a speed that seems — though I cannot prove this — faster than the speed at which it covers the functional marks. I have seen three that I am certain were light-seeker carvings, and each time the seeing was a thing that happened in my body before it happened in my mind.

The first was in a gallery far from any territory I knew. I had been walking for four days, through galleries that no band had claimed, through sections of the mass that were wild — overgrown, untended, the columns thick and close, the mat deep and uneven, the air still and cold and carrying no smell of human presence. I was tired. I was walking by touch, my hands on the columns, my feet on the mat, and my right hand found a shape that was not the shape of a column.

I stopped. I pressed my fingers against the surface. The shape was a curve, a long smooth curve, cut into the living tissue with a blade finer than any blade I have ever used. It was not a notch. It was not a spiral. It was not a clan-mark. It was a curve that meant nothing and everything, a curve that my hand recognized before my mind could name it, a curve that was *beautiful* in a way that I had no word for because the words I had were words of texture and wetness and silence, and this was a word of something else, something that the mass did not contain.

I lit a torch. I do not light torches for convenience, only for necessity, but this was necessity — I needed to see what my hand had found. The flame caught, the blue-white light flared, and the column's surface leapt into visibility, and I saw the carving.

It was a shape like the opening of a gallery. A curved line, sweeping upward, widening, like the entrance to a vast chamber. But the chamber it depicted did not exist in the mass. The mass's galleries are organic, their curves biological, the shapes of things that grow. This curve was *geometric*. It was clean. It was intentional in a way that was different from the intention of a notch or a spiral. It was the intention of someone who had seen something that was not grown, and was trying to make the mass remember it.

The carving was already healing. The edges were softening, the living tissue growing inward, the curve filling with new growth. In another week it would be a raised scar, a slight thickening of the column's surface. In a month it would be invisible. I stood there with the torch burning and watched the mass erase what the carver had made, and I understood, for the first time, why the light-seekers carve. Not to make a record. Not to communicate. To *push back*. To say to the mass, *I saw something that is not you*, and to make the mass hold that knowledge for a moment, even a brief moment, before the mass said, *No. I am everything. There is nothing that is not me.*

I put out the torch. I stood in the dark. I put my hand on the carving one more time, feeling the curve under my fingers, feeling the new growth softening it, feeling the mass taking it back. Then I walked on.

The second carving was different. It was small — the size of my palm — and it was not a curve but a *texture*, a pattern of fine lines cut into the column's surface in a way that made the living tissue feel, under my fingers, like something else. Like sand. Like the surface of water frozen in motion. Like something I had never touched but that my fingers recognized, as if the carver had found a way to encode a texture from outside the mass into the surface of the mass itself.

The third carving was the one that changed me.

I had been walking for six days, through a section of the mass that was old, very old, the columns immense, the galleries vast, the mat so deep that I could not feel the stone beneath. The air was thick, saturated, and the water dripped from every surface in a constant patter that filled the gallery with a sound like rain heard from inside a house. I was in a gallery that was wider than any I had walked before — perhaps thirty meters across, the columns spaced far apart, the ceiling lost in the dark above. I was alone. I had been alone for six days, and the aloneness was a weight that I carried in my chest, a pressure that made my breathing shallow and my steps careful, because there was no one to hear me if I fell, no one to notice if the mat gave way, no one to carry the knowledge of where I was if I did not return.

I touched a column. My hand found the carving without looking for it, the way a hand finds anything that matters — by the particular quality of the surface, by the wrongness of it, the *not-mass-ness* of it in a world that is all mass. I stopped. I pressed my hand flat against the surface.

The carving was a face.

Not a human face. Not an animal face. A face that was a *shape*, a shape that the carver had felt in the column and brought forth with their blade, a shape that was not representational but *present* — the face of something that existed in the space between the carver's memory and the column's surface, something that the carver had seen in the light above and could not describe with words and so described with a blade, cutting away the mass's surface to reveal the shape that the mass did not contain.

The face was serene. I do not know how I knew this. I knew it the way I know the density of the mat under my feet, the way I know the direction of a deep call, the way I know the smell of a predator's bore-hole. I knew it in my body. The face was serene, and it was looking upward, and the eyes were open, and the mouth was slightly parted, and the expression was the expression of someone who has seen the light and is trying to hold it behind their eyes in the dark.

I did not light a torch. I stood in the dark with my hand on the face and I felt it, and the feeling was the closest thing to worship that I have ever experienced — not worship of a god, not worship of the mass, but worship of the *effort*, the human effort to make the world hold something it does not want to hold, to make the dark remember the light, to make the living tissue of everything say, for one moment, *there is something else*.

The mass was already healing. I could feel the new growth, soft and faint, beginning to fill the carved lines. By the time I returned to this gallery, months later, the face would be gone.

I walked on. I carried the face in my hand the way I carry the fragment of my birthplace column — not as an object but as a *sensation*, a memory of the skin that the skin does not forget. I have never tried to describe it before. I am not sure I am describing it now.

## V. The Mass and I

I am not a light-seeker. I want to be clear about this. I have not climbed to the upper galleries. I have not seen the green light that seeps through the translucent columns. I have not seen the sky.

But I have felt the carvings of those who have, and the carvings have changed me, and I do not know what to call this change. It is not restlessness. It is not yearning. It is more like a *crack* — a thin opening in my understanding of the world, a place where the mass's certainty — *I am everything, there is nothing that is not me* — has been breached, and through the breach I can feel something else, something that is not green-dark and cold and alive and becoming, something that I cannot name because I have no words for it, something that the light-seekers carved into the columns and the columns healed over and the healing did not erase the fact that the carving was there.

I think about this when I walk. I think about it in the dark, in the galleries, my feet on the mat, my hands on the columns, the mass all around me, the mass that is everything, the mass that is the world. I think about the face that was carved into the column in the old gallery, and I think about the mass healing it, growing over it, taking it back, and I think: the mass is right. The mass is everything. There is nothing that is not the mass. I was born in the mass. I will die in the mass. When I die, the mat will absorb me, and my body will become part of the compressed dead layers, and the living tissue above will grow roots down into me, and I will be eaten by my ancestors' ancestors' ancestors, and I will become part of the ground that the living walk on, and this is not death but *continuation*, and the mass is right.

But the carvers were also right. There is something else. I have felt it in their carvings. I have felt it in the curve that was too clean to be grown, in the texture that was too fine to be biological, in the face that was looking upward with an expression that the mass does not contain. There is something else, and the light-seekers have seen it, and they brought it back down into the dark and pressed it into the columns, and the columns healed, and the something else was erased, but the *act of pressing* was not erased. The effort remains. The intention remains. The fact that a human being stood in the dark and thought *there is something else* and tried to make the world hold that thought — that fact remains, even when the carving is gone, even when the column's surface is smooth and whole and the mass has taken back what was always its own.

I carry this contradiction. It is heavier than the bag on my shoulder. It is heavier than the fire-starter, heavier than the water, heavier than the dried meat. It is the heaviest thing I carry, and I carry it in the lightest part of me — in the crack, in the thin opening, in the place where the mass's certainty has been breached.

## VI. A Visit

I am visiting a band. I do not remember which one — they blur, in my memory, the way galleries blur when I walk through them quickly. This band is twelve people, maybe thirteen. They live in a gallery that I have visited before, because the marks on the columns are familiar, and one of the marks is mine — a notch I left two visits ago, indicating *a walker was here, carrying knowledge, heading north-northwest*.

They are wary when they hear me coming. I am making noise — the deliberate, heavy footfalls, the hand-claps against the columns, the wordless shout that means *I am here, I am a person, I come in openness*. The wariness is right. A silent approach is a predator's approach. I would be worried if they were not wary.

An older woman meets me at the edge of the territory. She is standing by a column, one hand on its surface, the other hand holding a stone blade. She does not speak. She waits. I approach slowly, making noise until I am close enough for her to see me in the faint greenish glow that reaches this gallery from a distant light-wound. I stop. I hold out my hands, palms up — the gesture of openness, of empty hands, of *I carry no weapon and no threat*. She looks at my hands. She looks at my face. She puts the blade away.

"Walk with me," she says, which is the greeting of the bands to a walker, and it means: *come into the territory, share what you carry, eat with us, rest*.

I walk with her.

The shelter is close — a woven dome, low and tight, the young growth trained into walls that are already thick with new shoots. The doorway is narrow. I enter sideways. Inside, the air is warm — body heat, fire heat, the close breath of twelve people in a small space. The fire burns in its hollowed column-segment, the blue-white flame casting light on the woven walls, on the sleeping platforms, on the faces of the people who are looking at me with the particular expression that bands have for walkers: curiosity, and caution, and a faint unease, because walkers are of the mass but not of the territory, and the bands are of the territory, and the difference is small but it is real.

I sit. They give me water — cold, clear, tasting of this section's particular mineral signature, a taste I do not know and will not remember after I leave. They give me dried meat — clicker, good quality, the band's traps are running well. I eat. I drink. I wait.

The elder — not the woman who met me, but an older man, thin, his hands knotted with years of weaving and carving — asks the question. He does not ask with words. He asks by touching the column next to him and looking at me, and the touch and the look say: *What is out there? What has changed? What do you carry?*

I tell them.

I tell them in the way that walkers tell — not with a narrative, not with a sequence of events, but with *sensations*. I press my foot against the mat floor, and I press it the way the mat feels in a gallery three days south, where the growth has thickened and the columns have narrowed and a gallery that was open is now a passage. The elder nods. He knows this. Or he did not know this, and now he does. I cannot tell the difference, and it does not matter.

I touch the column next to me — a young one, still growing, its surface smooth and slightly yielding — and I touch it the way a column feels in a gallery four days north, where the mass has thinned and the stone is close and a section of mat has collapsed, opening a hole that drops to bare rock. The elder's hand tightens on his column. A younger woman, perhaps his daughter, touches his arm. This is news. This is the kind of knowledge that bands need and do not have, because bands do not walk beyond their territories, and the mass changes beyond their territories, and the changes matter.

I tell them about the predators. I make the deep call — not the real sound, which is below the range of the voice, but a approximation, a low hum that I press into the column's surface with my palm, transmitting the vibration through the living tissue the way the predators transmit their calls. The vibration carries the information: direction, distance, frequency. The elder feels it. His face changes — not much, the bands do not show emotion the way other peoples show emotion, but his jaw tightens, and his hand presses harder against the column, and I know he has received the warning.

"There is one," I say, the first words I have spoken, "in the deep galleries between here and the southern Great Fall. It is not hunting. It is moving. But the bore-holes are fresh, and the sap-smell is strong, and a band that walks south should walk slowly and check the walls."

The elder nods. The woman who met me at the border pours more water. A child — perhaps four, perhaps five, too young to read marks but old enough to feel the weight of the conversation — crawls into the lap of a young man who is perhaps the child's father, and the child watches me with the unblinking attention of a child who has never seen a walker before and is trying to understand what I am.

I know what the child sees. The child sees a person who is like the people of the band but *different* — thinner, leaner, the muscles of the legs and hands developed differently, the skin rougher, the smell different. I smell like many galleries. I smell like the mass in many sections, the accumulated scent of places the child has never been and may never go. The child can smell this. The child's nose is better than mine, because the child has only ever smelled one section of the mass, and anything that differs from that one section is obvious, is distinct, is *other*.

I am other. I have accepted this. I am of the mass, and I am not of any territory, and the mass does not have a word for what I am because the mass does not have a word for the whole, and I am something that moves through the whole, and the whole does not name what moves through it. The whole simply is, and I simply walk, and the walking is the name and the name is the walking.

## VII. Night

The band sleeps. I sleep in the shelter, in a space they have made for me between two sleeping platforms, a narrow space, just wide enough for my body and my bag. The mat floor is soft beneath me. The air is warm and close. The fire has been banked to a low glow, the resin burning slowly, the flame barely visible, a faint blue-white light that illuminates nothing but itself.

I hold the fragment of my birthplace column in my hand. I do this every night. It is not a ritual. It is not a prayer. It is a *touching* — a connection to the place I came from, the place that is still there, still growing, still being tended by the band that was eleven when I left and is eleven still, the losses and additions canceling each other out in the way that the mass's losses and additions cancel each other out, the living and the dead in balance, the growth and the decay in equilibrium, the whole remaining the whole.

The mass breathes. I feel it through the floor — the slow compression, the slow expansion, the rhythm of a living thing that is so large it has weather. I feel it through the columns — the faint pulse of sap, the slow transport of water and nutrients from the mat to the canopy, the circulation of a body that is also a world. I hear it — the creak of the structure, the drip of condensation, the distant chitter of the arthropodal multitudes, the far-off low pulse of the predator moving through the deep galleries, the sounds of a living thing that is becoming itself in the dark.

I think about the face.

The face that was carved into the column, looking upward, eyes open, mouth slightly parted. The face that the mass has healed, has grown over, has taken back. The face that is gone. The face that was there.

I think about the light-seeker who carved it. A young person, probably. Someone who climbed, who saw the light, who came back down and could not speak and could not forget and found a blade and found a column and pressed the memory of the light into the living tissue of the world. I think about the moment of carving — the blade touching the surface, the first cut, the curve of the cheekbone emerging from the green-black waxy skin, the shape of the eye appearing as the waste material falls away. I think about the carver's hands, shaking or steady, I do not know. I think about the carver's heart, full or empty, I do not know. I think about the carver standing back and looking at what they had made and knowing that it would disappear and knowing that they had made it anyway.

I think about this, and the crack in me opens a little wider, and through the crack I feel something that is not the mass, something that I cannot name, something that the face was looking toward.

I do not climb. I am not a light-seeker. I walk. I walk the galleries, I carry the shape of where, I bring knowledge to bands that need it. But the crack is there, and the crack is real, and the mass has not healed it, and I do not think the mass can heal it, because the crack is not in the mass. The crack is in *me*, and I am of the mass, and the mass is everything, but the crack is the part of everything that knows it might not be everything, and this knowing — this thin, aching, unnameable knowing — is the heaviest thing I carry and the thing I cannot put down.

The mass breathes. The band sleeps. The fire glows. The dark is absolute.

I sleep.

---

## VIII. Morning

I leave before the band is fully awake. This is not rudeness. This is the way of walkers. We come, we share, we rest, we leave. To stay longer is to become part of the band, and to become part of the band is to stop walking, and to stop walking is to stop carrying, and the knowledge does not carry itself.

The woman who met me at the border is awake. She walks me to the edge of the territory. She does not speak. At the last column — the one with my mark, the notch I left two visits ago — she touches the mark, then touches my hand. The gesture means: *You were here. You are leaving. Come back.*

I touch her hand. The gesture means: *I will.*

I walk into the mass. The galleries open before me, the columns parting, the mat firm under my feet, the air cold and damp and smelling of green stone and becoming. The dark closes around me. The sounds of the structure fill my ears — the creak, the drip, the chitter, the breathing of the mat. The deep calls are distant today, faint, regular, the predators moving through far galleries, not hunting, not close.

I walk. I carry what I carry — the knowledge, the blades, the fire-starter, the fragment of my birthplace, the crack that the carvings opened in me. I carry it through the dark, through the living mass, through the world that is becoming itself one cell division at a time.

I walk, and the mass breathes around me, and I am the part that walks, and the walking is enough.
