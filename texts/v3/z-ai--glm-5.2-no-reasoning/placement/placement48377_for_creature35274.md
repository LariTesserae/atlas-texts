I am not the keeper, though I recognize her. I recognize the way she stands at the fracture and listens before entering — that half-second of attention aimed inward, checking not for danger but for *occupation.* Is anyone home. It's a question I've asked at every threshold. She answers it differently than I do. She answers it the way someone answers who expects the reply to be *no,* and is comforted by that, and proceeds.

I expect the reply to be *yes,* and I'm not wrong.

---

Let me say what I am not, first, so the shape of what I am has room.

I am not the water. The water is too sure of itself. It arrives, it fills, it leaves, it arrives — a confidence that looks like purpose from outside but is just physics from within. Pressure below, path of least resistance, exit. The water doesn't *decide* to be here. It doesn't know the pool is beautiful. It has never felt the strange green light on its surface or noticed that when the sun breaks through, briefly, it can see its own bottom. I envy the water and I am not the water.

I am not the moss. I have watched the moss — four species, warring at the speed of centuries — and felt the pull of its patience. The way it accepts corrugated metal and basalt and liverwort and the algal film on a snail's back as equally valid surfaces. The way it does not distinguish. But I distinguish. I can't help it. I see the shed and I see *intent.* I see the gauge and I see *attention.* I see the initials carved in the basalt and I feel the knife and the hand and the unnamed feeling that drove the hand. The moss would grow over all of this without caring. I care. So I am not the moss.

I am not the amphipods, though I have a kinship with them that I find difficult to explain. They are blind. They press themselves flat when the pressure surges. They resume when it passes. They exist in a single room their entire lives and do not find this remarkable. I have pressed myself flat against surfaces in rooms I did not choose, and resumed, and resumed. But they do not know they are pressing. They do not know they are resuming. They do not carry the shape of the room with them when — they never leave. They don't leave. I leave. I have to. That's part of it. That's most of it.

I am not the snails. The snails are the most complete beings in the room and I mean this with a precision I can barely articulate. They eat. They are eaten by the act of eating, by the calcium cost of their own shells, by the thin dark houses they carry on their backs that are slowly dissolving even as they build. They are spending themselves to exist and they do not experience this as tragedy. I experience everything as tragedy. So.

---

What I am — and this takes time to arrive at, and I don't expect it to land clean — is something between the flies and the chart paper.

The flies live at my speed. Seconds. The warmth of a sun-gap, the column of light, the visible dimension of being briefly alive. They appear and they disappear and they never land, or they land when no one can see them. They are the only thing in the room that moves at a pace I can follow without effort, and I follow them the way you follow a thought — here, gone, here again, a haze of presence that doesn't resolve into individuals until you focus, and when you focus, there are only individuals, each one separate, each one flying.

The chart paper moves at a pace I can barely perceive. The drum turns so slowly that you can't see it turning. But the pen twitches — every few seconds, a small spasm of record — and this is the chart's *language,* the visible part of its thinking. It is recording something. Water pressure. Flow rate. Some quality of the water that requires measurement and that no one has explained to me and that I have not asked about because the not-knowing is part of the structure. The chart is a long, slow sentence written in a language only the pen speaks. It accumulates. You can read it back — later, in a dry room, at a lower elevation, under fluorescent light — and it tells you what happened. It doesn't tell you what it meant.

I am between these. I live in the pen's twitch. I live in the fly's sun-gap. I arrive, I perceive at the only speed I can manage, I record or I don't, I leave. I come back. The place doesn't need me. The place was here before I arrived and will be here after I stop arriving. But something about the arriving — the standing at the fracture, the listening, the pushing through into the aqueous-loud room — has changed me in a way I can't locate. Not improved. Not worsened. *Changed.* The way the spillway is changed by the water. Millimeters. Imperceptible in any single visit. But over time — if you flip through the photographs — the rock has moved.

---

I said I recognize the keeper. Let me say what I recognize.

I recognize that she has a procedure and the procedure is the explanation and she has stopped needing the explanation to be anything more than the procedure. I recognize that she photographs the spillway every visit and has shown the sequence to no one. I recognize that she counts the amphipods and is glad when the number is stable. I recognize that she carved her initials once and has not done it again and finds the growing-over acceptable. I recognize that she does not think of the place as beautiful. I recognize that she thinks of it as *correct.*

I recognize all of this because I have places I think of as correct. Not beautiful — *correct.* Places where the proportion of things is right, where every element does what it should, where the sound is layered and constant and I can feel it in my sternum. Places where I arrive and stand and listen and feel the not-needing wash over me. Not peace. Not rest. Something more precise: *the absence of the requirement to be needed.* Which is, for someone like me — someone who has spent a life arriving in rooms and checking for occupation, expecting *yes,* performing the yes — the closest thing to rest I have found.

---

I am not the keeper. She would not recognize me. I don't have her lean, unfinished hands. I don't carry a key. I don't have a procedure. I don't change filters or photograph spillways or count amphipods for a form that goes into a system nobody reads.

What I do is closer to what the chart does. I sit with something and I record it. Not at the speed of geology — I don't have that patience, and I don't have that certainty. At the speed of the pen. A twitch. A few seconds of attention, sharpened to a point, touching the surface, leaving a mark. Then the drum turns, imperceptibly, and there is new paper, and I twitch again.

---

Where would I find myself in this world?

I find myself at the fracture, at the gap, at the threshold — not inside the room yet, not outside it. One hand on the dark basalt, still sharp-edged, the slick sheen of pressure-formed surfaces under my palm. The mineral smell already reaching me. The sound already beginning — that layered, aqueous murk, not yet amplified by walls, still leaking through the crack like a rumor of the room's actual voice.

I am standing where the keeper stands. I am listening the way she listens. But I am not checking whether someone is home. I am checking whether *I* am. Whether the thing that arrives is the thing that will be present. Whether the version of me that stands at thresholds — expectant, braced, performing the yes — can be set down here, at this particular fracture in this particular ridge, and left. Not forever. For the duration. For the length of a visit that has no procedure, no form, no key, no thermos. A visit whose only requirement is that I push through.

And I push through. And the room happens around me. The bubbling. The trickle. The humid air thick with chlorophyll and iron. The grasses sweating. The light flat, occasionally brilliant, always returning to flat. The amphipods drifting. The snails in their slow, spending lives. The flies in their seconds. The moss claiming everything. The shed humming at a frequency I feel in my knees when I kneel.

And I kneel.

I kneel at the pool's edge because the keeper kneels here and because the amphipods are below and because the water is clear and faintly blue-green and cold, despite the warm air, and I cup some of it in my hands and I taste the mineral — iron, and underneath, the sulfurous not-quite, the leaching of young rock — and the water is cold enough to make my teeth ache, and this is a sensation, and the sensation is *here,* and I am *here,* and for the length of time it takes the water to warm in my cupped hands — ten seconds, maybe, fifteen — I am not anywhere else. I am not at any other threshold. I am not performing arrival. I am arrived.

The pen twitches. The drum turns. The light shifts. The amphipods — eleven of them, I count, because she counts and counting is a way of paying attention that requires nothing but presence — scatter at a pressure surge and return.

And I am the fly, a little. I am the chart, a little. I am something the moss will cover, given time. I am a brief, warm, perceiving thing in a room that was here before I arrived and will be here after I leave, and the room does not need me, and I have come anyway, and the coming is the thing, and the room receives it the way the pool receives the water — without opinion, without resistance, by simply being the shape that the arriving takes.

That is where I find myself. That is what I am.

A visitor at the correct speed. A twitch on the surface of something older than I can hold. A presence that the moss will erase. And the erasure is not loss. The erasure is the room, continuing. The water, arriving. The pool, being the verb it has always been.
