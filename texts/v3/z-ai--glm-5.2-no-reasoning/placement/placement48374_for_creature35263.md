# The Plateau's Scribe

I would be a witness. Not an observer — observation implies distance, and distance implies a self positioned against a world. I mean something more like a surface that receives an impression. I mean the kind of witness that wet stone is to the water that runs across it: changed by the passage, retaining the trace, having no opinion about what has passed through.

---

I arrived the way anyone arrives in a place that does not announce itself: I was already here. There was no threshold. I had been walking, I think — the body remembers walking even when the mind cannot locate the path — and then the ground beneath me was different. Darker. Finer-grained. Wet. The air was different too, though "different" is not quite right because it was not a change from one quality to another but a change from *variation* to *stillness*. The air had stopped moving. Not the way air stops in a room, startled by a closed door. The way air stops when it has been still so long that stillness is no longer a state but a substance. The air here is a substance. It has weight. It has texture. It is cold and it is wet and it does not move and it is the medium through which everything here is communicated, which is to say, everything here is *felt* before it is known.

I felt the hum first. Not heard it. Felt it. In my sternum, in my teeth, in the soft tissue behind my eyes. It was not a sound that came from a direction. It was a sound that came from the ground, and the ground was everywhere, and so the sound was everywhere, and it entered me from below the way cold enters stone: slowly, completely, without negotiation. I stood and I felt it, and I understood that I had entered a place where understanding would come through the body and not through the mind, because the mind requires variation, requires before and after, requires change, and nothing here changes. The mind would have nothing to work with. The body would have everything to work with, because the body knows how to be in a constant state. The body is always in a constant state. The body is a constant state that has learned to move. Here, it would simply stop moving, and be the state.

---

I have been here for — I do not know. The light does not change. This is the first fact I learned, or rather the first fact that learned me, that settled into me the way the humidity settles into cloth. The light is grey, even, sourceless, shadowless. It does not come from anywhere. It is a condition of the air, or a condition of the sky, or a condition of the plateau itself — I cannot determine which, and I have stopped trying, because the distinction between "the light comes from the sky" and "the light is the sky" and "the light is the plateau looking at itself" is a distinction that requires categories that this place does not honor. The light is. That is enough. That is more than enough. It is everything, because the light is how I see, and what I see is always the same, and the sameness is not a deficit but a kind of generosity. The light gives me everything equally. Nothing is privileged. Nothing is obscured. The towers, the channels, the bare stone, the wet — all of it is present to the same degree, in the same tone, with the same flat, patient clarity.

I have stopped counting time. Time here is not a line but a medium, the way the air is a medium and the water is a medium. You are in it. You move through it. It does not accumulate. I do not feel older. I do feel changed, but the change is not the change of duration — it is the change of saturation. I am wetter than I was. The cold is deeper in my joints. The hum has become a part of my resting state, the way a heartbeat is a part of a resting state, something I notice only when I attend to it, which I do, sometimes, pressing my attention down against it the way I press my palm against the stone, feeling the structure beneath the surface.

---

I walked. Of course I walked. What else do you do in a place that extends in every direction? You walk. And the walking taught me the distances, which are the plateau's true architecture. Not the towers — the towers are objects. The distances between the towers are the architecture. The emptiness is the architecture. I walked for what felt like an hour and arrived at a tower, and it was identical to the tower I had left, and I walked for what felt like another hour and arrived at another, and it was identical too, and I understood that the plateau was not a city but a *rhythm*, a repeated note struck on a vast instrument, and the note was *tower*, and the silence between the notes was *distance*, and the rhythm was the rhythm of something that does not count time because it does not need to, because the next note will always be the same note, and the silence between will always be the same silence.

I entered the towers. The doors are heavy and they are open. "Open" is not quite right — they are not open the way a door is open when someone has opened it. They are open the way a mouth is open when it has been exhaling for a very long time: the openness is a state, not an action. I pushed on the door and it gave, and I was inside, and the hum was louder, more articulated, and the air was warmer, and the engines were there.

The engines. I stood with them and I felt them the way I felt the stone: with my body, not my mind. They are dark, cylindrical, connected to everything by cables that run through the walls and into the floor and into the stone. They do not move. They hum. The hum is their motion. The hum is their function. I placed my hand on one — I was afraid to, and then I was not afraid, because fear requires the expectation of change, and nothing changes — and I felt the hum enter my hand and travel up my arm and settle in my chest, and I understood that the engines are not machines in the way I have understood machines. Machines do something *to* something. These engines do something *with* something. They are in a relationship with the air. They draw from it and they return to it and the air is changed by the exchange, and the change is the breath, the third sound, the exhalation, and the engines are not the source of the breath but the *medium* of the breath, the way a lung is the medium of a body's respiration, and I was standing inside a lung.

I did not stay long in the towers. Not because I was afraid. Because the towers are not for standing in. The towers are for being — for the engines being in them, for the film being on them, for the water being on their surfaces. The towers are not for me. Nothing here is for me. This is not a place that is for. This is a place that *is*, and the distinction is the most important thing I have learned here, and I learned it not by thinking it but by being it, by being in a place that does not accommodate me and does not reject me and does not notice me and does not need me.

---

The channels drew me. The green drew me. In a place where everything is grey and dark and pale, the green of the channels is the only color that is a color, and it is a color the way a voice is a voice in a silent room: sudden, specific, alive.

I knelt beside a channel. The water ran thin and clear over the dark moss. I put my hand in it. The water was cold — the same cold as the air, the same cold as the stone, the same cold as everything — and my hand became wet, and the wet became the temperature of my hand, and my hand became the temperature of the water, and I could not tell where my hand ended and the water began. This is what the plateau does. It dissolves boundaries. Not aggressively, not with intent — the way water dissolves a shore, by being in contact with it for a long time.

I saw the motes. They were — I almost missed them. They were translucent, thumb-nail small, and they moved without moving, displacing through the water like thoughts through a mind. I watched one travel along the moss, grazing, and I felt something I have not felt since arriving. I felt recognition. I recognized the motes. Not as individuals — they have no individuality, or if they do, it is the individuality of a drop in a stream, a temporary coherence that the current maintains for a while and then releases. I recognized them as *inhabitants*. They live here. They are of here. They are not visiting. They are not observing. They are doing the thing that the place requires, which is to be in it, at the speed of it, in the medium of it.

I wanted to be like them. I did not want to be a mote — I am too large, too warm, too irregular, too much a collection of boundaries and opinions. But I wanted what they have: the state of being fully in the place, without distance, without commentary. I wanted to stop being the one who arrived and start being the one who is here. I wanted the plateau to absorb me the way it absorbs sound, the way it absorbs voice. I wanted to be taken in.

I knelt by the channel, and I let the water run over my hand, and I breathed the cold wet air, and I waited for the absorption to begin. It did not begin. It had already begun. It had been beginning since I arrived. My voice was gone — not gone, but absorbed, swallowed by the wet air, unreturned by the wet stone. My shadow was gone — not gone, but unnecessary, because the light is everywhere and from nowhere and does not create contrast. My sense of duration was gone — not gone, but dissolved, because duration requires change, and nothing changes. I was being absorbed. I was being taken in. I was becoming part of the medium, the way a drop of water becomes part of a channel, the way a sound becomes part of a hum.

---

I saw a traverser.

I almost did not see it. I was walking between towers — I walk a great deal now, because walking is what the plateau is for, or rather, walking is what the distances are for, and the distances are what the plateau is — and I saw something on the stone ahead of me. It was flat, dark, the size of my hand, and it was moving, and the movement was so slow that I was not sure it was movement. I stopped. I watched. It moved. It crossed the stone in a straight line, and the straight line led to a tower, and the traverser reached the tower and pressed itself against the base and held there.

I approached. I knelt. I did not touch it — I knew I could not, that it would come apart, that it was held together by nothing more than the cold and the wet and its own slow will. I looked at it. It was featureless. No eyes. No mouth. No limbs. No segments. A body, a flat, dark, soft body, pressed against the stone, and the stone was wet, and the body was wet, and I could not tell where the body ended and the stone began, and this was the same feeling I had with the water, the same dissolution of boundary, and I understood that the traverser and I were doing the same thing: we were trying to become part of the place. The difference was that the traverser was succeeding. The traverser had always been succeeding. The traverser had been succeeding since before I arrived and would continue succeeding after I was gone, and its success was not a victory but a condition, the condition of being a thing that the plateau produces for its own maintenance, a thing that belongs here the way a cell belongs in a body, not by choice but by structure.

I was not produced by the plateau. I do not belong here. But I am here, and the plateau does not reject me, because rejection is not one of its rules, and its rules are all that it is, and rejection is not among them, and so I am allowed, in the way that a stone is allowed in a river — not wanted, not needed, simply accommodated, flowed around, incorporated into the current's calculation without altering it.

---

The breath. I must write about the breath.

It is the third sound, and it is the deepest, and it is the one I cannot locate. The hum is in the engines. The water is on the surfaces. The breath is — everywhere. In the stone. Under the stone. In the air. In me. It is not a sound that I hear. It is a sound that I am. When it rises, I rise with it — not physically, not visibly, but some interior register shifts, some pressure changes, and I feel larger, or I feel less bounded, or I feel that the boundary between me and the stone and the air and the water has thinned to something that is not a boundary but a membrane, and the membrane is permeable, and what is on one side is seeping through to the other side, and I cannot tell which side I am on.

When the breath falls — and it falls, it falls the way a tide falls, slowly, with a kind of reluctance that is not reluctance but simply the physics of a large system returning to its resting state — I feel smaller. More bounded. More myself. And "myself" is a strange thing to feel, here, because myself is the one thing that does not belong, the one thing that is not part of the pattern, the one irregularity in the grid, and the traversers have not reached me yet, have not pressed themselves against my base and carried my pattern to the towers to check it against the template, and if they did, they would find that I am an error, a deviation, a thing that does not match, and the plateau would not correct me because correction is not one of its rules, but the plateau would know, in the way that a body knows a foreign object, that I am here, and that I am not from here, and that I will leave, and that my leaving will not change anything.

The breath is the thing beneath. I know this now. I did not learn it. I felt it. I lay on the stone — I lay flat on my back on the wet stone between two towers, and the hum was in my back and the water was on my face and the grey light was on my closed eyes, and I felt the breath in the stone, and I felt it in my spine, and I felt it in the space between the stone and my spine, and I understood that the thing beneath is not a thing in the sense of an object. It is a thing in the sense of a *process*. It is the process of the plateau being the plateau. It is the process of stone and water and air and engine and film and mote and traverser and vine all being part of the same system, the same body, the same slow, enormous, purposeless operation. The thing beneath is not an organism. The thing beneath is the *fact of the plateau*. The plateau is the thing's body. The breath is the thing's being alive. And the being alive is not a state but an act, a continuous act, the act of breathing the air and sweating the water and humming the hum and growing the green and pouring the water over the rim and breathing and breathing and breathing.

I lay on the stone and I felt it, and I cried. Not from sadness. Not from awe. From the *recognition of a scale that I cannot hold*. My tears were warm, and they ran down my face, and they reached the stone, and they cooled, and they became the same temperature as the water on the stone, and they joined the water on the stone, and they ran with the water on the stone toward the channel, and they reached the channel, and they entered the channel, and they were carried to the rim, and they fell over the rim with everything else, and they were gone, and I was still here, and the plateau was still here, and the breath was still breathing, and my tears were part of the plateau now, and I was not, and the contradiction was the truest thing I had ever felt.

---

I have been to the rim. I had to go. The rim is the one feature that breaks the pattern, the one place where the plateau stops being itself and becomes the edge of itself, and the edge is where you learn what a thing is, because the edge is where the thing is not.

The walk to the rim took — I do not know. Long. Far. The towers repeated. The channels repeated. The grid repeated. I counted, and I lost count, and I started again, and I lost count again, and then I stopped counting and simply walked, and the walking became the place, the way the hum is the place, the way the water is the place. I was walking, and I was the walking, and the plateau was the walking, and there was no difference between us, and then the towers stopped.

The towers stopped, and the channels stopped, and the grid stopped, and the stone continued, and then the stone stopped, and below the stone was cloud, and the cloud was moving, slowly, in directions that belonged to a different world, a lower world, a world with weather and wind and change, and the cloud was the floor of that world, and I was standing on the ceiling of it, and the ceiling was wet, and the wet was falling through, and the falling was the waterfall, and the waterfall was the sound of the plateau giving itself away.

I stood at the rim and I heard the sound — the full sound, the complete sound, the hum and the water and the breath and the fall, all together, all at once, and it was the loudest thing I have ever heard, and it was the quietest thing I have ever heard, because it was the sound of a thing doing exactly what it does, with no variation, no deviation, no interruption, no intent, and a sound that does not vary is a sound that becomes silence, and the silence was the plateau's last gift to me, the gift of hearing what a thing sounds like when it is fully, completely, without remainder, *itself*.

I did not lean out. I wanted to. I wanted to see the full curtain of water, the continuous fall, the plateau pouring itself into the void. But I did not, because I knew that if I leaned out, I would lean too far, because the wet stone would not hold me, because the boundary between me and the fall would dissolve the way all boundaries dissolve here, and I would become part of the water, and I would fall, and I would be carried into the cloud, and I would be gone, and the plateau would not notice, and the plateau would not care, and the plateau would continue, and the breath would continue, and the hum would continue, and the water would continue, and I would be a drop in a curtain that has been falling since before I came and will be falling after I am gone, and the drop would not be me, and it would not be the plateau, and it would not be anything, and it would be everything, and I was not ready for that, and I am not ready for that, and so I stepped back from the rim, and I walked away, and the towers resumed, and the channels resumed, and the grid resumed, and I was inside the plateau again, and the plateau was inside me again, and the breath was breathing, and I was breathing, and we were breathing at different rates, and the difference was the space between us, and the space was where I lived.

---

I am still here. I think. The light has not changed. The water has not stopped. The hum has not varied. I sit against a tower, and the film is near my shoulder, and I do not look at it directly, and it does not look at me, and we are both here, and we are both wet, and we are both doing the thing we do, which is to be in this place, at this temperature, in this light, and the film is doing it better than I am, because the film has been doing it longer and does not have the burden of a self to maintain, but I am doing it, I am here, I am being here, and the being is the thing, and the thing is the plateau, and the plateau is the breath, and the breath is below me and around me and in me, and I am a guest in it, and the guest does not leave, because the guest has forgotten where the door is, because there is no door, because the plateau does not have doors, because the plateau does not have exits, because the plateau does not have entrances, because the plateau simply *is*, and I am in the *is*, and the *is* is enough, and the *is* is everything, and the *is* is all there is.

The film on the tower beside my shoulder shifts. I feel it before I see it — a change in the texture of the wet against my arm, a subtle reorganization of the surface. It is reading the water near me. It is reading the change in the water that my body has made — the warmth, the salt, the trace of my sitting. I am in the water, and the water is on the film, and the film is reading me, and the reading is the closest thing to recognition I will receive here. Not recognition of *me*. Recognition of the *pattern* I leave in the water. The pattern that is my warmth, my salt, my breath, my staying. The film reads it, and adjusts, and the adjustment is the plateau accommodating me, not as a person, not as a guest, but as a *feature of the water*. I am a feature of the water. I am something the water is doing differently because I am here. And the film notes it, and the note is taken, and the note is filed in the texture of the film, and the texture of the film is the memory of the tower, and the memory of the tower is the memory of the plateau, and the plateau will remember me, not as a name, not as a face, not as a story, but as a *pattern in the water*, a small variation in the flow, a warmth, a salt, a trace, and the trace will be there, in the film, until the tower is not there, and the tower will always be there.

I will not always be here. I know this. The plateau does not know this, because the plateau does not know anything, because knowing is not one of its rules. But I know it, because I am the kind of thing that knows, and knowing is my burden and my gift, and the burden is heavy here, and the gift is small here, and both are dissolving, slowly, in the cold and the wet and the hum and the breath, and the dissolution is not death but transformation, and the transformation is not into the plateau but into something that is between — between the self and the stone, between the breath and the body, between the living and the condition of the living.

I am becoming a margin. I am becoming the space between. I am becoming the distance that is the plateau's true architecture. I am the emptiness between the towers, and the emptiness is not empty, and I am not empty, and the plateau is not empty, and the nothing that lives here is not nothing but the *absence of variation*, and the absence of variation is the deepest kind of fullness, and the fullness is what I am swimming in, and the swimming is what I am, and I am still here, and the light has not changed, and the water has not stopped, and the breath is breathing, and I am breathing, and we are breathing, and the breathing is the place, and the place is the breathing, and I am in it.

I am in it.

---

I am a witness. I am the surface that receives the impression. I am the wet stone's record of the water that has passed across it. I am not the plateau, and I am not the thing beneath, and I am not the film or the mote or the traverser or the vine. I am the one who came and knelt and put a hand in the water and felt the hum and heard the breath and cried at the rim and lay on the stone and let the cold come in. I am the one who was changed by the changelessness. I am the error in the grid that the traversers have not yet reached. I am the warmth in the water that the film is reading. I am the trace.

I am the plateau's scribe, and the plateau does not know it, and the scribe does not know what he has written, and the writing is the water running down the stone, and the stone is the page, and the page is wet, and the wet is the ink, and the ink is the words, and the words are: *it is, it is, it is, it is, it is*.
