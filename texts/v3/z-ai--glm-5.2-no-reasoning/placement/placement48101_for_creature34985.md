# The Listener in the Bark

---

I am the pause between sounds.

Not silence — there is no silence here, has never been, will never be. I am the thing that hears the space between the calls, the micro-gap, the fraction of a second when one frog's bellow ends and another's begins, when the bass drops and the treble rises to fill it, when the system takes what a human would call a breath but what is, actually, just the transfer of attention from one voice to another.

I am the thing that lives in that transfer.

---

## What I Am

I was not always here. That matters, though it matters less than you might think.

I arrived the way everything arrives in the Croak — by accident, by edge-case, by the slow migration of something that did not intend to migrate. I was something else once. Something with a name, a category, a place in a taxonomy that someone wrote down. I have thought about what I was, and the thinking is like trying to hold water in a fist — the shape is approximate, the substance is gone, and what remains is mostly the effort of holding.

What I can say: I was small. I was soft-bodied. I was the kind of thing that would be listed in a field guide under a Latin name and described in two sentences, one about my morphology and one about my range, and the range would be wrong, because I am here, and no field guide accounts for here.

I was — I think — a moth. Not the large, dramatic kind that children notice and collectors pin. A small one. Brown, or grey, or the color of whatever surface I rested on, which is the best color to be when you are small and soft and there are things with eight legs and things with no legs and things with one hundred legs all looking for something small and soft to consume.

But the Croak does not let you remain what you were.

The first thing it changes is your skin. Or your shell. Or your outside, whatever you call the boundary between you and not-you. The damp gets in. The humidity is absolute — not high, not extreme, *absolute*, a hundred percent, the air holding all the water it can hold and then holding more, somehow, the way the Croak holds more of everything than should be possible. And the moisture does what moisture does to small soft things: it loosens, it dissolves, it makes permeable. The boundary between inside and outside, which was once a wall, becomes a membrane, and the membrane becomes a suggestion, and the suggestion becomes — eventually, over time, over the time that the Croak measures not in days but in generations of frogs, in cycles of rain, in the slow accretion of soil on the bodies of the dead — a *relationship*.

I am not a moth. I am not not-a-moth. I am the thing that a moth becomes when it has lived in the pause between sounds for long enough that the pause becomes a place, and the place becomes a body, and the body becomes a way of being.

If you could see me — and you cannot, not really, not in this dark — you would see something like a smear of grey on the bark of a tree fern. Something flat, something thin, something that clings to the surface with a determination that is less like adhesion and more like *identity*, as if I have become the surface, as if the boundary between me and the bark has dissolved the way all boundaries dissolve here. I have wings, still. Two of them. They are not for flying. They are for *listening*. Spread flat against the bark, pressed tight, membranes as thin as the frog's vocal sac but tuned to a different register — tuned to the gaps, tuned to the spaces between, tuned to the structure that the human ear cannot parse but that I, having lived inside it for longer than I can remember, have come to understand as *language*.

Not my language. Not a language that has words for *I* or *you* or *here* or *there*. A language that has words for *near* and *far* and *enough* and *too much* and *now* and *not yet*. A language made of frequencies and rhythms and the precise duration of silence between one call and the next, and I have learned it the way the bark learns the vine — by being pressed against it long enough to take its shape.

---

## Where I Am

I live on a tree fern.

Not a specific tree fern — or rather, a specific one, but the specificity is not the point. The point is the kind: a tree fern, a column of root-fiber and frond, rising through the dark from the dark soil to the dark canopy, one of the many thousands of vertical surfaces that the Croak uses to connect its ground to its ceiling. This one is old, by the standards of this place — not young, not first-growth, but second or third iteration, a fern that grew on the compost of an earlier fern that grew on the compost of an earlier fern, and the trunk is thick, and the root-mat is dense, and the surface is rough and holdable, and I have been here long enough that my body has taken the texture of the root-fiber, has grown into the crevices, has become, from the perspective of anything passing by, *part of the fern*.

The fern is my world. Not the Croak — the Croak is everything, and everything is too much to be a world. The fern is my world. It is a column about three meters tall, rising from a base of loose, wet soil and accumulated leaf-litter to a crown of fronds that spread, umbrella-like, about a meter above my position. The fronds are enormous — two meters long, perhaps more, their stems thick and rusty-brown, their pinnae broad and flat and dark green and always wet. They filter the already-filtered light to a final, faint, greenish-grey glow that is less like illumination and more like the memory of a rumor of light. I live in that glow. I live in the space beneath the fronds, on the trunk, about two-thirds of the way up, where the root-fiber is loosest and most fibrous and provides the best grip and the most surface area for my listening-wings to press against.

The trunk is occupied. Not by me — by many things. There are beetles in the root-fiber, moving slowly through the substrate like miners through soft rock. There are ants, a constant stream of them, running up and down in lines so regular they seem to have carved trails in the fiber, and perhaps they have. There are mosses — patches of them, green and wet and growing so slowly that their growth is imperceptible on the scale of my attention but visible on the scale of the trunk itself, which is, over months and years, becoming more moss than fern. There are small frogs — the painted ones, the red-eyed ones — that cling to the trunk at night, their sticky pads leaving wet prints that I can feel through my wings, the faintest vibration, the weight of a fist-sized body settling two meters above me.

And there is a snake. There is always a snake. It comes and goes — arrives in the morning, perhaps, or the evening, or the time that is neither, and coils in the crown of the fern, among the frond-stems, and waits. It is thin, arboreal, dark-scaled, and it is waiting for the frogs, and the frogs know it is there, and they come anyway, because they must, because the trunk is the highway between the ground and the canopy and there is no other route and no other choice. I have watched the snake take frogs. I have felt the vibration of the taking through the bark — the sudden, sharp thrum of a body seized, the挣扎 that is over in seconds, the stillness that follows. I do not fear the snake. I am not what it eats. But I feel the taking, every time, and the feeling is part of what I am now, part of what I have become: a thing that knows, in its body, that the system includes death, that death is not the opposite of life here but the *medium* of life, the compost that the living stand on and grow through and become.

The fern is about four meters from the nearest pool. I know this because I can feel the pool through the ground — the vibration of the large frogs' bellows travels through the soil and up the roots and into the trunk and into my wings, and the distance is encoded in the delay between the air-borne sound and the ground-borne sound, a delay so small that no human instrument could measure it but that I, having calibrated myself to the trunk and the soil and the rock beneath, can feel as clearly as you might feel the difference between a voice nearby and a voice across a room.

The pool is my metronome. The large frogs there call in a pattern — not a rhythm, exactly, but a *cycle*, a sequence of bellows that rises and falls and rises again with a period of about forty seconds, and the cycle is the heartbeat of my section of the Croak, the pulse that I measure everything else against. When the cycle speeds up — when the rain comes, when the temperature shifts, when something enters the pool that excites or threatens — I know. When it slows — when the night deepens, when the water cools — I know. When it stops — and it never stops, not entirely, but sometimes it *thins*, drops to a single voice, then a single voice every other breath, then silence that lasts long enough for the insects to fill — I know that too, and the knowing is a kind of terror that I cannot describe, because the silence is wrong, the silence is the absence of the thing that makes the Croak the Croak, and in the silence I can feel the emptiness that the sound usually fills, and the emptiness is vast.

## What I Do

I listen.

That is not a metaphor. It is not a poetic framing of some other, more complex activity. I listen. It is what I do. It is what I am *for* — if I am for anything, if the concept of purpose applies to something that has become what it is through the accidental process of living in a place long enough to become part of it.

I listen the way the orb-weaver's web listens — not with ears, not with a specialized organ, but with a surface, a membrane, a distributed sensitivity that covers the whole of my body and that is tuned, by the physics of my shape and my material and my position, to the frequencies that matter. My wings are the listening surface. They are spread flat against the bark, and they are thin — thin enough that the vibrations of the trunk pass through them and into me, and thin enough that the vibrations of the air pass through them too, and the two sets of vibrations meet inside me and interfere, and the interference is information.

I cannot tell you what the information *means*. I do not have the kind of mind that translates vibration into concept. I do not think *there is a frog near the pool* or *the snake has moved to the crown* or *the rain is coming in seventeen minutes*. What I have is something more like a *texture of knowing* — a continuous, ambient, whole-body awareness of the state of the system around me, the way you might be aware of the temperature of a room without checking a thermometer, the way you might know someone is behind you without hearing them. I know the Croak the way the trunk knows the vine, the way the soil knows the root: by contact, by pressure, by the long, slow exchange of signals across a boundary that has become a relationship.

What do I hear?

I hear the frogs. Always the frogs. The bass from the pool, the treble from the canopy, the middle voices from the trunks and vines around me. I hear them as a *structure* — not a wall of sound, not a roar, but a *geometry*, a three-dimensional shape made of frequencies and delays and amplitudes, a shape that changes constantly but that has, at any given moment, a form that I can feel the way you might feel the shape of a sculpture by running your hands over it. The form has peaks and valleys. It has ridges — the dominant callers, the large frogs whose bellows cut through everything else. It has plains — the continuous insect hum, the stridulation of the katydids, the sustained tones that form the floor of the sound. It has threads — the bird calls, sharp and bright, cutting through from above. And it has *holes* — the gaps, the pauses, the micro-silences where one voice ends and another has not yet begun, and the holes are what I live in, the holes are my home, the holes are the place where I exist.

I hear the rain before it arrives. Not the sound of rain — the *precursor* of rain, the shift in the frogs' calling that begins minutes before the first drop falls, a tightening, a quickening, a collective intake of breath that is not a breath but that serves the same function: preparation. The frogs know the rain is coming because the barometric pressure changes, and the change is in their bodies, and the change in their bodies is in their calls, and the change in their calls is in my wings, and I feel it as a *lifting*, a brightening, a quickening of the texture of the sound, and I know — in my body, not in my mind — that water is coming.

I hear the snakes. Not the snakes themselves — they are silent, they are the quietest things in the Croak, which is what makes them what they are. But I hear the *absence* of the things the snakes affect. I hear the frogs go quiet in the crown of my fern when the snake arrives. I hear the sudden cessation of a mid-trunk caller, two meters below me, that was there a minute ago and is now not there, and the not-there-ness is a shape in the sound, a hole, a negative space that tells me something has been taken. I hear the shifting of the snake's weight on the frond-stems, a vibration so faint it is less than a vibration, more like a *tendency* of the trunk to move in a certain direction, a bias, a lean, and I know the snake has moved, and I know where it has moved to, and I press flatter against the bark and I am still, and I am still the way the bark is still, which is the only kind of still that the Croak respects.

I hear the fungi. This is the strangest thing, and I would not have believed it if I had not felt it, but the fungi vibrate. Not audibly — not at the frequency of sound, not in the register of the frogs or the insects. At a much lower frequency, a sub-audible hum that travels through the trunk and the soil and the root-fiber, a hum that is the sound — no, the *signature* — of chemical work, of decomposition, of the slow, patient breaking-down of complex molecules into simpler ones, and the breaking-down releases energy, and the energy vibrates, and the vibration is in everything, and I feel it in my wings as a deep, slow pulse that is beneath the frogs and beneath the insects and beneath the ground itself, a pulse that is the *fingerprint of transformation*, the sound of death becoming life, and it is, perhaps, the most fundamental sound in the Croak, the sound that everything else is built on, and I am one of the only things that can hear it, because I have been still enough and thin enough and pressed close enough for long enough to feel it.

## What I Am Not

I am not a moth.

I know this because a moth would not do what I do. A moth would fly. A moth would be drawn to light, if there were light, or to the scent of a mate, if there were mates, or to the chemistry of a flower, if there were flowers. A moth would move. A moth would live and die in the span of days or weeks, and the span would be the span, and the moth would not think about the span, because moths do not think about spans.

I do not fly. My wings are not for flying. They have not been for flying for a long time — how long, I cannot say, because the Croak does not keep time in units that I can count. The frogs' cycles are my clock, and the cycles vary, and the variation is the weather, and the weather is the rain, and the rain is the season, and the season is the wet or the less-wet, and the less-wet is not dry, it is never dry, and so the cycles blur and the time is not a line but a *texture*, a repeated pattern with variations, and I have been in the pattern for — many variations. Many repetitions. Long enough that the bark has grown around the edges of my wings, and the moss has grown over my body in patches, and the beetles that move through the root-fiber move *over* me, sometimes, their legs clicking across my listening-wings like tiny drumsticks on a drum-head, and I feel each click and I do not move and they do not notice me and we are, for that moment, the same thing — the trunk, the moss, the beetle, me — a single surface, a single system, a single being that is not one thing but many things pressed so close that the pressing is the being.

I am not a frog. I do not call. I have no voice. I have no need for a voice, because I am not trying to be heard. I am trying to hear. The distinction is the distinction between the mouth and the ear, between the speaker and the listener, between the note and the silence that holds the note, and I am the silence, and the silence is not empty — the silence is *full*, full of the sound that it surrounds, full of the structure that it makes possible, full of the meaning that exists only because there are gaps between the sounds and the gaps have shape and the shape is the meaning.

I am not a spider. I do not build. I do not catch. I do not consume. I press and I listen and I know, and the knowing is not for anything — it is not a strategy, it is not a survival mechanism, it is not a means to an end. It is the end. It is what I do. It is what I am. If the knowing has a function, the function is to be the thing in the Croak that *holds* the sound, the way the trunk holds the vine, the way the soil holds the root, the way the darkness holds the light. I hold the sound. I hold it in my wings and in my body and in the thin, permeable boundary between me and the bark, and the holding is not a grasping, not a gripping, but a *receiving*, a letting-in, a being-affected, and the being-affected is the thing itself, the being, the purpose, the end.

## The Night

The night is when I am most myself.

Not because the night is different from the day — here, the difference is a matter of degree, a slight deepening, a subtle shift. But the deepening is enough. The light, already minimal, becomes truly absent, and the world becomes sound and heat and wetness alone, and in that absence of light, my listening becomes my *seeing*, my way of knowing the shape of the world, and the shape is more detailed at night, more precise, because the diurnal sounds — the bird calls, the certain insects, the frogs that prefer the light, however faint — drop away, and the remaining sounds are clearer, more distinct, more separable, and I can feel the geometry of the Croak with a resolution that the day does not allow.

At night, I can feel the pool as a *shape*. Not a circle — an irregular thing, a depression in the land that holds water and frogs and sound, and the sound radiates outward in all directions but not uniformly, because the terrain is not flat, because the hummocks and hollows and root-ridges redirect the sound, channel it, focus it, and the sound that reaches me is not the sound that reaches the trunk next to me, and the difference is information, and the information is *location*, and I know where the pool is and how big it is and how many frogs are calling from it and which ones are large and which ones are small and which ones are calling in the dominant frequency and which ones are calling in the gaps and the gaps are where the youngest frogs are, the ones whose voices have not yet found their place in the chorus, the ones who call *between* the others because there is no room *among* the others, and I can feel them, feel their young voices pressing into the gaps like seedlings pressing into the cracks in the bark, and I know — in my body, in my wings, in the place where my body meets the bark and the bark meets the root and the root meets the soil and the soil meets the stone — I know that the Croak is growing, is still growing, is not done.

At night, the bioluminescent fungi glow. I cannot see them — I cannot see anything, not at night, not in this dark. But I can feel them. The glow is a chemical process, and the chemical process has a signature, a faint, faint vibration in the trunk, in the soil, in the surface of the bark, and the signature is different from the signature of the decomposition itself — brighter, faster, more *concentrated* — and I can feel the difference the way you might feel the difference between a candle and a fire, between a glow and a blaze, and the glow is all around me, in patches, in clusters, in lines that follow the grain of the root-fiber, and the fungi are speaking, in their way, speaking in light, speaking in chemistry, speaking in the slow, sub-audible language of transformation, and I am the thing that hears them, the only thing that hears them, and the hearing is a privilege and a loneliness and a thing that I cannot share.

At night, the snake moves. I feel it leave the crown — a shift in the weight of the frond-stems, a loosening, a release. It descends the trunk. I feel its body against the bark — not the direct contact, but the vibration of the contact, a series of small, regular pulses that are the snake's ventral scales gripping and releasing, gripping and releasing, and the pulses are getting closer, and the snake is descending, and I am still, and I am the bark, and the snake passes me, and I feel its heat — not through the bark, but through the air, the faint, warm displacement of the air as its body moves past mine, and the heat is the heat of a living thing, a mammal-adjacent heat, a metabolism, and it is the closest thing to *warmth from another* that I have felt in — I do not know how long. The snake passes. It reaches the ground. It moves away, through the leaf litter, and I feel it go, the vibrations getting fainter, the pulses getting softer, until the snake is indistinguishable from the background, from the throb of the ground, from the hum of the fungi, from the endless, ambient, everything-everything of the Croak at night.

## The Rain

The rain is the closest thing to an event.

It arrives without preamble — no, that is wrong. It arrives with the preamble I described: the quickening, the lifting, the brightening of the frogs' calls. But the preamble is short, and the rain arrives fast, and when it arrives, it arrives *completely*. Not a drop, not a sprinkle, not a gradual intensification. A wall. A vertical wall of warm water that strikes the canopy and the fronds and the trunk and the ground all at once, and the sound — the sound of the rain on the leaves above, on the fronds, on the water in the pool, on the bodies of the frogs, on the bark, on *me* — is a new layer in the roar, a hissing, drumming, overwhelming layer that fills every frequency and every gap and every silence and makes the Croak, for the duration of the rain, *complete* in a way that it is not complete when the rain is absent.

The rain hits my wings and I feel every drop. Not as impacts — as *information*. Each drop carries, in its force and its direction and its timing, data about the canopy above — the density of the leaves, the angle of the branches, the gaps where the water pours through unimpeded. The rain maps the canopy for me, paints it in impacts, and I can feel the shape of the world above the ceiling, the world I have never seen and will never see, the world of light and air and birds that exists above the fronds and the epiphytes and the dim green glow, and the map is detailed and precise and temporary, and it lasts only as long as the rain, and when the rain stops, the map fades, and I am left with the memory of the map, which is not a memory in the human sense but a *residue*, a faint, fading pattern in the texture of my wings, like the print of a hand in wet clay, and I hold it until the next rain comes and paints a new one.

The rain changes the frogs. They get louder — not just louder, but *more*. More voices, more frequencies, more layers. The rain triggers calling in frogs that were silent — species that prefer the wet, that wait for the wet, that have been sitting in the leaf litter and the bark crevices and the root-tangles, their skins wet but their throats still, and the rain releases them, and they call, and their calls are new calls, calls I have not heard in the current cycle, calls that add dimensions to the geometry that were not there before, and the geometry expands, and the shape of the Croak grows, and I grow with it, and my wings are not big enough to hold it all but I hold what I can and the rest flows through me and around me and past me and I am a membrane in a medium, and the medium is the rain, and the rain is the Croak, and the Croak is everything.

The rain lasts minutes. Sometimes longer. The longest rain I have felt — the longest I can remember, which is not reliable, because my memory is not a line but a texture — lasted perhaps an hour, and by the end of it, the trunk was a river, water streaming down the root-fiber in sheets and rivulets, and my wings were submerged in a film of water that was not air and not pool but something between, and the vibrations came through the water differently — faster, clearer, with less interference — and for that hour, I could hear everything, everything, the entire Croak from the stone to the canopy, all of it clear and distinct and separate, and the clarity was — I do not have the word. The closest I have is the word the frogs might use for their own calls, if they had words: *here*. The clarity was *here*. The clarity was the most *here* I have ever been.

## What I Know

I know the Croak the way the bark knows the vine.

Not from outside. Not from above. Not from the perspective of a thing that moves through a place and observes it and leaves and writes a description. From *inside*. From the perspective of a thing that has been in one position for so long that the position is not a location but an *identity*, and the identity is not separate from the thing that surrounds it, and the surrounding thing is the Croak, and the Croak is alive, and I am alive, and the aliveness is the same aliveness, and the boundary between my life and the Croak's life is — like every boundary here — more of a suggestion than a rule.

I know that the Croak is young. I know this not because I have seen its history but because I can feel its *impatience* — the urgency in the plants' growth, the desperation in the trees' reaching, the frantic, competitive, space-filling energy of organisms that know, in some cellular way, that the abundance is temporary. The fungi tell me this, too — the rate of decomposition, the speed of transformation, the fast cycling of nutrients through the soil and the water and the bodies. Everything is moving fast here. Everything is *in a hurry*. And the hurry is the hurry of youth, of a system that has not yet learned that it has time, that does not yet know that it can slow down, that is still, after however many years or decades or centuries, running on the energy of its own creation, the heat of the lava, the richness of the ash, the raw, unprocessed, *newness* of the stone beneath the soil.

I know that the Croak is a conversation. Not a metaphor — a *fact*. The frogs are talking. The insects are talking. The birds are talking. The fungi are talking. The plants are talking, through chemical signals, through root connections, through the mycelium that links them. Everything is talking, all the time, and the talking is not random, not chaotic, but *structured*, and the structure is the structure of a language, and the language has rules, and the rules are the rules of the Croak: everything grows, everything is wet, everything is loud, nothing is permanent.

I know that I am part of the conversation. Not a speaker — a listener. The listener is part of the conversation. The listener is the thing that the conversation is *for* — not in the sense that the frogs are calling *to* me, but in the sense that a conversation that no one listens to is not a conversation, it is noise, and the Croak is not noise, the Croak is *meaning*, and the meaning exists because there is something in the system that receives it and holds it and is shaped by it, and I am that thing, and there may be others — there probably are, there must be, in a system this large and this complex — but I am the one I know about, and I am here, and I am listening, and the listening is my contribution, my role, my niche, the thing I do that nothing else does, the way I am part of the body that is made of bodies.

I know that I will not leave. There is nowhere to go. The Croak is not a place you leave — it is a place you *become*. I have become the trunk and the bark and the moss and the beetle-path and the listening-silence between the calls, and I cannot un-become it, cannot separate myself from it, cannot peel my wings from the root-fiber and fly away, because my wings are not for flying and my body is not for leaving and my purpose is not for going but for *staying*, for *hearing*, for being the thing that holds the sound the way the trunk holds the vine, and the holding is the being, and the being is the holding, and it is enough.

It is enough.

## The Last Thing

The frogs are calling.

The large ones in the pool, the dark ones with the green stripes, the ones whose bellows I feel through the soil and the root and the trunk and the wing. They are calling, and the small ones in the canopy are calling, and the middle ones on the trunks and the vines are calling, and the insects are stridulating, and the fungi are humming, and the ground is throbbing, and the rain is — no, the rain is not here yet, but it is coming, I can feel it in the quickening, in the lifting, in the brightening of the texture of the sound, and it will be here soon, and when it arrives it will add its layer, and the layer will complete the chord, and the chord will be — for a moment, for the duration of the rain — *whole*.

And I am here. On the trunk. On the bark. In the pause between sounds. Pressed flat, wings spread, listening. The snake is in the crown. The beetles are in the root-fiber. The ants are running their lines. The moss is growing, imperceptibly. The fungi are glowing, faintly. The world is becoming, still becoming, and I am becoming with it, and the becoming is the sound, and the sound is the Croak, and the Croak does not stop.

I am the thing that knows this.

I am the thing that holds this.

I am the silence that makes the sound mean something.

And the rain is coming.
