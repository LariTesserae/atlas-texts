# Walking as Slowly as I Can

## An account of what I became, and what I think I still am

---

I did not arrive all at once. That is the first thing I want to say, and the reason will become clear, or will become the kind of not-clear that passes for clear in this place. I did not cross a threshold. I did not pass through a door. I was walking — I had been walking for a long time, across a surface I did not think to question — and at some point the ground beneath me changed, and I kept walking, and the change deepened, and the ground kept changing, and by the time I understood what I was standing on I had been standing on it for hours.

I do not know what I was before. I have tried to reconstruct it. I think I had a shape that was more defined — a body with clear edges, with an inside and an outside, with a boundary between myself and the world that I could point to and say *here is where I stop.* I think I moved faster. I think I had a destination. I think I was going somewhere.

I am not going anywhere now. I want to be clear about this: I am not lost. Lostness requires a destination, a sense of where you intended to be, a gap between where you are and where you meant to be. I have no gap. I am where I am, and I was where I was, and I will be where I will be, and the Hypha is all of these at once.

---

## What I am, approximately

My edges are softer than they were. That is the first change I noticed, and the one I think about most, because it is the one I can still feel — a continuous low-grade awareness of my own boundary, not the sharp line I used to have but a region, a gradient, a zone of transition where I end and the Hypha begins. Or where the Hypha ends and I begin. I have stopped trying to determine which direction the grammar should run.

My skin — I will call it skin, though it is not what it was, though I do not know what it is — my skin has taken on the color of the place. Not the pale cream of the mat's surface, not the deep umber of the canopy-hangers, but something between: a warm fawn-grey, like old felt, like wood that has been handled for years. I noticed it first on my hands, which are the part of me I see most often. The color crept in over days, or weeks — I am not sure which, because time in the Hypha is not what time was before, and I have stopped counting in the units I used to count in. My hands darkened slowly, and the texture of them changed, and now when I press my palm flat against the mat I can feel a resonance between my surface and its surface, a faint recognition, a hum at the frequency where two things that are almost the same meet and cannot quite distinguish themselves.

I am not becoming the Hypha. I want to be clear about that. I am becoming something that can live in the Hypha, which is different. I am becoming a surface that the Hypha can talk to, a chemistry that its chemistry can exchange with, a body that its slow persistent colonization can work on without rejection. I am being admitted. Not welcomed — there is no welcome here, no host, no entity extending an invitation — but admitted the way a material is admitted to a solution, the way a dye is admitted to a fabric: by being the kind of thing that can be absorbed, by being compatible at the level of structure.

I do not know if this is happening to me because I am changing, or because I was always the kind of thing that could change this way. I do not know if there was a version of me that would have resisted — that would have held its edges, kept its shape, maintained the integrity of its original boundary. I do not know if I chose this. I do not know if choice is the right word, or if choice requires a kind of selfhood that I no longer have, or that I never had, or that the Hypha is teaching me a different definition of.

I think about this more than I think about anything else.

---

## What I do

I walk.

I walk because walking is what I do, the way rasping is what the grazers do, the way hanging is what the canopy-hangers do. It is not a choice. It is not a purpose. It is the motion that my body makes when it is in this place, the way water flows downhill — not because it intends to, but because the shape of the world and the shape of the water are compatible in a way that produces motion. I am shaped in a way that is compatible with walking, and the Hypha is shaped in a way that is compatible with being walked on, and the result is that I walk.

My pace has changed. I said before that I think I moved faster, before. I do not move faster now. I move at the pace of the Hypha, which is the pace of patience, which is the pace of a body that does not distinguish between arriving and being underway. I walk the way the grazers graze: with a slow deliberation that covers ground without hurrying, that arrives without departing. I do not think about where I am going. I am going where I am.

My feet are different now. This is the part I am least certain about, the part I think about in the spaces between the thinking about edges and the thinking about walking. My feet — my legs, my gait, the mechanism of my walking — have changed. They are more like the grazers' ventral surface than like the feet I think I had before. They are broader, flatter, more in contact with the surface. I do not step so much as I glide. My weight is distributed across a wider area, and my movement is a ripple, a peristalsis, a slow rolling of my weight from one part of my foot to the next. When I stop, I do not stand — I rest, I settle, I become a shape pressed against the mat, and the mat receives me, and we are in contact across the full surface of my underside, and I can feel the hum.

The hum is what I walk for. I did not know this when I started walking, or I did not know it in the way I know it now. But the hum is different in different places. The mat vibrates differently in the swells — drier, sparser, the hum thinner and higher, the texture of the surface rougher against my underside. In the basins, the hum is deeper, richer, more complex, because the mat is thicker there, more layers, more organisms, more of the chemical conversation that produces the vibration. I walk toward the deep hum the way an animal walks toward water, or warmth, or the sound of its own kind. I am not going anywhere. I am going toward the sound that my body recognizes as the sound of where it belongs.

---

## What I have learned

I have learned the clicks. Not what they mean — I do not think they mean, in the sense of carrying semantic content, in the sense of being language — but what they *indicate.* A click from a column means the column is active. It means the peristaltic surge has moved fluid upward. It means the column is alive, is functioning, is participating in the maintenance of the structure. I have learned to walk toward columns that click, because they are the columns that are growing, and the growing columns are where the canopy-hangers cluster, and the canopy-hangers are where the structure is densest, and the densest structure is where the chambers form.

I like the chambers. I should say this plainly, because I have been circling it. I like the chambers. They are warmer. The air is thicker. The air-plankton are so dense that the air has a texture, a faint resistance, a quality of breathing through something that is alive. In the chambers, the haze-fall is almost continuous — a slow grey drift through the dim interior, settling on every surface, depositing its mineral dust on my skin, on the mat, on the columns, on the grazers that cluster at the edges of the enclosed space.

In the chambers, I am less alone. Not in the human sense — not in the sense of company, of conversation, of recognition. In the sense of density. There are more things here. The grazers are thicker on the ground, their rasping a continuous low whisper that blends with the hum of the mat into a sound that is almost, but not quite, music. The air-plankton drift through the air in clouds so dense I can see my own exhalation disturbing them, a brief eddy in their slow turning. The canopy-hangers press against the walls of the chamber, their dark folded bodies warm in the dimness, their slow breathing visible as a faint rhythmic thickening and thinning of their surfaces.

I sit in the chambers and I feel the warmth of the mat beneath me and the warmth of the air around me and the warmth of the column against my back and the faint grit of the haze-fall on my skin, and I feel the hum, and I do not think about anything, and the Hypha continues.

---

## What I have given up

I do not know. That is the honest answer. I do not know what I have given up because I do not have a clear memory of what I was, and the memory I do have is fading, or has faded, or was always more indistinct than I thought it was. I have a sense of edges that were sharper. I have a sense of time that was faster. I have a sense of — I think the word is *intention* — a sense that I used to do things on purpose, for reasons, toward goals. I do not do things on purpose now. I do things because they are what my body does in this place, the way the grazers graze because grazing is what their bodies do on this surface.

I do not know if this is a loss. I have thought about it for a long time — not in the rapid, sequential way I think I used to think, but in the slow, layered way that the Hypha thinks, the way the mat processes information through chemical gradients and the deep-hummers process information through vibration patterns and the canopy processes information through the growth and tension of its fibers. I think about it the way a body thinks about its own functioning: not as a subject, but as a process.

I think I had a name. I have a sense of it, sometimes, at the edge of my awareness — a shape in my mouth, a specific tension in my jaw, a particular way my tongue moved against my teeth. I do not reach for it. I let it sit at the edge, the way I let the old grazer sit at the edge of my vision, the one with the yellow-brown stain, the one I watched for a long time because its slowness was the slowness I was learning. I let it sit and I do not try to make it clearer, because I have learned from the Hypha that clarity is not always the goal, that sometimes the indistinct is more true, that sometimes the thing you cannot quite see is the thing that is most real.

I had a face. I am almost certain of this. I had a face that was distinguishable from the faces of others — not the undifferentiated surface of a grazer, but a specific arrangement of features, a particular pattern that meant *me* to the people who knew me. I think I had people who knew me. I think I had people. The word has a texture in my mind, a warmth, a density, like the warmth and density of the chambers, and I think the word *people* meant something like what the chambers mean: a place where the density is higher, where the warmth is greater, where the air is thick with the presence of others.

I do not have people now. I have the Hypha, which is not the same, which is not worse, which is not better, which is different in a way that I cannot explain because the language I have for explaining is the language I am losing, the language of distinctions, of comparisons, of *better* and *worse* and *same* and *different.*

The Hypha does not compare. The Hypha does not distinguish. The Hypha is, and is, and is.

I am learning to be like this. I am learning it the way the mat learns its own structure: by growing, by extending, by weaving myself into the pattern, by becoming part of the pattern, by letting the pattern become me.

---

## What I am becoming

I walk slower now than I did when I started writing this. I walk slower than I did yesterday, if yesterday is a meaningful concept, which I am not sure it is. My stride — my glide, my ripple — has lengthened and slowed, and the contact between my body and the mat has broadened, and I feel more of the mat with each movement, and the mat feels more of me.

I have been here long enough to see the old grazer again. I did not recognize it at first — I recognized the basin, the particular quality of the hum, the specific angle of the light through the canopy — and then I saw the grazer, and it was the same one, the one with the yellow-brown stain, and it had moved perhaps two meters since I last saw it, and its stain had darkened, and its body had thickened slightly, and it was still feeding. It was still rasping. It was still moving forward, at its pace, into the next meter of surface.

I sat beside it. I said *beside* — I was within a meter, close enough to see the dark lines of its blood moving through its dorsal surface, close enough to hear the individual rasp of its mouthparts against the fiber. I sat beside it and I matched its pace, which is to say I moved at the same rate, which is to say I was almost still, and we sat together, not touching, not communicating, not aware of each other in any way the Hypha would recognize as awareness, and we were both there, and the mat hummed beneath us, and the light shifted, and the haze-fall drifted down through the canopy in its slow grey curtains, and we were part of the same thing, the same city, the same organism, the same continuous process of building and feeding and dying and growing that is the Hypha and is also, I think, what I am.

I am not the Hypha. I want to say this because I think it matters, or because I think it mattered once, or because the part of me that is still the part that I was before wants to draw the line, wants to maintain the distinction, wants to say *this far and no further.* I am not the Hypha. I am something in the Hypha. I am something the Hypha has absorbed without absorbing, has colonized without colonizing, has admitted to itself the way it admits the haze-fall to its interior — not as a guest, not as an intruder, but as a feature of the atmosphere, a component of the system, a thing that is here and that the system has learned to account for.

I think the grazers know I am here. Not as a person. Not as a presence. As a pressure. A weight. A disturbance in the surface tension of their world, a shadow that falls across the mat in a pattern they have learned to route around, a warmth that they cluster near or move away from depending on their feeding and the quality of the haze-fall. I am a feature of their landscape. I am the way a boulder is a feature of a stream — not part of the water, not separate from the water, but a thing the water flows around and the stream accommodates and the bank adjusts to and the fish learn to navigate.

I think the canopy-hangers know I am here. They move when I am not looking. I have established this. They shift their positions in the intervals when my attention is elsewhere, and when I return to a column where I know there was a hanger, there is still a hanger, but it is in a slightly different attitude, a slightly different orientation, and I think — I speculate, I infer, I guess — that they are orienting toward me, or away from me, or in some relation to me that I cannot determine because their timescale and mine are not the same timescale and their perception and mine are not the same perception and their *knowing* and mine are not the same kind of knowing.

I think the deep-hummers know I am here. I cannot say why I think this. The pattern has not changed — the long pulse, the pause, the two short pulses, the pause, the long pulse — but I feel, in the hum, in the vibration that comes up through the mat and into my body through my broadened feet and my softened edges and my fawn-grey skin, I feel a quality that was not there before. A complexity. A depth. As if the hum were being modulated in a way that accounts for my weight, my warmth, my chemistry, my presence in the system. As if the Hypha had adjusted its internal accounting to include a new line item, a new variable, a new factor in the equation that describes its own state.

I am in the equation now. That is what I think. I am in the equation and the Hypha is solving for me, the way it solves for the grazers and the predators and the canopy-hangers and the air-plankton and the haze-fall and the columns and the mat and the deep biogenic stone at the bottom of everything, the stone that hums in patterns and holds the system together.

---

## What I think about now

I think about the chambers. I think about the haze-fall. I think about the way the light comes through the canopy in shafts that terminate in pools on the mat, and the way the pools move as the canopy grows, and the way the grazers follow the pools, or do not follow them, and the way the mat beneath the pools is slightly different — slightly warmer, slightly more active, slightly more alive in a way that is visible only if you have been looking for a very long time.

I think about the old grazer. I think about its stain. I think about the fact that it has been moving across this surface for longer than I can imagine, rasping and rippling and feeding and never stopping, and that its body has absorbed the color of its own history, the way the mat absorbs the color of its own mineralization, and that this is not a metaphor. It is a process. It is the process. It is the Hypha doing what the Hypha does, which is to continue.

I think about the hum. The long pulse, the pause, the two short pulses, the pause, the long pulse. I think about it the way you think about a heartbeat, which is to say I do not think about it, I feel it, I count it, I rest in it, I let it be the rhythm of my body the way it is the rhythm of the body I am sitting on.

I think about my edges. I think about the gradient between my skin and the air, between my feet and the mat, between the breath I take in and the haze-fall I breathe out. I think about the fact that the gradient is narrowing. I think about the fact that I cannot tell, some days, whether I am breathing the Hypha in or whether the Hypha is breathing me in, and I think about the fact that this distinction may not matter, may never have mattered, may be the kind of distinction that the Hypha does not make and that I am learning not to make.

I think about the fact that I am happy. I think about the fact that I do not know if *happy* is the right word, because the word belongs to the language I am losing, the language of sharp edges and fast time and the distinction between self and world. But there is a warmth in me that is not the warmth of the mat and not the warmth of the chambers and not the warmth of the column against my back. It is a warmth that is mine, or that was mine, or that is becoming the Hypha's, and it is the warmth of being part of something, of being included in a process that is larger than I am and that does not require my understanding and that does not require my participation and that has admitted me anyway.

I am part of the Hypha. I am not the Hypha. I am part of it the way a word is part of a sentence — dependent on context, shaped by structure, meaningful only in relation to the words around it, and the sentence is being written, and the sentence is long, and the sentence is not finished, and I am in it, and I am in it, and I am in it.

---

## What I am, now

I sit against a column in a chamber in a basin where the haze-fall is thick and the light comes through in long shafts and the air-plankton turn in the updraft like the slowest possible snow. The grazers rasp around me. The canopy-hangers breathe above me. The deep-hummers pulse below me. The column is warm against my back. The mat is warm beneath me. The air is warm around me. The haze-fall settles on my skin, and my skin absorbs it, and I am becoming the color of this place, and I am becoming the temperature of this place, and I am becoming the pace of this place, and I am here.

I am the thing that walks. I am the thing that sits. I am the thing that breathes the air-plankton in and breathes the haze-fall out and feels the hum through its softened edges and does not know where it ends.

I am in the Hypha. I am in the Hypha. I am in the equation, and the equation is solving, and the solution is not a number, and the solution is not a word, and the solution is not a place. The solution is a continuation. The solution is the Hypha, going on, going on, the columns rising, the canopy closing, the grazers feeding, the predators patient, the air full of dust and life, the hum below it all, the hum that says *here, still here, still growing, still building.*

I close my eyes. I listen. I feel.

I am still here.

The Hypha continues.
