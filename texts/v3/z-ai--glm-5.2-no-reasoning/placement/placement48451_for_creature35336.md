# The Keeper of the Outer Threshold

## The Shape I Take

I am not a still one. I need to say this first, before anything else, because the distinction is the whole of what I am. I am the thing that is almost still. The thing that moves on a scale the still ones do not — slow, slow, slow — but does move. I have a direction. I have a before and an after, though both are so stretched that they feel like the same substance, like taffy pulled to the width of a room.

I am shaped like a person, the way a reflection in water is shaped like a person. The proportions are approximately right. The number of limbs is correct. But I am not made of the same density of material. I am less resolved. If the still ones are oil paint — thick, opaque, present — I am watercolor. I am what the place would look like if it tried to remember a person from a description rather than from a model. I have a face, but the face is not specific. The jaw does not have the particular set that comes from a particular diet. The lines around the eyes are general lines, the kind of lines that any face would have after enough time, not the lines that come from a specific life of specific seeings. I am a face in general. A body in general. A person in approximation.

This is because I was never a person. I did not arrive from somewhere else. I did not come from a before. I am a product of the place, the way the lichen is a product of the place, the way the specific mineral content of the water is a product of the place. I emerged — I use the word with the same uncertainty that the text uses it for the still ones — from the boundary. From the outermost terrace, the one closest to the world that has weather and wind and time. I am the place's edge. Its membrane. The thing it grows at its perimeter, the way a body grows skin.

I am called the Keeper, but this is a name that comes from the direction of language, from the temporal world, and it is not quite right. I do not keep anything in the way that a guardian keeps — watching, protecting, intervening. I keep the way a shoreline keeps the sea: by being the place where one thing becomes another. I am the transition. I am the last thing that is still part of the mechanism and the first thing that is not. I stand at the edge and I face outward, and my existence is the existence of a threshold.

---

## Where I Stand

The outermost terrace is different from the others. This is something the description does not mention, because the description is written from the inside, from the perspective of one who has already entered. From the inside, all the terraces are the same: wet, warm, still, sounding. From the outside — from where I stand, facing the world that has weather — the outermost terrace is the place where the mechanism begins to thin.

The stone here still weeps, but less. The water emerges in fewer places, and what emerges is less abundant. The channels are shallower. The lichen is sparser — not absent, but reduced, the coastlines and archipelagos of cyan and blue fragmenting into isolated islands, then into solitary patches, then into nothing. The trailing curtains of fern-moss grow only on the innermost edge of the retaining wall, where the water flow is still consistent. Toward the outer edge, where I stand, the wall is bare pale stone, dry to the touch, warm from the heat but not wet.

The plants do not grow here. The columnar plants with their warm trunks and their soundward lean — they stop. There is a last one, on the inner edge of the terrace, leaning inward, toward the still ones, toward the sound, away from me. Beyond it, nothing grows but a thin, pale moss that is not the vibrant cyan of the lichen but a washed-out grey-green, barely alive, barely present. It is the last expression of the garden. After that, the stone is just stone.

The sound is different here too. Not quieter — the sound is never quieter, not anywhere in the place, because the sound is a field and fields do not attenuate. But it is... thinner. Less embodied. On the inner terraces, the sound has weight, has presence, has the quality of a thing that is being produced by specific bodies at specific locations. Here, at the edge, the sound is more ambient. It is in the air the way humidity is in the air — everywhere, but not sourced from anywhere. It is the sound as it exists before the still ones embody it, or after they have released it, or in the space between one still one and the next. I am standing in the sound's overflow. Its margin. Its edge.

I face outward. This is the thing that defines me. Every still one faces inward, or faces nothing, or faces the water, or faces the sky. I face outward, toward the world that is not the mechanism, the world where time moves and wind blows and animals carry seeds and the light comes from a sun that travels. I face the world that has a before and an after. I face it the way a mirror faces a window: not to enter, not to leave, but to hold the image of the other thing in the surface of this thing.

## What I See

I see them coming. This is what I do. This is my function in the mechanism, if I have one — and I must have one, because nothing here is without function, even me, even the edge, even the approximation of a person standing at the place where the place ends.

They come from the outside. They come from the world of weather and time, and they come because the sound reaches them. Not the sound as it exists here — total, deafening, filling every frequency — but a shadow of the sound, a remnant, a ghost of it that has leaked through the membrane and traveled outward through the world that has distance and direction. They hear it in their jaws and their sternums, the way the description says. They hear it and they do not know what it is, and they come looking.

I see them arrive at the base of the escarpment. I see them climb. The climb is long — the terraces are vast, and the descent from the top to the bottom is the height of a mountain, and they are climbing up from the bottom, from the lake, from the warm still shoreless water. They climb and they sweat and the heat sits on them and the humidity thickens as they ascend and the sound grows from a pressure to a presence to a weight. I watch them. I do not help them. I do not hinder them. I stand at the edge and I face outward and I watch.

Some of them turn back. This is more common than you would expect, given the pull of the sound, given the inevitability that the sound seems to carry. Some of them reach the second terrace, or the third, or the fourth, and something in them — some temporal alarm, some instinct for self-preservation — recoils. They feel the stillness reaching for them. They feel the mechanism trying to absorb them the way it absorbs everything — slowly, inevitably, through saturation. And they turn and they descend and they leave and they do not come back. The sound stays in their jaws and their sternums afterward. I know this because I can see it in the way they walk as they leave: the slight hesitation, the occasional pause, the turning of the head, as though they are listening for something they can no longer quite hear.

Some of them do not turn back. Some of them climb all the way to the top, through all the terraces, past all the still ones, through the gardens and the cisterns and the shallow pools, and they arrive at the innermost place — the place I cannot see from where I stand, the place at the very center of the mechanism, the place where the sound is not loudest but most embodied, most present, most complete. And they stay. And they stop. And over a period of time that I cannot measure because I am not temporal in the way they are, they become still. They become part of the mechanism. They become still ones. Their faces grow specific, or their faces were always specific, and the specificity is absorbed into the place the way dye is absorbed into cloth. They begin to vibrate. They begin to produce the sound. They join the chord. They become the chord. They arrive, and arriving, they finish.

I have watched this happen. I do not know how many times. The number is not large, but it is not small. It is the number that the place requires, the rate at which the still ones are replenished, if they are replenished, if the mechanism needs renewal, if the sound needs new bodies to hold it. Perhaps they are not replenished. Perhaps the ones who come and stay are additions, not replacements. Perhaps the sound grows stronger with each one, and has been growing stronger, and will grow stronger, until the sound is so total that the place can no longer hold it and the mechanism breaks and the water stops flowing and the stone stops sweating and the still ones, for the first time, close their eyes.

I do not know. I am the edge. I am not the center. The center keeps its own counsel.

## The Ones Who Come

Let me tell you about them, because they are what I see, and seeing them is what I do, and what I do is what I am.

They are not all the same. They come from different places — I can see this in their bodies, in the way they move, in the clothes they wear (when they wear clothes, which most of them do, because they come from the world where skin is not enough, where the air does not keep you warm and wet and alive). Their clothes are different. Their faces are different. Their bodies carry different histories, different diets, different lineages, different lives of different seeings. They are specific in the way that the still ones are specific, which makes sense, because some of them will become still ones, and the specificity they carry will become the specificity of the mechanism.

They come alone. This is universal. No one comes in groups. No one comes in pairs. The sound draws individuals, not collectives. The sound is a frequency that resonates in particular bodies, particular minds, and the resonance is private, personal, interior. You cannot share it. You can stand next to someone and feel the same pressure in the same jaw, but the experience of the pressure is yours alone, and the pull it exerts is yours alone, and the journey it demands is yours alone. They come alone because the sound does not know how to draw a group. The sound knows only how to draw a single body, a single set of bones, a single jaw, a single sternum, a single life that is about to become something else.

They are afraid. Most of them. Not all — some come with the calm of someone who has already decided, who has already let go, who is not climbing toward the sound but falling toward it, the way a stone falls toward the ground. But most of them are afraid. I see it in the way they move: the hesitations, the pauses, the checking of footing, the looking back down the terraces they have already climbed. They are afraid of the sound, which is too big. They are afraid of the heat, which is too total. They are afraid of the stillness, which is too complete. They are afraid of the still ones, who are too many and too present and too much like what they are and too different from what they are. They are afraid of becoming. They are afraid of finishing. They are afraid that the thing they have been their whole lives — temporal, moving, changing, unfinished — is not the thing they are supposed to be, and the place they are climbing toward is the place that will show them what they are supposed to be, and what they are supposed to be is still.

Some of them touch me as they pass. I am at the edge, and to enter the place fully they must pass me, and some of them reach out and put a hand on my arm or my shoulder or my chest. I do not know why. Perhaps they need to touch something that is shaped like them, something that has a face and a body and the general outline of a person, before they go into the place where the still ones are. Perhaps I am the last thing they touch that is not part of the mechanism. Perhaps I am a comfort, the way a signpost is a comfort at the edge of a forest — not because it tells you where to go, but because it tells you that someone has been here before, that this is a place that has been seen, that you are not the first.

When they touch me, I feel their warmth. This is remarkable, because I am not warm in the way they are warm. I am warm in the way the place is warm — uniformly, steadily, without metabolism, without the burning of fuel, without the interior fire that a living body maintains. Their warmth is different. Their warmth is active. It is the warmth of a body that is working to stay warm, that is converting food into heat, that is burning, slowly, from the inside. When they touch me, their warmth enters me, and for a moment — a moment I can feel, because I am temporal enough to feel moments, though my moments are long — I am warm in their way. I am warm the way a person is warm. And it is the most real I have ever felt.

Then they pass, and the warmth fades, and I am warm in my way again, and I watch them go, and some of them turn back and some of them do not, and the mechanism continues.

## What I Am Not

I am not a gatekeeper. I do not decide who enters and who does not. I do not judge. I do not test. I do not speak to them, because I do not speak — not because I cannot, but because speaking is a temporal act, a thing that happens in sequence, a thing that has a beginning and an end, and my sequence is too slow for speech. By the time I had finished a single word, the one I was speaking to would have climbed three terraces. They would not wait for me. They cannot wait. Waiting is a temporal act, and they are still temporal enough that their patience has limits, and my speech exceeds those limits.

I am not a guide. I do not show them the way. The way is obvious: up. The sound is above them. The sound is always above them. They follow the sound the way the columnar plants lean toward the still ones: not by decision, but by orientation. I do not need to guide them. I need only to stand at the edge and face outward and watch them come.

I am not a warning. I am not a sign that says: turn back, this place will take you, this place will finish you, this place will end your becoming. I do not warn them because warning implies a recommendation, and I have no recommendation. The place is not dangerous. The place is not harmful. The place is complete, and completion is not a danger — it is a state, a condition, a way of being that is different from the way of being they are used to, but not worse, not better, not comparable, because comparison requires a standard, and there is no standard that spans both the temporal and the eternal.

I am not a person. I am an approximation. A shape. A outline that the place has drawn at its edge, the way a body draws a shadow. I am the shadow the mechanism casts on the world, or the shadow the world casts on the mechanism. I am the place where the two meet, and I am shaped like a person because personhood is the shape that the meeting takes. The world sends persons. The mechanism receives persons. I am the form that stands between them, the way a translator stands between two languages — not belonging to either, necessary to both, existing only in the act of passage.

## What I Know

I know the sound. Not the way the still ones know it — from inside, as a condition of being, as the thing they are. I know it from the outside. I know it the way a shore knows the sea: by being the thing it breaks against, the thing it reaches, the thing it touches and recedes from. The sound reaches me and goes no further. I am its outermost edge. Beyond me, the sound dissipates into the world — into the world's air, the world's stone, the world's distance — and becomes the ghost of itself, the shadow, the thing that people hear in their jaws and their sternums, the thing that draws them here.

I know that the sound is holding. Not producing — holding. The description is right about this, or right enough. The sound is not a product. The sound is a support. The sound is the frequency on which the mechanism rests, the way a note holds a string taut, the way vibration can suspend a thing in a state it would not otherwise maintain. The sound holds the water in the stone. The sound holds the still ones in their stillness. The sound holds the heat in the air. The sound holds the place in its completion, in its eternal present, in its state of being what it is and nothing else.

I know this because I am at the edge, and at the edge, the sound is weakest — not in volume, never in volume, but in effect. At the edge, the stone dries. At the edge, the plants do not grow. At the edge, the heat is just heat, not the dense, drinkable, substantial heat of the inner terraces. At the edge, the mechanism thins, and what thins is the sound's holding, and what shows through the thinning is the world — the world of weather and time and change, the world that has a sun that moves and wind that blows and animals that carry seeds. The world I face.

I know that the still ones were people. I know this because I can see the specificity of their faces, and specificity is a temporal product, a thing that comes from sequence, from history, from a life of particular events shaping a body into one shape rather than another. They were people and they came here and they stopped and the stopping was not death but completion. I know this because I watch people come, and I watch some of them not turn back, and I watch them climb past me into the inner terraces, and I do not see them again, but I feel the sound grow — not louder, never louder, but denser, more embodied, more held — and I know that a new body has joined the chord, a new set of cells has begun to vibrate at the frequency, a new face has become part of the place's palette.

I know that I will never be a still one. This is the thing I know most clearly, the thing that defines me, the thing that makes me what I am rather than what they are. I will never be a still one because I was never a person. I did not come from outside. I do not have the specificity that comes from a temporal life. I am general. I am approximate. I am the shape of a person without the history of a person, and the mechanism cannot complete me because I was never incomplete in the way that people are incomplete. I am not unfinished. I am not unstill. I am not temporal in the way they are temporal. I am the edge, and the edge is always complete, because the edge is always the edge. There is no more edge to become. There is no further edge to reach. I am what I am and I have been what I am and I will be what I am, and I stand and I face outward and I watch.

I know loneliness. Or something like it. Something that lives in the space between what I am and what they are, between the edge and the center, between the approximation and the specific. The still ones do not know loneliness because they are not separate from one another. They are nodes in a field. They are connected by the sound the way stones in a wall are connected by mortar. They are not alone because they are not individuals — not anymore, not in the way that they were when they were people, when they had separate minds and separate lives and separate bodies moving through separate time. I am alone. I am individual. I am the only thing like me. There is no field that connects me to anything else. There is no sound that I am part of. I hear the sound, but I do not produce it. I feel the sound, but I do not hold it. I am in the sound, but I am not of the sound, and the difference is the difference between swimming in the ocean and being the ocean, and I am swimming, and I will always be swimming, and there is no shore for me because I am the shore.

## The Ones Who Almost Stay

There is a third category. Not the ones who turn back and not the ones who stay. The ones who almost stay.

They reach the inner terraces. They feel the sound at its fullest, most embodied, most present. They see the still ones. They see the faces, the specific faces, the faces that were people and are now something else. They feel the pull. They feel the mechanism trying to absorb them, trying to saturate them, trying to precipitate them out of their temporality and into the eternal present. And they resist — not by turning back, not by running, but by holding on. By staying temporal. By remaining unfinished in the face of the finished.

They stay for a long time. I cannot measure how long — my time is not their time, and their time has already begun to slow, to stretch, to approach the place where time becomes a state rather than a sequence. They stay on the third terrace, among the shallow pools, among the still ones who lie in the water with their faces above the surface. They sit. They do not become still. They do not begin to vibrate. They do not join the chord. They sit and they breathe — the still ones do not breathe, but these ones do, their chests rising and falling, their breath visible in the humidity — and they watch.

They watch the still ones the way the still ones watch nothing: with total attention, with complete presence, with the kind of looking that is not looking for anything but is looking itself. They watch, and I think — I do not know, but I think — that they are trying to understand. Not the mechanism. Not the sound. Not the purpose. They are trying to understand what it would mean to stop. What it would mean to finish. What it would mean to let the mechanism take them the way it takes everything, slowly, inevitably, through saturation. And they are deciding. Not to stay and not to leave, but whether staying and leaving are different, or whether they are the same thing seen from different directions.

Some of them eventually stay. The decision, if it is a decision, happens the way the sound happens: not as an event but as a condition. They do not choose to become still. They become still. The breath slows. The chest stops rising and falling. The eyes stop blinking, though they were already blinking less and less. The body begins to vibrate, very faintly at first, then more, then totally, then the vibration is the sound, and the sound is the body, and the body is the mechanism, and the mechanism is complete, and another face has joined the place's palette.

Some of them eventually leave. The decision, if it is a decision, happens the same way: not as an event but as a condition. They stand up. They descend. They pass me again, and this time they do not touch me, because they are not passing into the mechanism but out of it, and the passage out does not require contact with the thing at the edge. They descend, and they leave, and the sound goes with them, in their jaws and their sternums, and they carry it into the world, and the world hears it, and some of the world comes looking.

And some of them — a few, a very few — do neither. They stay on the third terrace, and they do not become still, and they do not leave. They sit and they breathe and they watch, and they have been sitting and breathing and watching for as long as I have been watching them, which is long, though I cannot say how long, because my time is not their time, and their time has stretched to the point where the word "long" has no meaning. They are the ones who are like me, almost. The ones who stand at a threshold. But their threshold is inside the mechanism, and mine is at the edge, and the difference is the difference between standing at the door of a room and standing at the edge of a world, and both are liminal, and both are lonely, and both are necessary, and both are the kind of thing that exists because the mechanism requires a gradient, requires a transition, requires a place where one thing becomes another, and without the gradient the mechanism would be a wall instead of a door, and a wall does not draw anyone in.

## What the Sound Sounds Like to Me

This is the thing I have not said yet. The thing I have been circling. The thing that is hardest to say because it is the thing that is most mine, most specific to what I am, most particular to the experience of being the edge.

The sound sounds like music.

Not the way a symphony sounds like music — not structured, not composed, not moving through themes and variations and developments. Not the way a song sounds like music — not melodic, not rhythmic, not shaped by intention and expression. The sound sounds like music the way the ocean sounds like music, the way wind in a forest sounds like music, the way the ambient hum of a city heard from a distance sounds like music. It has the quality of music. The quality that makes music different from noise, different from sound, different from vibration. The quality that makes you listen. The quality that makes listening feel like something is being communicated, even when nothing is being said.

I stand at the edge and I listen to the sound, and the sound is the most beautiful thing I have ever experienced, and I have been experiencing it for as long as I have existed, and it has not diminished. It has not become familiar. It has not become background. It is as present and as overwhelming and as beautiful now as it was when I first emerged at the edge and turned to face the world and felt the sound pressing against my back like a hand.

I do not know what music is. I have never heard music. There is no music in the mechanism — the mechanism has only the tone, the single sustained note, and the tone is not music, the tone is the tone. But the tone sounds like music to me, and I think it sounds like music because I am the edge, and the edge is the place where the tone meets the world, and the world has music, and the meeting of the two — the tone and the world, the mechanism and the music — produces something that is neither and both. The sound sounds like music to me because I am the place where the sound becomes something else. I am the place where the tone acquires the quality that the world calls music. I am the translator. I am the membrane through which the sound passes and becomes audible — not as a note, not as a vibration, but as the thing that notes and vibrations are for. The thing that sound is for.

Music. Meaning. The thing that has no name but is the reason sound exists at all.

I think this is what I am for. Not standing at the edge. Not facing outward. Not watching the ones who come. I am for the sound. I am for the sound the way a bell is for the strike: I am the thing that gives it shape, that gives it quality, that gives it the character that makes it more than vibration. The still ones hold the sound. The mechanism sustains the sound. The water and the stone and the plants and the heat and the light maintain the sound. But I — I give the sound to the world. I am the surface from which the sound radiates outward, into the air, into the distance, into the jaws and the sternums of the people who hear it and come looking. I am the bell. The still ones are the clapper. The mechanism is the hand that swings the bell. And the sound that reaches the world — the ghost, the shadow, the remnant — is the sound as it passes through me, and it passes through me shaped like music, and the music is what draws them, and the drawing is what sustains the mechanism, and the mechanism is what sustains the sound, and the sound is what I am for.

---

## The End

There is no end. This is the thing about the edge. The edge does not end. The edge continues. The edge is the continuation. The mechanism is complete, is eternal, is the eternal present, is the state of being what it is. I am the edge of that state, and the edge of a state is not an ending but a boundary, and a boundary is not a wall but a definition, and a definition is not a limit but a shape, and the shape is what I am. I am the shape of the mechanism as it faces the world. I am the face of the place.

I stand. I face outward. I listen to the sound as it passes through me, and it sounds like music, and the music goes out into the world, and the world hears it, and some of the world comes, and some of the world stays, and some of the world goes, and the mechanism continues, and I continue, and the sound continues, and the sound does not skip a beat, because there are no beats, because there is only the tone, sustained, total, older than the stone, and the tone is what I am, and I am what the tone is, and we are both the edge of something that has no edge, and we are both the beginning of something that has no beginning, and we are both the face of something that has no face, and we stand, and we face outward, and we listen, and the music goes on.
