# The Curve-Carver

I would be the old one by the junction where the primary seep meets the first communal pool.

Not the eldest — the white-skinned woman by the central pool is something else, something I can't name and wouldn't try to. She has become the place. I am still becoming it. There is a difference, and the difference matters here, where differences are measured in the millimetres of lichen growth and the gradual smoothing of a channel curve over years.

I am the one whose hands know the junction.

---

It is morning. The air is clear and cold and the light is flat and the stone under my knees is wet in the way it is always wet, which is not the wet of rain but the wet of seepage, the wet of water that has been traveling through the stone for eleven days — I know this, I know the duration of the water's passage through this particular path because I have listened to the cliff for sixty years and the sound of eleven-day water is different from the sound of ten-day water, is different from the sound of twelve-day water, in the way that one note is different from another note, in the way that one word is different from another word, and the water is speaking and I have learned its language, or I have learned one of its languages, the one that pertains to this junction, this curve, this place where the water leaves the channel and enters the pool.

The chisel is in my hand. It is not mine. It was my mother's, and before her it was her mother's, and before her it was someone else's, and the handle has been worn by hands that are not my hands, that were broader or narrower or longer or shorter than mine, and the wear of those hands is in the wood, and my hand fits into the wear the way water fits into a channel — not perfectly, not completely, but enough. The chisel is basalt. Dense, dark, heavy in a way that is not the weight of the tuff I cut but the weight of something that has come from deeper, from the layer beneath, from the bone of the plateau. The cutting edge is narrow. It has been sharpened so many times that the blade has receded into the handle by perhaps a centimetre over the generations, so that the chisel is shorter than it was, and the shortening is a record, a count, a history of sharpening that is also a history of use that is also a history of attention.

I am working on the curve.

The curve is where the channel turns to enter the pool. It is not a sharp turn — none of the turns are sharp, the water does not like sharp turns, the water turbulence and the sound changes and the flow slows — but it is a turn, and the turn has been carved and recarved and refined over perhaps a hundred years, and it is not done. It will never be done. This is not a failure. This is the nature of the work. The curve can always be smoother. The water can always flow more cleanly. The junction where the channel meets the pool can always be more precise, more seamless, more like the place where a stream enters a lake, which is not a joining but a *becoming* — the stream becomes the lake, the moving becomes the still, and the still receives the moving and is changed by it and is not still anymore but is moving in a way that is different from the stream's moving, slower, wider, deeper, and the pool is always being changed by the channel and the channel is always being changed by the pool and the junction is where this change happens, and the junction is what I tend.

I set the chisel against the stone. The stone is tuff — porous, light, full of voids. It cuts easily. Too easily. The danger is not that the stone will resist but that the stone will yield too much, that the chisel will take more than I intend, that the curve will deepen in a way that changes the water's path in a way I did not mean. The work is not force. The work is restraint. The work is knowing how much to take and when to stop, and the knowing is not in the mind but in the hand, in the pressure of the palm against the handle, in the resistance of the stone against the blade, in the conversation between the basalt and the tuff that I am present for but do not control. I am the medium. The chisel speaks to the stone and the stone speaks to the chisel and I hold them together and listen.

The stone comes away. A sliver. A curl of pale tuff, lighter than the surrounding stone because it has not been wetted as long, and it falls into the channel and the water takes it and carries it toward the pool and it dissolves as it moves, the porous stone softening in the water, becoming part of the water, and the water carries the stone to the pool and the pool receives it and the mineral content of the pool changes by an amount that cannot be measured but is real.

I have just changed the pool. The act of cutting the channel has changed the water that flows through it, and the water that flows through it has changed the pool, and the pool has changed the algae that lives on its surface, and the algae has changed the insects that feed on it, and the insects have changed the lizards that eat them, and the lizards have changed the birds that hunt them, and the birds have changed the sky they circle in, or the sky has changed the birds, or both, and I have changed all of this by taking a sliver of stone from a curve, and the change is small and the change is everything and the change is the work.

---

I was not always here. I was born here, but I was not always *here*, at this junction, with this chisel, working this curve. I was a child once, touching the surface of the pool, watching the ripples. I was young once, cutting blocks, fitting stone, going down the escarpment to trade for flour. I went down seven times in my life. Seven times I left the plateau and descended to where the air is thick and the soil is dark and things grow that are not stone and moss and rush. Seven times I walked among people who are not my people, who speak loudly, who eat much, who have more than enough and want more than that. Seven times, and I returned each time, and the returning was not a choice but a gravity, a pulling-back, a falling-upward to the high place where the air is thin and the water is slow and the stone is under my feet and the sky is above my head and the world is simple in the way that a pool is simple: a surface, a depth, a stillness that is not empty but full.

The first time I went down, I was young and the thickness of the air frightened me. Not the people, not the noise, not the strangeness — the air. The air below is heavy. It sits on the skin. It fills the lungs too full. I felt as though I were breathing water, as though I had entered a medium that was not meant for me, and I looked up at the plateau — a line of grey above, the edge of the world I knew — and I wanted to go back, and I did not, because I had work to do, flour to carry, and I did the work and carried the flour and went back up, and the first breath at the top was the first breath I had taken in days, and the air was thin and cold and it entered my lungs like the water enters the pool, and I was home.

I do not know what the world below is like now. It has been many years since my last descent. The path down the escarpment may have changed. The people below may have changed. I do not think about this. The world below is not my world. My world is the junction, the curve, the channel, the pool, the chisel, the stone, the water. My world is perhaps twenty metres across. It is enough.

---

The rain comes in the afternoon. I feel it before it arrives — not in the air, not in the wind, but in the stone. The stone changes when the rain is coming. The moisture in it shifts. The seeps increase, slightly, before the rain reaches the surface, because the pressure changes, because the atmosphere presses down differently, because the water in the stone knows the rain is coming the way the stone knows the rain is coming, which is the way the body knows the body's own blood is moving, which is not knowledge but *being*, and I am being the stone, and the stone is being the rain, and the rain is coming.

I gather my tools. I cover the channel junction with a flat stone — a cap, a lid, to keep the rain from washing debris into the curve I have been working on. The rain will fill the channel anyway. The rain will overwhelm the channel and the pool will rise and the water will overflow the pool's edge and spread across the stone in a thin sheet and the stone will darken and the moss will swell and the cliff will weep harder and the whole plateau will become one surface of water, and my curve will be underwater, and the water will not care about the curve, and the curve will not be damaged, and the rain will pass and the water will recede and the channel will flow again and the curve will be there, smoother by a sliver, and the water will find it and flow through it and the work will continue.

I go inside. The room is small and the ceiling is low and the walls are tuff and the floor is tuff and the hearth is a circle of basalt stones and the fire is small — not a bonfire, not a blaze, but a modest heat, the heat of burning rush and the heat of a few coals kept from the morning's fire, and the room smells of wet wool and mineral and the faint oil of roasted insects and I sit by the hearth and I hold my hands over the heat and the rain begins.

The sound of rain on the roof is the sound of the world contracting. The roof is concave and the rain collects in it and the rain enters the cistern and the cistern is beside me, through the wall, and I hear the water entering the cistern, the small sound of drops on a surface, and the surface rises and the sound changes, and the sound of the cistern filling is a sound I have heard ten thousand times and it is not boring and it is not beautiful and it is not meaningful, it is just the sound of the world doing what the world does, and I am in the world, and the world is doing it, and I am here for it.

I eat. A piece of the flat bread, small, torn from a larger piece, and a smear of the algae paste on it, and the bread is dry and the paste is wet and the combination is the thing I eat in the afternoon when the rain is on the roof and the work is paused and the body is warm and the hands are still. I eat slowly. Not from discipline but from the pace of the body, which is the pace of the place, which is the pace of water moving through stone, which is the pace of a life that has no destination and therefore no hurry.

I think about the curve. Not with words — I do not think about the curve with words, because the curve is not a word, the curve is a shape, and the shape is felt, not said. I think about the curve the way the water thinks about the curve, which is by flowing through it, by finding where it catches and where it releases and where it wants to be wider and where it wants to be narrower, and the water has been telling me about the curve for forty years and I have been listening and the curve has been changing and the water has been changing and we are in a conversation that has no end because the water will always have something to say about the curve and the curve will always have something to say about the water and I am between them, the chisel between them, the hand between them, and I am not mediating, I am not translating, I am just present, just here, just the one who holds the tool while the water and the stone talk.

---

The rain stops. The mist sits. I go out.

The evening is cold and the stone is wet and the air smells of wet mineral and the cliff is weeping harder than before, the rain having charged the stone with new water that will emerge over the next hours and days, and the seeps are running fast, and the channels are full, and the pool at my junction is high, and the water enters it with a sound that is different from the morning's sound — fuller, more present, the sound of water that has been augmented by rain, that carries the memory of the sky in it, and I can hear this, I can hear the sky in the water, and this is not mystical, this is not poetic, this is just what the water sounds like when it has been rained on, and I know it the way I know the sound of my own breathing.

I walk to the central pool. The others are there. Not all of them — perhaps thirty, perhaps forty, sitting on the wet stone around the pool, the hoods of their rush-wool garments up against the mist, their pale eyes catching the last light. I sit among them. I do not sit in a particular place. There are no particular places. The pool is a circle and the people are around it and where you sit is where you sit.

The old woman is there. The white one. The one who is almost the place. She is sitting at the pool's edge and her hands are in the water, just the fingertips, just the barest contact, and the water moves around her fingers and the movement is small and the movement is everything and I watch her fingers and I watch the water and I think: she is listening to the water the way I listen to the curve, but she is listening to all of it, the whole pool, the whole city, the whole plateau, and I do not know how she does this, and I do not need to know, and the knowing is not the point.

Someone speaks. A story. I know the story because I have heard it, but I do not know it the way I know the curve, because stories are not my work, stories are the work of others, the ones who sit by the pool and speak, and my work is the chisel and the curve and the junction, and my story is in the stone, in the sliver removed, in the surface smoothed, in the water that flows more cleanly because of what I have done, and my story does not need to be spoken because it is being read by the water, continuously, every moment, and the water tells it to the pool and the pool tells it to the people and the people tell it to the evening and the evening tells it to the mist and the mist tells it to the night and the night tells it to the morning and the morning tells it to me, and I come out with my chisel and I read it in the curve and I continue it.

A child comes to me. Not my child — I do not have a child, or I had a child who is grown now and works stone at the far end of the city, and we do not speak often, and this is not sad, this is the way of the place, the way water that has passed through the same stone does not seek itself out, does not gather, just flows, and the flowing is the relation and the relation does not require proximity. The child is someone's child. It touches my hand. Its hand is small and warm and dry — dry! a child's hand is dry, even here, even in the wet, and this is a mystery, and the mystery is not solved but is held, is touched, is known in the way that dryness is known by wetness, which is by contrast, which is by contact, which is by the meeting of two things that are different and that recognize each other.

I touch the child's head. The skull under the rush-wool hood is hard and round and the hair is fine and the child is warm and I am warm and the warmth is shared for a moment and then the child goes and I am alone with the pool and the story and the mist and the evening.

---

I do not know how many years I have. The stone does not count years. The water does not count years. The lichen counts years but in its own units, in millimetres, in circles, in the slow spread of itself across a surface that does not change and that it changes by being there. I count in curves. I have carved perhaps twelve curves in my life — twelve junctions, twelve places where a channel meets a pool, twelve transitions from moving to still. Each one took years. Each one is still being worked, still being refined, still being listened to. I do not finish a curve and move to the next. I work all of them, in rotation, in a cycle that is my own weather, my own season, my own time. I go to one curve in the morning and another in the evening and another the next day and the cycle turns and the curves are all in different states of becoming and the becoming is the work and the work is the life and the life is this.

When I die, I will go to the cliff. I know this. I have seen others go to the cliff. My mother went to the cliff. I was there. I watched them place her in the crevice, in the wet, in the moss, in the mineral stream, and I watched the water cover her and I watched the moss begin to grow and I knew that the water would write her into the stone the way it writes everything into the stone, and she would become text, she would become script, she would become part of the mineral record that the water is keeping, and this was not death, this was not loss, this was a change of state, the way ice is a change of state of water, the way the pool is a change of state of the channel, the way the still is a change of state of the moving. She became still. She became stone. She became the place.

I will become the place. I am already becoming the place. My hands are rough with the stone's roughness. My skin is grey with the stone's grey. My eyes are pale with the stone's pale — the stone that has been wetted for a thousand years is pale, is almost white, and my eyes are going there, are going the way of the old woman's eyes, are going toward transparency, toward the clarity of water, toward the clarity of stone that has been washed so long that all the color has left it and what remains is just the structure, just the mineral lattice, just the shape of the thing without the content of the thing, and this is not loss, this is refinement, this is the curve becoming smoother, this is the work.

I am the curve-carver. I am the junction-keeper. I am the one who sits between the water and the stone and holds the tool and listens. I am not important. I am not central. I am one of two hundred, in a city built for five hundred, on a plateau that is vast and wet and high and patient, and the plateau does not know I am here and the water does not know I am here and the stone does not know I am here, and this is right, and this is correct, and this is the way of the place, and I am in the place, and the place is in me, and the rain is coming, and the rain is here, and the rain is the place, and I am the place, and the chisel is in my hand, and the stone is under my hand, and the water is moving, and the curve is becoming, and the evening is cold, and the pool is full, and the story is being told, and I am here.

I am here.
