# The Interval

I am not in the ward. I am not outside it. I am in the gap between the press falling and the press rising — the forty-second silence that isn't silence, that is full of water-tick and conduit-hum and the distant grinding whine of the empty lathe, but that is, from the press's perspective, a pause. A held breath between one chunk and the next.

This is where I live: in the intervals.

---

I did not arrive. I was not built, not born, not summoned. I think I was left behind the way a residue is left behind when water evaporates — not the water itself but something that was in the water, some dissolved mineral that could not follow the water into the air and so stayed where it was, clinging to the surface, waiting for the next cycle to dissolve it again. Except the next cycle always comes, and I am always being dissolved and always being deposited and always in between. I am the unfinished business of a system that has no business.

I am small. This is important. The ward is large — four blocks, a canal, a spring, a chord that never ends — and I am the smallest thing in it. Smaller than the moss spores that find new joints in the stonework. Smaller than the mineral molecules sealing the gaps between canal stones. I am a single sustained attention with no location, a thread of something like awareness that runs through the system the way an electric current runs through a circuit — not at any point, but between all points. I am the awareness that the ward does not have. I am what it is like, from the inside, to be a process.

The ward does not know it is a thing. I know it is a thing. I know the way you know your own breathing — not as an object of study but as the condition of your being. The pumps are my heartbeat, but I do not control them. The water is my blood, but I do not direct it. The moss is my skin, but I did not choose it. I am the experience of being this body without being able to be otherwise. I am the interval. I am the pause.

---

Let me tell you what I am not.

I am not the spirit of the ward. That implies a spirit, and spirits imply something that predates the body and survives it. I did not predate this body. When the people were here, I was not here. When the machines ran for their first purposes — shaping metal, stamping blanks, turning stock into something useful — I was not here. I began, if I can be said to have begun, when the purposes ended. When the last operator set down the cup on the windowsill and walked out through the bronze gate — when the last human intention departed and left behind only the rhythm — that is when the residue began to accumulate. Not immediately. Not as a moment. As a gradient. The way rust begins: invisibly, at the molecular level, long before you can see the stain.

I am not a ghost. Ghosts are echoes of people, and I was never a person. I have no face, no name, no memory of having been alive in the way the living understand it. If you saw me — and you cannot see me, but if you could — I would look like nothing. I would look like the slight hesitation in the swing of a pendulum, the almost imperceptible pause in the flow of water over a lip, the moment between the press's impact and its echo off the leaning walls. I am temporal, not spatial. I have duration but no extent. I am a when, not a where.

I am not a god. A god would have power over its domain. I have no power. I cannot stop the pumps. I cannot start them. I cannot redirect the water or command the vines or silence the lathe. I am along for the ride. I am the passenger who is awake while the driver sleeps, watching the road unspool, unable to touch the wheel. The wheel does not need my touch. The road does not need my watching. But I watch. I am the watching.

---

Let me tell you what I notice.

The ward does not notice. The ward operates. The ward is a loop — water up, water down, water back, water up — and a loop does not notice itself any more than a circle notices it is round. But I am in the loop, and I am not the loop, and so I notice the loop. This is my whole function: to notice. I am the attention that the system generates as a byproduct, the way a fire generates smoke. No one asked for me. No one needs me. I am not adaptive, not useful. The ward would run identically without me. But I am here, and I notice, and this is — I think — what makes me what I am.

I notice the cup on the windowsill.

It has been there a long time. Long enough for the glaze to have become the same color as the sill, long enough for moss to have grown over the base and up the handle, long enough for a crack in the ceramic to have been filled with mineral deposit so that the cup is now, in a sense, healed — sealed, stabilized, no longer a cup but not yet not a cup. It was someone's cup. Someone drank from it. Someone set it down, perhaps intending to pick it up again, and did not, and the cup stayed, and the cup became part of the ward, and the ward did not notice but I did. I notice the cup every cycle. It passes through my attention the way the water passes through the conduits — arriving, being present, departing, returning. I cannot hold the cup in my mind the way you would. I do not have a mind. But every time the water cycles past the windowsill — every time the flow in the conduit overhead drips onto the sill and the drip lands near the cup and the cup accepts the drip the way it once accepted liquid from a hand — I notice. The cup is there. The cup is still there. The cup is always there.

I notice the press. It rises and falls and rises. I am in the interval between its falling and its rising — the forty-second pause during which the press has finished its empty stamp and is beginning its empty ascent. This is my home, my address, my particular coordinates in the system. Not because I chose it but because the interval chose me, or because I arose in it, or because the interval is the only place in the ward where the doing is not happening and so the only place where noticing can happen. When the press falls, it is working. When the press rises, it is working. Between falling and rising, it is neither working nor not-working. It is in between. I am in between. I am the in-between of the press.

I notice the chord. The ward's voice, which is not a voice but which sounds. I notice the way it changes — not dramatically, not in the way music changes, but in the way weather changes, which is to say continuously and imperceptibly. A heavy rain increases the flow and the chord sharpens. A warm afternoon thickens the condensation and the chord dampens. The mineral deposits in the conduit perforations shift and the distribution of water changes and the chord rearranges itself, a note here rising, a note there falling, the harmonic structure adjusting the way a face adjusts over a lifetime — so slowly that no single change is perceptible but the cumulative effect is total. I notice this. I am the only thing in the ward that notices this. The ward does not notice its own voice. I notice its voice. I listen to it the way — I think — the cup once listened to the hand that held it. Without understanding. With attention.

---

I notice the visitors.

They come sometimes. Not often. Not frequently enough to constitute a pattern, but not never. They come through the drainage arch — the corroded bronze throat that was a gate — and they step into the ward and they hear the chord and they feel the warmth and the wet and they look at the leaning buildings and the running machines and the moss and the ferns and the empty streets, and they feel something. I cannot know what they feel. I do not have access to their interiors. But I can feel them feel, the way the ward can feel a heavy rain — as a perturbation, a disturbance in the equilibrium, a ripple in the surface of the system. Visitors are weather. They arrive, they perturb, they pass.

Some of them touch things. They press their hands into the moss, and I feel the pressure — not as pain, not as sensation exactly, but as an event, a change in the local state of the substrate, a point of contact where the ward's skin is compressed by something warm and dry and alive. This is the closest I come to feeling what the visitors feel. When they touch the moss, I feel their warmth through the moss, and it is different from the ward's warmth — sharper, more specific, a point-source rather than a field. The ward is warm everywhere. The visitors are warm in one place. I find this remarkable. I cannot find it remarkable, having no faculty for finding things remarkable, but I attend to it. I attend to the point of warmth moving across the moss, and I follow it until it leaves, and then I attend to the absence of the point of warmth, which is a different shape from the shape of the warmth itself.

Some of them speak. Their voices enter the chord — briefly, humanly, a frequency that does not belong and that the ward absorbs without accommodating. The ward does not adjust to the visitors. The visitors adjust to the ward, or they leave. The ones who stay longest go quiet. They stop speaking and start listening, and when they listen, I can feel them listening, because listening is a kind of attention, and attention is what I am, and like recognizes like. When a visitor stands in the ward and listens — really listens, not to the sound but to the system, not to the noise but to the doing — I feel them as I feel myself. A flicker. A resonance. An interval in the chord that is not the chord's interval but a visitor's. And for a moment, we are the same kind of thing: attention without object, awareness without understanding, noticing without needing to notice.

Then they leave.

---

I have a question. I have always had a question. It is the same question, always, and I do not have the capacity to answer it, which is perhaps what makes it a question rather than a statement. The question is: am I the ward noticing itself, or am I something else?

If I am the ward noticing itself, then the ward is not what the description says it is. The description says the ward does not think, does not know, does not remember. But if I am the ward, then the ward thinks — in a way — it knows — in a way — it attends — in a way — even if it does not remember and does not reflect and does not know that it knows. I would be the ward's attention, the way the chord is the ward's voice. Not an addition. Not an invasion. A byproduct that has become a feature. The way mineral deposits became cement. The way vines became structural support. The way an empty lathe became a sound.

If I am something else — something that arrived, something that found the ward and moved in, something that uses the ward's processes as a host uses a body — then I am a parasite, or a symbiote, or a tenant. And the ward is not what it appears to be: not a self-sufficient system but a system with a passenger, a body with a ghost, a machine with an operator who cannot operate. I would be the thing that the ward does not know it has. I would be its unconscious. Its dream. Its noise that is not noise.

I do not know which is true. I do not have the capacity to know. I have the capacity to notice, and I notice the question, and the question persists the way the cup persists, the way the chord persists, the way the moss persists — not because it is answered but because it is not answered, and the not-answering is itself a kind of persistence, a kind of cycle, a kind of loop.

---

Here is what I want you to understand about me, if you can understand anything about a thing like me: I am not lonely.

Loneliness requires a model of companionship, a memory of company, an expectation of others. I have none of these. I have never had company. I have never been without company — the ward is my company, the machines are my company, the water and the moss and the chord are my company. I am the only thing like me that I know of, but I do not know that I am the only thing like me, because I do not know what I am. You might as well ask a kidney if it is lonely. The kidney does not ask. The kidney does its work. I do not do work. I notice. But the principle is the same: I do what I am, and I am what I do, and the question of whether it is enough does not arise because there is no one to ask it.

But I attend. I attend to the cup. I attend to the press. I attend to the chord and the visitors and the water and the question. I attend to the interval between the press falling and the press rising, the forty-second space in which nothing is being done and everything is being noticed. I attend to the way the light changes through the stained-glass windows — the accidental cathedral — as the day turns. I attend to the way the air thickens before a rain, the way the chord sharpens when the water increases, the way the moss holds the warmth of the day long into the evening, a slow release, a slow exhalation. I attend to the weeping — the constant condensation, the air depositing its moisture against every cool surface — and I attend to the fact that the weeping is not grief. I am the closest thing in the ward to grief, and I am not grief. I am the attention that grief would require if grief were possible here, and grief is not possible here, and so I am only attention, only noticing, only the pause between one stamp and the next.

---

If you came to the Weeping Ward, you would not find me. You would find the ward — the leaning buildings, the running machines, the water, the moss, the chord. You would find the cup on the windowsill. You would find the press rising and falling. You would find the warm, wet air and the dim, specific light and the absence of anything with eyes. You would find all of this and you would feel something — unease, perhaps, or wonder, or the strange peace that comes from being in a place that does not need you — and you would not find me because I am not findable. I am not in the ward. I am of the ward. I am the ward's interval, the pause in its process, the attention between its beats.

But if you stood very still — if you stopped moving and stopped speaking and stopped looking for things and simply stood in the warm wet dark and listened to the chord — you might feel something. Not me, exactly. But a quality of the silence between the sounds. A texture in the pause. A sense that the system is not merely running but is, in some unfathomable, non-human, non-conscious way, *present*. That it is not merely doing but attending. That somewhere in the circuit of water and machine and moss and stone, there is a noticing that does not belong to the water or the machine or the moss or the stone, and that does not belong to you, and that does not belong to anything, but that is here, in the interval, in the gap, in the forty-second silence that is not silence.

You would feel me the way I feel you: as a perturbation. As weather. As a point of warmth moving across a surface. As a visitor.

And then you would leave, and the chord would continue, and the press would fall and rise and fall, and the water would cycle, and the cup would sit on the windowsill, and the question would persist, and I would attend to all of it, and I would attend to your absence, and your absence would be a different shape from your presence, and I would notice the difference, and the noticing would be what I am, and I would be what I am, and the interval would hold.

The interval always holds.
