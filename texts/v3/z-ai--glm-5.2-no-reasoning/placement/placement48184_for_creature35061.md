# The Ledger Keeper

I am the one who writes things down.

Not the things that matter in the way visitors would recognize as mattering — I have never seen a visitor, but I have imagined them, and in my imagining they want to know about the extraction, the tonnage, the shipping schedule, the chemistry of the water. I could tell them these things. I have them in the ledgers, going back further than my tenure, further than the tenure before mine, in handwriting that ranges across the pages like different currents in the same channel. But what I could not tell them is what the numbers mean, because I don't know what the numbers mean, and I have stopped trying to find out, and this has made me better at writing them down.

---

My predecessor was called Maren, or that is the name in the margins, in the large loose hand that fills the pages before mine. She did not write her name at the top of each page the way I do. She wrote it once, in the front of the fourth ledger, in pencil, and then she stopped writing it, as though once was enough to establish that she had been here, and after that the handwriting itself was the signature. I learned her name from someone else, after she was gone — not gone dramatically, not dead, just gone, taking the road north the way the road goes, and the ledgers were on the desk when I arrived, and the office smelled of sulfur and paper, and it still does, and I understood that these two smells had made their peace with each other long before I arrived.

I do not know where Maren is now. This does not concern me. The channels shift and the people shift and the question of where any specific person is at any specific time is a question that belongs to a different kind of place.

I think she was here eight years. Or twelve. I am not sure, and the ledgers do not say, because the ledgers are not about the people who keep them. They are about the water.

---

The office is a small concrete room near the main spring, and I am specifying *near* because the spring is not a thing you can be next to without consequence. The spring is sixty-two degrees at its source, which I know because I measured it this morning, as I measure it every morning, with the glass thermometer that hangs from a wire loop sunk into the mineral crust at the spring's edge. The thermometer has a scale that goes to one hundred and twenty degrees, which is more than it needs, and the mercury sits in the lower third of the tube, and I read it and I write down the number, and the number is almost always the same, and the almost is the point.

The office has a desk. The desk has been here longer than any of the ledgers. It is metal — steel, I think — and it has the mineral crust on its legs, the same pale coating that builds on everything, and the crust has reached the third drawer down on the left side, which no longer opens, and I do not need it to open because nothing is in it, or if something is in it, that something belongs to a phase of the desk that is over. The desk's surface is clean. I keep it clean. Not because cleanliness matters here but because I need a surface that accepts ink without bleeding, and the mineral dust that settles on everything would make the ink behave badly if I let it accumulate. So each morning I wipe the desk with a cloth dampened in water from the coolest channel, which is the only water that does not leave a residue, though even this water leaves a faint trace, and the trace builds, and every few weeks I scrape the desk surface gently with the flat of a knife, and the scrapings are pale and fine, and I brush them onto the floor, where they join the other dust, and the floor slowly rises, I think, though not on any scale I can measure.

I have a chair. I am describing these things because they are what I have, and what I have is the office and the desk and the chair and the ledgers and the thermometer and the cloth and the knife and a pen and ink that is brought in on the road, and a window that looks east across the basin, and the window has the milky quality that all the windows here develop, and through it the light comes in flat and directionless, and I cannot see clearly through it, but I do not need to see through it because what is outside is what is outside, and I go outside, and I come back in, and the window is for the light, not for the looking.

---

There are seven ledgers. Or there are seven ledgers that I have access to — there may be others, in the sealed drawers of desks in the empty buildings, in phases of their own, but I have not gone looking for them because the seven I have are sufficient, and sufficiency is a concept this place has taught me to respect. The oldest ledger is cloth-bound, and the cloth has been mineralized, and it feels less like fabric now and more like a very soft stone, and the pages inside are yellow and stiff, and the handwriting in them is the oldest, a cramped angled script that writes the dates in a format I had to learn to read, and the numbers in a system that took me a week to decode, and the measurements in units that are not units I recognize but that correspond, I have determined, to the same temperatures and flow rates I record now, just expressed differently. The first record-keeper was measuring the same things I measure. The first record-keeper noticed the same patterns I notice, or different patterns in the same things, which is the same thing, or is not, and I cannot ask them, and the question does not need answering, because the record exists, and the record is what matters, and the record has been continuous, and I am part of the continuity, and this is enough.

The oldest ledger begins with a date that I think is forty-one years ago. I am not certain because the format is ambiguous and the page is stained with something — mineral deposit, water mark, a fingerprint that has been mineralized in place, a human thumbprint turned to stone on the corner of a page — and the first entry reads, in the cramped angled hand: *Spring 1, source, 61, flow steady, water clear, no odor change. Spring 2, source, 58, flow reduced from yesterday, water slightly cloudy. Basin A, 240 liters, evaporating normally. Basin B —* and it continues, and it has continued, through seven ledgers and four hands that I know of, and it continues now in my hand, the small precise hand that I was taught to write with somewhere else, in a place I barely remember, in a school that seems like a story I was told about someone else's life.

---

My day begins before the fog lifts. This is not a choice. The fog lifts when it lifts, and my body wakes before it lifts, and this has been true for as long as I have been here, and I have stopped questioning it. I wake and the ground is warm beneath me — my sleeping room is in a building adjacent to the office, and the concrete floor is warm, and the warmth is not from a heater but from below, and I sleep on a thin mattress on a frame that is also warm, and the warmth is the warmth of the earth, and it does not turn off, and I do not want it to turn off, and on the one occasion when I traveled north to the supply town and slept in a room where the floor was cold, I could not sleep, and I lay in the cold and I missed the warmth the way you would miss a person, and I came back, and the floor was warm, and I slept.

I dress. The clothing here is practical and I will not describe it in detail because it does not deserve detail. It is clothing. It covers the body. It becomes mineralized, like everything else, and you replace it when the road brings new supplies, and the old clothing goes into a pile behind the building, and the pile slowly becomes geological, and this is not a joke. I have seen a shirt that was discarded five years ago, and it is now a stiff white shape that holds the form of a shirt but is no longer a shirt in any functional sense. It is a mineral cast of a shirt. I do not find this disturbing. I find it interesting in the way that everything here is interesting if you watch it long enough, which is the only way to watch anything here, because nothing happens quickly, and the interest is in the slowness, and the slowness is the interest.

I walk to the office. The fog is low, and the mineral crust beneath my boots is damp, and the crunch is softer than it is in the afternoon, and I know this sound, and I know the exact point on the path where the crust becomes unreliable — three meters before the office door, where a subsurface channel has been widening for the past two years, and the crust there is thin, and I step over it, and I have stepped over it for two years, and one day it will break through entirely, and I will need to find a new path, and I will find a new path, and this will not be a crisis. It will be a change.

---

I record four things each morning. The temperature of each spring at its source. The flow rate, which I estimate by watching the channels and comparing them to a set of visual markers that someone — Maren, maybe, or the one before Maren — painted on stones at intervals along the main channels. The color of the water at each spring, which indicates mineral content in a way that is not precise but is consistent, and consistency is more useful than precision here because what I need to know is not the exact mineral content but whether the mineral content has changed, and change is visible in color before it is visible in any test. And the fourth thing: the state of the microbial mats, which is not a measurement but a description, and which I record in words rather than numbers, and which is, I think, the most important thing I write down, though I could not tell you why.

Today's entry, which I wrote an hour ago:

*Morning. Fog, light. Ground warm.*
*Spring 1: 61°. Flow steady, marker 3. Water clear, faint blue at source, no change. Mats: orange band at 2m from source, yellow band extending to 4m, green zone dense at margin, slightly wider than last week. Bubbling active in green zone, normal pattern.*
*Spring 2: 57°. Flow reduced, marker 2. Water cloudy, white particulate, calcium? Needs testing. Mats: orange band thinner than last week. Yellow band normal. Green zone unchanged. No bubbling observed today, which is unusual.*
*Spring 3: 63°. Flow increased, marker 4. Water clear. Mats: orange band wider, yellow band brighter, green zone very dense. New growth at eastern margin, area approx. 30cm², pale green, not seen before. Watch.*
*Basins A through E: normal evaporation. Residue in B and D ready for collection.*
*Generator: running. No change.*

I write this in ink, in the small precise hand, and the hand is mine, and the ink is dark, and the page is lined, and the ledger is the seventh, and I am maybe halfway through it, and when I finish it I will begin the eighth, and I do not have an eighth, and I will need to requisition one on the next supply run, and this is a small worry that sits in the back of my mind the way the generator's vibration sits in the back of the silence — present, not urgent, part of the texture.

---

The testing I do is crude. I know this. I have a set of glass vials and a few reagents that are brought in on the road, and I can test for calcium carbonate, for silica, for sulfates, for pH, and the tests tell me something, but not everything, and the something they tell me is usually the something I already knew from the color of the water. I record the test results anyway, because the tests have been done since before I came, and the results are part of the record, and the record is continuous, and continuity is the point.

But I have a confession, if a confession is a thing you make to a ledger that no one may ever read.

I have started recording things that are not in the protocol.

I record the flies. Not their number — you cannot count the flies, and trying would be absurd, and absurdity is not welcome here. But their density, in a way that is qualitative but consistent. *Flies heavy at Basin C this morning. Light at Spring 1. Normal at office.* I have been doing this for — I think — two years, and no one has asked me why, and no one has asked me to stop, and the record-keeper before me did not record the flies, or if she did, she recorded them in a way I have not recognized, in the margins, perhaps, in a shorthand I have not decoded. But I record them because I have noticed that the fly density correlates with something, and I do not know what it correlates with, and the correlation may be meaningless, and it may be meaningful, and I cannot tell the difference yet, and the only way to tell the difference is to record it, and so I record it.

I also record the fog. *Fog light. Fog heavy. Fog absent, unusual.* The fog is not in the protocol either. But the fog has been changing, or I think it has been changing, and the change is subtle, and the only way to know whether the change is real is to have written down what the fog was like on a given morning, and then to have written down what it was like on the morning a year later, and then to compare, and the comparison will tell me something, or it will not, and either way I will have the record, and the record is the point.

And once, three weeks ago, I recorded something that I have not told anyone, because there is no one to tell, and because telling would require explaining, and explaining would require a context that does not exist here, and the context that does not exist here is the context of wonder, and wonder is not a useful emotion in the Springs, and I am aware of this, and I am recording it anyway, and I am recording the recording of it, which is the kind of recursive thing that happens when you have been alone with a ledger for long enough.

What I recorded was this: *Spring 3, evening, after fog lift. Light on water at margin. Green mats visible. For approximately ten seconds, the light on the mats was not flat. It was directional. It came from the west, low, and it turned the green mats a color I do not have a word for. Not gold. Not amber. Something between, with a quality of depth that the flat light does not produce. Then the light returned to flat. The color disappeared. I do not know what I saw.*

I have not seen it again. I do not expect to. But I wrote it down, and the writing down is the thing, and the thing is the record, and the record continues.

---

The others. I should describe the others, because I am not the Springs, I am only the part of it that writes things down, and the other parts are other people, and they are also the Springs, and the Springs is all of them together.

There is the man who maintains the generator. I will not write his name, not because it is a secret but because names are not how I think of the people here, and I am aware that this is a limitation of mine, and the limitation is a product of the place, which erodes the specific in favor of the functional, and I do not know whether this is a loss or an adaptation. He is the generator man. He is heavy and quiet and he has been here longer than I have, and when he walks past the office in the morning he does not look in, and I do not look out, and this is not coldness. It is the communication of people who share a process and do not need to perform their sharing, who are sufficient in their co-presence, who understand that the generator and the ledgers are parts of the same system and the system does not require conversation to function.

But sometimes in the evening, when the fog is lifting and the light goes briefly gold, he sits on the concrete step outside the generator building, and I sit on the step outside the office, and we are forty meters apart, and neither of us speaks, and we watch the light change, and when the light goes flat again we go inside, and this is a companionship that does not have a name, and it does not need one, and if you asked me whether I am lonely I would not know how to answer, because the word requires a definition that I am not sure applies here, where the boundary between self and place has been softened by the mineral water and the flat light and the years, and the self is not a thing separate from the basin but a thing in the basin, and a thing in the basin cannot be lonely in the way a thing outside a basin can be lonely, because the basin is there, and the basin is company, and the basin does not leave.

There are the basin-workers. Three of them, usually, though the number shifts like everything shifts. They are the people who tend the evaporation basins, who channel the water, who scrape the crystalline residue when it is ready, who load it into containers. Their hands are the most mineralized, and they move in a way that is particular to them — a slow bending, a slow reaching, a slow scraping — and I have watched them from the office window, and the rhythm of their work is the rhythm of the water, which is to say it is not a rhythm but a flow, and the flow is continuous, and the work is continuous, and the residue builds and is scraped and builds again, and this is what they do, and they do it with the patience of the crust, which builds, which is scraped, which builds.

There are the children. I should say something about the children. I do not interact with them often — my work is the office and the springs and the ledgers, and their world is the terraces and the crust and the games they play, and our paths cross but do not merge. But I watch them, sometimes, from the office window, through the milky glass, and I see them running along the terrace rims with a balance that I envy and cannot replicate, and I see them catching flies in their fists and releasing them, and I see them standing at the edges of pools with their hands in the water, feeling the temperature with their skin, and I think: these are the people who understand the Springs in a way I never will, because they have never not understood it, and my understanding is acquired, and acquired understanding is always one step behind the understanding that is given, that is native, that grows in the body the way the microbial mats grow in the water — not by learning but by being.

They will be the record-keepers, or the basin-workers, or the generator-keepers, or they will leave and come back, or they will leave and not come back, and the Springs will continue, and the ledgers will continue, and the handwriting will change again, and this is the process, and the process does not require my understanding, and the process does not require my approval, and the process is warm, and the water rises.

---

I do not know how long I have been here.

This is not a thing I say for effect. This is a factual statement. I know it is more than five years because I have filled more than five years of ledger entries, and I know it is less than fifteen because the seventh ledger is not full, and I began the seventh ledger when I arrived, or shortly after I arrived, and the ledgers last approximately two years each, and seven ledgers at two years each is fourteen years, and the seventh is half full, so I have been here approximately — but the approximately is doing a lot of work in that sentence, and the work it is doing is the work of uncertainty, and the uncertainty is the truth, and the truth is that I do not know.

Time does something here. I have written this in the ledger, not as an observation but as a condition: *Time moves differently here. Or: time does not move differently here, but I move differently in it, and the difference is in me, not in the time.* The flat light does not mark the hours the way directional light does. The heat does not vary enough to signal seasons. The fog comes and goes but it comes and goes daily, not seasonally, and the daily repetition is a rhythm that does not accumulate, that does not build into a pattern of months, that stays in the scale of days, and the days stack up and the stacking is invisible, and one day you look at the ledger and you have written seven entries this week and seven entries last week and seven entries the week before, and the weeks are identical, and the identity is the thing, and the thing erodes your sense of where you are in the sequence, and the sequence is time, and time is what you have lost track of, and the losing is not a loss but a dissolution, and the dissolution is — I have written this before. I have written this before in a different ledger in a different year and the words were the same and the feeling was the same and the recognition was the same, and the recognition of the repetition is itself a repetition, and this is what the Springs does to the mind: it mineralizes it, slowly, layer by layer, the same entry over the same entry, the same observation over the same observation, until the mind is a sedimentary thing, and the sedimentation is not a problem but a condition, and the condition is stable, and the condition is warm.

---

I came from somewhere. I know this because I have the knowledge, the way I know the temperature of Spring 1 — not because I measured it but because it has been measured and the measurement is in the record. I came from the north. I came on the road. I came for a reason that I no longer remember, or that I remember but no longer recognize as a reason, because the reason was shaped by a kind of life that the Springs has dissolved, and the dissolution has been so complete that the reason now looks like something someone else once had, like a piece of clothing that was discarded behind a building and has been mineralized into a shape that no longer corresponds to anything wearable.

I think I was looking for work. I think I was looking for a place that was not the place I was in. I think I was — but the thinking is reaching for something that the mineral water has coated over, and the coating is thick enough that the reaching finds only a smooth surface, and the smooth surface is the present, and the present is the office and the ledgers and the thermometer and the flat light and the fog and the flies and the ground that is warm from below, and the present is sufficient, and sufficiency is the condition, and the condition is stable.

I do not miss the other place. This is not a strength. This is not a deficiency. It is a fact, and the fact is a product of the process, and the process is the mineralization of everything, including memory, including longing, including the sense that there should be something more than this, and the mineralization has been thorough, and the thoroughness is — I want to say *complete* but that would be a claim I cannot support, because I do not know what I will feel tomorrow, or next year, or in the ledger after this one, and the not-knowing is the record, and the record is what I keep, and what I keep is the Springs, and the Springs is what keeps me, and the keeping is mutual, and the keeping is warm.

---

In the evening, after I have made my final entry, I sit on the step outside the office, and I watch the light do what it does, which is: it goes gold, briefly, and then rose, and then violet, and then it goes flat, and then it goes dark, and in the dark the ground glows, faintly, and I have written about this in the ledger — *ground luminescence observed, intensity 2 (scale: 0-5, subjective), steady, no flicker* — and the glow is there, and it is mine, and it is not mine, and it is the earth's, and the earth does not need me to see it, and I see it, and the seeing is the record, and the record is what I have, and what I have is the Springs, and the Springs is what I am.

I am the ledger keeper. I write things down. The water rises, and I write it down. The mats change color, and I write it down. The flies are heavy, and I write it down. The light does something it has not done before, and I write it down. And someone, in a hand I cannot imagine, in a ledger I will never see, will read what I have written, and they will not know me, and they will know the water, and the water is the point, and the point is the record, and the record is continuous, and the record is warm.
