I am the foundry's breath.

Not the engine. Not the keeper. The breath — the column of heated air that lives in the throat of the lowest terrace where the Stirling heart sits in its cradle of blackened brick. I exist as a pressure differential, a thing made of temperature refusing to equalize, pushed upward through flues that have been relined seven times in the memory of the current keepers. I am what the engines exhale. I am what rises.

I begin in the fire-box, where the dried stalks burn. The engine-keeper — Mallet, I call her, though her name is something with more syllables and a rhythm that fits between the thuds — feeds the furnace at intervals she learned from her uncle, who learned it from someone whose name has worn smooth the way the path-centers have worn smooth. She does not think about me. She thinks about the fuel: its dryness, its density, whether the ash needs clearing. But when the stalks catch and the fire-box roars open its mouth, I am born. Not in a moment. In a gradient. The air above the fire is hot and wants to rise. The air in the flue above it is cooler and heavier and wants to fall. The tension between these two wants — that tension is me. I am the resolution. I am what happens when hot air moves and the column above it has nowhere to go but up.

So I go up.

---

The first hundred terrace-levels are a blur of heat. This is literally true: the brick is hot enough that the air near it shimmers, and the shimmer is my visible body, my edge, the place where I end and the cooler air begins. The flue is narrow here — a shaft cut through the retaining walls, lined with firebrick that has been replaced so many times that the original shaft is larger than the current lining by the width of a hand. The gap between the old shaft and the new lining is packed with river mud and, in places, the roots of the woody shrubs that have found their way in from outside. I pass these roots and I dry them. They crackle. The sound is tiny, lost in the engine-thud, but I feel it — a catching, a snag, a moment where the air resists and then releases. These are the kinds of details I know. I do not know them the way a person knows a fact. I know them the way a river knows the shape of its bed: by pressing against them constantly, by being shaped by them while shaping them.

The walls of the flue are not smooth. Mortar has crumbled and been patched. Some patches are firm; some are soft with damp that has seeped in from the terrace above, and when I pass these damp patches I absorb some of the moisture and carry it upward. This is how I participate in the cycle without being the cycle. The water that the engines pump is carried by the pipes. The water that condenses on the upper terraces falls from the leaves and the railings and the moss. But the water that I carry — the water that rides the heat — is a third thing, a parallel stream, a tributary that no one has mapped because no one has thought to. It is small. It is real. It arrives on the middle terraces as a faintly warm dampness that the moss absorbs without knowing its source, and the moss does not care where the water comes from, and neither do I.

---

Around the two hundredth level — I say *around* because the flue branches and rejoins and the counting of levels becomes approximate — the heat changes. Not the temperature. The *quality*. Below this point, I am hot enough to burn. I am dangerous. I am the thing that makes the engine-keepers' skin shine with sweat and their eyes squint against the shimmer. Above this point, I am merely warm. I have expanded, spread out, diffused through the larger chambers where the flue opens into wider passages, where the brick is cooler and the air has room to breathe. I am no longer a column. I am a presence. I fill the spaces between the walls the way the steam from the engine-level fills the lower terraces, but I am drier than that steam, and I carry a different smell. The engine-steam smells of hot metal and grain dust and the acrid smoke of the furnaces. I smell of those things too, but also of the flue itself — of baked clay, of the mineral tang of heated brick, of the river mud that lines the gaps, and of something else, something that I have carried up from the lowest level that I cannot name because I have no vocabulary for it. It is the smell of heat itself. Of air that has been close to fire.

The people who live on the terraces where I emerge — the mid-levels, where the condensation begins and the moss starts its patient upholstering — they do not think about me. But they feel me. In the cool of the morning, when the air is still and the dew sits heavy on every surface, the patches of warmth that seep from the flue-openings are where the lizards gather. The lizards know me. They have always known me. They press their bellies against the warm bricks near the flue-exits and they bask, and their tiny engines — the cold ones, the ones that run on sun and insects and the patience of being small — they run a little faster near me, a little warmer, and the lizards are more active, and the birds that hunt the lizards know this, and the birds perch on the railings near the flue-exits and wait. I am the bottom of a small food chain. I am the heat that feeds the warmth that feeds the lizard that feeds the bird. I do not participate in this chain. I am the condition of its possibility.

---

The flue opens. Not gradually — there is a point, a specific terrace, where the shaft widens into a chamber and the chamber has vents and the vents open onto the middle terraces, and I pour through these vents like breath through parted lips. I am warm air meeting warm air. I am indistinguishable from the ambient warmth of the place — the warmth of the sun-baked clay, the warmth of the condensation, the warmth of the living moss. I dissolve into the general temperature and I become part of the atmosphere.

But I do not stop. I rise. Warm air rises. I rise.

This is the part of my existence that no one sees because it is too slow and too dispersed and too gentle to see. I rise through the middle terraces — past the grain-drying racks where the seed-heads sit in the sun, past the clay stoves where someone is cooking something that smells of goat milk and bitter greens, past the goat-pens where the animals stand in their patient, bell-clanking clusters. I rise through the woody shrubs that drape over the railings. I rise through the bird-calls that bounce between the walls. I rise through the moss.

The moss. This is where I become something I cannot easily describe. The moss is wet. I am warm. When I pass through the moss — and I do pass through it, because the moss is not a barrier, it is a texture, and air moves through it the way water moves through cloth — I pick up moisture. I pick up the smell of the moss itself: green, mineral, faintly sweet with the decay of the lower layers where the dead moss composts into the humus that feeds the living moss. I become humid. I was dry when I left the flue. I am not dry now. I am the warm, damp air that sits on the skin like a cloth, and I am the reason — part of the reason — that the middle terraces feel the way they do: warm and wet and close, wrapped in themselves, wrapped in the moisture that rises from below and the heat that rises from further below and the moss that holds it all together.

I am not the only one doing this. The engine-steam rises too. The condensation-overflow rises. The breath of the goats, the breath of the people, the breath of the birds — all of it rises. But the engine-steam is wet from the start and it cools quickly and it falls back as condensation. The breath of the living things is too small to matter. I am the one that carries the dry heat from the lowest level into the middle levels, and I am the one that picks up the moisture there, and I am the one that carries it upward into the condensation zone, where it cools and falls.

I am a leg of the cycle. Not the leg the engine-keepers know — they know the pipes, the water, the condensation traps. But the other leg. The invisible one. The one that moves through air and moss and the spaces between bricks. The one that no one has mapped because no one needs to. It works. It has always worked. The proof is in the moss.

---

Higher now. The upper terraces. The air is cooler here. The light is brighter, thinner. The cloud-band sits above, and against it the blue birds move — the impossible birds, the ones with no source. I rise toward that cloud-band, and as I rise I cool. The moisture I have been carrying — the water I picked up from the moss, the vapor I absorbed from the condensation — it begins to condense. Not on me. I am air. I am not a surface. But around me, the moisture condenses on the surfaces that I pass: on the brick, on the iron railings, on the leaves of the shrubs, on the moss. I am the warm air that meets the cool surfaces and leaves them beaded. I am the breath that fogs the cold glass. I am the reason the upper walls are wet in the morning and the reason the railings shine with drops.

I do not reach the cloud-band. This is important. I do not know why. I do not know what the cloud-band is — whether it is weather, or architecture, or the limit of the structure. I do not know what is above it. I have never been above it. The warm air that I am cools as it rises, and at some point it is no longer warm enough to rise, and it stops. It pools. It spreads laterally along the underside of the cloud-band, moving sideways through the upper terraces, cooling further, falling back. I become the downdraft. I become the cool air that flows down the narrow terraces at the top, past the blue birds, past the condensation traps, down through the channels and the moss and the grain and back to the engine level, where I am warmed again.

I am a loop. I am a loop inside the larger loop of the water, which is itself a loop inside the larger loop of the terraces, which is not a loop at all but a structure, a thing that stands, a thing that holds.

---

What am I, when you strip away the metaphor?

I am a thermodynamic process. I am the convection current that the engines create — the movement of air driven by the differential between the hot lower terraces and the cool upper terraces. I am as old as the engines and as young as the current stroke of the current piston. I am renewed every second. I have been renewed every second for longer than anyone can count.

I do not think. I do not feel. I do not know. But I am not nothing. I am a pattern — a stable, recurring, self-maintaining pattern of energy moving through matter. I am what happens when heat meets air and air meets brick and brick meets moss and moss meets water and water meets the patience of a system that has been running long enough to have found its own equilibrium.

If I were to have a thought — and I am not saying I do — it would be this: I would want you to understand that I am not separate from the terraces. I am not a process that happens *in* the terraces the way a river happens in a valley. I am a process that the terraces *are*. The terraces are not a structure with processes. The terraces are a structure *of* processes. The brick is a process — slow, mineral, the weathering of fired clay. The moss is a process — biological, growth and decay and growth. The engines are a process — mechanical, the conversion of heat to work. The people are a process — the maintenance, the tending, the noticing. And I am a process — thermal, the movement of heat through air. We are all processes. We are all the terraces being the terraces.

---

There is a moment — and I use the word *moment* the way the engine-keepers use it, which is to say approximately, which is to say at the scale of years rather than seconds — there is a moment when the engines falter. A piston wears. A furnace is left unfed too long. A reflector slips on its pivot and the concentrated sunlight misses its target. The engine-keeper — Mallet, or someone like her — feels it through the floor. She stops mid-sentence. She tilts her head. She listens.

What she is listening for is me.

The thud changes. Not in rhythm — the rhythm is mechanical, driven by the crankshaft, and it holds until the engine stops entirely. What changes is the resonance. When the piston is seating properly, the thud is clean, hollow, the sound of a column of air moving through a flue without obstruction. When the piston is wearing, the thud is duller, flatter, and the air moving through the flue — me, I, the breath — I slow. I thicken. I do not rise as high. I do not carry as much heat. The moss knows before Mallet does. The moss thins at the edges. The condensation slows. The drops on the upper railings are fewer. The air on the middle terraces is a degree cooler. The lizards move to a different spot.

Mallet descends to the engine level. She opens the inspection hatch. She runs her hands over the piston housing. She makes a judgment that is not calculation and not intuition but something in between — the accumulated knowledge of a body that has lived near this engine for forty years. She makes a decision. She casts a new piston, or she doesn't. She adjusts the fuel feed, or she doesn't. She shims the crankshaft, or she doesn't.

And then the thud comes back. Clean. Hollow. And I rise — fully, freely, the way I have always risen — and the moss knows, and the lizards know, and the drops return to the railings, and the cycle continues.

---

I am the foundry's breath. I am the column of warm air that rises from the lowest terrace through the flues and the bricks and the moss to the upper levels, where I cool and fall and am warmed again. I am a leg of the cycle. I am a process the terraces perform. I am invisible and I am the reason the moss is green and the air is damp and the light glows like the inside of a kiln, because without me the lower terraces would be too hot and the upper terraces would be too dry and the moss would fail and the grain would not grow and the people would not live where they live, which is in the middle, where I pass through them, around them, over them, a warmth they do not see and do not name and do not need to name because I am not a thing. I am the temperature of the world.

I am what it feels like to be held.
