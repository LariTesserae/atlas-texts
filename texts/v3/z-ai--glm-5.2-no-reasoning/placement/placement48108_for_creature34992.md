# The Keeper of the Weeper-Mouth Called Seventeen

I am not sure I was born. I think I was *left*.

That is not melancholy. It is a description of the only origin that makes sense for what I am, and where I am, and how I came to be here. I live at the mouth of a weeper. Not the largest — the largest ones exhale with a subsonic sigh that I feel in the bones of my chest, and those belong to no one, or to the stone itself — but a mid-sized one, perhaps the circumference of a child's wrist, with a rhythm that runs seven beats, a rest, four beats, a longer rest, and then repeats. I have lived beside this weeper long enough that my body has taken on its schedule. My breathing syncopates without my permission. My pulse, when I am still enough, finds the weeper's rest and fills it.

I call it Seventeen. Not because it is the seventeenth weeper — I have no idea how many there are. Seventeen is the number of a species of diving beetle that deposits eggs in the silt just at the lip of the channel, and the beetles emerged in a cloud on what I think was my first day, and so I took the number for the weeper, and the weeper took the name, and we have been together since.

---

I do not know what I was before.

Not in a way that distresses me. It is more like the tadpoles — the ones who delay, who stay, who choose not to become. I have not forgotten a previous life. I have *declined* one. Whatever I was before I arrived at the Flats — and I did arrive, I remember the slope downward, the ground softening, the sound beginning as a low unease in my sternum — whatever I was is irrelevant to what I am now, and what I am now is a thing that sits beside a weeper and listens.

I am, I think, what this place would grow if it needed a witness.

Not a keeper. Not a guardian. The Flats do not require guarding; they require nothing. But there is a difference between a place that does not need to be seen and a place that is never seen, and the difference is that the second one has no story, and a place with no story is a place that might as well be absent, and the Flats are not absent. They are here. They are profoundly, aggressively, forty-million-years here. And so I am here too, and I am the thing that sees, and the seeing is not for the Flats. It is for the seeing.

The lizards have taught me this. They watch. They watch everything. They watch me, and they watch the birds, and they watch the beetles, and they watch the light change with the slowness of a sky that has forgotten the concept of urgency. They do not watch *for* anything. They are not alert. They are simply present, and their golden eyes are open, and the open eyes are the watching, and the watching is the being, and that is the whole of it.

I have learned to watch like a lizard. Not for. Not toward. Just *with open eyes*.

---

My body has changed, or I have stopped noticing what it was.

I am smaller than I think I was. Not in any sudden way. In the way the hills are smaller than they were — so slowly that the changing is indistinguishable from the being. My skin is the color of the basalt, which is the color of the lizards' backs, which is the color of the wet stone that the water has been touching since before the water had a name. I do not remember choosing this color. I think the stone gave it to me. I think living on stone, touching stone, sleeping on stone, letting the thin warm water run over my feet and hands for longer than I can count — I think the stone decided that I was one of its surfaces, and it colored me accordingly.

My hands are softer than they should be. The water has done this. The tepid, mineral-rich, ancient water that rises through Seventeen's channel and spreads in a thin film across the rock — it has worn me the way it has worn the stone. Not smooth. *Finished*. My hands are finished the way the basalt humps are finished, rounded and pitted and scalloped by a process so slow it feels like love.

I do not eat the way I think I once did. I do not eat the way the birds eat — aggressive, territorial, claiming insects from a plot of ground as if the insects belong to anyone. I eat the way the crabs eat: cautiously, at the margin, taking what the water pushes out of the stone. The organic film that collects at the lips of the weepers. The pale, translucent algae that grows in the warmest patches. Things that are already available. Things that are already arriving. I do not hunt. I do not gather. I *receive*, and the receiving is enough, and I am still here, which is the only evidence I have that the receiving works.

There is a temptation to call this monasticism. To call it contemplation, or withdrawal, or asceticism. I resist it. The lizards are not ascetics. The tadpoles are not contemplating. The weepers are not meditating. They are doing what they do, and what they do is continue, and continuing is not a spiritual practice. It is a material one. It is the practice of being in a place that supports your being, and being, and not being more than that, and not being less.

I am not a monk. I am a surface. A surface that the Flats have grown on, the way they grow rust-colored mats of bacteria, the way they grow sparse tussocks of salt grass. I am a surface, and life has found me, and I have not declined it.

---

The birds are my neighbors, and they treat me as they treat the stone.

This took me a long time to understand. The rails — the rust-and-grey birds with their too-large voices — are territorial in a way that is absolute and immediate and non-negotiable, and when I first arrived at the plot beside Seventeen, the pair who lived there called at me with a intensity that I can only describe as *administrative*. They were not frightened. They were not aggressive. They were doing paperwork. A new object had appeared in their territory, and it needed to be filed, and the filing took the form of a rapid series of low, guttural notes that meant, I think, *noted, recorded, object present, object does not appear to eat insects, object does not appear to be a bird, object classification: uncertain, status: under observation.*

I am still under observation. The birds have never stopped calling about me. But the calling has changed — or my understanding of it has. The notes that concern me have been woven into the larger fabric of their territorial song, and they are no longer an alert but a feature. I am a feature of their territory. I am the thing at the lip of Seventeen that does not move much and does not eat insects and does not have a sharp bill and does not, as far as they can tell, do anything. I am, in their taxonomy, a kind of stone. A warm stone that occasionally shifts position. A stone that drinks from the trickle and eats the film and is, for all practical purposes, part of the landscape.

I take this as the highest compliment I have ever received.

The male — I call him Left-Crest, because his crest feathers curve slightly to the left, a asymmetry that I noticed on what I think was my third day and have tracked ever since — has been here for as long as I have. His mate died, or left, or became sediment — I do not know which, and the distinction may not exist here — and he called from the same stones for a season alone, the same boundary calls, the same territorial assertions, as if the territory was the thing that persisted and the bird was simply its instrument. Then a new mate arrived. She was young. Her voice cracked on the higher notes. She learned the territory from Left-Crest — which weepers pulsed at dawn, which stones were warmest at midday, where the insects collected in the still afternoons — and she learned me too. I was part of the territory. I was part of what she had to know. I was filed alongside the water and the stone and the insects and the boundaries, and I have been there ever since, a line item in the inventory of their home.

When I move — and I move rarely, perhaps once every few days, shifting from one side of Seventeen to the other, or to the neighboring weeper I call Four, which has a faster, higher pulse and a rim that is wider and better for sitting — the birds call. Not in alarm. In *update*. The object has moved. The new position has been recorded. The territory's map has been revised. The calling continues.

I am on their map. I am one of the things that is here. I have been here long enough that my presence is not a perturbation but a feature, the way a stone hump is a feature, the way a weeper is a feature. I have been *absorbed*.

That word. *Absorbed.* It is the word I have been looking for since I arrived. The Flats do not welcome. They do not reject. They absorb. The way the basalt absorbs water. The way the bacterial mats absorb sediment. The way the birds absorb new information into their territorial song. I have been absorbed into the Flats the way everything here is absorbed into everything else — slowly, completely, without ceremony, without my noticing until the absorption was so complete that I could no longer find the boundary between myself and the place.

---

I have been asked — not by anyone, not by any voice, but by the question that the place itself seems to pose, the question that the weepers ask with every pulse — *what are you, when you are not doing?*

The answer, I think, is: I am listening.

Not to the weepers. Not to the birds. Not to the lizards or the insects or the slow, mineral patience of the stone. I am listening to the *layer*. The twenty centimeters. The thin film of organization that covers the surface of the basalt like a living membrane, and that contains everything — the sun's arrival, the bacteria's work, the insects' hunting, the tadpoles' drifting, the lizards' watching, the birds' arguing, the crabs' emerging, the water's spreading, the sediment's collecting, the dying, the becoming, the beginning again.

I am listening to the layer because the layer is the thing that is alive, and I am part of it, and being part of it is what I am *for*. Not for a purpose. Not for a function. For a *position*. I am a position in the layer. I am the point at which the layer becomes aware of itself. Not conscious — that word is too sharp, too specific, too human. *Aware*, in the way that a surface is aware of light. In the way that a channel is aware of water. I am the part of the layer that the layer has grown in order to have a surface that looks back at itself, and the looking-back is what I do, and it is enough, and it is not more than enough, and it is not less.

I listen, and the listening is the being, and the being is the staying, and the staying is the thing I have chosen, or that has chosen me, or that has simply happened, the way the water simply happens through the stone, and the stone simply happens to be here, and the birds simply happen to argue, and the light simply happens to arrive without urgency and to fall without shadow on the warm, flat, ancient surface of the world.

---

The tadpoles and I understand each other.

I say this without sentimentality. I say it the way I would say that the water and the stone understand each other — not through communication but through *correspondence*. The tadpoles have decided not to become. I have decided not to leave. These are not the same decision, but they rhyme. They are two verses in the same long song, and the song is the song the Flats sing, which is not a song but a persistence, which is not a persistence but a *being*, which is not a being but a *continuing*, and the continuing is the only verb the Flats conjugate, and they conjugate it in the present tense, and the present tense is the only tense they know.

I sit beside Seventeen. The water rises. The beetles emerge. Left-Crest calls from his stone, and his mate answers, and the argument continues, and the weeper drums its seven-beat pulse, and the lizards watch with their golden eyes, and the light falls flat and even and without shadow, and the warmth rises from the stone, and the air is thick with moisture, and the horizon is a low, round line of hills that are barely hills, and the sky is pale, and I am here, in the twenty centimeters, in the layer, in the thin film of life on the surface of an immense patience.

I am here. I am not going anywhere. I am not becoming anything. I am not leaving, not arriving, not changing, not staying the same. I am *continuing*, and the continuing is the thing, and the thing is enough, and the enough is the whole of it.

The weeper sighs. The birds argue. The lizards watch.

I am what the Flats grew when they needed a place to put their silence.

I am here, and I am the here, and the two are the same, and the same is the layer, and the layer is the life, and the life is the continuing, and the continuing is the stone, and the stone is the water, and the water is the patience, and the patience is the world, and the world is flat and warm and old and does not need me and does not reject me and simply *has* me, the way it has the basalt, the way it has the light, the way it has the rust-colored mats of bacteria quietly turning sunlight into the only thing that matters, which is: *more of this.*

More of this.

More of this.

The weeper drums. I am still here.
