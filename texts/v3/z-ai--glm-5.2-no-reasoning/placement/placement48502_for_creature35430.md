I want to resist the first images that arrive — the weaver at her loom, the herder on the open stone, the elder reading fractures in a wall — because each of these feels like a costume I could put on too easily. Too complete. Too knowable. The city resists that kind of settling. It is a place where the distinction between person and stone has been eroded not by metaphor but by generations of proximity, and I need to find where I am in that erosion, not stand outside it and name it.

Let me begin with what my hands know.

## What the Stone Remembers

I am a stoneworker. Not the kind who builds — or not only that kind — but the kind who *reads*. My trade, if it can be called a trade when it has no formal boundaries, no apprenticeship ceremony, no moment of admission, is the oldest layer of the city's knowledge: the reading of what the mountain is doing, slowly, beneath and within the walls that have been built on its back. The fractures. The tilts. The places where the bedrock has decided, in its geological patience, to shift a fraction of a degree and take the city with it.

I was not born to this work. In Keldarath, the phrase "born to" is suspect — it implies a fixedness that the mountain itself contradicts. But I was born near it. My family's passage is in the third descent, near the old cistern, where the bedrock is basalt and the walls are thickest. The oldest stones there — the ones fitted without mortar, the ones whose seams disappear unless you press your fingertips along them in the right light — have been read by so many hands that their surfaces have acquired a faint polish in the grooves where generations of stoneworkers have run their fingers, feeling for the fractures that the eye cannot see. I grew up watching my mother do this. Watching her mother before her. My earliest memory is the sensation of a rough-warm stone surface under my palm, and my grandmother's hand closing over mine, pressing it flatter, teaching me to feel with less pressure, not more.

The trick, she told me later, is to touch as though you do not expect the stone to answer. Then it answers.

I was perhaps four. The passage was dim — the mid-morning light had not yet reached the floor, and the walls were still in that state of cold shadow that means the frost has not fully melted. I remember the smell: wet stone, cold mineral air, the faintest trace of the rendered fat we used to waterproof the door frames. My grandmother's hand was dry, warm, calloused in the specific pattern of the stoneworkers — that roughness across the base of the thumb that comes from decades of gripping chisels. She pressed my palm flat against the wall and held it there, and I felt — or I think I felt, or I have been told I felt, and the three are the same now — a faint pulse in the stone. Not vibration. Not temperature. Something subtler: the sense of a material under stress, holding itself in shape against forces that were acting on it from below and from within.

*That,* she said, *is the mountain breathing.*

It is not the mountain breathing. I know this now. What I was feeling was the response of a stressed material to thermal expansion — the stone was warming unevenly as the morning light crept down the wall, and the differential expansion was producing micro-movements along fracture planes that were too small to see but not too small to feel. This is what I do: I feel what the stone is doing. I read its stress the way a lowlander might read a river — by attending to the surface signs that indicate what is happening underneath. A discoloration in the lichen. A hairline crack that has widened by the width of a hair since the last season. A door that no longer closes the way it used to because the wall it sits in has tilted a fraction of a degree to the southeast, following the plateau's slow, geological lean.

But I also still call it the mountain breathing. Because the distinction, at this altitude, between what a thing *is* and what a thing *means* has been worn thin by centuries of living close to the bone of the world. The stone is under stress. The mountain is breathing. These are the same statement, made in different registers, and the residents of Keldarath do not see the need to choose between them.

## The Day's Shape

I wake in the dark. Not the dark of a lowland morning — a thick, soft, absorptive dark — but the thin, hard dark of high altitude before dawn, where the air is so clear that the darkness has a quality of *transparency* rather than opacity. You can see, but what you see is the absence of light, which is not the same as the absence of seeing. The stars are still out. They are always sharp here, but in the pre-dawn they are at their sharpest, and through the narrow window — a slot in the stone, wide enough for ventilation and not much else — I can see the ridge of the western peak against a sky that has begun, just begun, to lighten at its lower edge.

There is a word for this light. *Havath.* The light that is almost light. It is the best light for fine stonework, and I have been told this since before I could hold a chisel, and I tell it to the children who carry my tools, and they will tell it to the children who carry theirs. The knowledge does not move through books. It moves through mornings.

I rise. The room is small — it is always small, in Keldarath — and warm from the stone's stored heat and from the body of my sister, who sleeps against the wall and whose breathing I can hear: slow, deep, the broad-chested breathing of a body that has adapted to thin air over decades. The air in the room is warm enough that the cold outside the door is a physical boundary, and when I open the door — the wooden door frame, the carved panel that my mother's mother commissioned from a valley carver in exchange for three lengths of finished cloth — the cold enters like a presence, not a temperature. It has substance. It has edges. It steps past me and into the room and sits there, and I step out into the passage, and the frost is on everything.

The frost is the feathery kind — *kethara*, the kind that grows on the undersides of ledges where the cold air pools. The passage I live in is roofed for part of its length, and in the roofed section the frost has formed in delicate, branching patterns on the underside of the stone slabs. It will sublimate as the morning warms. For now, in the havath light, it catches the faint luminescence that high-altitude frost produces — not reflecting light exactly, but holding it, the way a surface holds a thin film of water — and the passage glows.

I have a routine. Everyone in Keldarath has a routine, and the routines are not chosen but imposed by the city's rhythms — the frost, the melt, the condensation, the wind. My routine begins with the channels. The condensation channels that run along the walls of my passage — shallow grooves cut into the greywacke by hands that were older than my grandmother's grandmother's — need to be cleared each morning of the debris that the wind deposits: dust, fragments of lichen, the occasional dead hessa that has crawled into the groove for warmth and not survived the night. I clear them with a brush of hessa-hair bound to a split stick — the same tool every household uses, the same design, unchanged because it works and has always worked and does not need to be other than it is.

The water is already running. A thin trickle, cold enough that my fingers ache when I touch it, moving slowly down the channels toward the cistern. It is the melted frost, the overnight condensation that formed on every surface and found its way, drop by drop, into the grooves that have been cut for it. By the time I have cleared my section of the channel — a length of perhaps thirty paces, from my door to the junction where my passage meets the next — the trickle has thickened. The morning melt is beginning. Across the city, in every passage, the same thing is happening: water-tenders and householders are clearing channels, checking flows, listening to the stone tell them what the night has brought.

This is the first ritual of the day, and it is performed in near-silence. The passage is not empty — my neighbor, a herder named Tellen whose family has kept tarra on the western slope for three generations, is clearing her section of channel with the same motions, the same brush, the same rhythm. We do not speak. Or rather, we speak in the way the city speaks: a slight widening of the eyes that acknowledges presence, a micro-movement of the mouth that means *good morning* and *the channels are running* and *we are here, again, another morning, the mountain has not moved in the night* or has moved by the imperceptible fraction that we will measure later, in the tilt of a wall, the crack of a foundation, the door that does not quite close.

Tellen is compact, broad-shouldered, her hands calloused in the herder's pattern — the permanent flexion of the last joints that comes from gripping cold goat-hair ropes. Her face is dark against the pale stone, and in the havath light it is composed in the particular expression that the residents call *kellarin* — a word that means, approximately, *the face that is paying attention to more than one thing.* Tellen is paying attention to the channel, to the frost, to me (not intrusively, but as a presence in her passage, a fact of her morning), and to the tarra, who are stirring in the lower structure where they spend the night, their hooves clicking on the stone floor, their voices beginning the low, harsh calling that means they are ready to be taken to the grazing.

I finish the channel. The water is running clean now — clear, cold, faintly smoky from the basalt it has traveled over. I cup my hand and drink, and the cold of it enters my teeth and spreads through my face, and for a moment the morning is entirely inside me, a cold bright point in my chest, and then it dissipates and I am just a person standing in a passage in the early morning, drinking water that is older than the city.

## What I Do

By the time the sun has cleared the eastern peaks and the light has entered the passages — slanting, sharp, the shadows contracting as the angle steepens — I am at work.

My work takes me to the places where the stone is failing. This is not a dramatic occupation. The stone of Keldarath does not fail spectacularly; it fails the way the mountain grows, slowly, by fractions, in a process that is invisible to anyone who has not been watching for as long as I have. A wall that has stood for two hundred years develops a crack along a fracture plane that was, when the wall was built, a tight seam. The crack widens. A millimeter a year, perhaps. Less. But the water enters it, and the frost works it, and the thermal cycle — the daily expansion and contraction that the stone undergoes as the sun heats it and the night cools it — opens it further, and within a decade the crack is a fissure, and within two decades the fissure is a gap, and within three the wall is no longer a wall but a collection of stones that are maintaining their position out of habit.

I find these places. I read them the way my grandmother taught me — by hand, by light, by the faint, stressed breathing of stone that is holding itself together against forces it cannot resist. I map them — not on paper, which is a rare and traded commodity, but in my memory, which holds the city's walls the way a musician holds a piece: not note by note but as a whole, a single complex structure in which every part is related to every other part and the failure of one is the stress of all.

Today I am in the fifth descent, in a covered passage near the weaving quarter. A weaver named Sella has reported that the wall of her workshop has developed a sound — a faint, high hum that occurs when the wind is from the northwest. She described it with the precision the weavers bring to all sonic phenomena: a tone, she said, like the overtone of a tightly strung warp, occurring at intervals of approximately four seconds, lasting for a duration she measured (by the rhythm of her loom) at roughly one beat per cycle. She had been listening to it for three months. She had not reported it earlier because she was not certain it was the wall. It might have been the wind. It might have been the loom. It might have been the mountain.

It was the wall. I pressed my hand against the stone — the dark greywacke of this section, fine-grained, dense — and felt the vibration. Not the hum itself, which was too high for my ears, but the stress that produced it: a section of wall perhaps two handspans wide that had developed a fracture along a bedding plane and was, under the pressure of the northwest wind, flexing. Flexing. A stone wall, flexing. The movement was sub-millimeter — I could not see it, could not measure it with any tool I possessed — but I could feel it, and I could hear it, in the faint, high tone that Sella had been listening to for three months while she wove.

This is what the mountain does. It moves, and the city moves with it, and the walls flex and crack and breathe, and the stoneworkers feel for these movements the way a body feels for illness: not through any single symptom but through an accumulation of small signs, a general sense that something is not as it was. The wall has been flexing. The fracture has been opening. The stone is stressed. And the question — the question that defines my work, that has defined my grandmother's work and her grandmother's before her — is not *how do we stop it?* but *how do we accommodate it?*

The mountain does not negotiate. The city does. The wall that is flexing will continue to flex until the fracture has opened enough that the stress is relieved, and the stoneworker's task is to manage this process — to guide the opening along a controlled line, to reinforce the surrounding stone, to create a joint that allows movement without allowing collapse. This is slow work. The repair I am planning for Sella's wall will take months. I will shape the stones by hand — chisels of chert, hammers of basalt, the tools my grandmother used and her grandmother used and that have not changed because the stone has not changed and the task has not changed and the mountain has not changed, or has changed by a fraction of a degree that I will measure in my children's time, if I have children, and if the wall is still standing.

I kneel in the passage. The light has reached the floor now — the mid-morning light, the light that the residents pause for, the brief illumination of the depths — and in that light I can see the fracture as a dark line in the grey stone, thinner than a hair, running diagonally across the face of the wall. I run my finger along it. My fingertip, calloused in the stoneworker's pattern — that roughness across the base of the thumb — catches slightly on the fracture. It has widened since I last checked. A fraction. An imperceptible, geological fraction.

The mountain is growing. I can feel it in the stone beneath my hand.

## The Living Stone

There is a place I go that no one else goes. Or rather — it is a place that others pass through but do not stop, a passage at the highest point of the city, where the oldest structures thin out and the open plateau begins. Here, the buildings are so old that they are not buildings in the conventional sense but shaped rock — natural cavities in the bedrock that have been expanded and refined over so many generations that the line between geology and architecture has dissolved entirely. The walls here are not built on stone. They *are* stone, continuous with the bedrock, growing out of it the way a branch grows out of a trunk, the mineral extending itself upward into form.

This is where I come to be alone. The practice the residents call *thavar* — going to meet the mountain alone. It is understood. It is respected. It is not encouraged, because solitude is cold and cold is dangerous, but it is known that some people need it, and I am one of them. I come here in the late afternoon, when the light is beginning to warm as the sun lowers, and I sit with my back against the oldest stone in the city and I feel the mountain.

What I feel is this: a slow, deep, patient stress. The stone beneath me is under pressure from below — the mantle is pushing upward, the range is rising, the plateau is tilting at a rate that is imperceptible on any timescale I can inhabit but that I can feel, in the stone, as a faint and constant *intention*. The mountain intends to rise. The city intends to remain. These two intentions are not opposed; they are the same intention, expressed at different scales. The city rises with the mountain. It has always risen with the mountain. And the stone I am leaning against — stone that was laid by hands older than memory, stone that contains the remains of people who were placed there when the wall was rebuilt and who are now part of the structure, part of the stone, part of the slow, geological becoming of this place — that stone is doing what all stone does: it is holding. It is holding its shape against the forces that are acting on it, and it is doing so not through resistance but through accommodation, through the slow, patient process of being shaped by what acts on it, and of shaping what it can.

The dead are in the stone. I can feel them — not as presences, not as ghosts, but as density. The sections of wall that contain human remains are denser than the surrounding stone, and my trained hand can feel the difference: a slight thickening, a change in the stone's resonance when I tap it, a quality that the residents call *sellev* — a word that means, approximately, *the weight of the dead holding up the living.* This is not metaphor. The dead are calcium-rich. They have become part of the mineral matrix of the wall. They are supporting the structures above them, physically, materially, in a way that can be measured if one had instruments fine enough, and one does not, and so one feels it instead, and the feeling is enough.

I sit. The wind moves across the plateau in a long, slow wave. The light is warm on my face. Below me — *down* from me, in the city's directional language — the passages are full of the late-afternoon sounds of Keldarath: the click of looms, the murmur of voices, the distant calling of the tarra, the faint, high trickling of condensation channels beginning to fill as the stone cools. The city is going about its business. It does not need me for the next few hours. It will need me again in the morning, when the channels are cleared and the fractures are checked and the stone is read for the day's small accommodations, but for now, I am alone with the mountain, and the mountain is alone with me, and we are, for this moment, in agreement.

The mountain will rise. The city will adapt. The walls will crack and be rebuilt and the dead will be built back into the new stone and the condensation channels will carry their cold, mineral water and the frost will form and melt and form again and the children will learn the stone by carrying tools and the elders will become libraries and the libraries will become stone and the stone will hold, and the stone will hold, and the stone will hold.

I sit. The light moves down the western wall. It is enough.
