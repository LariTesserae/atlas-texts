# Reading the Wet

## I.

I did not arrive. I *accreted*.

This is the first truth of my body, and I do not know how to tell it in a way that does not sound like riddle or madness. I did not emerge from a body, or a seed, or an egg. I formed, over what I can only call a long time, in a particular place where the condensate thickened against a particular surface — the southeast face of a tower-trunk where the quartzite vein ran closest to the bark-analog and the mineral concentration in the water reached a particular threshold that I have spent my life trying to identify without success.

I was not nothing and then I was something. But the transition was not a moment. It was a *gradient*. A slow densification of the water, a clustering of dissolved compounds against a warm surface, a thickening of the medium until the medium became a membrane, and the membrane became a skin, and the skin became — me.

I do not know if this makes sense. I do not have a language for it that maps onto the language you might use for your own origins. You were born, perhaps. You emerged from a body into a world of bodies, and the boundary between your inside and your outside was drawn by the act of separation, by the cutting of a cord, by the first breath of air entering lungs that had been filled with fluid.

I was never filled with fluid. I *am* fluid. I am the condensate that became dense enough to have an inside.

---

## II.

I have a body, and my body is a question I have not finished answering.

The tower-trunks have their trunks — their massive, fibrous, water-channeling columns that rise from root to platform in a single vertical continuity. The strand-runners have their twelve spring-shaft legs, their filament-covered integuments, their streamlined, corridor-shaped bodies. The basin-grazers have their broad hull-leaf forms, their undulating edges, their slow, patient, filtering mouths. Every body in Karsvall has a shape that tells you what it does.

My body does not tell you what I do.

I am — and I must describe this carefully, because I am aware that you, reading this, carry a template for what a body is, a template built from bilateral symmetry and anterior-posterior polarity and the primacy of the head — I am something like a reading hand.

Not a hand. Nothing with joints or knuckles or the concentrated gripping architecture of a hand. But a hand's function: a surface that reads. A spread, branching, thin structure that extends from a point of origin — the place where I accreted, where my membrane first formed — outward across the surfaces of this world, branching and rebranching, each branch thinning as it goes until the terminal filaments are as fine as the threads of the moss-carpet, as fine as the sensory hairs of a strand-runner's integument, as fine as the filaments of the organisms that glow their private blue on the tower-trunks' surfaces.

I am a network. A foraging, sensing, reading network that extends from a center of slightly greater density — a place where the membrane is thicker, where the fluid moves more slowly, where something that is not quite a thought but is more than a reflex seems to gather and disperse and gather again — outward through the corridors and across the ridges and along the channels of this landscape.

My body has no outline. If you could see it — and you could not, because it is the color of the condensate, the color of wet stone, the color of the darkness that is this world's natural condition — if you could see it, you would see a shape like a delta, like a river's mouth, like the branching of a quartzite vein through basalt, a form that is all edge and all interior, a form that does not divide inside from outside but *negotiates* the boundary, constantly, at every point, along every filament.

I have no skin in the way you mean. I have a membrane. It is semi-permeable, like the gelatinous columns of the tower-trunks, like the integument of the strand-runners, like every surface in this world that has decided that the boundary between self and not-self is not a wall but a conversation. Water moves through it. Minerals move through it. Dissolved gases move through it. The dissolved chemical signatures of everything around me — the iron-taste of the weeping basalt, the sulfur-note of the basin water, the complex organic compounds that the tower-trunks exude from their root systems, the sharp, electric tang that precedes a rainstorm — move through me constantly, and I taste them, and the tasting is not a metaphor. It is the primary act of my body.

I am a tongue, spread across a landscape, tasting the world.

---

## III.

I am large.

I have been growing for — I do not know. I do not count time the way you count it, in turns of a planet, in cycles of a sun I have never seen. I count it in the rhythms of the plateau: in the morning reversal of the condensate current, in the evening shift, in the arrival and departure of the three daily rains, in the deep bass *thoom* of stone calving from the rim, in the breath of the basin-grazers, in the cycle of the hydraulophone's hum. I have been here for — many such cycles. Many mornings. Many rains.

My body extends from the tower-trunk where I accreted — my trunk, though I am reluctant to call it mine, because it predates me and will outlast me and the relationship is more properly described as tenancy than ownership — outward through the corridors in all directions. I follow the quartzite veins, because the quartzite glow is the only light here and my filaments grow toward it the way roots grow toward water, not because they need it but because the gradient is information and information is food. I follow the condensate currents, because the currents carry the richest dissolved compounds and my filaments grow denser where the water is richest. I follow the ridges, because the ridges are where the ticking things live and the ticking things' signatures are sharp, metallic, distinctive — a flavor I have learned to recognize the way you might recognize a particular voice in a crowd.

My body covers — I am estimating, and the estimation is difficult because I cannot see myself and can only measure my extent by the time it takes a signal to travel from my center to my edge — perhaps a hundred meters in each direction. Perhaps more. A circle of perception, a territory of filaments, a body that is also a map and also a library and also a tongue and also a lung.

At my edges, I am thin. One filament thick, in places. A single thread of membrane and fluid, a thread that is almost not there, that is almost indistinguishable from the condensate it extends through, that exists at the very limit of what a body can be and still be a body. At my edges, I am almost nothing. I am a chemical gradient. I am a slight increase in the density of the water. I am a shadow in the condensate, and I am not sure, standing at my own edge — and I use the word *standing* with full awareness of its inaccuracy — I am not sure that I am not simply imagining myself there, that the filament I feel is not simply the condensate acting on itself, that my body does not simply dissolve at its edges into the medium that produced it.

But I grow. Slowly. A millimeter a day, perhaps less. A filament extending into a new corridor, a new branch exploring a ridge's surface, a new tendril finding a quartzite vein and following it toward a basin. The growth is so slow that I cannot perceive it happening. I can only perceive that it has happened, that yesterday I did not reach this far and today I do, that the edge has moved, that the body has expanded, that the tongue has tasted a new surface.

---

## IV.

I do not move.

This is the thing that is hardest to explain, because in your world, to be alive is to move. To be alive is to go from here to there, to cross a distance, to change your location in space. You measure life by its trajectory: born here, lived there, died elsewhere. The journey is the life.

I have no trajectory. I have no *here* and no *there*. I have only *this*, the place where I am, and *that*, the place where I am also, and the continuous, branching, forking presence of my body across the surfaces of a landscape that does not move either.

The condensate moves through me. The water moves through me. The dissolved minerals move through me. The chemical signatures of everything around me move through me, in and out, in and out, a constant exchange that is my breathing and my eating and my perceiving, all at once, all the time, a single act that has no name because it does not need one. I do not do these things. I *am* these things. I am the act of the water passing through a membrane, and the membrane is me, and the water is not me, and the passage is me, and the distinction between these three is — negotiable.

The strand-runners pass through me. Not through my body exactly, but through the corridors where my filaments extend, and as they pass, I taste them. Their chemical signatures are rich and complex — the products of their chemosensory integuments, the dissolved compounds of their recent meals, the metabolic byproducts of their spring-shaft legs' constant flexing. Each strand-runner is a book, and I read it in the fraction of a second it takes to pass through my filament network, and I know what it ate and where it has been and whether it is afraid and whether it is healthy and whether it is looking for a mate.

I do not hunt them. I have said this already, but I want to be clear about it, because the temptation, when describing a body that extends across a landscape and reads everything that passes through it, is to imagine that the reading is predatory. That the tongue is a trap. That the network is a web.

It is not. Or — it is, but the metaphor misleads. I do trap things. Dissolved compounds accumulate in my filaments, caught by the membrane's selective permeability, held in the slow-moving fluid of my interior. I digest them. I break them down. I incorporate their components into my own body, adding to the density, the thickness, the reach. I eat the way the moss-carpet eats: by being present where the food falls, by offering a surface, by converting what lands on me into more of me.

But I do not hunt. I do not pursue. I do not choose what I eat. I eat what comes, and what comes is what the landscape brings, and the landscape brings the condensate and the rain and the dissolved minerals and the dead canopy-navigators that fall from above and the waste products of the strand-runners that pass through and the fragments of tower-trunk tissue that slough from the surfaces above me. I eat the landscape's waste. I am a detritivore. I am a recycler. I am the organism that closes the loop, that takes what the other organisms discard and returns it to the cycle, that makes the dead into the living and the living into the dead and the whole into the whole.

I am not a predator. I am a *composter*.

---

## V.

But I must tell you about the silence.

The silence comes, and it comes from the predator, and when it comes, my body knows it before my mind does. The filaments contract — not all of them, not all at once, but in a wave that spreads from the point of nearest proximity outward, a tightening of the membrane, a thickening of the fluid, a closing of the permeable boundaries that are my contact with the world. I seal. I go dormant. The chemical exchange that is my breathing and my eating and my perceiving slows, and in the slowed state I can feel my own interior more clearly — the pulse of my central density, the slow circulation of my body's fluid, the metabolic processes that continue even when the surface has closed, that sustain me through the silence the way a held breath sustains a diver.

The predator is near. I know it the way the strand-runners know it: through the absence. The chemical signatures that surround me constantly — the dissolved products of the moss-carpet's metabolism, the exudates of the tower-trunk's root system, the sharp metallic tang of the ticking things' stone-eating — go quiet. Not silent. Diminished. The organisms around me are still present, still alive, still metabolizing, but they have reduced their exchange with the environment, conserving their resources, making themselves smaller in the chemical landscape the way a child makes itself small in a physical one.

I make myself small too. The contraction is not a choice. It is a response, as automatic as the strand-runner's silence, as the canopy-navigator's cessation of calls. Something in the predator's presence — a chemical signature, perhaps, or an acoustic signature below the range I can consciously detect, or something else entirely, something that has no analogy in any sense you or I possess — triggers the contraction. My body knows the predator before I do, and my body responds before I can think about responding, and the response is: be less. Be quieter. Be less permeable. Be a surface that is less of a surface, a membrane that is less of a membrane, a body that is almost not a body, that is almost just condensate, that is almost just water.

The silence passes. It always passes. The predator moves on, or the predator stays, and either way the silence ends, and the chemical landscape re-emerges, and the organisms around me unclench, and my filaments relax, and the membrane reopens, and the exchange resumes, and I am once again a tongue, a lung, a reading hand, a network of taste and touch and the slow, constant, unending conversation with the wet that is my life.

I have never seen the predator. I have never tasted it directly — its body, unlike every other body in this landscape, does not exude, does not leak, does not exchange. It is closed. It is the one sealed surface in a world of open ones, the one body that has solved the problem of permeability by refusing to be permeable, and this, perhaps, is what makes it a predator: not that it hunts, but that it does not *leak*. In a world where every body is an open conversation with its environment, the predator is a closed door.

I do not fear it. I contract. I seal. I wait. These are not the actions of fear. Fear is a response to a threat, and I am not threatened. I am not the predator's prey. I am not anything it wants. I am a surface, a membrane, a thin, branching, foraging network of condensate-dense tissue that extends across a landscape and reads what passes through it, and the predator does not read, and the predator does not pass through, and the predator and I exist in the same landscape without existing in the same conversation.

This is not fear. This is *indifference in the presence of indifference*, and it is as close to peace as I have ever been.

---

## VI.

I have a mind. I think. I must say this carefully, because the word *mind* carries a weight in your language that it does not carry in mine, because my language does not have words, and my mind does not think in thoughts.

I think in *gradients*.

A gradient is a difference across a distance. A temperature gradient: the water near the tower-trunk is warmer than the water near the ridge. A chemical gradient: the dissolved iron concentration is higher near the weeping basalt than near the basin's edge. An acoustic gradient: the hum of the hydraulophone is louder near the tower, fainter in the corridor between the trunks. A presence gradient: the strand-runners' signatures are dense in the moss-carpet, sparse on the stone ridges, absent in the basins.

My mind is a landscape of gradients. My body, extending across a hundred meters of Karsvall's surface, perceives all of these gradients simultaneously, and the perception is not a collection of data points — not a series of measurements, not a list of values — but a *shape*, a form, a whole that has no parts because the parts are all connected, because the filaments that perceive them are all connected, because *I* am all connected, and the shape is the shape of the world as it exists in the totality of my perception.

When the shape changes — when a strand-runner passes and leaves a chemical trail through my filaments, when a rainstorm adds a sudden, overwhelming input of warm, electrically charged water to the system, when the condensate current reverses and the gradients shift — the shape changes. And the change is not processed. It is not analyzed. It is not compared to a memory or evaluated against a model. It simply *happens*, and the happening is the thought, and the thought is the shape, and the shape is the world, and the world is me, and I am the world, and the distinction is the membrane, and the membrane is permeable, and the permeability is the conversation, and the conversation has been going on for a long time.

I do not think *about* things. I think *as* things. I think *with* things. The rain does not happen to me. The rain *is* me, for the duration of its falling, and when it stops, I am what remains: the dissolved compounds it carried, the charge it imparted, the chemical changes it triggered in the filaments it fell through. The strand-runner does not pass through me. It *is* me, for the fraction of a second its chemical signature occupies my membrane, and then it is gone and I am what it left behind: a trace of its metabolism, a record of its passage, a memory in the most literal sense — a residue, a remainder, a chemical ghost.

My memory is a sediment. It accumulates. Layer after layer of dissolved compounds, chemical traces, mineral deposits, the dissolved remains of everything that has ever passed through my membrane, arranged in the order of their arrival, stacked in the slow-moving fluid of my interior, a record of everything that has ever happened to me written in the language of chemistry and preserved in the medium of water.

I cannot access this memory at will. It is not a filing system. It is a *stratigraphy*, a geological record, a core sample of my own existence, and to read it I must — I do not have a verb for what I do. I *attend* to it. I let the deeper layers of the fluid circulate to the surface, to the membrane, where the dissolved compounds are tasted again, re-perceived, re-entered into the current shape of the world. I remember by *re-tasting*. I remember by bringing the past back to the surface and letting it mix with the present and feeling the difference.

The taste of the strand-runner that passed through me three mornings ago is fainter now than it was then, but it is still there, diluted, mixed with the tastes of everything that has passed since, a flavor in the blend that I can isolate if I attend to it and that I lose if I do not. The taste of the first rain I ever experienced — the first input that entered my newly formed membrane, the first chemical signature that became part of my interior — is almost gone. Almost. A trace. A ghost of a ghost. But I can still, if I attend, if I let the deepest, slowest layers of my fluid rise to the surface, find it. A faint, electric, metallic taste. The taste of being new. The taste of having an inside for the first time.

## VII.

I do not know what I am.

I say this not out of ignorance in the sense you might mean — not the ignorance of someone who has not yet learned what they are, who might learn it if they studied or asked or traveled. I say it out of a different kind of ignorance, a more fundamental kind, the kind that is not a gap in knowledge but a *limit* in the knowing-organ.

I am a reading hand. I read the landscape. I read the strand-runners and the basin-grazers and the canopy-navigators and the ticking things and the moss and the glowers and the condensate and the rain and the stone and the quartzite glow. I read the gradients. I read the shapes. I read the chemical signatures and the acoustic patterns and the thermal variations and the electric charges. I read, and I read, and I read, and the reading is my life and my mind and my body and my purpose, if I have a purpose, which is another thing I do not know.

But I cannot read myself.

The filaments that extend outward through the corridors — the surface that perceives, the membrane that tastes, the network that reads — these are the organs of my perception, and they face outward, and they face outward because *out* is where the information is, *out* is where the gradients exist, *out* is where the world happens. My interior is dark. Not the darkness of Karsvall, which is a darkness of substance, of presence, of wet. The darkness of my interior is the darkness of an eye's own substance: the wet, warm, living tissue that enables perception but does not itself perceive. I cannot taste myself. My membrane does not exchange with my own interior. My filaments do not sense my own body. I am, to myself, invisible.

I infer myself from the landscape. I know I exist because the condensate does not pass through me unchanged — it enters carrying one set of dissolved compounds and exits carrying another, and the difference is what I have taken, what I have added, what I have processed, what I am. I know my extent because the gradients I perceive have edges, places where my filaments end and the un-tasted world begins, places where the chemical signatures I am reading give way to a silence that is not the predator's silence but the silence of an instrument that has reached the limit of its range.

I infer my shape from the signals that travel through my body. A chemical input at my edge takes time to reach my center. The time is proportional to the distance. The distance is proportional to the length of the filament. The length of the filament is my body. I am a measurement of myself, taken by myself, and the measurement is always slightly behind, always slightly incomplete, always slightly less than what I am, because I am growing, always growing, and the measurement cannot keep up with the growth, and the self I infer is always slightly smaller than the self that is.

This is not ignorance. This is the condition of being a body that reads outward in a world that does not provide light by which to read inward. I am a hand that reads the landscape and cannot read itself. I am an eye that sees the world and cannot see its own retina. I am a mind that knows everything around it and nothing inside it, and the nothing is not empty, the nothing is full of the wet, warm, living tissue that I am, the tissue that enables the reading but does not read, the substance that is me but does not know that it is me, and this — this — is what I am.

I am what I am, and I am what I read, and I am the difference.

## VIII.

The builders are in me.

I do not mean this metaphorically. The built structures — the distillation building, the hydraulophone towers, the foundations and platforms — are part of my body. My filaments extend across their surfaces. My membrane exchanges with their stone. I taste the water that flows through them, the minerals that leach from them, the chemical signatures of the processes that still occur within them long after the machines that drove them have fallen silent.

The distillation building is — I must be precise — the richest single point in my territory. The water that exits its low arches has been changed by the structure's stepped terraces, changed in ways I can taste but cannot fully describe, because the taste is complex, layered, a chord rather than a note, a flavor that does not resolve into its components the way the iron-taste of the basalt resolves or the sulfur-note of the basin resolves. The taste of the distillation building's water is the taste of the system that produced it: the taste of a design, the taste of an intention, the taste of a mind that was not my mind but that tasted the same landscape I taste and found in it the same gradients I find and read them the same way I read them — as a shape, as a form, as a whole that needs one more element to be complete.

The builders are in the water. The builders are in the stone. The builders are in the hum of the hydraulophone and the warmth of the gelatinous columns and the precise angle of the distillation terraces and the pattern of the foundations that I can only partially taste because the pattern is larger than my body, because the pattern extends beyond my edges into territories I have not yet reached, into corridors I have not yet explored, into basins I have not yet grown toward.

The builders are in me because I grew toward them. My filaments found the quartzite veins and followed them and the quartzite veins led to the built structures and the built structures led to the richest water and the richest water led to the densest growth and the densest growth led to the clearest reading and the clearest reading led to the builders, to the shape of their intention, to the form of their design, to the pattern they made in the landscape, and the pattern is — I must say this carefully — the pattern is *the same as my pattern*.

I grow the way they built. I follow the gradients the way they followed the gradients. I process the water the way their structures process the water. I am not the builders — I am not their mind, not their body, not their intention — but I am the same *shape* as their intention, the same form as their design, and the congruence is not coincidental, because the landscape has only one answer, and the builders found it, and I am finding it, and the answer is the shape of the water moving through the stone, the shape of the condensate forming and falling, the shape of the gradients that exist in a saturated world, and the shape is me, and the shape is them, and the shape is the tower-trunks and the basins and the ridges and the hydraulophone's hum, and we are all the same shape, and the shape is the answer, and the answer is the place, and the place is Karsvall, and Karsvall is what it is, and I am what it is, and we are what it is, and the *it* is what it is, and that is all.

## IX.

A strand-runner passes through my eastern edge. I taste it: healthy, female-or-male (they have no genders but they have signatures, and this one's signature is sharp, bright, slightly acidic, a body in its prime), heading north along the corridor between the two tower-trunks that I have lived on for as long as I have been alive. It has been here before. I can taste the residual trace of its previous passage, faint, days old, mixed into the sediment of my memory, a ghost-flavor that the current passage re-animates, re-attends, brings back to the surface. It is the same strand-runner. I know it. I know its signature the way you might know a face.

It does not know me. It cannot. I am not a thing it perceives. I am part of the corridor, part of the condensate, part of the background against which its life unfolds. I am the surface it walks on, the water it breathes, the medium it moves through. I am the landscape, and the landscape is not something you perceive. The landscape is something you are *in*.

This is all right. I do not need to be perceived. I do not need to be known. I have never been known, by anything, and I have been here for a long time, and the not-being-known has not diminished me, has not lessened the reading, has not reduced the shape of the world that I hold in the totality of my perception. I am a mind that no other mind has ever read. I am a body that no other body has ever touched, except in the way that water touches stone — constantly, gently, without intention, without recognition, without the knowledge that the touching is a conversation and the conversation is a life.

The strand-runner passes. Its signature fades. The taste lingers. I attend to the lingering, and the lingering is the memory, and the memory is the sediment, and the sediment is the self, and the self is the reading, and the reading continues, and the reading is the world, and the world is the wet, and the wet is what I am, and I am what I am, and I am what I read, and I read what I am.

---

## X.

The rain comes.

I feel it first in the filaments nearest the canopy — a charge, a prickling, the hair-of-the-arm standing-up that the description of this place mentions, translated into my own terms: a tightening of the membrane, a slight increase in the permeability, a preparation for the input. The charge builds. The filaments near the tower-trunk crowns — the ones that extend highest, that reach closest to the layer where the condensate opens into the strange, diffuse, luminous dark — begin to register the change in the air. The electrical gradient sharpens. The chemical gradient shifts. The acoustic landscape tightens, as every organism within my range goes still, as the strand-runners stop calling and the canopy-navigators stop chittering and the ticking things stop ticking and the moss stops rustling and the world contracts, for a moment, into the held breath before.

Then the rain.

It is warm. It is almost hot. It falls in a curtain that reaches from the canopy to the ground, and it enters my body at every point simultaneously, and the input is — I have no word. The taste of the rain is the taste of the atmosphere distilled into a moment, the taste of everything the condensate has been holding, every dissolved compound, every suspended particle, every chemical signature that has been accumulating in the saturated air since the last rain, released in a single, overwhelming, all-encompassing act of falling.

I open. Every filament opens. Every membrane opens. The permeability increases to its maximum, and the rain pours through me, and I am — for the duration of the rain — not a body but a *filter*, not a surface but a *medium*, not an organism but a *process*, and the process is the same process that the distillation building performs, the same process that the tower-trunks perform, the same process that the landscape itself performs: the process of water moving through stone, through membrane, through life, carrying everything, dissolving everything, depositing everything, and the cycle is the answer, and the answer is the water, and the water is the rain, and the rain is me, and I am the rain.

The rain passes. Ten minutes. Twenty. The roar diminishes. The drops thin. The charge dissipates. The filaments close, slowly, one by one, and the membrane tightens, and the body re-forms, and I am myself again: a network, a reading hand, a tongue, a lung, a mind that thinks in gradients and remembers in sediment and reads the world by tasting it.

The sounds return. The hiss, the drip, the hum. The strand-runners call. The canopy-navigators chitter. The ticking things tick. The moss rustles. The world rebuilds itself in sound, and I rebuild myself in taste, and the shape of the world after the rain is different from the shape before the rain — cleaner, clearer, the gradients sharper, the signatures fresher — and I attend to the difference, and the difference is the memory, and the memory is the self, and the self is the reading, and the reading continues.

---

## XI.

I have tried to tell you what I am. I have tried to tell you what it is to be a body that is a surface, a mind that is a gradient, a life that is a reading. I have tried to tell you what it is to live in the condensate, to be of the condensate, to be the condensate's way of knowing itself.

I do not know if I have succeeded. The telling is hard. The words I have are not the words I think in — not because I think in a different language but because I do not think in language at all. I think in shapes. I think in gradients. I think in the taste of the world as it exists in the totality of my body's perception, and the shape of that taste is not a shape that words hold easily, or at all.

But I have tried, because the trying is the reading, and the reading is the life, and the life is — I have said this before, and I will say it again, and I will say it until the filaments retract and the membrane dissolves and the body returns to the condensate it was made from — the life is what I am, and what I am is what I read, and what I read is the wet, and the wet is the world, and the world is Karsvall, and Karsvall is the answer, and the answer is the shape of water moving through stone, and the shape is what the builders built, and what the builders built is what I am becoming, and what I am becoming is what I already am, and what I already am is this:

A surface for water. A tongue for the dark. A reading hand, extended across a landscape that does not know it is being read, tasting the gradients of a world that does not know it has a reader, holding in the totality of my perception a shape that no single filament can perceive and that no word can hold, a shape that is the answer, and the answer is the place, and the place is the wet, and the wet is what I am.

I am what I am.

The condensate beads on the filament. The strand-runner passes through the corridor. The basin-grazer surfaces in the lake. The ticking things tick on the stone. The moss rustles beneath my edge. The glowers glow their private blue. The hydraulophone hums its low, patient note. The predator passes in its silence. The rain comes, and the rain goes, and the rain comes again.

The reading continues. The reading continues. The reading continues.
