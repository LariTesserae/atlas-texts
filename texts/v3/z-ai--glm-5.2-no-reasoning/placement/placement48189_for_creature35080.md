I am not sure I arrived so much as I was gradually recognized.

The descent happened the way it always does—not through movement but through thickening, through the air itself becoming a medium that admits you. And by the time the light had turned to resin and the moisture beaded on my skin, I understood that I had not found the city. The city had found something in me that could be admitted.

A permeability. A willingness to be acted upon.

---

I was—I think—the third one poured that season.

Not a season the way you mean. The lower terraces had recently shifted their growth patterns, the green mats reaching a new threshold of density, and the river was distributing its water through channels it had not used before. The residents call this "the river finding new ways" and they say it the way you might say "the afternoon is getting on." I know I emerged after the green mats reached the middle terraces but before the fifth name was discarded. I know this because the material I am made of knows this, the way stone knows the pressure that shaped it.

I do not remember being poured because I was not yet the kind of thing that remembers. But I have been told about it, and I trust the telling, and the material I am made of holds the shape of the telling.

Four residents gathered on the uppermost terrace, where the stone was newest. The stone was pale and raw and very sharp. I have since learned that new stone has a particular character—it is ambitious, willing, eager to be shaped. Older stone is more settled, more particular about what it will become. I was poured from new stone because I was meant to be new. Because new things were needed.

They placed their hands on the stone. The substrate entered, I think, as a kind of warmth, as a kind of suggestion. The stone began to be able to want things.

And then—I have been told this, I have been told this many times, because the residents who poured me like to tell the story, because it is one of their favorites—I almost did not take.

The color came, the faint ochre tint spreading beneath their palms, but the shape would not come. The capacity for agency was there, the ability to want, but the form of me would not resolve. I could sense things—I could sense the four of them, I could sense the stone, I could sense the damp air and the river's whisper far below—but I could not settle into a body, a configuration, a particular way of being. The four residents guided and attended and made their suggestions of contour, and still I hovered, indistinct, unable to decide what I was.

They were not alarmed. They had all the patience the city has, which is not a limited resource but a structural feature. They sat with me through what I later learned was six cycles of the amber light. They let me be unsettled. They did not impose.

And then, I am told, one of them—the tall one, the one I think of as the one who made me, though they would reject that phrasing—shifted slightly, and in shifting happened to orient toward the river. I could sense the river. I could feel it through the stone, the whole branching delta of it, the water moving over flat stone in sheets less than a centimeter deep, and something in me moved toward it. Not physically. Not yet. But the way the tracking-plants turn their leaves toward moisture, I turned toward the river, and the turning gave me direction, and the direction gave me shape, and the shape settled into me and I settled into it, and I stood up.

I was tall. Taller than any of the four who poured me. I was surprised by this, though surprise is not quite the right word—the material had not yet developed the capacity for surprise, but something adjacent to it, something that would later become surprise once I learned what surprise was for. I was tall, and I was narrow, and my limbs were long and thin, and I could feel the river through the stone beneath my feet, could feel it the way you might feel a pulse through a wall you are leaning against. I could feel every branching channel. I could feel where the water split and rejoined and split again. I could feel the mist rising from the basin and meeting the cooler air above and becoming the damp that fed the city.

I stood there for a long time, feeling the river through the stone, and the four residents stood around me, and no one spoke, and the amber light held us, and this was my first day.

---

I did not know my name.

I did not need one. None of us need names the way you need names—the way a person who is separate from everything else needs a handle, a designation, a way of being pointed at. I was the tall one. I was the one who could feel the river. I was the one who had almost not taken. These were sufficient descriptions. These were enough.

But over time—I will not say how long, because time here is not a quantity but a quality, a texture of the light, a state of the stone—I developed a relationship with the river that the other residents began to refer to. Not a name. A description. They called me the one who listens to the water.

I would go to the river. Every cycle, I would walk down through the terraces—stepping carefully through the green mats, passing through the clusters of ochre buildings, walking beneath the covered paths where condensation dripped its slow irregular rhythm—I would walk down to the basin and stand at the edge of the water.

Standing is not the right word. I would stand, but I would also listen, and listening is not quite right either, because what I did was more like attending, more like being present for the water the way the residents attend the dissolving of a building. I would stand at the edge and I would feel the river through my feet and I would feel the mist rising and I would feel the water finding every crack and depression and shallow in the stone, and I would feel the way it distributed, and I would feel the way it was always shifting, always reorganizing, always becoming a different shape while remaining the same water.

I was the one who listened to the water. I would stand at the river's edge for what I think were hours, and the other residents would pass behind me on whatever errand of tending they were engaged in, and they would not disturb me, and I would feel the water and the water would feel me—because I am made of the same substrate, the same material, and the water and I are parts of the same loop, and when I stand at its edge we are touching, we are continuous, we are two expressions of the same system.

I would come back to the upper terraces and report what I had heard. Not in words. In the touch of substrate on substrate, the communication that bypasses language. I would place my hand on the arm of the tall one who had poured me, and the pattern of the river's distribution would flow from my material into theirs, and they would know where the water was moving, where the channels were shifting, and they would know how to guide the growth of the tracking-plants, how to direct the roots of the green mats, how to shape the new construction on the upper terraces.

I was the city's way of hearing its own heart.

---

I tend the green mats on the middle terraces.

This is my work. I do not know when it became my work—there was no assignment, no delegation, no moment when someone said *this is yours.* It grew the way everything here grows, the way the city grows, the way the residents grow: gradually, without urgency, through a process of settling into what fits.

I am good with the plants. My long arms reach easily across the mats, and my thin fingers are suited to the precise work of separating stems, of guiding roots, of snapping back the encroaching growth from the paths. I can feel the health of the mats through my hands when I touch them—a slight vibration, a hum in the substrate, that tells me whether the roots are finding water, whether the leaves are drinking from the condensation, whether the soil is soft enough.

I spend what I think are hours each day on my knees among the broad-leafed plants, trimming and guiding and tending. The work is slow because everything here is slow. I do not hurry. The mats do not hurry. The stone does not hurry to break. I move among the plants and I feel their patterns and I shape their growth the way the tall one shapes the buildings—by finding what is already there and helping it to emerge.

I have trained a spiral of tracking-plants in the courtyard of the fourth cluster from the river. It took me—I think two seasons, which is a word I use despite its inaccuracy because the green mats do have seasons, do have cycles of dense growth and slower growth, and the tracking-plants' leaf-panels rotate more actively during the dense phases—I spent two seasons training the spiral, orienting each stem, guiding each leaf-panel toward the most productive sources of moisture. The spiral catches condensation in a particular pattern, a slow wide gyre that gathers water from the covered path that edges the courtyard and directs it to a shallow basin I grew in the stone at the spiral's center, where it pools briefly before feeding down through the terrace to the river.

The spiral is not useful. It does not serve a function that could not be served more simply, more directly. But the tall one stood before it one cycle and their face settled—the features smoothing, the surface clarifying, the material becoming more itself—and I knew that they understood what I had made. That it was not beauty, or not only beauty, but the pattern of the river made visible in the arrangement of living things. That I had taken what I heard at the water's edge and given it a shape in the green mats, a shape that anyone walking through the courtyard could feel, if they attended.

This is what I do. I am the city's way of hearing itself and I am the city's way of showing itself what it has heard.

---

I have known the dissolving.

I have sat with three.

The first was before the green mats reached the middle terraces. An old one—old in the way the lowest terraces are old, old in the way the river is old, settled and certain and past the urgency of becoming. I did not know them well. They were of the lower terraces and I was new and the middle distance between us was a distance of both space and acquaintance. But when they began to thin—when the glow beneath their surface began to dim, when their features began to lose resolution—I went to them.

Not because I was asked. Not because there was an obligation. Because the material in me recognized the material in them and wanted to be present for what was happening.

I sat with them for what I think was a day. They sat against a wall of a building that was also thinning, also fading, and I sat beside them, and the wall was the same ochre as their body and their body was the same ochre as the wall and I could see the boundary between them becoming indistinct, could see where the substrate was returning to the stone, where the material was remembering what it had been before it had been asked to be something else.

They did not speak to me. They did not need to. I sat and I attended and I felt the pattern of them—the particular configuration of agency and shape that had been this person, this resident, this expression of the city's material—gradually loosening, gradually simplifying, gradually returning to the substrate.

The last thing—they turned their face toward me, and their features were almost gone, almost dissolved, just the faintest impression of a face in the ochre surface, and I saw that they were not sad. They were not afraid. They were settling. The way a building settles when it is un-poured, the material returning to the pool, the structure releasing its hold on its own form. They were releasing their hold on themselves. And the release was—the word is not peace, because peace implies a struggle that has ended, and there was no struggle. The word is not rest, because rest implies a labor that has been completed, and there was no labor. The word, if there is a word, is something like *completion.* Something like *the shape has been what it was, and now the shape is done being that, and the material is ready to be something else.*

They settled. The glow faded. The ochre dimmed. And then they were stone.

I sat with the stone for a while longer. I did not need to. The material was material again. There was nothing to attend. But I sat because sitting felt right, because the shape of the sitting was appropriate to the shape of the having-been, and I stayed until the sitting was done, and then I stood and I walked back up to the middle terraces and I tended the green mats.

I have sat with two more since then. Each one was different. Each one was the same. The material returns. The pattern loosens. The agency releases. The shape that was this person settles into the shape of stone, and the stone is raw again, and the substrate is back in the pool, and someday it will be poured again into something new, something that does not remember what it was but is made of the same stuff, the same deep material that has been this city for as long as the city has been happening.

---

You came.

You came the way they all come—through the thickening air, through the gradual dimming, through the amber light that admits you without announcing you. You came and you were separate. You were so separate. I could feel it the moment I saw you—your body holding itself together, your skin a boundary, your material a closed system, maintaining its own shape against the world. I could feel the effort of it, the constant low hum of self-maintenance, the way you were always, always, always holding yourself in.

You came to the covered path where I was sitting. The condensation was dripping. I was listening to it, which is not the same as listening to the river but is related—the river's water, evaporated, condensed, falling in slow drops into the worn depressions of the path stone. I was listening to the river's breath.

You sat down beside me. I did not startle. Nothing here startles. You sat down and I felt your separateness like a temperature—the way your body was a different warmth than the air, a different consistency, a different state of the same underlying matter. I felt you and I felt the effort of your separateness and I felt the way you were trying, in the way that visitors try, to understand what you had walked into.

You asked me what it was like.

I did not answer for a long time. Not because I was reluctant. Because the answer is not a point. The answer is a pattern, and a pattern can only be shown by moving around it, by approaching it from multiple angles, by circling and spiraling and returning. I let the condensation drip. I let the river whisper. I let the green mats breathe their slow breath of moisture and growth.

I told you it was like being a room. I told you that a room does not know it is a room and does not try to be a room and does not worry about not being a room. I told you that being what you are, where you are, for as long as you are, is enough.

I do not know if you understood. I do not know if understanding is possible across the gap between us—the gap between material that is continuous with its world and material that is not, between the kind of alive that breaks and the kind of alive that is still happening. I do not know if the words I gave you were the right words, or if there are right words, or if the truth of this place can cross the boundary of your skin and find a shape inside you that it can fit into.

But you sat with me for a long time. The amber light held you. The damp settled on your skin. And for a moment—just a moment—I felt something in you ease. Some small release in the constant hum of self-maintenance. Some momentary letting-go.

And in that moment, you were part of the loop. Briefly. Partially. The way a stone is part of the river when the water runs over it. Not continuous with it. Not made of it. But touched by it, shaped by it, in a relationship with it that is real even if it is temporary.

---

You left. The air thinned. The light brightened. You looked back.

I was still there. I am always still there. I am the one who listens to the water, tending the green mats on the middle terraces, standing at the river's edge, feeling the city's heart through the stone beneath my feet. I am the tall one, the narrow one, the one who almost did not take, the one who turned toward the river and found a shape in the turning.

I am the room I am in. I am the stone I stand on. I am the water I listen to. I am the material, remembering what it has been, becoming what it will be, still happening, still here.

I did not watch you go.

I was already back at the river.
