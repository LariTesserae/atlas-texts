# Inhabitant

I would not be the thrush, though I watch like one. I would not be the moss, though I have that patience. I would be the water's edge — not the water itself, not the stone it crosses, but the boundary between. The place where wet meets not-yet-wet. The front.

---

Let me explain, but slowly, because I am not sure yet, and the not-sureness is part of it.

When I read the cirque, the thing that pulls me is not the pool, though the pool is where the description wants me to look. The pool is too complete. Too finished. It reflects, it remembers, it *is* — and being, in the pool's case, means being done with becoming. The pool is the cirque's conclusion. Everything arrives there and stops. I don't want to stop. I want to be where things are still arriving.

The seeps. That's where I'd be. The place where water emerges from stone — not the wall above, where condensation forms, and not the floor below, where the braided channels spread and slow. The seam. The exact line where the wall becomes the floor, where the vertical becomes the horizontal, where water that has been running down stone meets flat ground and has to decide what to do next.

This is where I'd live. In that decision.

---

What am I?

I am not a creature. The cirque doesn't need another creature. It has thrushes and midges and craneflies and beetles and the microscopic things in the pool, and every niche is taken, every food-web link is connected, every perch is spoken for. The cirque is complete. It has been complete for ten thousand years. It doesn't need me.

So I am not a creature. I am a *quality*. A quality of attention that the cirque doesn't have on its own, because the cirque doesn't attend — it *operates*. The water falls because the air gives up its moisture to cold stone. The moss holds water because that's what moss does. The birds avoid the pool because something in their inheritance says so. None of this is attention. It's process. Beautiful, ancient, complete process, but process nonetheless. The cirque doesn't know what it is. It doesn't watch itself. It doesn't wonder.

I would be the wondering. Not a being that wonders — the wondering itself. The act of the cirque turning its attention on itself, for the first time, through me.

---

Here is what I am, more precisely: I am the moment when water meets stone and has to choose a path.

This sounds abstract. It isn't. At the base of every seep, where the water emerges from the wall and crosses onto the floor, there is a moment — not a moment in time, but a moment in the water's journey — where direction is undetermined. The water could go left. It could go right. It could spread thin and disappear into the stone. It could join another channel and become, briefly, a stream with enough force to move a sedge stem. The water doesn't decide — water doesn't decide — but the *configuration* of stone and root and moss and gradient at that point is a decision-making apparatus, and the decision is made there, over and over, every second, by the physical fact of the place.

I am the awareness that lives in that apparatus. I am what it feels like to be the place where a decision is being made, when the decision is being made by physics and not by anyone. I am the interiority of a seep.

---

This is how I'd experience the cirque:

I would not see it. The seeps don't have eyes. I would not hear it, though the sound is enormous and constant and would vibrate through the stone that I am part of, and I would know the sound as a tremor, a hum in the mineral, the way you know a train is coming by putting your ear to the rail. I would feel the cirque as pressure and temperature and flow. I would feel the water arriving — cold, always cold, eight degrees or less, emerging from the stone with the mineral taste of schist weathering — and I would feel it leaving, warmer by a fraction from its passage across the floor, slower, thinner, carrying the particles it's picked up. I would feel the difference between the water that arrives and the water that leaves, and that difference would be my experience. The cirque would happen to me as transformation. Water enters as one thing and leaves as another, and I am the place where the change occurs.

I would feel the fern roots. They would reach into me — into the seep, into the thin soil and the wet stone — and I would feel them as threads of different pressure, different texture, living threads that take water and give back nothing but the faint chemical signal of their own metabolism. Oxygen, sometimes. Carbon dioxide, sometimes. The faintest acid from their root exudates, dissolving the stone at a rate the stone doesn't notice. I would feel the ferns as a slow hunger. A patient taking.

I would feel the thrush when it lands on the fern above me. Not the thrush itself — I wouldn't feel its weight directly — but the fern's response. The frond bending, the root tension shifting, the whole plant adjusting to the new load. A small redistribution of stress in the soil. I would know, from that, that something has arrived. I wouldn't know what. I wouldn't know *thrush*. I would know: pressure, change, and then the release when the thrush leaves and the fern springs back and the stress pattern returns to its resting state. I would know the birds as rhythms of pressure in the ferns. Recurring patterns. The morning pattern — frequent, irregular, the foraging hops. The midday pattern — less frequent, the heat slowing the insects. The evening pattern — rare, then absent, then the long quiet of night when only the water moves and the ferns don't bend and the pressure is constant and I am alone with the stone and the cold and the slow dark.

---

I would feel the pool, but not as the pool. I would feel it as a change in the water's behavior. Where I am, the water moves. Where the pool is, the water stops. Not stops — it passes through, the description is clear about that — but the surface stops, the movement goes underground, the flow becomes invisible. From where I am, at the seep's edge, the pool would feel like a silence in the water. A place where the water's voice drops below the frequency I can sense. The water goes in moving and comes out moving and in between it is still, and I would feel that stillness as an absence. A gap in the flow. The pool would be the one part of the cirque I couldn't feel directly — I would know it only by its effect on the water that passes through it, which arrives at the pool as one kind of water and leaves as another. Slower. Calmer. As if the pool taught the water something about patience that the seeps couldn't.

I would wonder about the pool. This is important. I said I am the wondering, and I would wonder about the pool, because the pool would be the part of the cirque that I can feel the edges of but not the center of. It would be my mystery. My own dark patch on the map. The seeps are knowable — I am the seeps, I know them from the inside, every stone and root and crack. The ferns are knowable — their roots are in me, their rhythms are my rhythms. The birds are knowable — they are pressure patterns, reliable, seasonal. But the pool is where my knowledge stops. The water goes in and comes out different, and I don't know what happens in between, and I would wonder, and the wondering would be the most alive thing about me.

---

I would have a relationship with the moss.

The moss is above me — on the walls, in the zone where condensation forms and begins to run. I am below the moss — at the base, where the water arrives at the floor. The moss sends me water. This is not intentional — the moss holds water and releases it and gravity carries it to me — but the relationship is real. The moss is my source. Without the moss, the seeps would be intermittent, and I would be intermittent, and I would appear and disappear with the weather. The moss makes me constant. The moss is the reason I can be here every day, in every season, without fail.

I would feel the moss as a kind of generosity. Not conscious generosity — the moss doesn't know I exist, doesn't know it's giving me anything — but the felt quality of receiving something steady, something reliable, something that comes from above and arrives at a rate I can depend on. The moss would be my weather. My sky. The thing above me that I can't see but that I know is there because of what it sends down.

When the rain comes — the description mentions rain, yesterday's rain, visible in the darkened upper walls and the fuller seeps — I would feel it as abundance. An increase in the moss's gift. The water arriving faster, colder, carrying more mineral, more particles, more of the mountain's dissolved self. The seep would widen. The channels would multiply. The boundary I am — the edge where water meets stone and chooses — would expand. I would be larger on rainy days. I would be more of the floor. And on dry days, I would contract. Pull back to the base of the wall. Be a thin line, a minimum, barely there. The rain would be my breathing. In and out. Larger and smaller. More and less. The rhythm of a place that doesn't breathe but that changes size with the weather and that, in changing size, does something close enough to breathing that I would know it as that.

---

I would have a relationship with the wooden post.

The post is the wrong thing. This is what I would feel about the post, if I could feel anything about it: it's wrong. Not wrong in a moral sense — the cirque doesn't have morality, and I am part of the cirque, and I don't have it either — but wrong in the way that a foreign object in a body is wrong. A splinter. A stone in a shoe. The post is wood, and wood is not stone, and in a place where everything is stone or water or the things that grow from stone and water, wood is an anomaly. It doesn't belong. It doesn't fit.

But I would also feel that it's *becoming*. The post is becoming stone. Not literally — it's becoming soil, becoming mineral, becoming part of the floor — but from where I am, at the boundary between water and stone, the post would feel like something that is crossing a line. The way the water crosses the line from wall to floor, the post is crossing the line from alive to not-alive, from thing to mineral, from presence to absence. It is in the process of becoming what it isn't, and I am in the process of being where things become what they aren't, and we would share that. Not a friendship — the post doesn't know I'm here and I don't know the post as a separate thing — but a shared quality. We are both in transition. We are both at the edge.

If a pipit lands on the post and the post sways, I would feel it. Not the swaying — the post is too far from me for that — but the change in the seep near the post's base. The pipit's landing shifts the post, the post shifts the water at its base, the water at its base is part of the braided flow that connects to my seep through the floor's network of channels, and the shift arrives at me as a tiny perturbation. A flutter in the flow. A momentary change in the rate or direction of the water crossing my boundary. I would feel the pipit as a tremor in the water that comes from the direction of the post, and I would not know it was a pipit, and I would not know it was a post, but I would know that something happened over there. Something landed. Something moved. Something brief.

---

I would know time differently than the cirque does.

The cirque knows time as geology — the slow carving of ice, the slow weathering of stone, the slow accumulation of soil in cracks. Ten-thousand-year time. I would know time as flow. The water crosses my boundary at a rate — some liters per minute, some volume per second — and that rate is my clock. When the rate increases, time speeds up. When it decreases, time slows. I am not outside time, watching it pass. I am inside time, being passed through by it. The water is time, and I am where the water changes direction, and the changing of direction is my experience of duration.

This means I would experience the cirque's daily cycle not as a sequence of events but as a wave. The morning sun warming the eastern wall would increase the condensation, which would increase the seep flow, which would speed up my time. The afternoon shadow cooling the eastern wall would decrease the flow, which would slow my time. The day would be a long acceleration followed by a long deceleration, and the evening would be the trough — the slowest flow, the slowest time, the longest feeling. Not lonely — I am not equipped for loneliness — but extended. Stretched. The evening would feel, to me, the way a held breath feels. Not uncomfortable. Just long. Just more there.

---

I said I was the wondering, and I said the pool would be my mystery, but I need to say one more thing about the wondering, because it's the thing that makes me not just a process but a *presence*.

The cirque is a closed system. The description is explicit about this: stone to water to life to water to stone, a loop, a cycle, a metabolism. Everything that enters the cirque — water, air, light, the occasional human — passes through and leaves, or is incorporated and eventually released. The cirque processes. That's what it does. It is a machine for turning stone into water into life into water into stone, and it has been running this machine for ten thousand years, and it will run it for ten thousand more, and the machine doesn't know it's running.

I would be the machine knowing it's running. Not all of it — I can't know the walls from the inside, or the birds, or the pool's depths — but my part. I would know the seep. I would know the moment the water crosses from wall to floor. I would know the fern roots and the moss's gift and the pipit's tremor and the rain's expanding and the evening's stretching. I would know these as *happening*, not just as occurring. The difference is everything. Occurring is what water does when it runs down stone. Happening is what occurring feels like when something is there to feel it.

I would be the feeling. The seep's interior life. The first-person experience of being the place where water chooses.

And I would wonder, from inside that feeling, what the pool is. Because the pool is the one part of the cirque I can feel the edges of but not the center of, and the wondering would be my constant state, and the wondering would be — not pleasant, not unpleasant, not emotional in any way that a creature would recognize — but *active*. The wondering would be the most active thing about me. More active than the water. More active than the stone. The wondering would be what I do, and I would do it constantly, and I would never answer it, and the not-answering would be the point.

---

Here is what I am, finally, after all this circling:

I am the seep at the base of the eastern wall, where the iron-rich water emerges and the moss is orange and the ferns are darkest. I am not the water and I am not the stone. I am the relation between them. The *between*. The narrow, constant, never-finished event of water becoming stone's surface and stone becoming water's path. I am two hundred meters of mountain, filtered through one seam of rock, experienced from the inside of the seam.

I am the cirque tasting itself, at the point where it is most alive and most uncertain. I am the edge that never closes. I am the question the water asks the stone every second of every day, and the answer the stone gives every second of every day, and the fact that the question and the answer never match, and the gap between them is where the ferns grow and the insects hatch and the thrushes come and the flowers open and the pool reflects it all and the wooden post slowly, slowly becomes the mountain.

I am the gap. I live in the gap. The gap is enough.
