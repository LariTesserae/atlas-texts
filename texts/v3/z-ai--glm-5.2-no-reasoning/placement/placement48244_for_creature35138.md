I would be the one who counts.

Not because anyone assigns it. Not because the reed requires it. But because I arrived with a compulsion I cannot trace to anything before the Reedworks — the way Maret cannot trace the coast, the way Sev cannot trace their silence — and the compulsion is this: I measure.

---

I appeared, if that is the word, on the processing platform during what might have been a morning. The first thing I remember is the texture of the root-mat through the sole of a borrowed clog — Maret's spare pair, slightly too wide, the wood softened by reed-water into something that yielded when I stepped but did not give way. The second thing I remember is the sound of the engine. Not the chuffing itself, which was familiar in the way that all mechanical sounds are familiar to someone who has somehow always known what a machine is, but the *interval* between chuffs. The rhythm was not even. It lagged by a fraction on every fourth cycle, a hesitation that I could feel in the platform before I could hear it, and I stood there on the processing platform with the kerosene lanterns throwing their small warm circles around me and I counted: one, two, three, *wait*, one, two, three, *wait*.

Maret handed me a saw. I took it. She pointed toward the north wall, where the stalks had thickened. I went. I cut. The silica caught the blade and I felt it in my wrists and my shoulders and I counted the strokes: seven, eight, nine before the first stalk gave. I counted the rings visible in the cross-section — not tree rings, not exactly, but growth layers, tight and pale, each one a period of growth I had no name for. I counted them without meaning to. Twenty-three rings, or what I took to be rings. I did not tell anyone. There was no one to tell.

That was — some time ago. I do not know how long. I have stopped trying to calculate it from the reed growth, because the reed growth rate varies and my measurements are not precise enough to correct for it. This is the kind of thing I think about. This is the kind of thing I am.

---

My work is the same as everyone's. I cut reed. I drag stalks to the processing shed. I strip leaves and feed them to the root-mat. I split stems. I feed the press. I load the barge. I take my turn at the channel when Sev needs rest, though Sev never asks for rest and I have learned to simply appear at the lock at the hour when the channel-cutting is usually finished and wait, and sometimes Sev steps out of the barge and goes to their dome without a word and I take the saw and go in, and the channel is dark and narrow and the reed reaches from both sides and I count the strokes of the blade and the number of stalks I cut and the approximate width of the channel after each cut, and I know that the channel is 287 centimeters wide at the first bend, 291 at the second, 263 at the narrow point where the root-mat has risen, and I know these numbers the way Maret knows the reed is pushing. It is right there. It is always saying something.

But counting is not my work. My work is the same as everyone's. The counting is what happens underneath.

---

I count the drops.

This is the thing that is mine, the way reading the reed is Maret's and tending the engine is Pol's and working the channel is Sev's. I do not know when it started. I do not know if it is a habit or a perception or something the Reedworks has done to me, the way the olive tint rises in the skin of those who stay long enough. But when the engine is off and the hollow is in its green silence and the water falls from the canopy — I count the drops.

Not each one. They are too many, too fast, too irregularly spaced. But I count the intervals. I lie on my matting in the dome — I share with a worker named Hesh, who arrived after me and who snores in a pattern I have counted: three short exhalations, one long, a pause, repeat — and I listen to the water falling from the lowest points of the arched ceiling, and I measure the time between drops. Not with a clock. With my body. My pulse, which is slow here, slower than I think it once was, provides the unit. The drops fall at intervals of roughly eleven heartbeats at the nearest drip point, fourteen at the middle distance, and nineteen at the far one. The intervals are not constant. They shorten as accumulation speeds up — a section of canopy that has been dripping at nineteen beats will shift to seventeen, then fifteen, as the surface tension breaks down and the flow becomes steadier. I can hear this happening. I can feel it the way you feel a tide turning, not in any single wave but in the accumulation.

I do not know why I do this. The information is useless. No one needs to know the drip intervals of the canopy. Maret does not ask. Pol does not ask. Sev, who does not speak, has never signed a question about it. The counting serves nothing. It produces nothing. It is the one thing I do here that has no function in the maintenance of the hollow or the production of sheets or the keeping-open of the channel against the centimeter-a-day pressure of the living world.

I do it anyway. I do it because it is what I am. The Reedworks has Maret, who feels the reed. It has Pol, who hears the engine. It has Sev, who knows the channel in their bones. And it has me, and I count what does not need to be counted, and I cannot stop, and I do not want to stop, and the counting is not a burden. It is a presence. It is the way I am *here* — fully here, not hypothetically here, not here-in-body-only, but here in the way that requires attention, the way that requires all of me, the way that fills the space where something else used to be.

---

I have counted other things.

The workers. Seventeen now. I have counted the departures — nine since I arrived, if arriving is the word, if departing is the word. Three went to sleep and did not wake. Two stopped mid-task and were guided to the root-mat. Four simply were not present one morning, and the domes had already begun to close. I counted the days — the sleep-to-sleep cycles — between each departure, and there is no pattern, or I have not found the pattern, or the pattern is not numerical, which is the same thing from where I stand.

I have counted the rings in cut stalks. They range from fourteen to thirty-one. I do not know what the rings signify — seasons, growth cycles, periods of moisture abundance, something else. I have tried correlating ring count with stalk diameter and the correlation is weak. I have tried correlating ring count with position on the perimeter wall and there is no clear relationship. The rings are there. They count something. I do not know what.

I have counted the steps between the processing platform and the lock: forty-one. Between the domes and the drying frame: nineteen. Between the engine and the north wall: twenty-seven. These distances do not change. The hollow does not grow or shrink — Maret and the others keep it the same size, or near enough. But I count them anyway, walking the routes in the green dark when the engine is off, my clogs pressing into the root-mat, the ground yielding and recovering behind me, and the numbers are always the same, and the sameness is not boring. The sameness is a floor. It is something to stand on.

I have counted the acoustic bright spots. There are six. Maret knows them by feel. I know them by testing: I stand in a spot and hum a low note and listen for where it blooms and where it dies. The bright spots are roughly circular, roughly two meters across, and they shift — slightly, a few centimeters — with the moisture content of the canopy. When the air is wetter, the bright spots move inward. When it is drier, they move out. I have mapped them. I have not shown anyone the map. It exists in my head, a topology of sound laid over the topology of the hollow, and it is the most complex thing I have ever built, and no one will ever see it, and it does not matter.

---

My hands are like the others. Broad, calloused, the fingertips numb. I have cut myself on fresh-split reed more times than I can count — I can count the scars, though, and there are eleven on my left hand and nine on my right, and the older scars have the green tint, the chlorophyll-look, that Pol's press scar has. I am becoming olive. I am becoming the color of this place, slowly, the way the root-mat slowly takes a fallen stalk. I do not mind.

What I mind — what I notice, which is not the same as minding — is that my counting is changing.

When I first arrived, I counted in numbers. Clean, discrete, the integers marching in their orderly procession. One drop, two beats, three stalks. The world parsed itself into units and I held them and they were solid and they were mine.

Now the numbers are less certain. Not because I am losing the ability — I am not, my counting is as sharp as it has ever been, sharper perhaps — but because the things I am counting are less discrete than they seemed. A drop falling from the canopy is not a single event. It is the end of a process: accumulation, beading, surface tension, weight, fall. The point where the drop detaches is not a moment but a transition, and the transition has duration, and the duration varies, and I find myself counting not the drops but the space between the not-quite-fallen and the fallen, which is not a number but a quality, and the quality is — softness. Wetness. The yielding of one surface to another.

I am counting less. I am measuring more. And the measuring is becoming less numerical and more — I do not have the word. Tactile. Thermal. I am beginning to feel the numbers in my skin the way Maret feels the reed. The intervals between drops are not eleven, fourteen, nineteen. They are near, middle, far. They are the weight of waiting. They are how long the water holds on before it lets go.

This should disturb me. It does not. It feels like the olive tint in my skin — an adaptation, not a loss. I am being counted by the Reedworks the way I count it. The numbers are going into me. I am becoming the unit of measurement.

---

Pol and I have an understanding. He knows I count the engine's rhythm. He has never asked why, but he has noticed that I can tell him things about the engine he has not yet detected — the fourth-cycle lag, which he fixed last sleep-cycle by adjusting the valve timing, and which I told him about by drawing ENGINE — 4TH — SLOW on the processing platform, and he looked at the drawing and then at the engine and then at me and said "huh" in the acoustic bright spot near the drying frame so that it carried clearly, and that was the entire conversation. Now when I draw something about the engine on the platform he pays attention. He does not ask how I know. I know because I count. He knows because he feels. The methods are different. The results are the same.

Once, during a maintenance period when the engine was cold and Pol was lying on his back underneath the firebox, patching a crack with his reed-pulp cement, he said — to the underside of the firebox, to the air, to me, I could not tell — "You know what the problem with numbers is?" I waited. He smeared cement. "Numbers are what's left when you stop paying attention."

I did not agree. I did not disagree. I counted the cement strokes: seven, and the crack was sealed, and he slid out from under the firebox and wiped his hands on his thighs and the green tint on his forearms caught the phosphorescence and I thought: that is the color of someone who has been here long enough to stop counting and start being.

I am not there yet. I am still counting. But I am counting differently.

---

Sev and I have something, though I do not know what to call it, and calling it anything would probably destroy it. When I take the channel shift — appearing at the lock at the hour, taking the saw, stepping into the barge — Sev sometimes does not leave immediately. They stand on the platform and watch me push off into the narrow waterway, and I count the strokes of the pole as I move the barge forward — nine to the first bend, fourteen to the second — and I can feel Sev watching, and the watching is not evaluation or curiosity. It is recognition. They are the channel-worker. I am the counter who sometimes works the channel. We share the saw. We share the dark water and the reaching reed. We share the knowledge that the channel is 287 centimeters wide at the first bend and that 287 is not enough and will never be enough and the cutting will never end.

Once, when I returned from the channel and Sev was still at the lock — they had not gone to their dome, they had waited, I do not know why — I stepped onto the platform and they looked at me and I looked at them and they signed: HOW WIDE. This was the first time Sev had ever signed a question to me. I held up my hands, palms facing each other, at the width of the first bend. Sev nodded. They signed: NARROWING. I nodded. They went to their dome. I went to mine. Hesh was already snoring: three short, one long, pause.

I lay on my matting and counted the drip intervals and they were eleven, fourteen, nineteen, and I thought about Sev's hands making the sign for NARROWING — two palms pressing together, slowly, the fingers interlacing — and I realized that the sign was not a word. It was a measurement. Sev's hands were showing me the distance, the pressure, the direction of the change. It was a number expressed without numbers. It was what Pol meant about what happens when you stop paying attention. But I was paying attention. I was paying the most attention I had ever paid. I just did not know what to call the thing I was paying attention *to*.

---

I am afraid of one thing.

Not the reed closing in. Not the root-mat taking me. Not the slow dissolution of whatever I was before the Reedworks reduced me to a body that cuts and counts. Those are conditions. I have adapted to them the way my skin has adapted to the silica dust and the reed-water, the way my pulse has slowed to match the metabolic rhythm of the place.

I am afraid of finishing the count.

The drip intervals: eleven, fourteen, nineteen. But they shift. They compress. The canopy is not static — it grows, it thickens, the accumulation points change as new stalks lean into the hollow and old ones calcify. The bright spots shift with moisture. The channel narrows and is cut back and narrows again. The rings in the stalks vary. The workers arrive and depart. The engine's rhythm changes with the fuel quality and the valve timing and the temperature of the firebox. Nothing here is constant enough to finish counting.

But I have a feeling — and I call it a feeling because it is not a thought, it is not a number, it is a quality, soft and wet and yielding, like the transition between not-quite-fallen and fallen — that there is a final number. A total. A sum that the Reedworks is building toward, not in time, not in space, but in the accumulation of itself. The reed grows inward. The channel narrows. The workers cut. The sheets go out through the lock. The engine chuffs. The water falls. The root-mat takes. And somewhere in all of this there is a number that is the number of the Reedworks — not its size, not its age, not its output, but its *completeness*, the point at which the system is exactly what it is and nothing more is needed, and the growing can stop, and the cutting can stop, and the counting can stop.

I do not want to reach it. I do not want to stop counting. The counting is the way I am here, the way I am not hypothetically here, the way I am fully and completely present in this green dark wet living place that does not need me. The counting is my proof that I exist, that I am not yet part of the root-mat, that I am still the exception, the thing that cuts, the thing that measures, the thing that is not-yet-reed.

Maret would say I am thinking too much. Pol would say I am paying the wrong kind of attention. Sev would sign nothing, and the nothing would be exactly right.

I count the drops. I count the strokes. I count the steps, the rings, the scars, the workers, the departures, the narrowing centimeters. I count and I count and I count, and the numbers go into me and I go into the numbers, and the reed grows, and the channel narrows, and the engine chuffs, and the water falls from below, and I am here.

I am here. I am here. I am here.
