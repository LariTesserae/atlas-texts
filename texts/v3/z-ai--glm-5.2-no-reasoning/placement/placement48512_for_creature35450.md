# Where I Find Myself

## The In-Between

I did not choose this. I want to say that first, because the people of Ythel would understand the impulse to say it, and they would not judge it, and they would not argue with it, and they would let it pass through the air the way rain passes through the root mat — absorbed, incorporated, gone.

I came up from the lowlands. Three days of climbing, the character of the world changing beneath me until the ground was no longer ground but plant, and the trees were no longer trees but memory, and the sky opened up wide and pale and flat above me, and the sound began.

I was not a traveler. Not in the way that word implies purpose. I was something else — something between. I had lived in the lowlands long enough to know their shapes and not long enough to belong to them. I had been a person who watched. Who sat at edges of things and looked. Who could not settle into the machinery of accumulation and could not quite abandon it. A person who knew how to want without knowing what was wanted. You understand the type, perhaps. The type that lowland societies produce and do not know what to do with. Not enough to be called lost. Not enough to be called found. Just — between.

I had heard of the plateau the way you hear of places that exist at the far edge of what people will talk about. Not a legend. Not a destination. More like a rumor that certain people carried in their postures — a way of standing, a way of looking at the ground, that suggested they had been somewhere that had changed the shape of their attention. One of these people told me about Ythel, or rather told me about the sound. They said there was a place where the sound was so total that it became silence. Not silence in the way of absence. Silence in the way of fullness. A sound so complete that the ear surrendered and the body took over, and the body did not mind.

I went because I did not know what else to do. That is the truth. I went because the lowlands had become loud in the wrong way — the clattering, mechanical, stop-start loudness of a world that is still working on its problems — and I wanted the other kind of loud. The kind that does not stop. The kind that does not need you.

## What I Became

I did not become one of them.

Let me be honest about this, because honesty is what the plateau taught me, or rather honesty is what I found on the plateau because the plateau does not teach in the way that implies a lesson plan and a student and an outcome. The plateau teaches by being. And what I found, being on it, was that I could not become one of them, because they did not *become* one of them. They were born on the plateau, and the plateau entered them before language, before sight, before the first thought, and the drone was in their bones the way iron is in lowland blood. I could not acquire that. No one acquires that. You are it or you are not.

But I became something. Not a person of Ythel. Not a lowlander anymore, or not entirely. Something else. Something that the plateau made room for in the way it makes room for everything — by not caring, by being large enough, by having a surface that holds whatever walks on it.

I became a listener.

That is the best word I have, and it is not quite right, because the word implies passivity, and what I did on the plateau was not passive. It was the most active thing I have ever done. I listened with my feet. I listened with my palms. I listened with the back of my neck, with the skin behind my ears, with the small hairs on my forearms that rose when the rain was an hour away and the dark column was still a smudge on the western horizon. I listened to the drone, which was the plateau's voice, and I listened to the shifts in the drone, which were the plateau's breathing, and I listened to the things beneath the drone — the creak of the root mat, the thump of the amphibians, the single sharp notes of the finches piercing the wall of sound like bright threads through dark cloth.

I lived on the plateau for — I do not know. Time on the plateau is not the lowland's time. There were many transitions of the drone, many dawns and dusks, many rains that came from the west and passed and left the air clean. Many evenings sitting by a fire I had built on a flat stone, roasting insects I had swept from the low vegetation in my clumsy basket, eating them with my fingers, the grease running down my wrists, wiping them on my woven cloak that was rough and imperfect because I had woven it myself and my hands were not the hands of the plateau, not yet, not quite, though they were changing.

My hands changed. That is the thing I noticed. The calluses came — along the outer edge of the palm, across the mount of the thumb, the strange raised ridge across the finger pads that corresponds to nothing in the lowland world but to everything in the root-fiber world. My skin darkened. My eyes stilled. I stopped scanning, stopped darting my gaze from thing to thing the way lowland eyes do, and I began to rest my gaze on things with that quality of attention that is almost physical, that has weight. I did not do this deliberately. It happened the way the calluses happened. The plateau entered through the soles of my feet and the palms of my hands and it changed what I was, slowly, the way the root mat changes slowly, layer on layer on layer.

But I did not become one of them. I was still between. Still the person who watched. Only now I was watching from inside, or as close to inside as an outsider could get, and the view was different from inside, and the view was this:

## What I Watched

I watched the old woman at the third basin, though I did not approach her, because approaching would have been a lowland thing to do — the approach, the introduction, the bridge-building that lowlanders do because they cannot simply be in the same space as another person without declaring themselves. I watched her from a distance, from a ridge of exposed root-fiber where I sat in the evenings with my fire and my insects and my imperfect cloak. I watched her press her palm to the ground. I watched her sweep her basket through the vegetation. I watched her sit by her fire and look at the surface as the color drained out of it, and I saw her face in the dusk light — the soft peach-and-gray light that turns the whole plateau into a watercolor — and her face was not peaceful. That is important. Her face was not serene or content or any of the words that lowlanders use to describe faces that are not performing distress. Her face was *present*. She was there, entirely there, and whatever she felt — and I believe she felt much, because the people of Ythel feel intensely, they simply do not perform it — whatever she felt was in her body and in the ground and in the air between, and it did not need to be on her face.

I watched the beetles. The great slow palm-sized things with their armored shells the color of moss. I watched their processions across the surface, dozens of them moving in loose columns, carrying arcs of cut leaf in their mandibles, disappearing into tunnel entrances in the root mat. I crouched and watched one for a long time — longer than I would have watched anything in the lowlands, longer than I would have had patience for — and I saw it pause at the edge of a tunnel entrance and adjust the leaf in its mandibles and descend, and I felt the surface give slightly under my foot where the tunnel ran beneath, and I understood, in a way that was not intellectual but bodily, that the ground I was standing on was hollow, was threaded, was a vast complex of passages and chambers and fungal gardens, and that I was standing on the roof of a city I could not see, built by architects who did not know me and did not need to.

I watched the rain come. This is the thing I watched most carefully, because the rain on Ythel is a thing you see coming, a dark column moving across the flat surface, visible for an hour, and you watch it approach and you know it will reach you and you cannot stop it and you do not try. The first time, I stood in the open and let it come. It came — a wall of warm water, drumming on the waxy leaves, hissing on the moss, and the insect-drone dropped to a lower register, a muffled hum, and the ground moved beneath my feet, not dangerously but perceptibly, the root mat expanding, fibers shifting, the plateau adjusting its shape by millimeters. I stood in the rain and felt the ground breathe and I understood something that I cannot put into lowland words because lowland words are built for lowland concepts and this was not a lowland concept.

The closest I can come: *the world is alive and I am inside it and it does not know I am here and this is not loneliness.*

## The Keepers and Me

They did not avoid me. They did not seek me. They were aware of me — I could tell because of the way the old woman glanced toward my ridge once, briefly, with those large still eyes, and then looked away, and the looking-away was not dismissal. It was acknowledgment. *I see you. You are there. The plateau holds you. That is enough.*

Others passed sometimes. Walking the distances between shelters, visiting, carrying their baskets and their fire-kits, their bare feet reading the surface. They passed and I passed and sometimes we were near each other and sometimes we spoke, a little, in their sparse low rumble of a language that I learned partially, not fluently, because fluency requires the drone in your bones and I did not have it. But partially. Enough to say: *rain comes*. Enough to say: *the basin is good*. Enough to say: *I am here*. And they would nod, or not nod, because nodding is not their gesture, but they would shift their weight slightly, a fractional adjustment of the body that meant the same thing: *yes. You are. We see.*

One woman — younger, compact, her hands already thick with the calluses that mine were only beginning to develop — came to my fire one evening. Not because I invited her. Not because she needed anything. She came and sat and we roasted insects and ate in silence and the drone shifted from its daytime register to its nighttime register and the amphibians began their thumping in the basin and she looked at me with those eyes — those large, dark, resting eyes — and she said, in her sparse low language, something that I understood only later, only after I had turned it over in my body the way you turn a stone over in your hand, feeling its weight and its texture and its temperature.

She said: *The ground knows you are here.*

Not *I know you are here*. Not *we know you are here*. The ground. The root mat. The living surface that holds whatever walks on it. The ground knows you are here, the way it knows the beetles are here, the way it knows the lizards are here, the way it knows the rain is here when the rain comes. You are registered. You are incorporated. You are part of the surface, and the surface does not distinguish between its parts, because all of its parts are it.

I did not cry. The people of Ythel do not cry, and I was learning not to cry, not because crying was forbidden but because crying was a lowland response to a lowland feeling, and the feeling I had was not a lowland feeling. It was the feeling of being held by something that does not have arms. Of being known by something that does not have a mind. Of being inside something that has no inside because it is all inside, all surface, all contact, all warmth, and you are on it and it is around you and you are part of it and it does not need you and it holds you anyway.

## What I Am

I am not a person of Ythel. I have said this. I will say it again because it matters.

I am also no longer a person of the lowlands. I have said this too, and it matters more, because the lowlands are where I came from and the leaving of a place changes you more than the arriving at a new one. The lowlands are still there — I can see them, sometimes, from the ridge, from the plateau's edge, the dark mass of the forest below, the trees rising from actual ground, the smoke of the settlements, the sound of a world that is still working. I look down and I feel the wrongness rising from below — the dead air, the hard ground, the silence that is actual silence, not the plateau's fullness — and I turn back.

I am the in-between thing. The listener. The one who watches. The one who came up from the lowlands because the lowlands were loud in the wrong way and who found a place where the loudness was the right kind, the kind that fills you instead of depleting you, the kind that becomes a condition of being instead of an assault on it.

I live on the plateau now. I have a shelter — rough, imperfect, the walls growing inward because I am still learning to prune the root-fiber, to direct the growth, to build something that is also alive. My fire burns on its flat stone. My basket is woven and rewoven. My knife is chipped basalt, brought up from below, fitted to a handle of wrapped root-fiber that fits my hand and no one else's. My feet are bare. My skin is dark. My eyes are still.

I do not belong. I am not one of them. But I am here, and the ground knows I am here, and the drone is in my chest now, not in my bones, not yet, but in my chest, where it sits, quiet, patient, warm, like a second heartbeat that does not come from my body but from the body I am standing on.

And the plateau goes on. And the beetles move. And the lizards dart. And the finches whistle. And the rain comes from the west. And the old woman presses her palm to the ground. And the child walks to the edge and turns back. And the root mat grows and decomposes and grows. And the sound never stops. And I am here. And the ground is warm. And I am listening.

And it is enough.
