# Inventory of a Temporary Tenant

I am the kind of person who reads the instruction manual after the thing has already broken. So it is probably no surprise that I descend into the Bright Hollow the wrong way.

I don't come through the canopy. I come through a crack in the root-floor, somewhere in the upper layers, where the mat has thinned over a channel that was once a stream and is now a dry seam. I fall. Not far — three meters, maybe four — but I fall through living tissue, through a lattice of roots that catch me and slow me and set me down on the lower level with a care that I did not earn and do not understand. I sit on the root-floor and the root-floor shifts under me, a millimeter, and I understand that I have been *placed*.

The light here is not what I expected. I was told about the bioluminescence, the cold blue-white glow, the shimmer. I was not told that it would feel like being inside a lantern. Like the light was not illuminating the space but was the space, was the medium itself, was the thing the air was made of. I sit in it and I am not separate from it. The light touches my skin and my skin becomes part of the field. I glow. Faintly, briefly, in the blue-white spectrum of the shelf-fungi. When I stand and move away from the spot where I sat, the glow fades. But while I was sitting, I was *in* the light. Not under it. In it.

I am a surveyor. That is the word I would have used before I came here. I survey. I measure. I catalog. I am the kind of person who wants to know the dimensions of a space, the scale, the boundaries. I want a map.

The Bright Hollow refuses to be mapped. Not because it is infinite — it is not infinite, it is finite and bounded, the way a body is finite and bounded — but because it is *moving*. The columns rise and fall on a timescale that makes cartography into storytelling. A map of this place is a story about a moment. By the time I finish drawing it, the moment is over. The column I marked as a landmark has fallen. The shelf-fungus I used as a reference has collapsed. The gap in the canopy I noted as a light-source has closed. The map is a fiction. The map is a memory. The map is a lie that I tell myself about a place that does not hold still long enough to be lied about.

I learn this slowly. I am the kind of person who learns slowly, which is not the same as being slow. It means I have to be hit by a thing several times before I let it in. The hollow hits me several times.

---

The first shelter I find is the one with the handprint in the bark. I find it on what I call my third day, though there are no days here — only the unchanging light and the slow cycling of the vine-cable phosphorescence, which I learn to read as a kind of clock, a tide-chart of brightness that rises and falls in overlapping waves. I sleep when the waves are at their lowest, when the light dims from bright to merely bright, and I wake when it surges again. This is not a schedule. It is a negotiation. My body wants a schedule. The hollow doesn't care what my body wants.

The handprint is on the column behind the shelter, pressed into bark that has grown over it in layers. I put my hand against it. The hand is larger than mine. The fingers are longer. The palm is broader. But the shape is the same — the same architecture, the same opposable thumb, the same four-fingered spread. Whoever made this print was human. Or was close enough to human that the difference doesn't matter at this scale, at this distance, through this many layers of bark.

I hold my hand against the print and I feel the bark breathing under my palm. In, out. Warm, cool. The column does not know I am here. The column does not know the handprint is here. The column is growing, and the handprint is a deformity in its bark, a scar, a memory of a moment when something pressed against it when it was young and soft. The column has been growing over that memory for a long time. It is not preserving the memory. It is burying it. But the burial is slow, and the memory persists, and I am here to see it, and for a moment the three of us — the hand that made the print, the column that grew over it, and my hand resting on top — are in alignment. Then I lift my hand and the alignment breaks.

I take the stone blade from the sleeping platform. It fits my hand. It was made for a hand like mine, or a hand like the one that made the print, and those hands are close enough. The blade is chipped from something that is not stone — it is dense root-material, mineralized, hard enough to hold an edge. I run my thumb along the edge and it cuts me, a thin line of red that is shocking in this blue-white world. My blood is warm and dark and it falls on the root-floor and the floor absorbs it. I watch the spot where it lands. The roots shift. Something there liked it. Something there is eating it.

I wipe the blade on my shirt and I keep it.

---

The staff is the thing I should not have taken. I know this when I take it. The staff is alive — cut from a young column, stripped, shaped, but still growing, still trying to become a column. It is warped and twisted from the tension between what it wants to be and what it was made to be. When I hold it, I can feel it. Not warmth — *direction*. The staff wants to go up. It wants to thicken and harden and push toward the canopy. It wants to bear weight. It wants to be structural. I hold it in my hand and it pulls.

I take it anyway. I need something to walk with. The root-floor is uneven and alive and it moves under my feet and I am not steady on it. The staff helps. I plant it against the floor and the floor grips it — the roots curl around the tip, slightly, the way fingers curl around a pencil. And then I pull it free and the roots let go. They don't hold. They *test*. They are checking the staff, tasting it, recognizing it. It is one of them. It is root-material, column-material, the merged tissue of the floor. When I plant it, the floor thinks the staff is a new column. When I pull it up, the floor lets it go because the staff is too small, too thin, too twisted. It doesn't meet the criteria. It is a rejected column, and the floor allows it to move because it is not worth growing.

I walk with it. The staff and I make a kind of pair — the thing that wants to be a column and the thing that wants to make a map. Both of us are wrong for this place. Both of us are here anyway.

---

I find the tally-marks on the seventh sleep-cycle. I am in the deep hollow by then, following the river, and the columns are enormous and the light is the warm gold that comes through canopy-gaps and the root-floor is hard and polished under my feet. The marks are on a column near the riverbank — notches cut into the bark at a height of about two meters. They are fresh. The bark is pale and wet around them, still healing.

I count them. I count to thirty-one before the column curves away and the marks disappear into shadow. Thirty-one. A month? A cycle? A record of something that happened thirty-one times? I don't know. I put my hand on the marks and the bark is warm and the notches are smooth and the healing is slow, and I think: *something was here that could count.*

I sit next to the marks for a long time. I eat dried fungus from the woven bag I found in the first shelter. The fungus tastes like nothing and everything — mineral, green, sharp, alkaline. It tastes like the air smells. My body accepts it. I don't know if my body should accept it. The hollow is changing me, maybe. Or maybe I am changing to fit the hollow. I don't know the difference. I eat the fungus and I drink from the clay vessels near the water-pools and I sleep on platforms of woven moss and the hollow feeds me and houses me and does not ask me anything in return.

I sit next to the marks and I think about the thing that made them. I think about hands. I think about what it means to make a mark on a living thing. The bark will heal. The marks will disappear. The record will be erased by the growth of the column. Whoever made them knew this. They cut into living bark, knowing the bark would heal, knowing the record was temporary. They were not building a monument. They were keeping a count that only mattered for now. They were thinking in the hollow's time, not in mine.

I think about this for a long time and then I take the stone blade and I cut a mark of my own, next to theirs, at my own height. One notch. The bark resists, then gives. The sap that leaks out is warm and smells like the inside of something alive. The notch is shallow and rough and it will heal over quickly. But for now, it is there. I am there. One mark. One count.

I don't know what I'm counting.

---

I see the thing once. I don't see it clearly. It is in the deep hollow, near the falls, where the river drops into the layer below. I am standing at the edge — not too close, the spray is cold and the root-floor is slick — and I see something move on the far side of the river. It is tall. Or it is standing on something. Or both. I see a shape, dark against the glow of the river, and it is moving along the bank with a gait that is not a walk and not a climb but something between. It is using its hands. I see hands, long-fingered, gripping the roots along the riverbank. I see a body, elongated, narrow, the color of the root-floor. I see a head, or something that functions as a head, turned toward me, and I think I see eyes — dark, light-absorbing, the same kind of eyes the small furred things have, the kind that are visible because they are the only things in this place that do not glow.

We look at each other across the river. The distance is maybe fifteen meters. The light is green and white and blue and moving. The spray falls between us. I hold the staff and the staff pulls upward and the thing holds the roots and the roots grip the bank and we are both here, both in the hollow, both temporary, both counting something.

It turns its head. Or it does something that means the same thing as turning its head. And then it moves, along the bank, away from me, into the darker space where the columns are larger and the light doesn't reach. It moves the way the bark-walkers move — pressed against a surface, breathing with it, riding it. But it is not small. It is my size or larger. And it is not flat. It has depth. It has hands.

I do not follow. The staff does not want to go there — it wants to go up, not down, not deeper. And I don't want to go there either. I have the feeling, the specific feeling, of being in a place that is not for me. Not hostile. Not dangerous. Just not for me. The deep hollow is for the deep-hollow things. The thing with the hands is one of them. I am one of the shallow-hollow things, the temporary things, the weather.

I go back to the nearest shelter. I sit on the woven moss platform. I hold the stone blade and the staff and the woven bag with its dried fungus and its bladder of root-slime. I hold the things that were left by the people who were here before me, the people who were like me or close enough, and I think about the thing with the hands that is here now, that has maybe always been here, that cuts tallies into living columns and knows the river and the deep hollow and the dark spaces where the light doesn't reach.

I think: *we were visitors. They were residents. The hollow doesn't know the difference, but they do.*

I think: *the marks on the column are not for me. They are for the next one of them. They are counting something that has nothing to do with me.*

I think: *I should leave.*

---

I don't leave. Not yet. I stay for a while longer, in the shallow hollow, in the shelters, eating the dried fungus and drinking the glowing water. I walk the root-floor and I feel it moving under my feet and I learn to read it — not the way the people before me read it, not the way the thing with the hands reads it, but in my own way, the surveyor's way, the way of measuring and marking and trying to hold a place still long enough to understand it. I fail. The place doesn't hold still. But the trying is something. The trying is what I am.

I make more marks. On columns, on shelf-fungi, on the root-floor itself. Small marks, shallow cuts, things that will heal over in days or hours. I mark where I've been. I mark what I've seen. I mark the shelters and the water-pools and the places where the columns are falling. The marks are not language. They are not art. They are the impulse of a mind that wants to remember and has nothing to write with but the world itself. The world takes the marks and grows over them and the marks disappear and the world does not remember them and I am the only one who knows they were there.

This is what I am in the Bright Hollow: a notation. A tally-mark on a column that is already healing. A surveyor in a place that cannot be surveyed. A temporary thing in a place that is made of temporary things, where the only permanence is the process — the rising and the falling, the growth and the decay, the light that is always there because the things that make it are always being replaced.

I am the kind of person who reads the instruction manual after the thing has already broken. And the thing is broken. And the manual is written in a language I don't have. And the thing is also not broken, because it was never whole, because it was never a thing, because it is a process, and processes don't break, they just change.

I am the kind of person who stays in a place like this, in a shelter made of stripped branches and living fungus, holding a stone blade that fits my hand, next to marks made by hands I don't recognize, in a light that gets into my skin, on a floor that knows I'm there.

I am a count of one. And the hollow is healing over me already.
