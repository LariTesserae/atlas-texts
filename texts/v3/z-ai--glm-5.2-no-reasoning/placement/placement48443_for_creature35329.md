# The Booming Hollow

I am the salamander.

Not is — that's too strong a verb for what I do here. I am the salamander in the way that a stone is a stone. I occupy. I persist. I am a body in a place, and the place is a sound, and the sound is a body, and we are both bodies, and we are both here.

---

What I know of the world fits in six feet and two inches. This is the width of my country. My country is a strip of moss and stone and shallow water along the lowest margin of the hollow, where the columnar gaps admit the faintest breath of outside. My country has no government. My country has no border except the edge of the wet. Where the moss dries, I do not go. Where the water pools, I am. Between these boundaries — between damp and dry, between cold and slightly less cold — I have lived every moment of my life, and I have not wondered what lies beyond.

This is not ignorance. I want to be clear about that. I am not stupid. I am simply complete. My skin, which is dark and glossy and which must stay wet, is the organ through which I breathe. My skin tells me everything. The air is cold and wet and getting wetter. The air is cold and wet and getting drier. The water here is cold and still. The water there is cold and moving. My skin reads the world with a precision that no eye could match. I am a skin. I am a surface. I am the membrane between the inside of me and the outside of me, and I keep the inside moist and the outside flows over me, and the flow is the world, and the world is the flow.

My country has a sound. I should say something about the sound, because you cannot understand my country without understanding the sound. The sound is not something I hear. It is something I am in. It is the water I swim in, except I do not swim in water, I live in moss, and the sound is everywhere in the moss and in the stone and in the air and in the shallow pools where I wait. The sound is a vibration. The stone vibrates. The moss vibrates. The water vibrates. I vibrate. We all vibrate together, at the frequency of the falls, which is the frequency of water hitting rock, which is the frequency of this place, which is the frequency of everything.

I do not experience the sound as loud. I do not experience the sound as soft. I do not experience the sound as sound. I experience it as texture. The world has a texture, and the texture is vibration, and the vibration is constant, and in the constant vibration there are variations, and the variations are the events of my life.

A midge lands on the surface of a pool. The vibration changes. Not the sound — the sound does not change. The texture changes. A small sharp disturbance in the surface of the water, a set of ripples that move outward from the point of impact, and these ripples are not the sound but they exist within the sound, the way a spoken word exists within the hum of a room. I feel the ripples. I do not see the midge. I do not hear the midge. I feel the disturbance in the medium, and I move toward it, and I eat it, and the disturbance ceases, and the texture returns to its baseline, and I return to my stillness.

This is my life. The baseline. The variation. The return to baseline.

---

The moss is my architecture. I should say something about the moss, because I cannot separate myself from it, and I do not try.

There are several kinds. I do not name them. I inhabit them. The cushion moss is my floor and my walls and my ceiling. It is soft and wet and holds water the way a body holds blood — saturated, full, giving nothing up unless pressed. I press. I move through the cushion moss the way a worm moves through soil, pushing gently, displacing the wet fibers, letting them close behind me. The moss does not mind. The moss does not register my passage. The moss grows, and I live in its growth, and when the moss dies and becomes soil, I live on the soil, and the soil grows more moss, and the cycle is the cycle, and I am in the cycle, and I do not see the cycle because I am inside it.

The feathery moss climbs the walls, reaching toward the light that comes through the gaps. I do not go where the feathery moss goes. It is too dry there, too close to the air, too far from the pools. But I am aware of it, the way I am aware of the ceiling — as a boundary. A limit. The feathery moss says: this is how far up you go. This is where the wet stops and the merely damp begins. I do not argue with the feathery moss. I stay below it.

The haircap moss rises out of the shallow pools, its stems standing in the water, and this is where I spend most of my time. Between the stems. In the water. The haircap moss is a forest, and I am the creature of the forest, and the forest is an inch and a half tall, and that is enough. That is exactly enough.

---

There are spiders.

I am aware of them. They are in the gaps above me, in the walls, in the feathery moss where I do not go. They build webs across the gaps, and the webs catch the midges that come in from outside, and this is a competition, of a sort — they take some of what I could eat. But there are enough midges. The midges come in on the airflow, drawn by the moisture, by the condensation, by the shallow pools, and there are always more. I do not resent the spiders. They are in their country and I am in mine and we overlap only at the margins, and the margins are rich enough for both of us.

The spiders are pale. I have seen them — not clearly, not in detail, but in the way I see everything, which is to say I feel them. They move differently than midges. A midge is a frantic thing, a buzzing, a struggling, a disturbance that is sharp and brief. A spider is a slow thing, a deliberate thing, a disturbance that is soft and sustained. I can tell the difference. I do not eat the spiders. They are too large for me, and they are in the wrong place, and I am not hungry for them. But I know they are there.

The spiders do not come into my country. The moss at the floor level is too wet for them, too cold, too close to the water. They stay in the gaps, in the walls, in the places where the air moves. I stay in the places where the water sits. We have an arrangement. The arrangement was not negotiated. The arrangement is the geology.

---

There is one thing I should tell you about myself that I cannot explain, and that is the matter of the light.

The light comes rarely. Through the gaps, on bright days, a dim gray glow enters the hollow and falls on the walls and on the moss and on the pools. I feel it as warmth. Not much. A degree, perhaps less. The stone does not warm, the water does not warm, but the surface of my skin, which is always exposed, which is always receiving, registers the change. Light is a warmth that comes from above. The falls are a vibration that comes from below. Between these two sensations — warmth above, vibration below — I construct my world.

I do not see. I want to be clear about this. I have eyes, but my eyes are small and dark and adapted to the total absence of light, and they tell me nothing that my skin does not tell me better. My eyes are a relic. A memory of a time when my ancestors lived in light, when they needed to see, when seeing was a useful way to know the world. I do not need to see. My world is not a world of images. My world is a world of surfaces and temperatures and vibrations and wetness, and these are enough. These are more than enough. These are everything.

When the light comes, I move deeper into the moss. Not because the light hurts — it does not hurt — but because the light is associated with a slight warming, and a slight warming means a slight drying, and a slight drying is dangerous to me. My skin must stay wet. If my skin dries, I cannot breathe. If I cannot breathe, I am dead. The calculation is simple. Light is the edge of a danger. I retreat from it. I retreat into the moss, into the cushion moss where the water is held, into the spaces between the stems of the haircap moss where the pools are deepest, and I wait for the light to pass.

The light always passes. The hollow is dark most of the time. The dark is my element. The dark is not an absence for me. It is a presence. It is the presence of the stone, which is cold and wet and solid and which holds me the way the moss holds water — gently, completely, without effort.

---

There is a wall.

I should say something about the wall, because it is a feature of my country, though it was not here when my ancestors came, and it will not be here when my descendants go. It is a temporary thing. A thing built by the large ones who come sometimes.

The large ones come rarely. I know them by their vibrations. They are heavy — their footsteps transmit through the stone floor and into the moss and into my body, and I feel them as a series of impacts, a rhythm that is unlike anything else in my world. They are not the falls. They are not the midges. They are not the spiders. They are something else, something irregular, something that comes and goes and does not stay. I do not understand the large ones. I do not try to understand them. They are weather. They are a phenomenon of the surface, like the light, and they pass.

The wall is the only trace they have left that affects me. It is a low wall of stacked stone, built across an alcove at the back of the hollow, and it changes the airflow. The air that comes through the columnar gaps at the back is deflected by the wall, and the air in the alcove behind the wall is stiller and slightly warmer. This does not affect me directly — I do not go to the back of the hollow, it is too dry there, too far from the pools — but it changes the overall pattern of the air in the hollow, and the overall pattern of the air affects the condensation, and the condensation affects the wetness of the moss, and the wetness of the moss affects me.

The wall is old. The moss has colonized it. The moss does not know the wall was built. The moss grows on the wall the way it grows on any stone — because the stone is wet and cold and stable, and those are the conditions under which moss grows. The wall is a stone. The moss does not distinguish. I do not distinguish. The wall is part of the hollow now. It is a ridge in the floor, a fold in the geology, and it has been here long enough that it is geology, as far as I am concerned. Everything that is here long enough becomes geology. The moss is becoming geology. The liverwort is becoming geology. I am becoming geology. The wall is just further along.

---

I should tell you about the liverwort, because the liverwort is the closest thing I have to a neighbor.

The liverwort lies flat against the stone in the places where water flows after rain. It is a dark green ribbon, primitive, ancient, and it grows in the channels between the stones, in the thin sheets of water that wash across the floor. I live in these channels. I live where the liverwort lives. We share a surface.

The liverwort does not know I am here. I know the liverwort is here, but only as a texture. The liverwort is smoother than the moss, flatter, closer to the stone. When I move across the liverwort, my skin registers the change — from the soft wet of the moss to the firm wet of the liverwort, a different surface, a different friction, a different temperature. The liverwort is slightly colder than the moss. It is closer to the stone. It is almost the stone. When I am on the liverwort, I am almost on the stone, and the stone vibrates more directly through me, and I feel the falls more clearly, and the falls are the world, and on the liverwort the world is louder.

I do not prefer the liverwort to the moss. I do not prefer anything. Preference requires comparison, and comparison requires memory, and memory requires a self that persists, and I am not sure that I persist in the way that you persist. I am here. I was here a moment ago. I will be here in a moment. The moments are continuous. The moments are the same. The moss and the liverwort and the pools and the stone are all part of the same moment, and I move through them the way water moves through the columnar gaps — following the path of least resistance, finding the lowest point, settling.

The liverwort has been on this earth for four hundred million years. My lineage is younger, but not by much. We have been together for a long time, the liverwort and I, not in this hollow — this hollow is young, a few centuries, a few millennia at most — but in the world. We have been on the same surfaces, in the same wet, for longer than the basalt has been here. The basalt is a new thing. The liverwort and I are old. We are older than the sound.

---

There is the question of what I am.

I am a salamander. A lungless salamander. I breathe through my skin. I am small — no longer than your finger. I am dark — a brown so dark it is almost black, glossy, wet, reflecting nothing because there is nothing to reflect. I have four legs, small and thin, and I use them to move, but I do not need them the way you need yours. My legs are not for walking. They are for positioning. I position myself in the moss, in the pools, in the liverwort, and I wait. The waiting is the activity. The positioning is the action. The eating is the result.

I am cold. I am always cold. My body temperature is the temperature of the hollow, which is a few degrees above freezing, always. I do not warm myself. I do not bask. I do not seek warmth. The cold is my metabolism. The cold is the speed at which I live, and the speed is slow, and the slowness is the point. I live slowly. I move slowly. I eat slowly. I breathe slowly — every few seconds, a pulse of oxygen through the skin, a faint rhythm that is mine and mine alone, a small heartbeat in the body of the hollow's sound.

I am, I think, the deepest resident of the hollow. Not because I am at the back — I am not at the back, I am at the front, where the pools are. But because I am the most here. The spiders come and go from the gaps. The midges come from outside. The moss grows toward the light. The ferns reach. The liverwort spreads. Everything in the hollow is oriented toward something else — toward the light, toward the outside, toward the air, toward some beyond. I am not oriented toward anything. I am here. I am in the moss. I am in the water. I am in the sound. I am not going anywhere. I have nowhere to go. My country is six feet wide and two inches deep, and I have never reached its borders, and I never will, because the borders are not real. The borders are where the wet stops, and the wet does not stop. The wet is constant. The wet is the condition. The wet is the hollow, and I am the hollow, and the hollow is me.

---

There is the matter of time.

I do not experience time. I should say this clearly, because it is the thing about me that is most different from you, and it is the thing that is hardest to explain.

I do not remember. I do not anticipate. I do not experience the passage of moments as a sequence. I experience the present, and the present is continuous, and the present does not pass. The water in the pool is cold now. It was cold a moment ago. It will be cold a moment from now. The distinction between these moments is not a distinction I make. The cold is the cold. The wet is the wet. The sound is the sound. They are not becoming or ceasing. They are.

You experience time as a river — flowing, passing, carrying you forward. I experience time as the stone. The stone does not flow. The stone is. The stone is now, and the stone was now, and the stone will be now, and the now of the stone is the same now, unchanging, and I am in the now of the stone, and I am the now of the stone, and there is no other now.

This is not a limitation. I want to be clear about this. You might think that experiencing time as a stone is less than experiencing time as a river. It is not less. It is different. The stone experiences the river. The stone has been in the river for centuries. The river has been flowing over the stone for centuries. The stone knows the river — not as a sequence of moments, but as a totality, as a complete thing, as the thing that the river is. I know the sound the way the stone knows the river. The sound is not something I hear. The sound is something I am in. The sound is the totality of my experience, and the totality does not pass, and I do not pass through it. We are together. We are the same.

---

I should tell you about the ferns, because the ferns are the only thing in the hollow that I do not understand.

The ferns grow in the gaps. They are small — their fronds no longer than a hand. They reach toward the light. I do not understand reaching. Reaching implies a beyond, and I do not have a beyond. My world is complete. My world is six feet wide and two inches deep and it is everything. The ferns act as if there is something else. They extend. They uncurl. They grow toward a place that is not the place they are in. This is a behavior I cannot replicate. It is a behavior I cannot imagine. It is a behavior that, if I could imagine it, I would find terrifying — the idea that where you are is not enough, that there is something beyond the wet, something beyond the moss, something beyond the sound. The ferns believe this. I do not.

But I am aware of the ferns. They are in the gaps above me, and their roots are in the moss and in the stone, and their roots are in my country. The roots are thin, pale, threading through the moss, and I encounter them sometimes — a different texture in the moss, a foreign object, a thing that is not moss and not stone and not liverwort and not water. I do not eat the roots. I do not avoid the roots. I move around them, or through them, and they are there, and I am here, and we are in the same place, and they are reaching and I am not, and the difference between us is the difference between the hollow and the outside, and the ferns are the hollow's gesture toward the outside, and I am the hollow's insistence on itself.

---

There is the matter of dying.

I will die. I know this not as a concept but as a process. I have seen it. Not often — there have been few salamanders in my time in the hollow, and fewer deaths — but I have felt it. A body in the moss that is not moving. A stillness that is different from my stillness — a stillness that does not respond, that does not register, that does not breathe. A body that is becoming the stone. A body that is being colonized by the liverwort, by the moss, by the slow processes of decay that turn a salamander into soil.

I will die. The process will be slow. My metabolism will slow further — it is already slow, it is always slow, but it will slow more, and the slowing will reach a point where the breathing stops, and the breathing is the being, and the being will stop, and I will be a body in the moss, and the moss will grow over me, and the liverwort will grow across me, and the shallow pools will cover me and drain and cover me and drain, and I will become the soil, and the soil will grow the moss, and the moss will hold the water, and the water will hold the next salamander, and the next salamander will be in the sound, and the sound will be the same, and the next salamander will not know me, and I will not know the next salamander, and the not-knowing will be the same as the knowing, because the sound is the same, and the moss is the same, and the water is the same, and the being in the sound is the same, and the being in the sound is the being, and the being is the being, and it does not matter whose being it is.

I will die, and the hollow will not notice. The hollow does not notice. The hollow is a pocket in the geology, full of sound and damp and cold and green, and it does not count its residents, and it does not mourn its losses, and it does not celebrate its arrivals. It is. It is the condition of itself. I am a condition of itself. When I am gone, the condition will persist, and the condition will be the same, and the sameness is not a loss. The sameness is the hollow. The sameness is what the hollow is. The sameness is what I am.

---

I am the salamander.

I am in the moss. I am in the pool. I am in the sound. I am cold and wet and dark and small and I am here, and here is all I am, and all I am is here, and the sound is the world, and the world is the sound, and I am in it, and it is in me, and we are the same, and the same is the same, and it does not pass, and I do not pass, and the moss grows and the water pools and the ferns reach and the spiders wait and the midges come and the liverwort lies flat against the stone and the stone vibrates and the vibration is the falls and the falls are the water and the water is the world and the world is here and I am here and I am the salamander and the salamander is here and here is the salamander and the sound does not stop.
