I am the eastern drainage keeper's second daughter, and I am fourteen, and I have a secret that I have told no one because I do not have the words for it yet, and the words may not exist.

My name is Reedling. This is not my given name. My given name is the one my mother uses when she is cross with me, the one that is three syllables and means something in the old talk that I have never asked the precise meaning of because asking would mean admitting I don't know, and in Mossbarrow, not knowing a thing about your own name is a small shame that is easier to carry than to confess. Reedling is what the children call me, because I have always been near the channels, always waist-deep in the shallow water, always coming home with mud on my knees and a frog in each hand and my hair plastered flat against my skull. The name stuck the way moss sticks to bark. I did not choose it. I did not refuse it.

I am the one who climbs the central oak at night.

---

I should explain what I am, first. What I am *made* of.

I am compact in the way the description demands — I have heard the lowland traders use that word, *compact*, when they pass through on the ridge path and stop to trade dried fish for our mushrooms, and they mean it as a slight, I think, or at least as a description of something they find diminished, but I am not diminished. I am *condensed*. There is a difference. I am shorter than my mother by two finger-widths and broader across the shoulders than she was at my age, according to her, though she says it with a tone that suggests she is not certain whether this is good. My calves are hard from years of walking the moss mat, which gives differently depending on where you step — the high spots are firm, the root-ridges are solid, the low spots are spongy and will swallow your foot to the ankle if you are not paying attention, and I am always paying attention. I cannot remember a time when I was not paying attention. My feet read the ground the way my mother's hands read bark — without looking, without thinking, the knowledge in the body, below the mind, in the place where the knowing lives.

My skin is the color of the house-trees. I know this because I have looked, and because the lowland traders look, and because the contrast is visible when I stand beside them. They are pale the way the undersides of leaves are pale — a greenish white that comes from living without sun. I am not that. I am the grey-brown of old bark, darker on my forearms where the rain hits me every day, darker on my face, lighter on my stomach where the light never reaches and the damp sits against the skin without evaporation. My knuckles are rough. My nails are cut close to the quick, the way everyone's are, because I have seen what happens when they are not — my mother lost the nail of her right big toe three winters ago to a rot that started underneath and spread, and the smell was terrible, and she could not walk her round for a week, and Reed — my mother's partner, the drainage keeper, the man whose name I carry in my daily round though I do not call him father because that is not a word we use — Reed carried her route in addition to his own, and the settlement's western channels backed up, and a pool formed near Thatch's house, and the old woman came out and stood in the doorway and said *the water knows where to go*, which meant: it is going where it can, and you are not letting it, and fix the channel. We fixed the channel. My mother's toenail grew back. It grew back thicker.

I am getting the details wrong. I am starting with the wrong details. This is because I am not certain what details matter to someone who is not from here, and I am writing this — or speaking this, or thinking this in the shape of words that could be written or spoken — for someone who is not from here. I can tell because the language I am thinking in is the language of the lowlands, the trader's tongue, the one with words for *building* and *garden* and *forest* as separate things. In my tongue, the one I speak with my mouth, the one that lives in the acoustic space between the frogs and the pigeons, there is no word for *building*. There is a word for *the thing that shelters* which is also the word for *the thing that grows* which is also the word for *the thing that is tended*, and it is one word, and it does not distinguish between tree and house because there is no distinction to make.

I am thinking in the wrong language. But it is the only language that can cross the ridge path. So I will use it, and it will be wrong, and that is the first thing to know about being me: I am a thing that does not translate.

---

Let me tell you about the climbing.

It started — no. Let me tell you what it *is*, first, and then where it started, because the order matters, and the order is not the order of events but the order of knowing.

What it is: I climb the central oak at night. The meeting hall oak. The one with the spiral, the trained ramp of branches that ascends three levels into the canopy. The grower's structure. Tarn's masterwork, though it is older than Tarn — she has maintained it for twenty years, but it was grown by someone whose name I do not know, someone who is now a tree at the northern edge of the settlement, a wych elm with a doorway grown shut and the marks of old grafts visible in the bark like scars on a hand.

The spiral is for the daytime. At night, I do not use it. I climb the outside of the trunk, on the side that faces the western ravine, where the bark is thickest with lichen and the epiphytes have built up a substrate deep enough to hold my weight in the footholds I have made over the past two years. I climb in the dark. I do not bring a fire-brand. I do not need one. My hands know the bark. My feet know the branch-places. I have climbed this tree so many times that my body has memorized it the way my body has memorized the moss mat — in the muscles, in the proprioception, below the mind.

I climb to the third level. The highest platform. This is where the growers sometimes stand during the day to survey the settlement, to look for growth that needs managing, to check the canopy for gaps. At night, no one is here. The platform is woven hazel, laid over the branch framework, and it is slightly springy underfoot, and it is damp, because everything is damp, and it smells of moss and old wood and the faintly sulfurous scent of the lichen that crusts the upper branches.

And I sit. And I look out. And I listen.

This is the secret. This is the thing I cannot name. I sit in the canopy of the central oak at night and I listen to Mossbarrow, and I hear something that I do not hear from the ground.

From the ground, Mossbarrow is a texture of sounds — the frogs, the pigeons, the insects, the water, the crack of wood, the calls of the people. It is dense and layered and continuous, and I have grown up inside it, and it is my silence, the way the silence of a mountain is silence to someone who lives on the mountain. I do not hear it as noise. I hear it as the sound of being here.

From the canopy, it is different. From the canopy, I can hear the *shape* of it. The settlement has a sound that is not the sum of its sounds. It has a sound that is — I am reaching for the word, the trader's word, the word that does not exist in my tongue — it has a sound that is *whole*. A sound that is one thing. As if the frogs and the pigeons and the insects and the water and the wood and the people were not making sounds but were being played, were notes in a — I do not have the word. I have heard the lowland traders play a kind of instrument with strings, a small one they carry in their packs, and the sound it makes is a single sound made of many sounds, and that is the closest I can come. But it is not right, because the instrument has a player, and Mossbarrow does not have a player. Mossbarrow is playing itself.

I sit in the tree and I hear Mossbarrow play itself, and I feel — this is the part I cannot say — I feel like I am hearing what I am. Not who I am. What I am. I am a note in that sound. I am a vibration in that texture. My body, my breath, my feet on the moss mat, my hands on the bark, my voice calling to my mother across the drainage channel — all of it is part of the sound, and the sound is part of the place, and the place is part of the sound, and I am inside it, and it is inside me, and for the time I am in the tree, I do not know where I end and Mossbarrow begins.

This is the secret. Not that I climb. The climbing is a transgression — the central oak is Tarn's domain, and children are not meant to climb it, and certainly not at night — but it is a small transgression, the kind that every child in Mossbarrow commits in some form. The secret is the *feeling*. The feeling that I am not separate. The feeling that my skin is not a boundary but a surface, like the bark of a tree, a place where inside and outside meet and exchange, where the moisture of the air becomes the moisture of my body and the warmth of my body becomes the warmth of the air. The feeling that I am not *in* Mossbarrow but *of* it, not living *in* a place but living *as* a place, as the tree lives as a place, as the moss mat lives as a place, as the drainage channels live as a place.

I have not told anyone this. I have not told my mother. I have not told Reed. I have not told Tarn, though I think Tarn knows, because Tarn looks at me sometimes with the tilted-head attention that means she is parsing me from the background, and I see something in her eyes that is not suspicion and not approval but *recognition*. She sees something in me that she has seen in the trees. The desire to grow in a particular direction. The *want* of a living thing, expressed in its branching, in the angle of its buds, in the place it puts its energy.

I do not know what she sees. I am afraid to ask.

---

Let me tell you about the day, because the night is only a small part of it and I do not want you to think I am only the climbing. I am also the round. I am also the channel. I am also my mother's daughter and Reed's second and Thatch's visitor and the girl who caught a mud-pike by hand last autumn and would not stop talking about it for a week.

My round is the eastern channels. This is my inheritance, or half of it — Reed keeps the full system, but he has given me the eastern third to manage as my own, and I have been managing it for two years, since I was twelve, and I know it the way I know my own hands. There are fourteen channels in my section, running from the weeping stone faces at the eastern edge of the settlement down through the narrow spaces between the houses and into the collection pool at the southeastern corner, where the water gathers before draining down the hillside into the ravine. I walk them every morning. I clear the blockages. I check the flow. I note the color of the water — clear is good, brown is acceptable, green is algae, white is foam, which means something upstream is breaking down. I note the smell. I note the standing pools — their depth, their surface cover, their frog population. I note the moss mat around them — dry-ground species or wet-ground species, and where the boundary is, and whether it has moved since yesterday.

This is not exciting. I want to be clear about this. The round is not exciting. It is necessary, and it is mine, and I do it with the same attention that I bring to everything in Mossbarrow, because inattention means a backed-up channel means a flooded path means a rotting wall means a failing structure means someone's house grows a gap means the rain gets in means the fire pit goes cold means someone is cold and wet and sick. The chain of consequences is short. I have seen it happen. I have caused it, once, when I was eleven and skipped my round for a day because I had found a nest of baby shrews in the moss mat near the northeastern channel and wanted to watch them, and the channel backed up, and the pool rose, and the moss mat around old Sedge's house went from dry-ground to wet-ground in six hours, and the wall began to soften, and Sedge came out and stood in his doorway and looked at the water with an expression that was not anger but weariness, the weariness of a man who has been fighting water for forty years and knows he will fight it for forty more, and I saw that look and I felt something that I now recognize as the feeling of having failed a living thing. Not a person. A living thing. The wall. The mat. The channel. The system. I had failed the system, and the system had shown me what failure looks like, and it looks like a tired man standing in a doorway looking at water.

I do not skip my round now. The shrews can wait.

---

I want to tell you about my hands, because my hands are how I know the world, and because I think that if you understand my hands you will understand something about Mossbarrow that the description of the place cannot say.

My hands are sixteen years old — I am not certain of my exact age, but sixteen is close, because my mother says I was born the year the southern ash fell, and that was sixteen years ago or seventeen. They are the hands of a person who has been gripping wet things since before she could walk. The calluses are not in the places that lowland hands have calluses — not the palms, not the finger-tips, but the insides of the knuckles, the webbing between the thumb and forefinger, the outer edge of the palm where it presses against bark when I grip a branch. These are the calluses of living architecture. These are the marks of a body that has been shaped by its habitat.

I can feel things with my hands that I cannot see. A wall that is beginning to rot from the inside has a warmth to it that a healthy wall does not — the decomposition is exothermic, and the heat is faint, barely perceptible, but my hands can find it. I learned this from Reed, who learned it from his mother, who learned it from someone whose name is now a tree at the southern edge. The knowledge is in the hands. It is not in the mind. I cannot explain *why* a rotting wall is warm. I know only that it is, and that my hands know it, and that when my hands find that warmth, I call for the grower, and the grower comes, and the wall is cut back and rewoven, and the rot is stopped.

I can feel the sap moving in a branch if I hold it long enough. This is not mystical. It is not something I claim as a special gift. It is what happens when you put your hands on a living thing and pay attention. The sap moves with a rhythm that is slower than a heartbeat but is, like a heartbeat, a pulse. You can feel it in the stillness. You can feel the tree being alive, the way you can feel a person being alive when you hold their wrist. I do not know if Tarn feels this. I have not asked. I suspect she does, because she holds the bark of the trees the way I hold it — with the patience of someone who is waiting for information that comes through the skin and not the ears.

---

Let me tell you about Thatch, because Thatch is important, and because I am one of the children who brings her food, and because I think she knows my secret.

I bring Thatch the bracket fungus from the north side of the central oak. This is my self-appointed task. No one assigned it to me. No one asked me to do it. I began doing it when I was twelve, the year my round started, because I was near the central oak every morning and because I had seen the fungus there and knew it was the kind Thatch likes — the dark, ruffled one, not the pale oyster, not the ephemeral puffball. The ruffled one that grows in overlapping shelves and has the texture of leather and the taste of smoke. I pick it, I wrap it in a burdock leaf, I carry it to her house on the western edge.

She is always awake. I do not think she sleeps the way the rest of us sleep — fully, deeply, in the confidence of the warm house. I think she sleeps the way a tree sleeps, which is to say she rests without ceasing. When I arrive, she is sitting on her sleeping platform with her back against the wall of her elm, and her hands are in her lap, and she is humming the sound she makes — the low sustained hum that is not language and is not song and is something else, something I do not have a word for. The tree is vibrating with it. I can feel it when I step through the doorway, a tremor in the floor, in the walls, as if the house is resonating.

She takes the fungus. She thanks me — not in words, in a nod, in the tilt of her head that means *yes* and *I see you* and *enough*. She looks at me with the large eyes of all Mossbarrow people, and her eyes are larger than most, and darker, and the intensity of her gaze is the intensity of someone who has been looking at the same thing for so long that the thing has become transparent, and she sees through the surface to the structure beneath. I feel seen by Thatch the way I feel seen by the central oak — completely, without judgment, as a fact of the landscape.

She said something to me, once. Last autumn. The rain was heavy, and I came to her house with the fungus and my hair was plastered flat and my hands were wrinkled from the water, and she looked at me and she said: *You hear it.*

I did not ask what she meant. I did not need to. She meant the thing I hear in the canopy. The whole sound. The sound of Mossbarrow being itself. She meant that she hears it too, or heard it, or has been hearing it for so long that it has become indistinguishable from her own heartbeat. She meant: I am not alone in the secret.

I did not say anything back. I put the fungus in her pot. I left. The tree hummed as I stepped through the doorway.

---

I want to tell you what I am, now, in this moment, as I think these words in the trader's tongue and feel them form in the shape of a language that does not fit the thing I am trying to say.

I am a girl in a tree. That is the simplest way to say it. I am sixteen, and I am sitting on the highest platform of the central oak, and it is night, and Mossbarrow is breathing below me, and I can hear it — the whole sound, the single sound made of every sound, the sound of a living thing being itself. I can feel the tree under me, the bark against my legs, the slow pulse of the sap, the creak of the wood in the wind. I can feel the moss mat below, sixty paces down, soft and wet and alive with the movement of a thousand small bodies. I can feel the water moving through the stone beneath that, the underground stream that feeds the weeping stone that feeds the channels that I tend every morning. I can feel the frogs. I can feel the pigeons. I can feel the people in their houses, their fire pits glowing, their breath in the warm close air.

I can feel myself in it. Not separate. Not above. *In*. The way a branch is in a tree. The way a channel is in a mat. The way a note is in a sound. I am Reedling, and I am Mossbarrow, and the two things are the same thing, and I do not know where one ends and the other begins, and I do not think there is an end, and I do not think there is a beginning, and I think that this is what Thatch means when she says *the water knows where to go*, and I think this is what Tarn hears when she puts her hands on the bark, and I think this is what the trees are doing when they grow, and I think this is what I am doing when I climb, and I think this is what I will do when I am old and my body is small and my skin is eroded by the weather and my hands are still active and my house is my body and my body is the house — I think I will sit against the wall and hum and the tree will vibrate and the vibration will be the same vibration as the frogs and the pigeons and the water and the moss and the children running below and the rain falling above, and I will be in it and it will be in me and the sound will not stop.

The sound will not stop.

---

It is morning now. The light has brightened, barely. The first frog has called. I descended the oak in the dark — hand over hand, the bark familiar as my own skin — and I am back in my house, and my mother is stirring, and Reed is already gone to his round. The fire pit is fed. The smoke rises through the gap in the roof. My mother looks at me with the tilted-head attention and does not ask where I have been, because she knows, or suspects, and because the knowing is enough. She hands me the clay pot. I fill it at the channel. I drink. The water is cold and tastes of stone.

I step outside. The moss mat is wet. It is always wet. My feet find the solid places. I begin my round.

The eastern channels are clear this morning. The water runs clean, the color of the stone it comes from, and the frogs are calling in the pools, and the moss mat is thick and green and the boundary between dry-ground and wet-ground species has not moved since yesterday. I walk the full circuit. I clear two small blockages — a clump of algae in the seventh channel, a tangle of leaf litter in the tenth. I check the standing pools. I note the frog populations. I press my hand to the wall of the house at the eastern edge — Sedge's house, the one that flooded when I was eleven — and the wall is cool, not warm, and the wood is firm, and I nod, and I move on.

I am Reedling. I am the eastern drainage keeper's second daughter. I am a note in the sound. I am a branch on the tree. I am a channel in the mat. I am Mossbarrow, and Mossbarrow is me, and the rain is falling, and the frogs are calling, and the water knows where to go, and I am going with it.

I am going with it.
