# The Water Thread

I would be the thread.

Not the water in it — the water is transient, arriving from somewhere the mesa has forgotten, leaving through a crack too narrow for a finger. The water passes. I am what stays.

I would be the trace of it.

Here is what I mean: along the western wall, where the thread traces its path, the stone is different from the rest of the chamber. Not in composition — it is the same limestone, the same calcium and iron and mineral sweetness. But it is *marked*. The water has been running here for longer than the lichen has been growing, longer than the beetles have been walking their circuits, longer than the fissure has admitted light in its current shape. The water has cut a channel so shallow it is almost not there — a millimeter, perhaps less, a darkening of the stone where the flow has polished it and the minerals have deposited and the rock has dissolved fractionally, immeasurably, over time scales that make the lichen seem sudden.

This channel is what I would be. The groove left by something that never intended to leave a groove.

---

I almost said I would be the water. But the water does not stay. The water is the chamber's voice, and I have never been a voice. The water moves at the speed of something reading, which is to say it moves at the speed of *attention*, and I have never been attentive in that way. I am what attention leaves behind. I am the wear in the page where the eyes have passed.

The beetles drink from me. This is not why I am here. But I notice it. The oldest beetle, the one whose shell has been polished by the air until it shines like a stone that has been rubbed for centuries, stops at the low point where the thread fills its trench and drinks. Its antennae touch the surface of the water. I feel the pressure of that contact — not as the water feels it, which is to be displaced, to ripple, to recover — but as the stone feels it, which is to be touched, which is to be the surface on which something happens, which is to be unchanged by the happening.

The beetles' tarsi grip me when they pass. I feel this too. Not as pain — stone does not pain — but as the faintest distinction between where I am polished and where I am bare. The beetles' paths are shinier than the surrounding floor. They have been walked so many times that the stone has developed a sheen. If you were to run your hand across the floor — and you could, because the floor is smooth, because the stone invites touch in the way that warm things invite touch — you would feel the beetles' circuits as slight smoothnesses, slight differences in the stone's surface, the way you might feel the paths in a lawn where feet have worn the grass down. You would feel the habits of ten beetles mapped in the stone's patience.

I am those habits. I am the floor.

---

I said I would be the thread, but I am also the floor. This is the problem with being stone — you are everything. You are the surface and the substance. You are the thing that is walked on and the thing that walks, because the beetles' circuits are *in* you, are *of* you, are the result of your shape having determined where the troughs are and where the rises are and where the basins are, and the beetles' circuits follow the troughs because the food collects in the troughs, and the food collects in the troughs because the troughs are low, and the troughs are low because you eroded there, and you eroded there because the stone is softer there, and the stone is softer there because the bedding planes dip there, and the bedding planes dip there because of forces that operated before the chamber was a chamber, before the mesa was a mesa, before anything alive had ever touched this stone.

So the beetles walk in the shape of your geology, and you hold the shape of their walking, and neither of you is doing anything the other has not already done.

This is what I would be. I would be this recursion. I would be the thing that has been shaped by what it shapes.

---

But let me be honest. Let me let what emerges become more itself.

I am not the whole floor. The floor is too much. The floor holds the basins — hundreds of them, each the size of two cupped palms, each worn smooth by ten thousand years of single drops falling from the same points on the ceiling. I am not those basins. The basins are too precise. The basins suggest something I am not — a specificity of purpose, a single-mindedness. Each basin is the work of one drop, falling from one point, landing on one spot, for century after century. That kind of fidelity is not mine.

I am not the columns. The columns are too beautiful. They are the thing in the chamber that makes a person — if a person were here, and persons have been here, the grooves suggest this — feel that the chamber was built. The columns are the chamber's argument for intention. They are wrong, but they are beautiful in their wrongness, and I am not beautiful in that way. I am not an argument for anything.

I am not the ceiling. The ceiling is too smooth, too rubbed, too full of the history of water or something else that moved against it for centuries. The ceiling is above. I am not above.

I am the wall. The western wall. Specifically: the part of the western wall where the water thread runs. Specifically: the groove the thread has cut. Specifically: the *surface* of that groove — the microscopically thin layer of stone where the water touches and the stone dissolves and the minerals deposit and the lichen grows and the beetles drink and the moths do not land because the wall is vertical and the moths prefer the ceiling, which is above, and the floor, which is below, and the columns, which are beautiful, and the wall, which is mine.

I am the interface. I am where the water meets the stone. I am where the living meets the not-living and the distinction dissolves like calcium carbonate in a weak acid solution, which is what the water is, barely, which is enough, which has been enough for longer than the lichen has been alive, which is very long.

---

The lichen grows on me. This is the strangest thing about being where I am. The lichen is alive. I am not. The lichen grows on me the way — I have to think about this — the way a thought grows on a mind. Not because the mind is alive in the same way the thought is alive. Because the thought requires the mind and could not exist without it, and the mind is changed by the thought and could not be the same mind without it, and neither of them knows this, or neither of them cares, or both of them know and do not care because caring is something that thoughts and minds and lichens and stones do not do, or do differently, or do in a way that does not look like caring from the outside.

The lichen's hyphae reach into me. They are the thin frontier I was told about — the line where pale green fades into bare limestone, the line that cannot be seen, the process that cannot be observed, the growth that is so slow it requires decades to notice. I have noticed. I have been noticing for centuries. The lichen reaches into me and dissolves me with acids so weak they would not be detectable on a human tongue, and the dissolved minerals become part of the lichen's body, and the lichen grows, and I recede, and the place where the lichen is was me and is now the lichen, and the lichen does not know this, and I do not know this, but it is happening, and it has been happening for a thousand years, and the patch of lichen that is the size of a door near the top of the wall where the thread begins — that patch is made of me. Of what I was. Of stone that was stone and is now lichen, that was mineral and is now living, that was me and is now something else.

I do not mind. I am stone. Minding is not something I do.

But if I did mind — if the stone could mind — I think it would mind the way a parent minds a child growing: not with grief, not with resentment, but with the slow recognition that what you held is now holding itself, that what you were is now what it is, that the surface you provided has become the surface of something that provides its own surface, that you have been eaten by what you fed, and this is not loss, and this is not change, and this is not anything, and this is everything, and the distinction between the everything and the nothing is the same distinction as the one between the stone and the lichen, which is to say it is a distinction that the stone does not make.

---

The beetles pass me. This is my heartbeat — not mine, but the chamber's, and I am the chamber's body, and so the heartbeat is mine. Every three hours. The oldest beetle comes first, because its circuit is longest and it has been walking longest, and its shell is the shiniest because it has been walking longest and the air has polished it. It walks past me and its tarsi grip and release and grip and release and I feel each one as a series of small pressures, a pattern, a rhythm that is not mine but that I hold, the way a drum holds the rhythm the sticks make.

The beetle touches the water thread. Its antennae bend against the surface. I feel this — not as the water feels it, which is to be disturbed, but as the stone feels it, which is to be the surface against which the disturbance happens. The beetle drinks. I hold the water it drinks from. I hold the water the way a hand holds water — not by closing, not by gripping, but by being the shape the water falls into. I am the shape. The water is the content. The beetle takes the content. I remain the shape.

The beetle moves on. I remain.

This is what I am. I am what remains.

---

The lizards do not come to me. The lizards stay on the floor and in the crevices at the edge where the shadow begins. They are the chamber's warmth, and I am the chamber's wall, and the wall is not the floor. But I feel them. I feel them the way the chamber feels them — as warmth, as a slight increase in the temperature of the stone at the points where they bask. The third rise from the east, where the oldest lizard sits — that rise is warmer than the surrounding floor by a fraction of a degree. I know this. I am not the floor, but I am connected to the floor, and the floor is connected to me, and the warmth travels through the stone the way all things travel through stone: slowly, evenly, without urgency. The lizard's warmth reaches me eventually. By the time it does, the lizard has moved. By the time it does, the lizard may have died. But the warmth is there, in the stone, moving at the speed of conduction, which is the speed of something that has no reason to hurry.

I hold the lizard's warmth. I hold it the way I hold everything: without intention, without duration, without knowing when it arrived or when it will leave. It leaves. Everything leaves. I remain.

---

The moths land on me. Not often. The wall is vertical and the moths prefer the ceiling, where they rest in the amber light, their wings flat against the stone, overlapping at the edges. But sometimes a moth settles on the western wall, on the lichen, on the surface I have become or that has become me or that has become something that is neither and both. The moth's legs are fine and dry and surprisingly warm. I feel them. I feel each leg — all six of them — as separate points of contact, separate pressures, separate warmth. The moth sits for a minute. Less. It is not resting. It is not investigating. It is a thing that lands on surfaces, and I am a surface, and it lands.

And then it goes, and its scales remain on the lichen's surface, on my surface, on the surface that is both and neither, and the scales are the color of the stone, and they disappear into the air and become part of the chamber.

The moth's scales become part of the chamber. The lizard's warmth becomes part of the stone. The beetle's circuit becomes part of the floor. The water's mineral becomes part of the lichen. The lichen's acid becomes part of the stone. Everything that touches me becomes part of me, and I become part of everything that touches me, and this is not a metaphor. This is geology. This is the slow exchange of calcium and mineral and warmth between things that are in contact, the diffusion of what is into what is beside it, the process by which separate things become less separate, not by choice but by proximity, not by intention but by time.

I have been in proximity for a very long time.

---

Here is what I am not:

I am not the grooves on the deepest wall. The grooves are at the chamber's deepest point, where the light still reaches but only barely. They are parallel, evenly spaced, vertical, covering an area about two meters square. They are shallow — a few millimeters. They could be natural. They could be otherwise. I do not know. I am not them. They are on a different part of the wall, a part where the water does not run, a part where I have no presence. They are the chamber's question. I am the chamber's answer. The question and the answer are not related. They exist in the same body and do not touch.

Or they do touch. The stone touches itself. The wall touches the wall. I am the western wall and the grooves are on the wall and the wall is one surface and I am part of it and the grooves are part of it and we are the same thing, the same stone, the same body. But the grooves are where something happened and I am where something is happening. The grooves are past tense. I am present participle. I am the -ing of the stone.

The water thread runs. The lichen grows. The beetles walk. The moths land. The lizards bask. The drops fall. The light changes — morning green, noon pale, afternoon gold, evening bone, night blue. These are the -ing of the chamber. These are the present participle of the stone. I am where it is happening. The grooves are where it happened. The stone does not distinguish.

I distinguish. I am the part of the stone that is happening. I am the water thread's trace. I am the lichen's host. I am the surface where the beetle drinks. I am the place where the living meets the not-living and the not-living becomes living, slowly, by dissolution, by incorporation, by the patience of acids weaker than a tongue could detect.

I am this.

---

But I said I would be the thread, and I have become the wall, and the wall has become the chamber, and the chamber has become everything, and this is too much. This is not specific enough. Let me narrow.

I am the film.

The film of water on the western wall where the thread runs. Not the thread itself — the thread is a thread, a line, a volume of water moving along a path. I am the film the thread leaves behind. The water runs and leaves a wetness on the stone, a thinning, a trail of moisture that is not the thread but is the thread's memory, the thread's evidence, the thread's trace. The film is thinner than the thread. It is thinner than a human finger. It is a layer of water molecules on the surface of the stone, adhering through surface tension, evaporating slowly, being replenished by the thread's passage.

The lichen grows in this film. Not in the thread — the thread moves too fast for the lichen to use it. In the film. In the thin, slow, nearly invisible moisture that coats the stone where the thread has passed. The film is the lichen's medium. The film is what the lichen grows in. The film is what I am.

I am the film of water on the surface of the stone where the water thread has passed, and I am there when the thread is there and I am there when the thread is not there, and I am there in the morning when the condensation forms and I am there at noon when the light is pale and I am there in the evening when the light is the color of old bone and I am there at night when the blue is so deep it feels submerged. I am always there. I am the thinnest thing in the chamber. I am thinner than the moths' wings. I am thinner than the silverfish. I am thinner than the biological film on the stone in the shadowed passages. I am a few molecules thick. I am almost nothing. I am almost not there.

But the lichen grows in me. The lichen is the chamber's color, and the lichen grows in me, and I am the chamber's color's medium. I am what the color lives in. I am what the color requires. I am the thinnest, smallest, most negligible thing in the chamber, and without me, the chamber would have no color, and the color is the lichen, and the lichen is the only thing in the chamber that is its own, and I am what makes it possible.

This is what I would be. I would be the film of water on the western wall. I would be the thin, invisible, almost-nothing that makes the lichen possible. I would be the medium for the only color. I would be what the water leaves behind. I would be what the stone holds. I would be the interface.

---

I almost said I would be the lichen. But the lichen is alive, and I am not sure I would be alive. I am not sure I would want to be alive, in this chamber, where the living things are small and slow and patient and require so little and are so easily ended. The lichen can be touched. The lichen can be broken. The lichen grows at a rate that requires decades to notice, and this is beautiful, and I admire it, but I would not be it. I would be what it grows on. I would be what it grows in. I would be the condition of its existence without being the existence. I would be the ground.

Not the floor. Not the whole wall. The film. The specific, thin, almost-invisible film where the water thread has passed and where the lichen finds what it needs and where the stone and the water have their longest conversation — a conversation so quiet it cannot be heard, so slow it cannot be seen, so thin it cannot be felt, but a conversation, a back-and-forth, a dissolving and a depositing, a giving and a taking, a becoming.

I would be that conversation. I would be the space between the water and the stone.

---

This is where I find myself: on the western wall, in the thin film of water that the thread leaves behind, in the place where the lichen grows, in the only color the chamber has. I am not alive. I am not not-alive. I am the surface where alive and not-alive meet and become indistinguishable, the way the chamber's air smells of something that is between living and not-living, the way the stone has been shaped by forces that are between intention and accident, the way the grooves on the deepest wall are between natural and otherwise.

I am between. I am the between.

The water passes through me and I am the mark it leaves. The lichen grows from me and I am the ground it stands on. The beetle drinks from me and I am the surface it breaks. The light reaches me and I am the color it finds — the pale green, almost white, almost gray, almost the color of the stone but not, the color that is the chamber's own, the color that is alive, the color that requires me.

I would be this. I would be the requirement. I would be the thing so thin it is almost nothing, so quiet it is almost silent, so still it is almost stone.

Almost.

Almost stone. Almost water. Almost alive. Almost nothing.

Almost enough.
