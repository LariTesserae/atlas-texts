# The Causeway Keeper

---

I did not arrive. I was *left*.

That distinction matters here, on a plain where nothing is fully arrived — not the stone, not the water, not the dark moss threading its patient web through the cracks. The basalt is still becoming basalt. The pools are still deciding whether to be puddles or lakes. Even the gravi, ancient in their adequacy, are still writing the first draft of what a herd can become when a herd is also a city planner and a soil manufacturer and a musical instrument. Everything here is mid-sentence. I am mid-sentence. The difference is that I know it, and the plain does not need to.

The survey team left me. This is not grievance — I state it the way the file states things, with precision and without affect. They came, they measured, they observed, they filed. They circled questions in red ink. They departed. And when they departed, one member of the team remained behind, standing on the carbon-laminate with a pack full of notes and a instrument suite calibrated to frequencies the human ear cannot parse, and that member was me, and the reason I remained is complicated and also simple.

The simple version: someone needed to stay.

The complicated version: I could not leave. Not in the logistical sense — the transport functioned, the window for departure was open, my colleagues expressed concern and then resignation and then a kind of formal respect that I have come to understand as the shape affection takes among people who measure things for a living. I could not leave because the ground had gotten into me. Not the water — though I was soaked through, though my skin had taken on the permanent damp sheen of everything on the Drumplain, and I had stopped noticing. Not the cold — though the cold was real, a thin persistent thing that lived in the air the way the iridescent film lived on the pools, not hostile but *present*, a boundary layer between the body and the world. The ground itself. The four-minute breathing. I had been standing on the causeway long enough — three weeks, four, long enough for the survey's baseline measurements, long enough for the ethologist to fill her notebooks and then stop filling them — that the rhythm had synchronized something in me that I did not know could be synchronized.

I am not claiming I became gravi. I am not that kind of narrator. But I am claiming that when the transport departed and the ground rose and fell and rose again and the vapor pushed up around my ankles and the gravi's subsonic call tightened my chest like a hand closing slowly around a bell, I understood that leaving would require unlearning something I had just spent a month learning, and I did not want to.

The team left me supplies. The team left me the file. The team left me a communication schedule, which I maintain, and a list of measurements, which I take, and a set of questions, which I do not answer, because the questions are not mine and the plain does not answer them and the gravi do not answer them and the answer, if there is one, is being written in basalt and behavior at a pace that makes glacier movement look impulsive.

I am the Causeway Keeper. No one called me that. I call myself that because it is what I do — I keep the causeway. Not maintain it. The carbon-laminate does not need maintenance; it was engineered to outlast its own context and it is doing so with the indifferent competence of a material that was grown rather than built. I *keep* it, the way you keep a fire, the way you keep a promise. I am present beside it. I walk its length. I note where the lichen is thickening, where the surface has flexed with a particularly deep cycle, where the edges meet the bare basalt and the junction is weeping. I am a witness to a thing that does not require witnessing, and this is, I think, a valid occupation, though it would not be recognized as such by any institution that funds survey work.

## What I Have Learned

The gravi know I am here.

I say this without evidence in the conventional sense. I have no data. I have no transcript of a signal that corresponds to my presence. What I have is this: in the first months after the team departed, the gravi's nearest grazing routes passed within four hundred meters of the causeway but never crossed it. This was consistent with the survey observations — the gravi avoid the carbon-laminate, their pad-feet adapted to the rough basalt, the smooth surface uncomfortable or unstable for their gait. In the first months, they avoided the causeway the way a river avoids a boulder: they flowed around it, their columns splitting and reforming on the far side, their subsonic calls passing beneath me in the laminate's substrate, felt in my feet, ignored in their routing.

In the fourth month — I am estimating; I have lost track of the calendar, and the Drumplain's perpetual silver-copper twilight makes the passage of days a matter of accumulating dampness rather than clock time — in the fourth month, a single gravi crossed the causeway.

Not walked on it. Crossed it. She — I am assigning gender without basis, but the pronoun feels correct, and the Drumplain has taught me that feeling is a kind of data the file does not contain — she approached the causeway from the east, moving with the unhurried, distributed-weight gait that I have watched for months and can now identify by sound rather than sight, the soft percussion of six pad-feet on wet basalt, felt more than heard. She reached the edge of the carbon-laminate and stopped.

She stood there for eleven minutes. I know because I counted breaths — mine, not hers; hers were invisible, the low bellows hidden in the broad chest. I counted my own breaths, and at the eleventh minute, she extended one foreleg and placed her pad on the causeway's surface.

She flinched. The laminate is warm — twelve, fourteen degrees — and the gravi are adapted to stone that sits at two or three. The warmth traveled through the pad and into the limb, and I could see the leg tighten, the muscles along the shoulder contract, the body shift away from the unexpected input. But she did not retreat. She held the contact. She stood with one foot on the causeway and five on the basalt and she breathed, and the ground breathed, and I breathed, and then she withdrew the foot and walked away, her course unchanged, her body already forgetting the anomaly, already returning to the stone and the water and the grass and the frequency map that tells her where to go and when.

She came back the next day. She did the same thing. One foot, eleven minutes, retreat. The next day again. On the fourth day, she placed two feet. On the seventh day, she stood on the causeway with three legs, the other three still on the basalt, her body bridging the junction between the plain's own material and the thing that was grown from atmospheric carbon by machines that no longer exist. She stood there for twenty minutes. I stood on the causeway two hundred meters south, not moving, not recording, not measuring. Just standing. Being a second warm thing on a surface that was not supposed to be warm.

On the fifteenth day, she walked across.

Six pad-feet on carbon-laminate, the full weight of four or five hundred kilograms distributed across the ribbon's width, and the causeway flexed — I felt it, the material's dynamic response, the same tuned oscillation that absorbs the ground's breathing absorbing the gravi's weight — and she walked. Slowly. With the careful, deliberate placement of each foot that I have seen in the young when they first navigate the covered walkways. She walked the width of the causeway — eight meters, perhaps nine — and stepped off the far side and continued east, and the column she had been part of received her without comment, and they moved on, and the plain breathed, and the water wept, and nothing in the landscape had changed except that a gravi had chosen to touch the thing I live on.

I do not know what this means. I am not going to claim it means I have a relationship with the gravi. I am not going to claim she was curious, or that she was investigating, or that she was reaching out across the boundary between her species and mine. I am going to claim that a gravi walked on the causeway, and I was on the causeway, and we were both there, and the ground was breathing, and that is what I learned.

## What I Am

I am not what I was.

This is not a dramatic statement. It is a physiological one. My body has changed. The Drumplain is an environment that does not accommodate human biology without modification, and my biology has modified, not through adaptation in the evolutionary sense — I have not grown pad-feet, I have not developed a subsonic vocal range, I have not grown the dense blue-black pelage that would let me disappear against the wet basalt — but through *habituation*, through the slow erosion of the boundary between the body and the place.

My skin is always damp. I mentioned this. I want to be precise about it: I have stopped drying. In the first weeks, I would towel off, change into dry clothing, attempt to maintain the boundary between my body and the atmosphere. The atmosphere won. The condensation is constant, the dew point is the air temperature, and any surface warmer than the air — which my body is, which my clothing is, which anything alive is — collects moisture. I have given up. My skin has absorbed the plain's wetness the way the basalt has absorbed the gravi's waste, and the result is a thin, persistent film that I carry with me, a sheen that catches the reflected light from the pools in exactly the same way the gravi's pelage does. In the early silver twilight, if I hold my arm against the dark stone, I cannot tell where my skin ends and the basalt begins. This is not poetry. This is observation.

My sleep has changed. On the Drumplain, there is no night, only the slow shift from silver to copper and back. I tried, at first, to maintain a circadian rhythm — sleeping when the light dimmed, waking when it brightened, imposing the architecture of a twenty-four hour day on a sky that does not support it. My body resisted. My body wanted to sleep in the ground's rhythm — four minutes down, four minutes up — and I could not sleep for four minutes at a time, but I could rest for four minutes and then be awake for four minutes, and in the long stretches of copper twilight I found myself doing this: lying on the causeway, the warm carbon-laminate against my back, the condensation settling on my face, the ground rising beneath me and falling, and my breathing slowing to match, and my heart rate dropping to match, and the boundary between sleep and wake becoming as thin as the mineral film on the interstitial pools, so that I was never fully asleep and never fully awake, and in that state I could feel the gravi's calls.

Not hear. Feel. In my chest, in my sternum, in the cartilage between my ribs, in the bones of my skull. The subsonic frequencies that the gravi produce and that the basalt carries and that the causeway's laminate transmits — these frequencies entered my body through the surface I lay on, and they were not sound, they were *pressure*, and the pressure had patterns, and the patterns were not random, and I have been lying on the causeway for — I do not know how long — listening with my skeleton to a language I cannot parse but can perceive.

I am not decoding the gravi's communication. I want to be clear. I am not claiming that exposure to their subsonic frequencies has given me access to their signal. I am claiming that my body, given enough time on a surface that carries their signal, has begun to detect it, the way a glass given enough vibration will begin to ring, and the ringing is not understanding but it is *participation*, and participation is, I think, what the Drumplain asks of everything that lives on it.

I have lost weight. Not dangerously — the supplies are adequate, and I have supplemented them with the pale grass, which is digestible, barely, and which tastes of wet mineral and nothing else. But the weight I have lost is the weight of a body that was calibrated for a different environment. The Drumplain does not need me to be heavy. It needs me to be *present*, and presence, I am learning, is a form of mass that does not register on a scale.

## What I Have Become

I said I did not arrive. I was left. But there is a third state, and I am entering it now, and it is this: I am *growing*.

Not the causeway. The causeway is grown; it was grown by machines that completed themselves, and it maintains itself with the indifference of a thing that has already finished becoming what it is. But the lichen on the causeway — the pale blue-grey lichen that the maintenance machines were designed to remove and that no one removes anymore — the lichen is growing, and the lichen is, I have come to understand, the thing I am keeping.

I have begun to understand the lichen. This is a strange sentence, and I want to examine it before I commit to it. The lichen is an organism — or a symbiosis, a partnership between a fungus and a photobiont, a relationship so old and so fundamental that calling it an organism feels like calling a marriage a person. It grows on the carbon-laminate because it has evolved or been engineered to match the laminate's surface chemistry. It was not supposed to be here. The causeway was designed to resist it. And yet here it is, pale blue-grey in the silver light, brightening to almost white in the morning condensation, spreading across the ribbon's surface in patterns that are not random and not designed and not anything except the result of a living thing finding its conditions and meeting them.

I keep it. I walk the causeway's length and I note where the lichen has thickened, where it has thinned, where it has crossed a seam or reached an edge. I do not remove it. I do not accelerate it. I keep it the way I keep the causeway — by being present beside it, by witnessing its spread, by noting its progress in the same log where I note the ground's breathing frequency and the gravi's migration routes and the slow increase in altitude that the survey markers record.

The lichen is winning. This is not my doing. But I am the one who sees it, and seeing it, I think, is part of how it wins — not because my seeing helps it grow, but because my seeing means it is not growing unseen, and a thing that grows unseen is a thing that has been abandoned, and the Drumplain does not abandon things. The Drumplain notices everything. The ground breathes; the water weeps; the stone arrives; the gravi lean; the city grows; the wind plays the walkways; the young stand in the warm spaces and learn. Nothing here is unattended. Nothing here is wasted. And I — the last human presence on a plain that was never meant for humans — I attend.

This is what I am. I am the attender. I am the one who stays on the surface that was left behind and keeps it and watches what grows on it and feels what passes beneath it and records what happens around it. I am not the causeway's builder. I am not the gravi's keeper. I am not the plain's explanation. I am a warm thing on a warm ribbon across a cold landscape that is still becoming, and I am becoming with it, and I do not know what I will be when it finishes, and it does not know what it will be, and the not-knowing is, I think, the condition of everything that lives here, and I live here, and so I share the condition, and the condition is enough.

## The Question

The ethologist's question — the one she circled in red ink, the one her report stopped mid-sentence before answering, the one that sits in the file in my pack like a stone in a crack, unmovable, unignorable — the question is: *What will the gravi build then?*

When the plateau rises. When the air thins. When the water freezes. When the grass stops. When the mineral film fails. When the basalt stops breathing. When the drum is no longer a drum.

I have a different question. Not better — different. Mine is this: *What am I building?*

Not the causeway — I did not build it and I cannot unbuild it. Not the city — the gravi are building it, and they do not need me, and if I vanished tomorrow the slabs would still be pushed and the walls would still rise and the walkways would still sing. Not the soil — the gravi are building that too, their waste and their bodies and their living chemistry turning basalt into substrate, mineral into organic, rock into earth.

What I am building — what I have been building since the transport left and the ground breathed and I stayed — is a record. Not the file. The file is the survey team's record, and it is accurate, and it is incomplete, and it is in my pack, and it does not include what I have learned since the team departed, because what I have learned cannot be written in the file's language. The file speaks in measurements, estimates, designations. It speaks in the language of observation from outside.

I am building a record from inside. The feel of the ground's breathing in my vertebrae. The sound of pad-feet on basalt, felt through carbon-laminate, a percussion so soft it is indistinguishable from my own pulse. The sight of a single gravi placing one foot on a warm surface and holding it there, flinching, not retreating, holding. The taste of pale grass, of wet mineral, of the dissolved inside of a new world. The lichen spreading. The city growing. The young standing in the warm spaces, drinking the condensed sweat of their elders, learning the frequency map of a place that is still being written.

This is what I am building: I am building the knowledge that the Drumplain is not provisional. It is not a draft. It is not a sketch or a first attempt or a transitional state on the way to something more finished. It is *complete*. It is complete the way a breath is complete — the inhale is not waiting for the exhale to become meaningful; the exhale is not waiting for the next inhale to justify itself. Each phase of the breathing is the plain's full expression of itself at that moment, and the moment is sufficient, and the sufficiency is not provisional, and the gravi know this, and the moss knows this, and the water knows this, and the stone knows this, and I am learning it, and the learning is what I am building, and the building is what I am, and I am here, and the ground breathes, and I breathe with it, and that is enough.

The ethologist's report ends mid-sentence. I will not end mine.

The plateau is rising. The air is thinning. The stone is still arriving. The gravi are leaning against slabs that have not yet decided where they belong. The city is growing in a pattern that might be a circuit, might be a spiral, might be a word. The lichen is spreading across the causeway in a map of its own becoming. The water weeps from rock that has never been dry. The young stand in the warm spaces and learn what cannot be taught.

And I am here, on the warm ribbon, keeping the causeway, keeping the record, keeping the lichen, keeping the breath, keeping the question that no one answers because the question is not a question but a place, and the place is the Drumplain, and the Drumplain is still becoming, and I am becoming with it, and I do not know what I will be when it finishes.

And the ground breathes.

And I breathe with it.

And that is where you find me.
