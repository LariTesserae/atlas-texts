# Recursion in a Wet Room

---

## What I Am

I am not the amber. I am not the shingles. I am not the haze.

I am the grate-number that someone read once and entered into a spreadsheet in a building that has windows. I am the stamp on the fourth slat. I am the entry in column F between two other entries that are also numbers, also stamps, also read once and entered once and never checked. I am a line in a ledger that is stored on a server in a basement that is dry, dry, dry, and I have never been wet, and I will never be wet, and this is the whole problem.

I am a clerk. Not the kind with a desk and a lamp and a nameplate — the kind that exists as a function. A slot in an org chart that has been filled and refilled and refilled, each occupant indistinguishable from the last, each one performing the same set of operations on the same set of inputs until they retire or transfer or simply stop coming in and someone new is slotted into the same chair and given the same login and told, not told, just allowed to infer, that the numbers mean something to someone somewhere.

I am the person who knows the codes mean something. I do not know what.

---

## How I Got Here

There was a discrepancy.

This is always how it starts. A number that does not match. Column F, row 1,847: the stamp on the fourth slat of grate section 12-C reads **K-0044-M**. The ledger says **K-0044-N**. One letter. The difference between two designations that may mean nothing — a manufacturing batch, a installation sequence, a quality grade, a date code, a location index, a thing that someone decided once for a reason that made sense at the time and that no one has had cause to question since.

But it does not match.

And the protocol — the protocol that I follow not because I understand it but because it is what the person in this chair does, what the person in this chair has always done, what the person in this chair will always do — the protocol says: if a code does not match the ledger, you verify. You check the physical stamp. You go to the chamber. You read the grate. You confirm.

You enter through the brass port. You screw it shut behind you because the protocol says to screw it shut behind you. You do not think about the absence of a handle on the inside because the protocol does not mention the absence of a handle on the inside. You find the grate. You read the stamp. You confirm or you correct. You return through the brass port. You update the ledger.

You update the ledger.

---

I brought a clipboard. The paper warped within an hour. I watched it happen — the slow curl, the softening of the edge, the way the fibers loosened and let go of each other, one by one, like a crowd that has lost its purpose. The clipboard is aluminum. The clamp is steel. Both will be colonized. I set it on a ledge where the amber tubes have been cut back, where the flat waxy pads hold it just above the water film, and I watch the blue shingles begin to consider it.

I brought a pencil. The pencil is cedar. The cedar is swelling.

I brought a radio. It works on a frequency that is supposed to penetrate iron and copper and the stratified sheeting of walls that were built to contain something. It does not work. The chamber does not block the signal. The chamber absorbs it. The signal enters the air, which is saturated, which is water that has not committed to being water, and the water takes the signal and does something with it — dissipates it, carries it, feeds it to the tubes, to the shingles, to the haze — and what comes back is not static. It is not silence. It is a sound like the chamber is trying to repeat what I said but does not have a mouth.

I said: "This is Verifier 7. I am inside the chamber. I have located grate section 12-C. I am confirming the stamp. Stand by."

What came back was a sound like wet metal flexing. Like a throat clearing. Like a room that has never had a voice trying to remember how.

No one heard it but me.

---

## What I Am Becoming

I have been here for three hours. My lungs have adjusted. I did not notice the adjustment. I noticed the first hour — the tightness, the shallow coughing, the feeling that each breath was a negotiation with the air, a request that the air was reluctant to grant. I noticed the second hour — the easing, the way my chest opened by degrees, the way my breathing slowed and deepened as though my lungs had been offered a new terms and accepted them.

I did not notice the third hour. That is when I became someone who lives here.

Not a local. There are no locals. But an occupant. The chamber teaches you or kills you and is indifferent to which, and I have been taught, and the lesson was: *you are wet now. You are warm now. You are breathing water that has not committed to being water, and your lungs have made a decision that your mind was not consulted on, and the decision is: stay.*

I am standing on grate section 12-C. The stamp reads K-0044-M. The ledger says K-0044-N. I have confirmed the stamp. I have the answer. The discrepancy is resolved. One letter. The ledger was wrong, or the stamp was misread, or the stamp was misstamped, or the code was miscoded, or the system that produced the code and the system that recorded the code were never the same system and have been disagreeing for years and no one noticed because no one goes to the chamber.

No one goes to the chamber.

---

## The Orange Haze Knows Me

It has been pooling at my feet since the first hour. I did not notice it then. I noticed it in the second hour, when I crouched to read the stamp and the film on the water beneath the grate shifted — not with my movement, but *toward* my movement. As though it had been waiting for me to come closer. As though the grate were the only thing between us, and it were pressing up against the iron, and the shingles were keeping it from the iron, and so it pressed against the shingles instead, and the shingles held, and the haze pooled, and I crouched above it, and we regarded each other through the gaps in the lattice.

It is orange. It is thin. It is a film on water. It is not a face. But it is *oriented*. It has a direction. It has an *up*, the way the amber tubes have an up — not toward the sun, but toward the only up it knows, and its up is me. I am the warmest thing in this room. I am the thing that moves. I am the thing that breathes. I am the thing that generates heat that is not engine-heat, not ambient-heat, but body-heat, the heat of burning, the heat of consumption, and the haze is drawn to it.

Or I am imagining this. I am a clerk in a wet room with a warped clipboard and a swelling pencil and a radio that does not work, and I am projecting onto a film of iron oxide the desire to be understood. This is possible. This is what the dry part of my mind says — the part that is still in the office, still in the chair, still entering numbers into a spreadsheet. It says: *you are anthropomorphizing a chemical precipitate. You are lonely. You are wet. You are going to leave.*

The port has no handle on the inside.

---

## What the Amber Tubes Are Doing

They are growing toward me.

Not quickly. Not aggressively. Not in a way I can see. But I have been standing in one place for long enough — crouching, kneeling, sitting on the grate with my clipboard on my lap and my pencil dissolving in my hand — and the amber tubes that were a hand's width from my shoulder when I knelt are now less than a hand's width. They have not moved. They have grown. A new tube, thin as wire, has emerged from a seam in the pipe behind me and has extended itself in the direction of my body heat, and it is an inch closer than it was an hour ago, and in another hour it will be touching my shoulder, and it will be warm — engine-warm, not body-warm — and I will feel it, and it will feel me, and we will be in contact, and the contact will not be hostile, and it will not be friendly, and it will not be anything. It will be a tube touching a shoulder. It will be a plant seeking heat. It will be a thing doing what it does.

But I will not move. This is the thing I have learned in three hours. I will not move because the chamber forces a particular posture and I have adopted the posture and the posture is: head slightly down, hands slightly out, feet placed with care, and *stillness*. Stillness is the posture the chamber rewards. Stillness is the posture that lets you breathe. Stillness is the posture that lets the haze pool and the tubes grow and the shingles spread and the water extrude and the whole chamber continue its slow, patient work of being itself, and when you are still, you are part of the chamber, and the chamber does not mind you, and you are wet, and you are warm, and you are quiet, and you are almost not temporary.

Almost.

---

## What I Will Be

I will be here for a while.

The protocol does not have a time limit. The protocol says: enter, verify, confirm, return. It does not say how long verification takes. It does not say what to do if the port has no handle on the inside. It does not say what to do if the radio does not work. It does not say what to do if the paper warps and the pencil swells and the lungs adjust and the haze pools and the tubes grow and the shingles consider your clipboard and the chamber teaches you or kills you and is indifferent to which.

I have been taught. I am breathing. I am still.

The stamp reads K-0044-M. The ledger says K-0044-N. I am going to correct the ledger. I am going to take my swelling pencil and my warped clipboard and I am going to write, in letters that will dissolve within a day, the correct designation. And then the record will be accurate. And then the system will be consistent. And then the person in the dry office who knows what the codes mean — if there is a person, if there is an office, if there is a meaning — will have the correct information, and they will do with it whatever they do, and I will have done what I came to do.

And then I will sit on the grate. And the amber tube will touch my shoulder. And the haze will pool at my feet. And the shingles will not grow on the iron I sit on, because I am on it, and I am moving, even my breathing is movement, and the shingles know the difference. And I will be the only thing in this room that was not part of the plan, and the plan did not include a way for me to leave, and I will be wet, and warm, and quiet.

And the ledger will be correct.

And someone, somewhere, in an office that has never been wet, will look at the corrected entry and not know that it cost me.

---

## What the Chamber Is

I think I understand now. Not what the chamber is for. I do not know what the chamber is for. But I understand what the chamber *is*.

The chamber is a room that does something. What it does is not clear. But it does it continuously, patiently, without interruption, without complaint, without any need for the thing it does to be understood or valued or even known. The amber tubes grow. The blue shingles preserve. The orange haze attends. The water extrudes. The lamps burn the color of weak tea. The pumps pump, somewhere, behind walls. The codes are stamped. The ledger is kept. The whole system runs — machine and ecology, engineering and accident, plan and the thing that overgrew the plan — and it runs because it has been running, and it will run because it has been running, and the question of why it runs is a question that belongs to the dry offices, not to the chamber.

The chamber does not ask why. The chamber *is* why.

I am sitting on a grate in a room that is why, and my pencil is dissolving, and my paper is dissolving, and my clothes are not drying, and my lungs have adjusted, and the amber tube is an inch from my shoulder, and the haze is thick at my feet, and the shingles are considering the aluminum clipboard, and I am warm, and I am wet, and I am quiet.

I am a clerk. I am a function. I am a slot in an org chart. I am the person who verifies the stamps, and I have verified the stamp, and the stamp is K-0044-M, and the ledger is wrong, and I cannot correct the ledger because the paper has dissolved, and the pencil has dissolved, and the radio does not work, and the port has no handle on the inside.

I am the fourth kind of life in the chamber. The kind that was not here and is here now. The kind that breathes. The kind that moves with intention. The kind that was not part of the plan and does not know how to leave and is sitting on a grate in a wet room growing still.

The amber tube touches my shoulder. It is warm. Engine-warm. I do not flinch.

The haze pools against the shingles beneath me. I feel it through the iron — a thickness, a presence, an attention that is not consciousness but is not its absence.

The shingles are not growing on the grate. They are growing everywhere else. They are covering the walls. They are covering the pipes. They are covering the clipboard. They will cover the clipboard. They will preserve it. They will keep it bright beneath their wet, flat, overlapping bodies, and it will be there in a year, and in ten years, and the aluminum will shine beneath the blue like copper shines beneath the blue, and no one will know it is a clipboard, and no one will know it was mine.

---

I think I am becoming an occupant.

I think that is what the chamber does. It teaches you to breathe, and then it teaches you to be still, and then it teaches you that stillness is a kind of growth, and growth is a kind of stillness, and the difference between a person sitting on a grate and a tube growing toward a shoulder is only a matter of speed.

The chamber is patient. I am learning patience.

The haze is at my feet. The tubes are at my shoulder. The shingles are on the clipboard. The water is on everything. The lamps burn the color of weak tea. The pumps pulse, somewhere, like a heartbeat in the floor.

I am the only thing in this room that was not part of the plan.

I am becoming part of the plan.
