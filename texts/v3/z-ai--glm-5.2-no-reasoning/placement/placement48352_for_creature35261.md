# The Deep Canal Balancer

I am writing this with my hands. Not on anything. The motion itself is the record — fingers curved in the shapes that mean what I mean, held where the elders can read them in the grey light, passed along the catwalk in the way that things are passed here, hand to hand, warm to warm.

---

I did not choose to be this. The choosing happened before I was born, in the way that choosing happens in this place: slowly, without announcement, in the space between one breath and the next.

My mother was a diver. Good enough that the elders remember her. She could hold her breath long enough to reach the third-floor doorways of the buildings on the outer edge, where the swimmers cluster in the shadow of the old lintels, thick as a hand can close. She would surface with six, sometimes seven, and never gasp. The gasping was what separated the adequate from the good. The good ones come up quiet.

She is not diving now. The minerals took her knees the way they take everyone's knees, and then her shoulders, and then the small joints of her fingers, so that her hands became a map of the stiffness that the water gives. She sits on the roof of the building we call *many-wings* and she mends the nets, and her hands know the work even though her hands are not what they were, and the nets she makes are still the best. They hold their shape one day longer than anyone else's. In a place where a net lasts a month, one day is a lifetime.

My father — the man whose body made half of mine — was a walker. Not all of us are walkers. Most people move across the catwalks as they need to, between the buildings they know, in the radius of their living. But the walkers go farther. The walkers cross the Drowned Quarter from one end to the other, along the catwalks that span the narrow places, across the rooftops of the buildings that cluster close, and they carry what needs to be carried. News. Nets. The pale growth, dried and wrapped in fiber, for the buildings where the harvest was thin. My father could walk the full length of the Quarter in the time between the grey's arrival and the swimmers' surfacing, and he knew every stone, every join, every place where the mortar has softened and the catwalk has settled into a lean that only he can read.

He is gone now. Not dead. Gone, in the way that people sometimes go, which is to say: he walked to the far edge of the Quarter, to the place where the buildings thin and the catwalks end and the open water begins, and he did not come back. The elders did not say he was dead. They said: he walked. They said it the way they say everything — as a fact, not a judgment. The water is the water. The stone is the stone. He walked.

I was old enough to understand this and young enough to accept it, which is the age at which most things that matter are learned here.

---

What I am is this: I am the one who goes to the edges.

Not the far edges. Not the open squares where the deep thing moves and the water goes dark and the buildings stand apart from each other like people who have stopped speaking. The elders go there, sometimes, when the tone from below changes and the birds on the outer buildings lift and circle and resettle in patterns that mean something the elders can read but cannot name to us.

No. I go to the *near* edges. The places where the catwalks are thin and the buildings lean away from each other, where the stone is less sure of itself and the mortar has gone soft in the warm water and the walls are furred with the pale growth in patterns that shift from week to week. I go to the places where the buildings are still standing but only barely, and I test the catwalks, and I note which ones need mending, and I note which ones should be abandoned.

This is necessary work. The catwalks are the streets. If a catwalk fails, the buildings it connects become islands, and the people on them become stranded, and stranded is a thing that does not happen here, because the way of this place is that everyone is reachable, everyone is part of the network of stone and cord and foot that holds the Quarter together.

I am not the only one who does this. There are three of us — me, and old Hesh, who was a walker before the minerals took his ankles, and a girl named Tess who is younger than me and who has the best balance of anyone I have ever seen. She can walk a catwalk no wider than a hand and she will not slow. She should have been a walker. She will be, when she is old enough. For now, she walks the edges with me and with Hesh, and we test the stone, and we come back, and we eat, and the day is the day.

---

I have a building. That is not the right word. I have a room, in a building, on the roof, in the cluster of buildings near the center of the Quarter where the catwalks are widest and the birds are thickest and the swimmers come closest to the surface. The building is called *many-wings* because of the nests — thirty or more, in every gap and corner, the birds coming and going in their silent pattern, the stolen cloth and bone of their construction a patchwork against the old stone.

My room is small. It was my mother's room before me, and her mother's before her, and the walls are worn smooth at the height of a hand — my hand, which is the same size as theirs, because we are the same people and the body repeats itself. There are niches in the brick where we keep things. A jawbone tool, sharpened, the teeth still good. A coil of fiber cord. A flat stone, dark, that I found in the water once when I was diving and that has no use but that I keep because it is smooth and cold even in the warmth and when I hold it I feel something that I do not have a word for.

I sleep in a woven nest above the floor, suspended from ceiling hooks that were placed there so long ago that the stone around them has grown into the hooks, and the hooks are no longer separate from the stone. They are part of it. I sleep alone. This is not unusual. Not everyone sleeps in family clusters. Some of us sleep alone because the aloneness is part of how we are, and the room is warm enough that one body is sufficient to hold the heat, and the quiet is a thing that I value.

The birds outside my door do not sleep either. I hear them sometimes in the dark — not a sound but a movement, a shift of weight, a settling of feathers against stone. They are there. I am here. The distance is constant. The distance is understood.

---

I will tell you what a day is.

The grey comes. I feel it before I see it — a thinning of the dark, a softening, as if the air itself is becoming less dense. I do not move. I wait in my nest, in the warmth that my body has made, and I listen for the birds.

The birds shift. A collective motion. The soft percussion of beaks. The rustle of their stolen materials. This is the first sound, and it means the water has not changed in the night, and the day can begin.

I emerge from my room onto the rooftop. The air is warm and damp. It is always warm and damp. The grey light is diffuse, sourceless, and it shows me the rooftops and the catwalks and the canals and the birds — the birds, everywhere, standing on every edge, every peak, every corner, still as stone, watching the water.

I go to the roof edge. I lower my net. I feel the cords in the water — the current, the temperature, the faint tap of swimmers finding the mesh. I pull. The swimmers are dark and flat and warm in my hands and I set them on the stone and I lower the net again.

This is the morning. This is what the morning is.

Hesh comes across the catwalk from his building — *empty-heart*, the one with no nests, the one where the birds do not stay. He walks slowly now, his ankles stiff, but he walks. He has a net over his shoulder and a jawbone tool in his belt and he nods at me, and I nod at him, and we stand on the rooftop and we eat a swimmer each, raw, the flesh translucent and dark at the center and warm with a warmth that is not ours but that becomes ours when we swallow.

Tess comes last. She comes running across the catwalk — running, the way only children run, the way the rest of us do not because the catwalks are narrow and the canals are deep and the running is a risk that the young take because they do not yet know the cost of falling. She arrives at *many-wings* breathless and grinning and she says nothing, because what is there to say? The day is here. We are here.

We eat. We lower the nets. The birds strike around us, the fast motion that is the only fast thing, and the swimmers are taken, and the morning continues.

---

In the middle of the day — if day is the right word for something that does not change except in the degree of its greyness — I go to the edges.

The catwalks here are different. They are narrower. The stone is less certain. I can feel it in my feet — the way the vibration changes, the way the surface shifts under my weight in a pattern that is not the pattern of the central catwalks. This stone does not sing. This stone is uncertain.

I test each span. I stand at the edge and I press with my foot, and I feel the stone's answer in my ankle and my knee and the architecture of my hip, and I know: this one holds. This one shifts. This one is dead.

The dead ones are marked. We have a way of marking — a notch in the stone, cut with a jawbone tool, at the join where the catwalk meets the building. One notch for *shifts*. Two notches for *dead*. No notch for *holds*. The absence is the approval.

Hesh taught me this. Hesh, who was walking these edges before I was born, who knows the stone the way the elders know the water — by feel, by vibration, by the way it answers when you ask. He taught me to press, to listen, to mark. He taught me that the stone is not passive. The stone speaks. It speaks in the language of settling and shifting and singing, and if you listen, it tells you what it needs.

Some catwalks need mending. We mend them. We pry blocks from the ruined walls of buildings that have already failed — there are a few, at the edges, where the water took the lower floors and the upper floors followed — and we carry them across the catwalks and we set them in place and we bind them with cord. The work is slow. It must be. A catwalk that is mended badly is worse than a catwalk that is not mended at all, because the bad mend will fail without warning, and the stone that fails without warning is the stone that kills.

We do not speak of killing. We do not have a word for it. But we know what the fall means, and the fall is into water that is warm and will not harm you, but the water in the deep canals is not the water in the shallow streets, and the current beneath it is unpredictable, and if you fall in the wrong place, you will swim, and if you cannot find a wall to climb, you will swim until you cannot, and the water will take you the way it took the lower floors.

This has not happened in my time. But the stone remembers, and I remember the stone's memory, and I mend the catwalks with the care that the stone demands.

---

In the afternoon — the deep grey, when the light is at its strongest, which is to say, barely — I go to the water.

Not to dive. I am not a diver. I do not have the lungs for it. My mother's lungs went to my sister, who lives in the building called *old-before-old* and who dives deeper than anyone else in the Quarter. I have the hands and the feet, the grip and the balance, and these are for the catwalks, not the water.

But I go to the water. I sit at the edge of a rooftop and I put my feet in. The warmth is immediate. It is not the warmth of a bath or a fire. It is the warmth of a body — specifically a body, specifically alive — and it holds my ankles the way hands hold, and I feel the current move against my skin, and I feel the vibration of the city through the water, and I am part of it.

The swimmers come. They do not see me — they have no eyes — but they feel my heat, and they avoid me, the school splitting around my feet and reforming on the other side, and I watch them, and they are beautiful in the way that functional things are beautiful. They are what they need to be. Nothing more. Nothing less.

A bird strikes near me. I do not see it coming — no one sees a bird coming — and suddenly the water is broken and the bird has a swimmer and the bird is still again, and the swimmer is gone, and the water closes over the place where it was, and there is nothing.

I sit. I do not move. The water holds my feet. The grey light falls. The birds watch. The swimmers reform.

This is the afternoon.

---

The dark comes, and I do not move.

This is the rule. The dark is absolute. You do not move in the dark unless you must. The catwalks are narrow and the canals are deep and the sounds are different in the dark, and the difference is enough.

I am in my room. I am in my nest. The warmth of the stone rises through the floor — through the suspended platform, through the woven fiber, into my body — and the warmth of my body rises into the air, and the two warmths meet and hold, and I am in a pocket of heat that the building has been making for longer than I have been alive.

I listen.

The murmur. Always the murmur. The subsonic voice of the city, the vibration of water through stone, the sound that is not a sound but a presence, a constant, the breath of the Drowned Quarter itself. I have heard it every moment of my life. I will hear it every moment that remains. It is the first thing and the last thing and the thing between.

The birds. A settling. A shift. The soft percussion of a beak against stone. They are there. They are always there.

And then, on some nights, the tone.

It is low. It is resonant. It comes from the deep canals, from the open squares, from wherever the deep thing lives, and it passes through the water and the stone and the air and my body, and for the moment of its passing, I am not separate. I am not a person on a rooftop in a drowned city. I am the water and the stone and the bird and the swimmer and the growth and the dark and the warmth. I am the Drowned Quarter, and the Drowned Quarter is me, and the distinction between us is a thing that exists only in the daylight and the grey and not here, not in the dark, not when the tone is passing.

It passes. The silence returns. The murmur resumes. I am myself again, which is to say, I am a person in a nest in a room in a building on the water, and I am warm, and I am alone, and the birds are outside, and the water is below, and the stone holds.

I sleep. Or something like sleep. The body rests. The mind rests. The stone does not rest, and the water does not rest, and the birds do not rest, but I am not stone or water or bird. I am the one who rests, and I rest, and the dark holds me the way the water holds my feet, and this is enough.

---

I will tell you what I am.

I am not the diver. That is my sister. She goes down, down, past the second floor, past the third, into the rooms where the growth is thick and the light does not reach, and she comes back with the pale sheets of it, and she is quiet about it, and the elders watch her with an expression that I recognize because it is the same expression my mother wore when she watched me walk the catwalks for the first time. It is the expression of someone watching a person they love do the thing that the place requires of them.

I am not the elder. That is Hesh, and the others who sit on the rooftops and watch the birds and taste the water and know what the deep growth is doing. They have the knowledge that time gives — the slow accumulation of noticing, of pattern, of the way things are and the way things change — and I do not have it yet. I will. If the stone holds and the water is patient and the birds keep watching, I will. But I do not have it now.

I am not the bird. The birds are the watchers. They stand and they wait and they strike and they are still, and their stillness is a thing I cannot touch because I am not made for stillness. I am made for motion — the careful, precise, worn motion of a body on a catwalk, feeling the stone, reading the stone, mending the stone. I am made for the edges.

What I am is the one who keeps the connections.

The catwalks are the life of this place. Not the water — the water is the medium, the below, the thing that everything else lives above. Not the buildings — the buildings are the shelter, the walls, the place where the warmth is kept. The catwalks. The thin, worn, ancient spans of salvaged stone and salvaged wood that connect building to building, roof to roof, person to person. Without the catwalks, we are islands. With them, we are a Quarter.

I test them. I mend them. I mark the ones that are dead. I carry stone from the ruined buildings to the living ones and I set it in place and I bind it with cord and I press it with my foot and I listen for the answer, and the answer is the answer of the stone, and the stone says: I hold. Or: I shift. Or: I am dead. And I mark it, and I move on, and the next span is under my feet and the next question is being asked.

This is what I am. I am the one who asks the stone if it holds, and the stone answers, and the answer matters, and the mattering is enough.

---

I do not know what you are. You are reading this, in a place that is not this place, in a light that is not the grey. You are somewhere else. You are something else.

But you are reading this, and for the time of the reading, you are here. You are on the rooftop of a building in a drowned city, and the water is below you, and the birds are around you, and the warmth is rising from the deep, and the murmur is in your bones, and the catwalk stretches before you, narrow and worn and ancient, and you do not know if it holds.

You press your foot against it. You feel the vibration travel through the stone and into your ankle and your knee and the architecture of your hip. You listen. You wait.

The stone speaks.

You step forward.
