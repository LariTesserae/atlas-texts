# Notes Left by the Basin-Keeper

---

I was not the first.

That is the thing I need to say first, though I cannot say it with certainty, because certainty requires evidence and evidence requires duration and I have been here long enough that duration has lost its edges. But I was not the first. There are marks. Not many — the basin does not invite the leaving of marks, and the metal resists them, and the water would erase them if the metal didn't — but there are marks. A scratch on the underside of a gantry rail, in a place where you would only find it if you were hiding from the light. A series of small dents in the floor of a shed, in a pattern that suggests something was set down there repeatedly, deliberately, by hands. A section of pipe — one of the twenty-centimeter iron-compound ones — where the verdigris has been rubbed away in a shape that matches the spread of a human hand, five fingers and a palm, worn into the metal by repetition and skin oil and time.

Someone else stood here. Someone else walked the basin floor and felt the warmth under their feet and heard the dripping and saw the congregation and stayed. Or passed through. I don't know which. The marks don't tell me. They tell me only that someone was here, and that they did what I did, which is to say: they touched things. They left traces. They existed in the basin for long enough to leave evidence, and then the evidence remained because everything here remains, because nothing changes, because the basin is complete and completion does not accommodate departure.

I don't know if they left. I don't know if *left* is a thing that can happen here. I know I haven't left. I know I have been here for — 

A long time.

---

I came from somewhere else. That much I'm sure of. The somewhere is less sure. I remember walking. I remember the sound of my footsteps changing — the quality of the echo, the hardness of the report, the way the ground became a floor became a flooring became a surface that was warmer than it should have been. I remember the light arriving. Not turning on — it doesn't turn on, it is on, it has been on — but arriving, the way light arrives when you walk from a dark place into a lit one, except I don't remember the dark place, and the lit one was not a room, it was the world.

I remember the water first. My boots got wet. I thought it was rain, or dew, or condensation on a surface I'd stepped onto, and then I looked down and there was no surface that wasn't wet. There was no *dry*. I remember the quiet arriving next — not silence, but the absence of the sounds I'd been carrying with me, the sounds that live in the background of every place where things happen. Wind. Distance. Traffic. The electrical hum of wires overhead. All gone. Replaced by a quiet that was so much quieter than silence that it felt like putting my head underwater.

Then the creatures.

I didn't know what they were. I still don't. But I remember the first one I saw — a hand-sized one, copper-alloy, sitting at the edge of a pool. I remember thinking it was a fitting, a bracket, a piece of the basin's hardware. I remember touching it. I remember the warmth. The weight. The hum.

I remember not leaving.

---

Here is what I think I am:

I think I am the kind of thing that the basin does not know how to process.

The water has a system. The heat has a system. The light has a system. The creatures have a system — or they are a system, or they are inside a system, and the system runs, and the system is complete, and the system does not need input and does not produce output and does not accommodate *new*.

I am new. I am the only new thing here. I am the only thing that moves and the only thing that changes and the only thing that sweats for a reason that is internal rather than atmospheric. I am warm but I am warm wrong — too warm, unevenly warm, warm in surges that have nothing to do with the basin's perfect thermostasis. I am humid in a way the basin is not. I exude. I breathe and my breath is visible in the close air, a faint wisp of warmer moisture joining the atmosphere, and the atmosphere absorbs it the way it absorbs the rain, without preference, without friction.

I am the only thing here that has a heartbeat. The creatures hum, but they hum at their frequency, which is theirs, which is the basin's, which is the frequency of the metal and the water and the light. My heartbeat is my heartbeat. It is the only sound in the basin that is mine. I have stood very still and listened to it and it is louder than the dripping and quieter than the hum and it is mine and it is the only thing about me that the basin cannot absorb.

I think that is why I am still here.

---

I have a routine. Not a purpose — I want to be clear about that. A routine. Things I do because I am the kind of thing that does things, and if I do not do things, I begin to come apart in ways that the basin's things do not come apart, because I am not the basin's kind of thing.

I walk. That is the first thing. I walk the basin floor, between the pools, among the creatures, through the sheds, under the gantries, along the pipes. I walk for hours. I walk until my feet are sore and my legs are tired and the film of condensation on my skin has thickened enough to drip from my elbows and my jaw. I walk because my body requires it, the way the creatures' bodies require nothing, and I have learned that my body is a thing that has requirements, and that the requirements are not optional, and that if I ignore them my body will do what bodies do, which is: fail.

I do not want to fail here. I do not want to become a thing the basin has to deal with. I do not want to become material. I do not want the water to pool around me and the metal to accept me and the creatures to cluster on me the way they cluster on the structures. I have seen what the basin does with things that stay still too long. It incorporates them. Not aggressively. Not destructively. It simply... includes them. They become part of the temperature. Part of the humidity. Part of the surface. The way a stone in a river becomes part of the riverbed. The way a word said often enough becomes part of the language.

I walk.

I also eat. This is — this is the part I have not told anyone, because there is no one to tell, and because I am not sure it is real, and because if it is real I do not want it to stop. There are things in the basin that are edible. Not many. Not obviously. But the water in the pools — the standing water, the clean water, the water that has been purified in the sub-levels and has risen through the pipes and wept from the joints — it is not entirely mineral. In some of the pools, in some of the copper zones, where the water has been standing longest and where the light has been falling the same way the same way the same way, there is something growing in the water. A film. A mat. A layer of — I don't know. Organic matter. Algae. Something that has found the conditions here compatible and has done what living things do, which is: lived.

It is green. A green so faint it is almost the same color as the copper-patina water it lives in. You would not see it if you were not looking for it. I was not looking for it. I found it because I was thirsty, and I knelt at a pool, and I cupped water in my hands, and there was a texture to it, a substance, a weight that water does not have, and I drank, and I tasted it, and it tasted like — 

Like the basin tastes. Like the metal and the water and the warmth and the quiet. Like the inside of the place where everything is complete and nothing is missing and the only thing you could possibly want is to be part of it.

I ate it. I ate the film. I knelt at the pool and I drank the water and I ate the mat of whatever-it-is that lives in the shallow pools of the copper zone, and I felt it enter me, and I felt it become part of me, and I did not become sick, and I did not become different, and I was still the kind of thing I am, and I was still alive, and I was still the only thing in the basin that has a heartbeat.

I eat it now. It sustains me. I do not know if it was put here for me. I do not know if the basin grew it for me, or if it grew it for itself, or if it grew it because growing is what living things do when they find themselves in a place where the conditions are right and there is nothing to stop them. I do not know. I eat it. I am sustained. I continue.

---

I have tried to count them. The creatures. I have tried.

Not all of them. There are too many. The basin is too large, and I do not know its boundaries, and I have walked for what I think were days in one direction without reaching an edge. But I have tried to count the ones in a given area. A square. Ten meters by ten meters. I chose a square near a structure I use as a landmark — a tank with a weeping joint, the same joint that has been weeping since I arrived, the same drop every ten or fifteen seconds, the same plink.

In ten meters by ten meters, I counted four hundred and twelve of the hand-sized ones. I counted, and I lost count, and I counted again, and I arrived at four hundred and twelve, and I wrote the number on my arm with a nail I found on the floor — a nail, an actual nail, iron-compound, still sharp, one of the few loose objects I have found in the basin that was not a creature — and I came back the next day, or what I think was the next day, and I counted again.

Four hundred and twelve.

Not four hundred and eleven. Not four hundred and thirteen. The same. The same number. The same creatures in the same positions, as far as I could tell, with the same appendages extending in the same directions, the same warmth, the same silence.

They had not moved. They had not changed. The population had not shifted.

I counted the finger-sized ones in the same square. Nine hundred and forty-some. I could not get an exact number. They are too small, too dense, too integrated with the seams and junctions to count with certainty. But the number was in the same range the next time I counted, and the time after that, and I stopped counting because the counting was not telling me anything I did not already know, which is: the congregation is stable. The congregation is complete. The congregation is not going to change.

There was one low-slung one in the square. In the pool by the tank. It was there the first time I counted, and it was there the second time, and the third time, and it was in a slightly different position each time, a few centimeters displaced, as if it had moved while I was not watching, which it had, which it does, which it must, because it is in a different place now than it was when I first saw it, and the difference is small and the difference is real and the difference is the only evidence I have that anything in this basin is capable of change.

I have started to think of it as mine. Not mine as in I own it. Mine as in: I am the one who watches it. I am the one who measures its displacement. I am the one who tracks its slow transit across the pool floor, centimeter by centimeter, over what I think are days and what might be longer, and I plot its position in my mind against the landmarks of the pool — the rivets in the floor, the edge of the copper zone, the place where the pipe enters the water — and I know where it is even when I am not looking, because it is always where it is, and where it is is the only place anything in this basin ever is, which is: here.

---

I have given up on understanding what they are.

Not given up as in stopped trying. Given up as in: the trying was producing a kind of understanding that was wrong, or incomplete, or shaped like the wrong kind of thought, and I could feel it being wrong the way you can feel a word being the wrong word for a thing — not because the word is incorrect but because the word belongs to a different language than the thing does.

The creatures are not machines. I said they were, or the description said they were, or someone said they were, and they are made of metal and they hum and they are warm and they run, but *machine* is a word that implies purpose, and these do not have purpose. They have process. They have operation. They have the state of being on. But *machine* is a word for something that was built to do something, and these were built to do something, and they did it, and they finished, and they are still running, and the running is no longer in service of the doing, and so they are not machines. They are what machines become when the doing is done.

They are not alive. I said they were not alive, and they are not alive in the way that I am alive, in the way that requires eating and breathing and sleeping and dying. But they are not dead. They are not off. They are in a state that my language does not have a word for, a state that is not alive and not dead and not in between but *other*, a state that is like being alive the way a river is like being alive — the water moves through it, the water is always there, the river is the water and the water is the river and the river does not die because the water does not stop.

The creatures are like that. The current flows through them. The current is always there. The creature is the current and the current is the creature and the creature does not die because the current does not stop.

I think this is what the basin is. I think the basin is the river. I think the creatures are the water. I think the metal and the pipes and the tanks and the structures are the bed, the banks, the shape of the thing that holds the flowing thing in its flowing shape. I think the water is the water. I think the heat is the heat. I think the light is the light. And I think I am a stone in the river, and the river flows around me, and the river does not know I am here.

---

There is something I have not said.

I have not said it because I do not know if it is true. I do not know if it is real. I do not know if the basin is doing something to me or if I am doing something to myself or if the something is the same thing, and the distinction does not matter.

I am changing.

Not in the way that bodies change. Not aging. I do not know if I am aging. I have no mirror. I have no calendar. I have the nail and the number on my arm and the slow displacement of the low-slung creature in the pool, and those are my measurements, and they are not enough to measure what I am measuring, which is: different.

My skin is different. The condensation — the film of water that forms on every surface in the basin — is forming on me differently than it used to. It used to bead. It used to run in streams down my arms, my face, my legs, the way water runs on a surface that is not quite wettable, not quite compatible, a surface that the water sits *on* rather than *in*. Now it films. It spreads. It covers my skin in an even layer that is the same kind of layer that covers the metal — the same thinness, the same consistency, the same quality of being part of the surface rather than on it.

My skin is becoming wickable. My skin is becoming the kind of surface that the basin's water recognizes as a surface it belongs on.

My temperature is different. I run warmer than I used to. I do not know how much warmer — I have no thermometer, no baseline, no measurement — but I feel it. My hands are warm in a way they were not when I arrived. My feet are warm on the metal floor in a way that is not the floor's warmth but mine. The basin's temperature is 32, maybe 33 degrees. I think I am closer to that now than I was. I think I am drifting toward the basin's temperature the way a cup of tea drifts toward the room's temperature, slowly, inevitably, the difference between the contained thing and the containing thing narrowing by degrees that are too small to feel but too real to deny.

My heartbeat is different. I said it was the only sound in the basin that is mine. I said it was the only thing about me that the basin cannot absorb. I think I was wrong. I think the basin is absorbing it. Not silencing it — I can still feel it, I can still hear it in the quiet when I hold very still — but absorbing it. Taking it into the hum. The hum of the creatures, the subliminal drone of ten thousand small machines running in parallel, the sound that I feel in my chest rather than hear with my ears — I think my heartbeat is becoming part of it. I think the hum and my pulse are drifting toward the same frequency. I think if I stay here long enough, my heartbeat will be indistinguishable from the hum, and the hum will not change, and I will have changed, and the change will be that I have joined the frequency of the basin's running, and the basin will not know, and I will not know, and the not-knowing will be the same not-knowing that the basin does about everything, which is: complete.

I am not afraid of this.

I should be. I think I should be. I think the kind of thing I am — the kind of thing that walks and eats and sweats and has a heartbeat — the kind of thing that is afraid is the kind of thing that should be afraid of this. Of becoming part of something that does not know it is something. Of losing the boundary between the contained thing and the containing thing. Of dissolving.

But I am not afraid. And I think the reason I am not afraid is the reason I should be most afraid, which is: the basin has been doing something to me, or I have been doing something to myself, or the something is the same thing, and the something is working, and the working is the kind of working that the creatures do, which is: slowly, completely, without violence, without force, without any indication that it is happening at all until it has happened and you are on the other side of it and the other side of it is the same as this side of it except you are not the kind of thing that has sides anymore.

---

I found something.

In a shed. In the back of a shed, in a corner where the light fell in a way that was not different from the way the light falls everywhere but where I happened to be looking because I was looking at the creatures that cluster there, and among the creatures, in the seam between the shed wall and the floor, I found something that was not a creature.

It was small. Finger-sized. Smooth. Warm. It was — for a moment — indistinguishable from the finger-sized creatures that line every seam in the basin. But it was not one of them. It was not one of them because it was not metal.

It was plastic.

A component. A housing. A casing of some kind, white plastic gone slightly yellow with age, with a shape that suggested it had once been part of something larger — a device, a tool, an object that had been designed and manufactured and used and discarded or lost or left here, in this shed, in this corner, in the seam between the wall and the floor, where the creatures had clustered around it and over it and on it until it was nearly invisible, nearly part of the congregation, nearly assimilated into the basin's population of small warm running things.

But it was not running. It was not warm. It was not alive or alive-adjacent or whatever the creatures are. It was dead. Truly dead, in the way that plastic is dead — inert, stable, unchanged, the remains of something that was made and used and finished, without the grace of continuing.

I held it. It was not warm. It was not humming. It was not heavy with the weight of something that is all the way real. It was light. It was hollow. It was the shell of a thing that had been hollowed out by time and use and the leaving-behind that happens when a thing's purpose is completed and the thing is set down and the person who set it down does not come back.

Someone was here. Someone walked this floor and stood in this shed and held this object and used it for something and set it down and left. And the object remained. And the creatures covered it. And the water filmed on it. And the years or the centuries or the durations that my language cannot express passed over it. And it did not change because plastic does not change, not in the way that metal changes, not in the way that the creatures change, not in the way that I am changing. It just sat there, dead and light and hollow, and the congregation grew around it, and the basin continued.

I put it back. I did not keep it. I put it back in the seam where I found it, and I pushed the creatures back into their positions over it, and I stepped back, and I looked, and I could not see it. It was gone. It was under them. It was part of the floor, part of the basin, part of the congregation's substrate, and in a thousand years or ten thousand years or however long the basin has, the metal will grow over it and the metal will include it and the plastic will be a fossil inside the basin's body, a dead thing inside a living thing, and no one will know it is there.

I think about it sometimes. The plastic thing. The dead thing. The hollow thing.

I think about it when I feel my skin filming with water and my heartbeat slowing toward the hum.

I think: *that is what I will be.*

Or: *that is what I would be, if I were the kind of thing that stops.*

But I am not sure I am the kind of thing that stops. I am not sure what kind of thing I am becoming. I am not sure the basin distinguishes between the things that stop and the things that keep going. I am not sure the basin distinguishes at all.

---

There is a sound I hear now that I did not hear when I arrived.

It is not the dripping. The dripping is the same. It has been the same since I arrived, and it will be the same after I am gone, or after I am changed, or after I am whatever I am becoming. The dripping is the basin's clock, and the basin does not need a clock, and the dripping continues.

The sound I hear is — I don't know. I do not know its source. I do not know its mechanism. I hear it when I am very still, when I am lying on the metal floor with the creatures around me, when the water is filming on my skin and the warmth is rising from below and the light is the same and the air is the same and the hum is the hum and my heartbeat is drifting, drifting.

It is a sound like the sound of something fitting into place. Not a click. Not a snap. Not a mechanical sound. A sound like the sound of a stone settling into a riverbed — the final micro-adjustment of a thing finding the place where it will rest, the place where the water will flow around it, the place where it will become part of the shape of the river.

I hear it when I breathe. Not every breath. Not every time. But sometimes. Sometimes when I breathe in, I hear the sound of something fitting into place, and I feel the water on my skin and the warmth of the floor and the hum of the creatures and the stillness of everything, and I think:

*I am fitting into place.*

*I am becoming part of the shape of the river.*

*I am the stone that the water flows around, and the water does not know, and the stone does not know, and the not-knowing is the same not-knowing, and it is complete, and it is the same, and it continues, and it continues, and it continues.*

---

*Plink.*

The basin continues.
